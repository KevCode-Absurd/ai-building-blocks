# How we write copy

These are rules, not suggestions. They apply to every word on the site and to every block's name, headline, subhead and summary. Set by Kevin, 18 Oct 2026.

## Promise the outcome

Every headline answers one question: **what does the reader get at the end?** Not what the skill does. What changes for them.

The formula: **[the outcome they want] + [how, when it adds trust]**.

| Don't write (a task) | Write (the outcome) |
|---|---|
| Audit your landing page | Get more sales from your landing page with a 30-point checklist |
| Run a 30-point check against a page | Find what's stopping visitors from buying, in 30 checks |
| Plan your tests | Know which ads to test next, and why |
| Meta ad policy check | Keep Facebook from blocking your ads, before you hit publish |
| Brand voice check | Make sure every ad sounds like your brand |
| Video script from customer research | Write a video ad script in your customers' own words |
| Reviews to ad angles | Turn your reviews into ad ideas your buyers already believe |
| Landing page layout from reference | Borrow the layout of a page you admire, for your own brand |
| Ad performance drop diagnosis | Find out why your ads stopped selling, and what to fix first |

Numbers in a headline must be true. "30-point checklist" only if the skill has 30 checks. Never invent a result, a time saving or a percentage.

Energetic outcome verbs are good when the promise is true: get, win, sell more, grow, boost, stop, keep, turn, fix, find out. Hype words with nothing behind them are not: "skyrocket", "explode", "10x" (unless it is literally ten times), "ultimate", "secret".

## Principles

1. **Clarity over cleverness.** If you have to choose between clear and creative, choose clear. Clear copy also sells better: when a reader has to decode a line, you've lost them.
2. **Benefits over features.** A feature is what it does. A benefit is what that means for the customer. Lead with the benefit; the feature can follow as the reason to believe.
3. **Specific over vague.** "Save time on your workflow" says nothing. "Cut your weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes" paints a picture. Use real numbers, real nouns, real examples.
4. **Customer language over company language.** Use the words customers use in reviews, interviews and support tickets. They say "my ads stopped working", not "creative fatigue".
5. **One idea per section.** Each section makes one point and moves the reader one step down the page.

## Style

1. **Simple over complex.** "Use", not "utilize". "Help", not "facilitate".
2. **Specific over vague.** Avoid "streamline", "optimize", "innovative", "powerful".
3. **Active over passive.** "It checks every line", not "every line is checked".
4. **Confident over qualified.** Cut "almost", "very", "really", "quite".
5. **Show, don't tell.** Describe the outcome instead of piling on adverbs.
6. **Honest over sensational.** Never make up a number, a testimonial or a result. Made-up statistics lose trust and can break the law.
7. **Real businesses in examples** (Kevin, 5 Nov 2026). Use real, named businesses that readers recognise and can check, not made-up or disguised ones. State only public facts and link the source. Never say they used AI Blocks or AI, or got a result, without a public source. Label any copy written for them as an example made for the page.

## Headings and subheads are never vague

Kevin, 18 Oct 2026: be most specific where people read first. A heading or subhead passes only if a stranger can say, after one read, **what they get, for what, and what they do to get it**.

| Vague | Specific |
|---|---|
| Save time on your workflow | Cut your weekly reporting from 4 hours to 15 minutes |
| Get the most out of it | The three inputs that change the result most |
| Powerful tools for marketers | Ad copy, landing page fixes and video scripts, written by your AI |
| Install it | Add it to Claude once, then ask for it in any chat |

- Name the deliverable (a script, a 30-point checklist, a table of objections), the reader (owners, media buyers) or the number, whichever makes the line concrete.
- Keep it logically true. If a heading promises "in any chat", the steps under it must deliver that in every tab.
- Keep it short. One idea, then stop.

## No AI tells

Buyers reject copy that reads as AI-written, and once they spot one tell they doubt the claims around it. Write like a senior copywriter: specific, uneven in rhythm, built from facts.

Never write these:

1. **Contrast reveals.** "It's not X, it's Y." "Not because X. Because Y." "Not just X, but Y." Say Y, with the reason.
2. **Negation lists.** "No setup call, no templates, no waiting on IT." Say what does happen. A real absence gets said once, near the button ("No card required").
3. **Trailing pile-ons.** A full claim, then a comma and more clauses: "...the data you already have, no exports, no spreadsheets." End the sentence at the claim.
4. **Self-answered questions and colon reveals.** "The result? 3x faster." "The best part: it learns." Just say it. FAQ questions and the reader's own question ("Need to share a screenshot?") are fine.
5. **Stock openers.** "In today's fast-paced world", "Whether you're X or Y", "Here's the thing", "Say goodbye to", "X, reimagined", "Unlock the power of", "Take it to the next level".
6. **Em dashes in short copy.** Headlines, subheads, buttons, ads, posts and subject lines use none.

## Quick quality check

Before anything ships, read it once for each:

- Any AI tell from the list above?
- Jargon an outsider wouldn't know?
- A sentence doing two jobs?
- Passive voice?
- An exclamation mark? (remove it)
- A buzzword with nothing behind it?

For a line-by-line pass, run the copy editing skill after the draft.

## Techniques that work

- **Be direct.** Get to the point. Don't bury the value under qualifiers.
  - Weak: "Our skill lets you review your ads against a range of policy areas, from health claims to before-and-after images."
  - Strong: "About to run an ad? Find the lines that would get it rejected, and fix them first."
- **Ask the reader about their situation.** "Ads stopped selling?" "Tired of rewriting the same product page?" A question that names their pain makes them picture it, so it can open a section. Use it to open, not on every line. A question that labels your own claim ("The result? 3x faster.") is an AI tell.
- **Use an analogy when it makes something abstract concrete.** "A brand file is the briefing you'd give a new hire, written once."
- **A little humour, when it fits.** A dry line makes copy memorable. Never at the cost of clarity, and never on a page about a serious problem.

## Names and headlines of blocks (skills and prompts)

Kevin, 19 Oct 2026: think of each skill or prompt as someone you hire. Its name is the job title, so a stranger reads it and knows at once what they'd hire it for. "Meta ads compliance checker" is the model.

- **Name = the job title.** What it works on, then the role: checker, writer, planner, finder, auditor, editor, scorer, maker. 2 to 6 words (7 at most), sentence case. Name the platform when the job is tied to one (Meta, Google, Shopify, TikTok). No colons, no internal jargon, no clever words. Similar skills share one spelling: "Clay town video ad script writer" and "Clay town video clip prompt writer". Image ads all end "image ad maker".
- **It reads naturally (Kevin, 19 Oct 2026, later).** Say it out loud: it should sound like something a person would say, and make sense the moment you see it. Use the words people already use (hook, influencer, swipe file, AI slop), and say where the input comes from when that's the point: "Video ad script writer from customer quotes", "Website brand brain builder", "Anti-AI-slop copy editor". "Anime video clip prompt writer" is the bar.
- **Headline = the name.** The page's big title is the same job title.
- **Subhead = the job description.** Two or three short sentences: what you give it, what it does, what you get. Name the real outputs, and the score if it has one. 25 to 50 words.
- **Summary (the card line) = the subhead squeezed:** "Paste X. Get Y and Z." ("and a score out of 100" only for a skill that scores). 18 words max.
- **General, never one industry.** Write for anyone: a job or a business, any field. Industry versions come later as their own skills.
- **A score only where judging is the job.** Checkers, audits, scorers, comparisons and data analysis give a score out of 100. Writers, makers, planners, builders and editors don't: they run their checks, fix what fails and say any check they missed.
- **True and logical.** Promise only what the skill really does. If the subhead says "a score", the skill must give one.

| Vague | The job |
|---|---|
| Keep Facebook from blocking your ads | Meta ads compliance checker |
| Make AI build the ad you pictured | Image ad settings picker |
| Write ads people believe | Fact-based ad copy writer |

Workflows keep their own names for now.

## Headlines of blocks (older rule, workflows only)

Every prompt, skill and workflow headline is short and sweet: 4 to 10 words that say the problem it solves or the result you get, in words a stranger understands on one read. Kevin, 18 Oct 2026.

| Too long | Short and to the point |
|---|---|
| Ads people believe, because every line rests on a real fact about your product | Write ads people believe |
| Know whether a story ad suits your product, and what to test, before writing a word | Know if a story ad suits your product |
| Get a timed shot list that keeps something new on screen every two seconds | A shot list with something new every two seconds |

The how and the proof go in the subhead, never in a "because" or "with" tail on the headline.

## Names of blocks (older rule, workflows only)

A block's name is its card title, so it's the first promise people read. Same rule as headlines, shorter: the outcome, verb first, sentence case, about 4 to 9 words. "Keep Facebook from blocking your ads", "Turn reviews into image ads that sell", "Borrow a landing page layout you admire". No colons, no brand names, no internal jargon (see `docs/WRITING.md`).

## Calls to action

Say exactly what happens: "Copy the skill", "Download the workflow", "Open in the builder". The same action keeps the same words everywhere, so "Download the workflow" is never "Export pack" somewhere else.

## Before it ships

Run `CHECKLIST.md`. For a line-by-line edit, use the copy editing skill in the library ("Edit a draft so it reads clean and sells").

Blog posts and guides read at grade 8 to 9 (Kevin, 5 Nov 2026). Grade 8 is the floor as well as 9 the ceiling, because very short, choppy sentences read as too simple for owners and marketers. Measure the prose only, meaning paragraphs and list items, with `node tools/readability.mjs`. Prompts, tables and headings drag the score down by several grades, so leave them out. Everything else a visitor reads stays at grade 10 or below. The `plain-writing` skill (`.claude/skills/plain-writing/SKILL.md`) has the voice, the word swaps and the steps for importing a block. Check any text with `node tools/reading-level.mjs`.

## Natural names come first (21 October 2026)

Kevin's latest review takes priority over the mechanical job-title formula above. Use the familiar name of the job: “Landing page audit”, “Ad hook writer”, “Brand profile builder”. A role suffix is optional when it makes the name harder to say. Avoid stacks of internal nouns such as “ad claim proof gap finder” and vague roles such as “keeper”. Keep the meaningful difference between similar jobs.

A card description says what the person provides and what useful result they get. Keep specialist names when they identify the actual tool or method the reader needs. Never imply a draft was sent, a live account changed, or an outcome proved unless the skill actually does that. Approved display edits live in `catalogue-items/display-copy.yaml`; the build applies them across cards, pages, packs and search metadata while preserving licensed skill source files and stable URLs.

## Catalogue clarity update (23 October 2026)

Kevin's latest instructions take priority over the older word limits above. Apply them to skills, prompts, agents, routines, workflows, tools and minds.

- Use a familiar, specific job name. Examples include Account balance change tracker, Team routines and ownership planner, Indian company profile writer, and Book value per share calculator. Keep the words that tell similar jobs apart. Keep people's names for minds and product names for named tools.
- Aim for grade 8–9 reading. Grade 10 is acceptable when the wording reads naturally. Keep a needed coding, marketing or other task term when a simpler substitute would hide the job. Explain it briefly where that helps.
- Card descriptions can use up to 25 words. Say what the reader brings and what useful result they get. Crisp and clear matters more than filling the word count.
- Introductions can exceed 50 words when the detail helps explain the inputs, process, result or limits. Cut repetition and generic filler.
- Keep licensed source instructions intact. A copy review is separate from a runtime test; award a Tested badge only under the existing evidence rules.
