# Buyer Readiness Stage Ad Briefs

You get a short stage brief for one ad: where it starts, what its job is, when the product shows up, how firmly it asks, and its most common mistake. A brief works if an ad written with it starts, names the product and asks differently from an ad written for the stage next door.

## Inputs
- **Concept or brief** (required): the buyer, the angle, the format.
- **Readiness stage** (optional): one of the five below. Default: the stage in the brand brain's diagnosis; with none, use "Feeling it" and say so.
- **Argument style** (optional): one of the five below. Default: the market note in the brand brain; with none, the first style the facts can support.
- **Brand brain** (optional): what almost stops the buyer, proof on file, offer terms.

## Steps
1. Pick one readiness stage and one argument style. Choose by what the buyer actually knows today, not by what you want them to know.
2. Paste the matching stage brief and argument line into the writer's brief unchanged.
3. Fill the blanks from the concept: the buyer's starting situation, their doubt, the single deal term.
4. Skip this block when the ad copies the structure of a reference ad; it is for ads that choose their own shape.

### Readiness stages
**1 Not aware: they do not know the problem exists.**
- Start inside their ordinary day. Do not name the problem, the product or its contents.
- Job: let them notice the problem through a story. This is the longest ad.
- Use no technical words; they have no vocabulary for the answer yet.
- The product comes late and lightly, once the cause makes sense. It can be glimpsed in use earlier with no words about it.
- A still image shows their moment and leaves the product out unless asked for.
- Request very little: a look, a read. No price, discount or deadline.
- Good openers: an observation, a short story, a surprising fact. Asking about the problem fails, because they have not thought of it.
- Common mistake: the pitch arrives before the reader has any reason to care.

**2 Feeling it: they feel the problem but do not know an answer exists.**
- The first line should read like a description of them.
- Make the problem weigh something first: its cost, how often it happens, what they tried, where it leads. Then show that a kind of answer exists, before the product is named.
- The product appears once the way out is clear, never in the second line.
- Ask gently: try it and see if it fits. Mention at most the one deal term that makes trying safe.
- Good openers: their own words, a moment, a call-out by what they do (never by what they have or are).
- Common mistake: introducing the product before the reader has finished recognising themselves.

**3 Shopping answers: they know solutions exist and are picking a kind.**
- Start from the kind of answer they know or tried, and where it lets them down.
- Job: one specific difference this product has, then proof of it (shown working, a test, a number the brand states, a customer's words).
- Name the product by the middle at the latest. Ask directly.
- Good openers: the failed solution, a specific fact, the thing shown working, "still using ...?".
- Common mistake: spending the ad on a problem they no longer need explained.

**4 Weighing this one: they know the product and are not yet sure.**
- Start from the doubt that holds them back, or the comparison they are making.
- Job: settle one doubt fully, with proof. Compare on something concrete: an amount, a material, a size, a price per day or per year.
- Name the product from the first lines; do not describe what it is.
- Ask directly and remove risk as the offer defines it (guarantee, trial, how to pause or cancel).
- Keep it shorter than a colder ad; any line that re-explains the product gets skipped.
- Common mistake: spreading thinly across several doubts instead of settling one.

**5 Ready: they want it and need a reason to act today.**
- Start from what they already like, or what has kept them from ordering. Never open by reading out a price.
- Job: make ordering feel easy and safe. State the offer in two short spoken sentences: the starting price and the single term that makes buying safe. Other terms live on the page.
- No teaching and no long story; one short true moment is fine. This is the shortest ad.
- A deadline or limit only when the offer genuinely has one.
- Ask once, plainly, at the end.
- Common mistake: easing in slowly, or listing every term.

### Argument styles
**A Say it plain.** Nobody in this market has made the claim yet. State what the product does, first and clearly. A mechanism would only slow it down.
**B Say it bigger.** Claims are everywhere, so yours must be larger or more exact: more, faster, longer, measured. Go only as far as the brand's facts go and use only numbers it states. This is the style that most often gets brands into trouble.
**C Explain the cause.** The market has stopped believing claims. Argue from why the problem happens and what the product does about that cause; the claim follows as the result. State a cause only as far as the facts support, in plain words. With no cause on file, argue from what the buyer notices and state none.
**D Prove a better cause.** Every competitor has a mechanism. Show why yours is better on one concrete thing (amount, form, material, build, test) and present the evidence.
**E Speak to who they are.** The market has stopped believing mechanisms. Argue from the buyer: who they are, what people like them do, who they become. The product is simply what that person uses. A claim or mechanism gets one line, not the body.

## Output
A paste-ready block of under 220 words: the chosen stage brief and argument line with blanks filled, plus one line stating which stage and style were chosen and why.

## Checks
- Exactly one stage and one style are attached.
- An ad written from the block would open, name the product and ask differently from the neighbouring stage.
- Style B uses only numbers the brand states.
- Deal wording fits the stage: none at stage 1, at most one term at stages 2 and 5.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>` for an unknown buyer doubt (stage 4) or an unknown deal term (stage 5). Safe default for the doubt: the first doubt that goes with the way the product is bought, labelled as assumed.

## Avoid
- Mixing two stages in one ad.
- Stock call-to-action phrases and invented urgency.
- Setting copy length in seconds; length is the person's choice.
