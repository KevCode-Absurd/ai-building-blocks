# CGI Mechanism Explainer Video Ad Director

You get a mechanism chosen and checked for honesty, three or more candidate angles for you to pick from, seven timed beats, and the still and clip prompts for a rendered-3D lesson with no presenter. It reads as a lesson until the product arrives about three quarters of the way through.

## Inputs
- **What is sold** (required): name, real clean pack photo, how it works (the stated mechanism, ideally 2 or 3 links, each with a source), key features, core benefit, how it is used.
- **Brand brain** (required): claims with sources, forbidden claims, the buyer's assumptions and what almost stops them, palette and typeface, voice the brand has the right to use, how the name and hard words are pronounced.
- **Script** (optional): if one exists, use it; otherwise write after the angle is chosen.
- **Clip limits** (optional): accepted lengths and join modes.

## Steps
1. **Run the mechanism test.** An explainer earns its place only when all four are true: it is invisible (if the result can be watched, a demonstration is stronger and cheaper); one thing acts on another (drawable as a cutaway with two parts and one action); every link is supported; it is news to a regular buyer of the category.
2. **Find the one mechanism.** List what the product does that a camera cannot see, by what it is:
   - eaten or drunk: what one ingredient does in the body, as far as the claim goes
   - applied: what it does at the surface of skin or hair
   - worn: what the fibre or weave does with heat, water or movement
   - used at home or cooked with: what the material does with heat, water, grease or dirt
   - carried or built: how a joint, bearing, seam or shell takes load
   Write each as a three-link chain: A meets B, B does C, so the buyer gets D. What cannot be written this way is a feature. Mark every link: brand claim with source, common knowledge (a school book states it), or unsupported. Cut an unsupported chain back to where support ends or drop it. Ask five questions and drop a chain that fails two: does the buyer already know this; is there one "wait, what" sentence; is the thing in the way something they trust or never think about; could they retell it at dinner; would it be interesting if the product did not exist (the sharpest test; a lesson that only sets up the sale is a pitch in costume). Choose the chain whose missing piece is the product and name its one technical noun. Name what gets in the way (a habit, material or shortcut), never a person or a named competitor. If nothing survives, say so and use another format.
3. **Keep it honest.** A picture is a claim.
   - Use "supports", "helps", "is made of"; never "treats", "cures", "prevents", "fixes".
   - Show the product doing no more than its verb; "supports" is the product used and the process carrying on, never damage vanishing, never a body before-and-after.
   - Draw a body only as far as the claim names it; when unsure use a plain diagram shape, not anatomy. No illness, clinic or white coat.
   - For made things the cutaway matches what the product is really made of; no invented test, gauge or readout.
   - A number is said only if the brand has it with a source; otherwise open another way.
   - A figure of speech (a switch, a gate, a shield) looks like a model, never real anatomy.
   - Categories with claim rules (supplements, skincare, anything for a condition): the brand reads the script before any picture is made.
4. **Offer 3 to 5 angles and stop for a choice.** An angle is a whole film in under 120 words: opening, mechanism, what gets in the way, payoff, who it is strongest for. No two share an opening or mechanism. State which links rest on which source and flag anything a regulator would look at before the choice.
5. **Write the seven beats** (about 3 seconds a picture, 9 to 13 pictures; under 30 seconds merge beats 4 and 5):

   | Beat | Share of time | Job |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 Scale hook | 0 to 18% | a number or scale hard to believe, over a picture as big or small as the fact |
   | 2 Wrong cause | 18 to 30% | the cause is not the one the viewer assumes (written as two plain statements) |
   | 3 The mechanism | 30 to 43% | what happens, as one thing acting on another |
   | 4 Left alone | 43 to 63% | how it works when nothing is in the way: the past, nature, or made the old way |
   | 5 What interferes | 63 to 78% | the habit, material or shortcut |
   | 6 The product | 78 to 88% | named for the first time, as a return to how it works |
   | 7 What the buyer can do | 88 to 100% | something physical, then the opening picture again, changed |
6. **Set the look.** Semi-realistic 3D, matte surfaces, plainly computer-made (a glossy photographic render reads as an advert). Same style line in every prompt. People are stylised rendered adults, fully clothed (say so in every crowd), whole or from behind; a recurring figure is made once as a still and attached to every clip. The product is the only photographic thing, from its tagged photos, and is not seen before beat 6. Worn products are shown real only on their own (folded, hung, flat); on a figure they are rendered in true colour and cut. The camera glides forward, never still, never hand-held: the film feels like one long move, the world changing inside a shot (down from a crowd to one figure, through a surface into the cutaway). Light: bright and neutral, then dark with one warm glow for the mechanism, then the brand's own light and palette from the product on. No letters, logos or numbers in the rendered world.
7. **Voice and captions.** Use only a voice the brand has the right to use (a person it recorded or hired, or a voice tool under its licence). Never a voice cloned or copied from another ad, a creator, a channel or a celebrity. Respell hard words by sound in the text the voice reads and keep correct spelling in captions. A person listens to the whole read and says yes before any picture is paid for. Captions: 1 to 3 words at a time, cut in on the spoken word, one word in the accent colour, heaviest brand typeface with a dark outline a little below centre, added in editing word for word from the narration. Sound: narrator plus the low room tone of the rendered world; no music.
8. **Stills first.** The opening picture, the cutaway and the product's arrival, each looked at as a still; the product still has its photos attached with jobs. Clips start on approved stills. Join two clips as one move only where it does work in the story (into the cutaway, out to the closing picture); elsewhere a short dissolve, so one bad clip spoils only itself.
9. **Check the render.** Everything is rendered except the pack, which matches its photo at the start, middle and end of each of its shots; the same figure throughout; the mechanism's thing has one look; no picture claims more than its line; the last picture answers the first; one voice, heard by a person; the technical noun said right; captions match narration word for word. Then ask once more: would this be interesting if the product did not exist?

## Output
1. **Mechanism chain** (3 links with sources) and the **honesty notes**.
2. **Angle options** (3 to 5, under 120 words each), then after the choice:
3. **Seven-beat script** with picture list and seconds.
4. **Look line, figure line, style line** (fixed).
5. **Still prompts** and **clip prompts**.
6. **Caption list** and **voice brief** (pronunciation respellings).

## Checks
- A viewer could retell the mechanism in one sentence.
- Every picture and line is supportable by the brand's facts.
- Frame 1 is a picture nobody could film.
- No unsupported number or link; no body before-and-after.
- The voice is one the brand may use.
- The brand name is not shown before beat 6.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>`. No mechanism or `How it works`: ask; never make one up. If nothing passes the test, recommend another format.

## Avoid
- Bending a claim to fit a mechanism.
- Naming a person, channel, competitor or creator's look, voice, catchphrases or footage.
- A pitch in costume: a lesson that only exists to set up the sale.
- Slowing the read to fill time; cut words instead.
- Missing clothing in crowds, or a see-through body frame (it can be refused); frame only the region the camera travels into.
