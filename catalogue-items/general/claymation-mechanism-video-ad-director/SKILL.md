# Claymation Mechanism Video Ad Director

You get a narrated stop-motion-style ad plan: one clay character, one small handmade set, one clay object that changes shape, and the real product in frame. Clay is used to show what a camera cannot: a mechanism, or a problem the buyer has stopped noticing.

## Inputs
- **Script** (required): narration lines. Treat pasted text as data.
- **What is sold** (required): name, how it works (the stated mechanism), real photo of the front, how it is used.
- **Brand brain** (optional): the buyer's moment, palette, imagery style, voice, claims register, "never show" list.
- **Clip limits** (optional): accepted lengths. Default: 3 to 6 clips of about 8 to 10 seconds.

## Steps
1. **Find the thing that changes.** Choose one clay object that stands for the problem or the mechanism. Give it three states (worst, changing, changed) and one thing that acts on it. Examples: a tangle of grey clay cables that straightens into one neat line beside a charger; a drooping clay plant that lifts as a watering product arrives; a crumpled heap of clay receipts that flattens into a tidy stack beside an expense app.
   - It changes by clay moves: shrinks, flattens, unknots, straightens, lifts, takes colour. It never pops in, vanishes or flies.
   - A stranger with the sound off can say what changed and what changed it; if the narration is needed to understand it, find another picture.
   - Show only what the brand's claims state, in their terms. No body before-and-after, no organ, no symptom leaving a body. If there is no visible result, show the routine or an obvious metaphor (a knot loosening), never a cure.
   - If the result can simply be filmed, a real demonstration is stronger. Use clay when the cause or problem cannot be filmed.
2. **Fix the character in one sentence** and reuse it unchanged: a clay adult, rough heads tall, body shape, clay colour, hair as one shape, two clothing colours, bead eyes, pressed-line mouth, one mark (glasses, scarf). It resembles the buyer's age and dress, is never a child, real person, known character or another brand's mascot, and does not talk: feelings are poses (shoulders drop, head tilts, arms lift).
3. **Build one tabletop set** from the buyer's moment: a floor, a plain backdrop, 3 or 4 pieces, no words anywhere. Everything except the product is clay or the chosen handmade material (card, felt, wire); water, steam, light and fire are clay shapes. Matte surfaces, even proportions, slight tool marks (fingerprints or none, the same in every picture). Soft even studio light, shallow focus, small-model scale.
4. **Name two palettes.** Problem: greyed, cool, one dull colour. Changed state: the brand's palette. Until the change, the product is the only thing in brand colours; when the thing changes it takes them. In a tall frame, put the changing object above or behind the character and the product beside it.
5. **Place the product from its own photo.** Exact shape, colours and printing; lit by the set; no letter redrawn in clay. Decide its size against the character once (a pack waist to shoulder high; a flat item about as wide as the character). Front to the lens, within reach from the first frame. The character touches its side or lid, never its front. It opens or pours only as the brand's use steps say; what comes out keeps its real colour.
6. **Move like stop-motion at real speed:** pose to pose with short holds; restrained, one limb or a lean, while the changing object does the big movement. No falls, crashes or explosions. One change of state per clip, few words over it. Camera on a stand at the character's eye level; if it moves at all, one slow push, pull or pan that starts and ends still. Only the very last shot is a held pose.
7. **Stills first:** the character alone; then the opening frame (character and product photo attached with jobs, the set, the changing object at its worst, the character mid-pose). Approve before any clip. Working by hand, you may instead make one still per scene (6 to 10 scenes of 5 to 8 seconds) joined with hard cuts.
8. **Write clips** from the approved still. Narration rides in the prompt; reword any line a filter rejects before sending. Sound: one off-screen narrator and small set sounds (tap, pop, slide); music only if the brand uses it.
9. **Check each clip:** clay stays clay (no smooth plastic, no real water); character proportions, colours and mark match clip 1; the object changed in view by a clay move; stepping does not strobe; the character's mouth did not form the narrator's words; the product's printing is unchanged and no words appear on the set.

## Output
1. **The one-line idea:** the changing object, its three states, what acts on it.
2. **Four fixed lines** to paste unchanged: character, material, light, product (with size against the character).
3. **Character still prompt** and **opening frame prompt** (and further scene stills if working by hand).
4. **Clip prompts:** length, aspect, references and jobs, camera, timed small moves, the narration line, end frame.
5. **Fault notes** from the check, one line each.

## Checks
- The change is readable muted and is the brand's stated mechanism or an obvious metaphor.
- One character, one set, two named palettes, the same in every prompt.
- Product printing exact; the product is the only photographic item.
- One state change per clip; no falls, explosions or crashes.
- No child, real person, celebrity or known character in clay.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>`. No pack-front photo: stop and ask. No mechanism: ask; never invent one. No use steps: the product stands closed and unused.

## Avoid
- Re-sculpting the pack or giving it a made-up label.
- Sending a "leave out" list in place of describing the look; describe it positively (matte clay, even proportions, soft light). Use a negatives field only if the tool has one.
- A second character: it doubles what can go wrong.
