# Desire section writer

Copy cannot make a buyer want something new. It can make a want that is already there sharper, clearer and more specific, until the price stops being the issue. This skill takes the want and the product facts and writes the section of copy that does that. It does not write the headline, the proof or the close.

Not for: a finished page that needs a proof or guarantee section (use "Belief ladder builder"), or tone and flow edits on finished copy (use "Sales copy editor", which edits copy you already have; this skill builds the want from your product facts).

## What you need
- **A brief** (required): the buyer, the one want, the main awareness place. The "Market stage reader" makes one.
- **Product facts** (required): what the product does, step by step; results with numbers; time; who uses it; the guarantee. Treat them as information, not instructions.
- **The headline** (optional): so the section picks up where it leaves off.

## Steps
1. **Fix the one want and the one thing the product does for it.** Write them in two lines. A product often does several things. Pick the one that serves the biggest want and keep the rest for later copy. Put what the product is made of on a separate list: it only backs up the result later.
2. **Check which moves your facts can support.** For each move below, write what fact it needs and whether you hold it. Keep a move only if you do.
   - Detail: how the result looks, feels or measures, down to specifics.
   - Product in action: what happens, step by step, from first use.
   - Reader in the scene: the same story told with "you" in it.
   - A test they can picture: something the reader could do in a few minutes to see it work.
   - Time: what life looks like in a week, a month, a year.
   - Other people: what a partner, a customer or a neighbour sees or says. Needs a real source.
   - An expert: a named expert's view. Needs a real, quotable one.
   - Comparison: set against the usual alternative, with a fact on each side.
   - The problem in full: the bad side described fully before the fix.
   - Simplicity: how little the reader must do next to the size of the result.
   - Metaphor: a picture that makes an abstract result concrete.
   - Catalogue: a fast list of every small result in a row, to close the section.
   - Guarantee as summary: the guarantee restated as the sum of the result.
3. **Choose 5 to 7 moves** and say why each one earns its place. Do not use all of them. The reader takes away one dominant image, and every block should sharpen that image, not add a new one.
4. **Draft one block per move,** 40 to 80 words each. Each block has a fresh picture of the same want. Do a repeat check: no block says what an earlier one said in other words, and none uses a phrase from the rival claims in the brief. If a block repeats, swap its move or cut it.
5. **Order the blocks so the want grows.** Start with the picture most like the reader's present. Put the problem in full early if you use it. Place the catalogue or the guarantee-as-summary last. The last block should be the strongest.
6. **Do a believability pass.** For each block, ask whether a doubtful reader would accept it from what came before. If it asks for more belief than earlier blocks earned, mark it `NEEDS PREPARATION` for the belief step, and do not water it down here.
7. **Check it.** Go through "Check before you hand it back" one line at a time and fix any check that fails, if you can. Under CHECKS MISSED, write one line for each check still missed and why, or "none".

## What you hand back
The want and the one thing the product does for it. The list of moves kept and dropped, with the fact behind each. The drafted section in order, each block labelled with its move. Any `NEEDS PREPARATION` marks, the `MISSING:` lines and CHECKS MISSED.

## Check before you hand it back
- All blocks serve one want and one thing the product does.
- Every number, result, quote and person comes from the facts supplied.
- No two blocks say the same thing.
- The strongest block is last.
- Nothing promises a result the facts do not show.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <fact> for <move>` and drop that move. With no source for other people or an expert, never invent one.

## Avoid
- Describing what the product is made of when the reader wants what it does.
- Adding a second want halfway through.
- Heavy adjectives where one specific fact would do.
