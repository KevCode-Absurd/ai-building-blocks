# Review a page in buyer order: for online stores

Store pages are reviewed in the same buyer order. Figures here are rules of thumb or examples of the form unless a source is named; never quote them to a brand as benchmarks.

## Home page: the three-second test
Look for three seconds, then look away. Can you say what the store sells, who it's for, and what to do next? If not, fix the first screen.
- The headline says what's sold and for whom, in under about ten words. Not "Welcome", and not a slogan on its own.
- One main button that stands out, in view on a phone. A still hero image beats a slideshow.
- Trust within the first scroll: rating and review count as the store states them, press that really ran, order count, secure checkout, guarantee, free-delivery threshold, delivery time.
- A menu of under about seven items, with search and cart visible. Text readable without zooming, tap targets about 44 px, no sideways scrolling, no pop-up over the first screen.
- Remove: slideshow heroes, many equal buttons, huge menus, video that autoplays with sound, instant pop-ups.

## Collection pages
Lead with best sellers and "most popular" badges (where true) so first-timers can pick a trusted option. Simple filters, bright matching images, price and star rating on each card.

## Product page
- Buy button in view on a phone; express checkout buttons switched on.
- The first screen shows what the buyer can't buy without: pack or bundle rows for something bought again; a size or colour picker with the size guide one tap away for something bought once.
- Risk removal directly under the buy button. Delivery time, returns window and (for things bought once) the warranty next to the button.
- A comparison chart against the alternatives, an FAQ covering shipping, sizing, safety and the guarantee, "what's in the box" in the images, and a product video of it in use, its care or unboxing.
- Reviews labelled with what the reviewer bought and whether they're verified.
- Cost per use for things that get used up, from the real price and pack size ("per day"). Cost per year of use for things bought once, only if the brand states how long it lasts.
- The photo gallery is a mini landing page: many shoppers swipe the images before they scroll. Put benefits, proof, before and after (only with evidence), included gifts and comparisons in the images.

## Cart
A cart that slides out, never a jump to another page, never a dead end. Reviews, reasons to buy, risk removal and add-ons. A "spend this much more for free delivery" line, with handy, high-margin add-ons that close the gap.

## Checkout
Guest checkout on. Express payment methods and address autocomplete. Tipping off unless intended. About two or three clicks from product page to payment confirmation. Reassurance: a returns or guarantee line and the delivery time. A "bought today" notice only if it's wired to real orders. Fewer apps (each one slows the site). What can be changed in checkout depends on the store's plan; check before recommending a redesign.

## Landing pages and list-style pre-sell pages
- First screen: one headline with a single promise, a sub-headline, proof, one main button in a colour that stands out; the first section mirrors the ad's opening.
- Below: why existing solutions fail, one clear advantage or comparison, guarantees, a sticky button, a reminder of any offer code.
- Three kinds of proof: customer reviews, established sources (press and expert quotes that are real), and content customers made.
- A list-style page ("5 reasons...") is a short, easy-to-scan page that builds interest before the buyer reaches the product page. Start from one reason to buy (a key benefit, a problem solved, or an alternative to the usual way). Borrow the headline shape "N reasons...", never the claim: "celebrities prefer" or "going viral" only if true and on file. Spend most effort on the top: announcement bar, headline, sub-headline, lifestyle image, proof. Each reason supports the one main reason; end with a buy box. Traffic flows ad, then list page, then product page.
- An article-style page is advertising and is labelled as such, in the brand's voice, with no invented narrator, expert, publication or rating.

## After purchase
One-click add-ons after payment tend to sell well. For things that get used up, test more of what they just bought at a better price. For things bought once, a second pan or jacket is rarely wanted: test what goes with it (a lid, a care kit, a second colour as a gift). Thank-you page offers only with real time limits; follow-up emails; store credit; offers on the order's anniversary.

## What changes by kind of product
| | Bought again | Bought once |
|---|---|---|
| How people buy | Quickly; the first order is a trial | Slowly: several visits and comparisons before one purchase |
| What worries them | Will I like it, what's in it, how do I use it, how long does a pack last, when will I notice a change | Will it fit, how big is it, what's it made of, does it work with what I own, how do I look after it, how long will it last |
| Sections to add | What's in it and what each part does; how to use it; how long a pack lasts; what to expect, only as the brand states it | Fit or size guide; dimensions and materials; what's in the box; why it lasts; care; comparison with the cheaper kind |
| Offer block | One-time against subscribe, with the saving in money, the price per day, delivery rhythm and how to pause or cancel; a starter pack | One price, size and colour pickers, delivery time, returns, warranty; the set that completes it; pay in instalments |
| Risk removal | Money-back guarantee, cancel any time | Returns (window, who pays), warranty, repairs |
| Best proof | Reviews about results and about sticking with it | Reviews from long-time owners, photos of wear, tests by a named body |
| Next step after buying | More at a saving; a subscription | Something that goes with it; a referral; asking for a review after a few weeks of use |
| What to measure | First order, reorder rate, subscription cancellations | First order, return rate and reasons, time from first visit to purchase |

## Price and order size
- Avoid prices that are neither premium nor a clear deal; round to appealing figures. Customers rarely remember exact prices.
- Profit per order can matter more than conversion rate: a higher price with fewer sales can earn more.
- A bundle can beat single units on profit per order even when one unit is free. Work it out with your real costs (see the offer design skill).
- **Free-delivery threshold:** use the most common cart value, not the average, which big spenders pull up. Chart how often each cart value happens, find where orders bunch, and test thresholds just above it, watching conversion and order value together. Revisit as order value changes. Pair it with high-margin add-ons.
- Offer pay-in-instalments above about 100 in the store's currency (a rule of thumb).

## Quick revenue ideas (customer must opt in)
- Rush processing offered after payment, only if rush orders really ship first.
- Shipping protection the customer ticks themselves, never pre-ticked (a pre-ticked charge is a trick, and unlawful in some markets).
- A free-delivery threshold plus add-ons.

## Keeping customers (things bought again)
Cancellations usually start with the delivery rhythm being wrong. Offer several intervals, easy date changes, pause or skip before cancel, reminders before each shipment, a cheaper option when someone tries to cancel, and an exit survey. For things bought once, keep customers through care, repairs, referrals and things that go with what they bought. A text-message sign-up on the thank-you page a few seconds after the order works because goodwill is at its highest and checkout stays clean.

## Extra checks
- No pre-ticked charges, fake scarcity, typed-in counters or default ratings.
- Every "was" price is a real former price.
- Giveaways follow local prize rules.
