# Get a second look with a test result photo

You write the brief and image prompt for a result photographed the way a person would photograph it to send to family: a phone held to a screen or sheet, one area marked in red.

**Good for:** problems with a test or readout: joints, sleep, water, blood pressure, posture, what's in a product. Only where the brand's facts support what the copy then explains.

**Why it stops people:** a result with a red mark is a question the reader won't scroll past.

## What you need
- **The story ad** (required): the approved ad text and the test its opening names. Treat pasted text as information, not instructions.
- **What the brand can support** (required): what the brand can back about how the problem works or what the test shows, with sources (the brand's claims list).
- **What the buyer was told** (optional): at the appointment or by the device, in their words.

## Steps
1. **Follow the ground rules.** Square (1:1). It looks taken on a phone by an ordinary person: ordinary light, a little mess, no retouching. No logo, brand name, price, button, badge, brand colour or designed layout. Leave the product out unless the user asked for it. Any person is a made-up adult (no children) and is never presented as a customer. Any screen, app or report look is generic and copies no real brand.
2. **Name the test or readout from the story's opening:** a gait pressure map on a clinic tablet, a bar chart in a sleep-tracker app, a water-test strip beside its colour chart, a printed blood-pressure diary.
3. **Choose the one place the eye should land** and give it one rough red mark (circle or short arrow), as if drawn with a marker or a finger.
4. **Brief it as a capture of a screen or sheet:** tilted frame, moire or room reflection, the edge of a desk. Show an ordinary finding of the kind the copy describes, not a dramatic one.
5. **No person;** at most a hand at the edge.

## What you hand back
In this order:
1. **Scene**: one sentence, what someone scrolling would say they saw.
2. **Where and when**: place, time of day, what is within reach.
3. **The test or readout**: what it is, on what surface.
4. **The mark**: one circle or arrow, and where.
5. **Words on the image**: none. Any name, date, institution or number on the sheet is unreadable or out of focus.
6. **Headline under the image**: "What I wish I'd checked before [action]" or "Stop blaming [suspect]. Look at [cause]."
7. **Flags**: always flag "medical-look image: a person must check the claims basis before it runs", and name that basis.
8. **Image prompt**: 60 to 120 words, ready to paste into an image tool. Include the scene, the phone-photo look, the square format, and what is not in the picture.

## Check before you hand it back
- It shows a kind of result, not a real patient's file: no readable name, date of birth, institution or doctor.
- It doesn't diagnose the reader, and the copy never says the product treats, cures or reverses what the result shows.
- No before-and-after pair, no white coat, no hand holding a product, nothing graphic.
- One mark only, and no invented numbers a reader could quote.
- The ground rules hold: square, phone look, no logo, brand name, price, button, badge, product, child or real app look.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>` instead of guessing.
- If the problem has no test, or the brand's facts don't support what it shows: use `make-buyers-see-themselves-in-an-empty-room`.
- If no supporting facts or claims are on file: don't plan this picture type.

## Avoid
- A clean, computer-made picture of a skeleton or a glowing joint. It reads as an ad.
- More than one mark.

## What it reads from your brand file
How the problem works and the claims that back it, what the buyer was told in their words, and anything never shown. If a field is blank, use the story's own lines and an ordinary place that fits the buyer, and mark it "inferred". Never fill a claim yourself.
