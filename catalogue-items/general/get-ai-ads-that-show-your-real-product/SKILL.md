# Get AI ads that show your real product

You fill a one-page plan for the picture or clip about to be made, and run a short check on every result. It rests on one idea: an image tool told about a product in words invents one, so show it real photos and look hard at what comes back.

## What you need
- **The brief** (required): the idea, format, size, length, approved words, and the job of the picture or clip.
- **Product photos** (required whenever a real product appears): the brand's own photos of the thing being shown.
- **The brand file** (optional): the product's record (what it looks like, how it's used, what never to show), the brand's world (places, props, light, people), and notes on movement and sound.
- **The mistakes list** (optional): rules that apply to this kind of work.
- **Tool notes** (optional): clip lengths, modes and prices of the chosen tool. Keep these apart from the plan, so a new tool means one edit.

## Steps
Fill the plan below, then run the check after each result.

1. **Show the product true.** Attach the product's own photos and name each one in the prompt ("Image 1 is the front, as sold: copy its shape, colours and printing exactly"). One photo per job, not a collage. Repeat the two or three most visible facts in the same words every time; where words and a photo disagree, image tools follow the words. The colour of a thing is its own colour, not the colour of the background it was photographed on. It opens, pours, fastens and is used the way the brand says, step by step; an action nobody described is not shown. A scene without the product still belongs to it: the thing in focus is the product, something it's made of, or the thing it replaces.
2. **Still before clip.** A still costs cents and a clip costs dollars. Make and check the opening frame first; ask for the clip only when it's right, starting from that frame. An unusual camera position is set by the opening frame, not by words. A later clip starts on the last frame of the one before, or extends it. Making video from words alone is for exploring an open idea; it never replaces product photos for an exact product. Test the hardest movement first, with the smallest useful clip.
3. **Make frame 1 the ad.** It's judged in under a second with the sound off. Something is already happening in it. It reads at thumb size: one subject, clear edges, brand colours, nothing vital in the top and bottom bands where app buttons sit. Paused anywhere, it's still a good picture.
4. **Movement that could be filmed.** Real speed (no slow motion or time-lapse). One small, continuous action per clip. Materials behave: liquid has weight, fabric hangs, a lid has a thread. Everything the clip uses is in view when it starts. One name per object. Start and end states agree with the action (a cup must be lifted before it's lowered). One visible action and one camera movement per shot.
5. **Plan the sound.** Say what is heard (the tear, the pour, the room), and whether there's music. "Natural sound only, no music" is a complete instruction; saying nothing about sound is not. One speaker at a time, short enough to say in the time. A person listens to every clip, because a reviewer that reads frames can't hear. Keep voice, room sound, effects and music separately editable where the tool allows.
6. **People.** Generated people are made-up adults and say nothing only a real customer could say. No generated children. A founder, named customer or expert appears only from that person's own photo or footage, with their permission. Describe hands fully (which hand, holding what, where the other one is); a hand never covers the label. For faces, choose skin depth, undertone, adult age and texture separately. Texture fits the area, crop and light (nose pores can be clearer than cheeks), never uniform pores, waxy smoothing or sharpening halos. Keep the approved age, marks and makeup across cuts. Fix one problem at a time in one named area, locking everything else.
7. **Words in the picture.** A video tool writes no words; captions, prices and calls to action go on afterwards in the brand's typeface. An image tool sets words only when given the exact words, order and type. No claim, price or offer appears unless it's in the brand's facts.
8. **Set the scene.** The place comes from the brand's world and the buyer's moment. Lifestyle scenes have a history (a mug left after breakfast), not random clutter; a clean studio shot stays clean. The product takes the scene's light direction, shadow hardness, colour temperature, reflections and contact shadows; changing the place means relighting. A phone-photo look keeps the background recognisable and the skin unretouched; one or two imperfections can help but never hide the product or the words.
9. **Frame on purpose.** Choose the framing (wide for context, full for fit, medium for action, close for a detail), then height, angle, viewpoint and focus as separate decisions. Describe a physical camera position and distance, not an imagined photographer. Alternatives differ in real camera choices, not height alone; a locked reference keeps its approved setup.
10. **Sequence with intent.** Say what the viewer thinks at the start, what changes, and the visible evidence that earns the change. Each shot adds one piece of information. Review the full edit without dialogue, then with sound.
11. **Keep the direction separate from the prompt.** Write a reusable direction note: the purpose shown through visible cues, scene and action, view and light, each reference's role, what is fixed and what is deliberately open, and how it will be judged. Translate it into the tool's real controls without adding or dropping goals. A helper suggests; it never quietly replaces approved words, facts or format. Unknown product facts are not creative freedom.
12. **Edits.** Name the current state, the changed element and area, the target, and how it fits perspective, scale, light and contact; list what stays. Telling the tool to keep things is a request, not proof: check the returned image.
13. **Borrow from references carefully.** Break a striking reference down into one motif, one readable surprise and one payoff, then translate them into this audience's world. A beautiful reference is not evidence that it sells. Keep a few things constant across a campaign, with different compositions and jobs.
14. **Upscale last.** Upscaling keeps the approved shot and size, and is checked against the source for the product's look, hands, text and edges. It's not a redesign.
15. **Spend small.** Read the price before making anything. Smallest thing first: one still, then one short clip, then a batch of two or three, not ten, until the first has passed. Don't retry a refusal or failure with the same words. Save the inputs, prompt, settings and outcome; change one deciding choice per attempt.

### Check after every result
1. The product matches its photo: shape, colour, finish, printing, size against a hand.
2. The action asked for is the action shown, and it could happen.
3. Frame 1 already has the product and the movement.
4. Real speed, with no slowed or sped-up part.
5. Hands and faces are anatomically sound; crops are on purpose; no stray letters, logos or captions.
6. The camera is where it was asked to be.
7. A person has listened to the sound.
8. For video, watch the whole clip at normal speed and step through key moments; compare the product, action, space and light, including the join to the next clip.

Write each failure as one line (what was asked, what came back) and add it to the mistakes list.

Each picture or clip has one job and is judged on it: an opening stops the thumb and holds three seconds; a demonstration lets a stranger say what happened; a proof picture shows a real, visible source; a product page image answers one buyer question without reading the page.

## What you hand back
The completed plan (items 1 to 15, with the choices made and the product photos named) before anything is made, then the check result, pass or fail per line, with the one-line failure notes.

## Check before you hand it back
- Every product photo is named with its job in the prompt.
- No clip was asked for before its opening frame was approved.
- No invented word, claim or price appears in any picture.
- Sound was planned, and a person listened.
- Spend was stated before it was spent.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>` (no product photos, no usage steps, no sound plan). A missing fact is a question for the brand, never a guess: show the product closed or at rest, and say so.

## Avoid
- Vague directions ("make it feel premium") with no visible cues.
- Treating a believable generated image as proof that the product works, or that a change happened.
- Adding detail the approved plan didn't contain.
- Treating a longer prompt as a more accurate one.
