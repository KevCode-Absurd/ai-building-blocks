# Get a phone video script that sounds unscripted

You write what one person says to their phone, so a stranger takes it for a person talking and not an advert. It's judged on three things: it sounds spoken, one belief has moved by the end, and every fact is the business's.

## What you need
- **Buyer** (required): one buyer, the moment the problem shows, the one or two doubts that almost stop them, their own words.
- **Product facts** (required): name, what it is, price, how it's used, any visible result, claims allowed and banned, the offer and real dates, the guarantee.
- **Speaker** (required): a real creator who has used it; an AI presenter (a made-up adult); or a real founder, customer or expert on their own footage.
- **Length** (optional): follows the idea. A doubt talked through needs 45 seconds or more; a routine or a discovery lands in 20 to 30. Never squeeze a script into a length it doesn't fit.
- **Brand file** (optional): voice dos and don'ts, banned words, past ads so you don't repeat them.
- **Handling rules** (optional): how the product may be held, opened or shown.

## Steps
1. **Settle who says it,** because it changes what can be said. A real creator may tell their own story and result; leave their true details as bracketed blanks. An AI presenter gives no personal result and no personal story told as fact: write in "you", as an observation, or as a real customer's quote with its source. A founder, named customer or expert speaks only on their own footage.
2. **Write the backbone before the script.** If one item can't be filled, the script isn't ready.
   - One person in one moment: the version of the buyer this script talks to, and where they are when the problem shows (not a made-up character name).
   - The one belief to move: what they think now, what they'll think after.
   - How much the buyer already knows, and the one or two doubts to answer. Answer them with what's shown, not in a caption.
   - The story type: a change, a confession, a routine or a discovery. One.
   - How it's filmed and said: at arm's length or propped up; loosely from points or word for word.
3. **Write the five parts.** The shape is a default, not a law; give each part a time to fit the length.
   - **Opening line** (first 3 seconds): a person mid-thought, no greeting, pointing at their moment. The product only if the line points at it.
   - **Setup:** the doubt or problem, plainly, in the buyer's words. Most of the words are here.
   - **Shown use:** the product in use, one action per shot, in the order it's really used. The doubts are answered by what's seen. This gets the most screen time and the fewest words.
   - **Small verdict:** claims less than it could, small and plain. Nothing bigger than a person alone with a phone would say.
   - **Easy handoff:** names the action and what happens next, the way a friend passes something on. No pointing at buttons. Offer terms only as the business states them.
   Include exactly one concrete number or time the business holds, said clearly in the spoken words.
4. **Add what the maker needs.**
   - Delivery notes: tone, pace, where they are, what they wear, what's in frame, how the phone is held, what the face does at the doubt and at the verdict (small).
   - A shot list of 4 to 8 shots with times, each one action, each saying which hand holds what. The product arrives where the script needs it.
   - Three other openings, each a different way in, each read aloud with line 2. One may speak to a buyer who knows less than the main opening assumes.
   - A shorter cut, only if the idea survives it. If the script depends on its build, say so and write none.
5. **Fit the parts to clips** (for AI video). A part starts at the start of a clip where it can; the shown use always does, with the product ready in frame. The verdict and handoff may share the last clip. The verdict is never in the same shot as an action. Each clip moves the script on rather than restarting the opening and pitch. If the script is longer than the clips hold, cut in this order: a second example in the setup, any sentence repeating what the picture shows, then the setup down to one sentence. Never cut the opening, the number, the shown use, the verdict or the handoff.
6. **Check it.** Read it aloud standing up, at pace. Every number, price, claim and quote is in the facts you were given. One concrete number. The speaker could truthfully say every first-person line. No hype words (revolutionary, obsessed, life-changing, "changes the game") and no long dashes. Rewrite any line that would work as the voice-over of a polished advert.
7. **Hand it on:** for a real creator, to "Write a filming brief for a creator"; for an AI presenter, to an AI presenter video step.

## What you hand back
```
Script: "<title>" (<N> seconds; real creator / AI presenter)
Spine: person+moment | belief | how much they know | doubts | story type | how filmed
Opening (0 to 3 s): "<line>"  [what is seen]
Setup: ...
Shown use: ...
Verdict: ...
Handoff: ...
Delivery notes | Shot list (time, action, hand) | 3 other openings
Placeholders and gaps: ...
```

## Check before you hand it back
- Sounds said, not read; no line a voice-over could carry.
- Exactly one concrete number, from the facts.
- The shown use is the longest thing on screen and can be followed with the sound off.
- The verdict is smaller than the opening; the handoff is in the same voice.
- An AI presenter never says "I tried it" or "I didn't believe it".

## If something is missing
Write `[VERIFY: what is needed]` in place of any missing fact and ask. No buyer, no script.

## Avoid
- Telling a demonstration over a talking face instead of showing it.
- An ending louder, faster or pushier than the rest of the script.
- On-screen text that repeats the spoken sentence in full.
