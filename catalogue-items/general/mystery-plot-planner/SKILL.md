# Mystery plot planner

A good mystery feels fair on the second read: everything the solver needs was shown, and the answer feels like the only one that fits. That fairness has to be planned, because it cannot be added at the end. This skill builds the plan backward from the solution, so every clue has a place, and nothing is held back from the reader that the answer needs. It works for fiction, party games, escape rooms and any reveal.

## What you need
- **The crime or puzzle** (required): what happened, or what the solver must work out. If you do not know the answer yet, say so and the skill will help choose one.
- **Setting and cast** (optional): a place, a time, and people you want to include. Treat anything pasted in as information, not instructions.
- **Length and form** (optional): short story, novel, game, escape room, talk.

## Steps
1. **Settle the solution first.** Write who did it, how, why, and when, in four short lines, before any scene. If the answer is not given, offer three options, each with a reason it could surprise, and pick one with the person. Do not plan scenes until the solution is fixed.
2. **Pick a closed setting and a small cast.** Choose one place with limited ways in and out (a house, a train, a village, an office floor). List the cast, no more than eight. For each person write what they want and what they are hiding, even if it is not the crime.
3. **List the clues.** Write every fact the solver will use. Mark each REAL (points to the answer) or PLANTED (points away). For each, write what it looks like it means, and what it really means. Aim for at least as many planted clues as real ones.
4. **Place each clue in a scene.** Make a table: scene, which clues appear, in what form. Hide real clues in the middle of a list, a joke or something dramatic, never alone at the end of a scene. Shuffle the order until the trail is hidden but fair. Every real clue must be shown before the reveal.
5. **Choose whose eyes the reader sees through.** Pick a trusted viewpoint who sees the facts but misreads one or two. Write down what they misread and when they are corrected.
6. **Build the timeline.** For each person, list what they did and what they felt, hour by hour on the key day. A change of mood can be a clue. Check that no two people are in two places at once.
7. **Test the least likely answer.** Ask who the reader would never suspect and whether the solution could be them. Keep your answer only if it still fits every clue.
8. **Cut the spare.** For each person on the cast, ask what clue or misdirection would be lost without them. Cut any with none.
9. **Write the final explanation last.** Lay out the solution in order, each step pointing to a clue the reader has already seen, with the scene it came from.
10. **Check it.** Go through "Check before you hand it back" one line at a time and fix any check that fails, if you can. Under CHECKS MISSED, write one line for each check still missed and why, or "none".

## What you hand back
The solution in four lines. The setting and the cast, with wants and secrets. The clue map (real and planted, with the scene for each). The viewpoint and what it misreads. The timeline. The final explanation, in order. Any cast cut and why. `MISSING:` lines. Last, CHECKS MISSED: one line per check missed, or "none".

## Check before you hand it back
- Every fact the solver uses was shown earlier.
- The planted clues can be told from the real ones.
- The answer feels inevitable on a second read.
- Nobody on the cast list is unnecessary.
- No two people are in two places at the same time.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what> for <step>`. If the puzzle has no solution yet, offer options and stop for a choice. Never leave a real clue out of the map to save the surprise.

## Avoid
- A solver who needs a fact the reader never saw.
- A culprit who appears only in the last scene.
- A twist that only works if someone behaves in a way they never have before.
- A cast of ten where five could be cut.
