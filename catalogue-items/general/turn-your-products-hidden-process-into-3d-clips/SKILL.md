# Turn your product's hidden process into 3D clips

You hand back a hidden process chosen and checked for honesty, three or more story options for the person to choose from, seven timed beats, and the still and clip prompts for a 3D lesson with no presenter. It plays as a lesson until the product arrives about three quarters of the way through.

## What you need
- **The product** (required): name, a real clean photo of the pack, how it works (ideally 2 or 3 linked steps, each with a source), key features, main benefit, how it's used.
- **Brand file** (required): claims with sources, banned claims, what the buyer assumes and what almost stops them, colours and font, a voice the brand has the right to use, how the name and hard words are said.
- **Script** (optional): if one exists, use it. Otherwise write it after the story option is chosen.
- **Clip limits** (optional): the clip lengths and join types your tool accepts.

## Steps
1. **Run the four tests.** An explainer earns its place only when all four are true: it's invisible (if the result can be watched, a demonstration is stronger and cheaper); one thing acts on another (you could draw it as a cutaway with two parts and one action); every link is supported; it's news to a regular buyer in the category.
2. **Find the one process.** List what the product does that a camera can't see, by what kind of product it is:
   - eaten or drunk: what one ingredient does in the body, only as far as the claim goes
   - applied: what it does at the surface of skin or hair
   - worn: what the fibre or weave does with heat, water or movement
   - used at home or for cooking: what the material does with heat, water, grease or dirt
   - carried or built: how a joint, bearing, seam or shell takes weight
   Write each as a three-link chain: A meets B, B does C, so the buyer gets D. Anything you can't write this way is a feature, not a process. Mark every link: brand claim with source, common knowledge (a school book says so), or unsupported. Cut an unsupported chain back to where the support ends, or drop it. Ask five questions and drop any chain that fails two: does the buyer already know this; is there one "wait, what?" sentence; is the thing in the way something they trust or never think about; could they retell it at dinner; would it be interesting if the product didn't exist (the sharpest test, because a lesson that only sets up the sale is a sales pitch in disguise). Choose the chain whose missing piece is the product, and name its one technical word. Name what gets in the way (a habit, material or shortcut), never a person or a named competitor. If nothing survives, say so and use another format.
3. **Keep it honest.** A picture is a claim.
   - Use "supports", "helps", "is made of". Never "treats", "cures", "prevents", "fixes".
   - Show the product doing no more than its verb. "Supports" means the product used and the process carrying on, never damage vanishing, never a body before and after.
   - Draw a body only as far as the claim names it. When unsure, use a plain diagram shape, not anatomy. No illness, clinic or white coat.
   - For made things, the cutaway matches what the product is really made of. No invented test, gauge or reading.
   - Say a number only if the brand has it with a source. Otherwise open another way.
   - A figure of speech (a switch, a gate, a shield) looks like a model, never like real anatomy.
   - Categories with claim rules (supplements, skincare, anything for a condition): the brand reads the script before any picture is made.
4. **Offer 3 to 5 story options and stop for a choice.** A story option is a whole film in under 120 words: the opening, how it works, what gets in the way, the payoff, and who it's strongest for. No two share an opening or a process. Say which links rest on which source, and flag anything a regulator would look at, before the choice.
5. **Write the seven beats** (about 3 seconds a picture, 9 to 13 pictures; under 30 seconds, merge beats 4 and 5):

   | Beat | Share of time | Job |
   |---|---|---|
   | 1 Scale opening | 0 to 18% | A number or scale that's hard to believe, over a picture as big or small as the fact |
   | 2 Wrong cause | 18 to 30% | The cause isn't the one the viewer assumes (two plain statements) |
   | 3 How it works | 30 to 43% | What happens, as one thing acting on another |
   | 4 Left alone | 43 to 63% | How it works when nothing's in the way: in the past, in nature, or made the old way |
   | 5 What gets in the way | 63 to 78% | The habit, material or shortcut |
   | 6 The product | 78 to 88% | Named for the first time, as a return to how it works |
   | 7 What the buyer can do | 88 to 100% | Something physical, then the opening picture again, changed |
6. **Set the look.** Semi-realistic 3D, matte surfaces, plainly computer-made (a glossy photo-like finish looks like an advert). The same style line in every prompt. People are stylised 3D adults, fully clothed (say so in every crowd), shown whole or from behind. A figure who comes back is made once as a still and attached to every clip. The product is the only photo-real thing, made from its tagged photos, and isn't seen before beat 6. Something worn is shown real only on its own (folded, hung, laid flat); on a figure it's made in 3D in its true colour and cut. The camera glides forward, never still, never handheld, so the film feels like one long move, with the world changing inside a shot (down from a crowd to one figure, through a surface into the cutaway). Light: bright and neutral, then dark with one warm glow for the process, then the brand's own light and colours from the product on. No letters, logos or numbers in the 3D world.
7. **Voice and captions.** Use only a voice the brand has the right to use (a person it recorded or hired, or a voice tool under its licence). Never a voice cloned or copied from another ad, a creator, a channel or a celebrity. Spell hard words by sound in the text the voice reads, and keep the correct spelling in captions. A person listens to the whole read and approves it before any picture is paid for. Captions: 1 to 3 words at a time, appearing on the spoken word, one word in the accent colour, the brand's heaviest font with a dark outline, a little below centre, added in editing word for word from the narration. Sound: the narrator plus the low background hum of the 3D world. No music.
8. **Stills first.** Look at the opening picture, the cutaway and the product's arrival as stills first. The product still has its photos attached, each with its job. Clips start from approved stills. Join two clips as one move only where it helps the story (into the cutaway, out to the closing picture). Elsewhere use a short dissolve, so one bad clip spoils only itself.
9. **Check what was made.** Everything is 3D except the pack, which matches its photo at the start, middle and end of each of its shots. The same figure throughout. The process's technical thing has one look. No picture claims more than its line. The last picture answers the first. One voice, approved by a person. The technical word said correctly. Captions match the narration word for word. Then ask once more: would this be interesting if the product didn't exist?

## What you hand back
1. **The chain** (3 links with sources) and **honesty notes**.
2. **Story options** (3 to 5, under 120 words each). Then, after the choice:
3. **Seven-beat script** with the picture list and seconds.
4. **Look line, figure line, style line** (fixed wording).
5. **Still prompts** and **clip prompts**.
6. **Caption list** and **voice brief** (with spellings by sound).

## Check before you hand it back
- A viewer could retell how it works in one sentence.
- Every picture and line is supported by the brand's facts.
- The first frame is a picture nobody could film.
- No unsupported number or link; no body before and after.
- The voice is one the brand may use.
- The brand name isn't shown before beat 6.

## If something is missing
Write `MISSING: <what>`. No process or "how it works": ask, and never make one up. If nothing passes the tests, recommend another format.

## Avoid
- Bending a claim to fit a process.
- Copying a person, channel, competitor or creator's look, voice, catchphrases or footage.
- A sales pitch in disguise: a lesson that only exists to set up the sale.
- Slowing the read to fill time. Cut words instead.
- Missing clothes in crowds, or a see-through body (it can be refused). Frame only the part the camera travels into.
