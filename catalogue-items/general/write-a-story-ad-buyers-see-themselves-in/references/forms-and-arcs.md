# Forms and arcs

The examples here are made up. Nothing in them goes into a real piece. The form decides the structure. Where it differs from the eight parts in the skill, the form wins.

## Forms (the shape of the story)

| Form | Opens with | Suits | Take care |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal history (the standard) | How long they have had the problem, said flatly ("I've bought six pans since 2015. I counted last week.") | standard, long | With a composite, the durations and attempts must be ones the quotes mention. Name kinds of product, never brands. Say "go read the reviews" only if the page has real ones |
| Number then family story | A real, sourced fact, then the turn to someone close ("My mother was one of them.") | long | The fact must be in the business's material with its source, said the way the source says it. If there is none, use personal history. Never blame an illness on a medicine, doctor or product, and never advise stopping treatment. The family member is part of the composite unless it is a real customer's story |
| Letter about someone | The other person, in a scene | standard, long | The narrator reports what they saw. The other person's results stay inside what customers report. Invented first name only. An adult, unless the product is for children and every word is supported |
| Forum post | A correction to what they believed ("For eight years I thought it was just me.") | long | Addressed to "anyone else who...", never set on a named forum. No claimed replies or votes. Costs are believable totals of things the research mentions. Never says a professional was wrong |
| Expert voice | The expert's standing and the pattern they see | standard, long | Only a real, named professional who agreed. No diagnosis, no advice to change treatment. Quotes in the P.S. are real customer words |
| Mistake I was making | The habit, said with the pride they once had in it | standard, long | A common, well-meant habit found in the research. The reader is never blamed. That the habit makes things worse must rest on the material, or be told as what the narrator came to believe. No dramatic harm unless supported |
| Please stop doing this | An instruction to stop the usual fix | short, standard | "Stop" applies to a kind of product or habit, never to medical treatment. The thing to stop is never something the business's own product also asks you to do |
| Rant | "I'm so sick of being told to [usual advice]." | short, standard | Anger at generic advice, never at people or brands. Every item is in the research. Swearing only if the brand's voice allows it. Pairs with the quietly angry arc |
| Short review | The problem, and what they were about to do about it | short | Only from a real customer's review or story. Otherwise write as an observer with real quotes and say the form changed. Never laid out like a named review site. No star rating |
| Story for strangers | One of five openings: a story, a warning, an expert who changed their mind, a confession, or a question the reader cannot answer yet | brief, short | The narrator is the founder or the business summarising customers. No invented expert. It hints at the product and never says the price. It closes by pointing to a page that teaches, not the product page. No "do you have [condition]?" |

**Order for the story for strangers:** the problem in the buyer's words, the belief everyone holds, what they tried, the real cause, a hint of the answer, one soft call to the page.

**The headline under its image takes one of four shapes:**
- "Before you [thing they are about to do], read this"
- "Why your [problem] keeps happening"
- A line that names what they blamed, then gives the real cause.
- "The reason you [behaviour they are embarrassed by]"

## Arcs (how the narrator feels)

| Arc | Mood | The weight of it leads with | The new explanation is framed as | The close leans on |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The searcher (the default) | Determined, a little worn | The full run of things tried, each done properly | What they found late at night when they could not stop looking | "The problem was never me." |
| Exhausted surrender | Tired, resigned | The attempt that cost the most time | An explanation that found them after they stopped looking | Not believing an ordinary life was still on offer |
| Quietly angry | Controlled, precise | The biggest promise that delivered least | The thing nobody selling in this category mentions | "I'm not making a promise. I'm telling you what happened." Anger only at kinds of product |
| Reluctant believer | Guarded | The attempt they had most hope for | The thing they nearly dismissed and came back to | Being glad they were wrong. Leans on the real guarantee if there is one |
| The self-blamer | Privately ashamed | The one they worked hardest at, and what they decided about themselves | Evidence it was never their fault: a reason built into the problem that effort could not fix | Being let off the hook. The story never blames the reader |

**Tone:** lift slowly and never end on excitement. Use numbers and specifics instead of adjectives.
