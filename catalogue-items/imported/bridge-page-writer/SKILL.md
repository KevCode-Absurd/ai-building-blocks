# Landing Page Copy Skill

This skill writes short-form bridge pages that sit between an ad and a video, quiz or offer page. It draws on direct response ideas from Schwartz, Makepeace, Georgi, Wiebe, Rogers and others.

The page has ONE job: **get the visitor curious enough to click through to the next step.** It does NOT sell. No price, no offer stack, no checkout, no email capture, no product details.

---

# Rule 0: Only true, supplied facts

Every authority, institution, study, statistic, user count, testimonial, name, location and result on the page must come from material the user gave you, or from a source you name that they can check. If the user did not supply it, write a placeholder such as `[REAL STAT + SOURCE]` or `[REAL TESTIMONIAL: name, place, result]` and list it under "Facts to supply". Never invent a doctor, university, number or quote. Never write "this video may be taken down" or any scarcity or suppression claim that is not true.

Stop and say so if the offer is a health, medical, financial or other regulated claim, or if the user cannot back its claims with evidence. Ask for compliance review first, and check the ad platform's policies. Never write cure, reversal, "diabetes-free" or similar claims.

If an input is missing (the ad, the offer, the visitor, the destination), ask once. Do not guess.

# Step 1: Understand the page

**What the page is:** a short page (300-800 words) between the ad and the next step. The visitor is curious but not committed.

**What it is not:** a sales page, an advertorial, a squeeze page or a product page.

**The visitor's questions:** "Is this legit? Is it worth my time? What is this about?" Answer the first two with yes. Keep the third open enough that the next click feels natural, without hiding what the next step is.

---

# Step 2: Classify the brief

- **Market and niche:** the language and proof types change.
- **Awareness level (Schwartz):**

| Level | What they know | Approach |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | No problem yet | Lead with identity or emotion |
| Problem-aware | The problem, not the solution | Name the pain, introduce the method |
| Solution-aware | Solutions exist, not yours | Show how your method differs |
| Product-aware | Your product | Short, proof-heavy, direct button |

Most bridge pages target problem-aware visitors from cold ads.

- **Destination:** video, quiz or long page. The button must match it.
- **Mechanism:** the method or idea being teased, as supplied by the user. It is the backbone of the page.

---

# Step 3: Write the three-paragraph framework

Three elements, in this order. Each may be 2-3 short paragraphs.

## Paragraph 1: The discovery (authority and root cause)

Purpose: show credibility and introduce the idea or method. Use a real expert, source or experience from the user's material.

Neutral pattern examples (placeholders, not facts):

> "[REAL SOURCE] found that most [people] lose [time/money] on [specific problem] because of [real root cause]."
> "A [REAL ROLE] explains the [named method] behind [real result]."

Elements: a real named source or the founder's own story; a specific root cause the user can support; plain, vivid language; a named method.

## Paragraph 2: Social proof (results and benefits)

Purpose: show real people already get results.

Use the strongest proof the user supplied, in this order: a named testimonial with a real result, review screenshots, a case study with numbers, an expert endorsement, media mentions, user counts, an anonymous testimonial. Use exact figures only when the user gave them. Otherwise use `[REAL NUMBER + SOURCE]`.

Match the proof to the visitor's main doubt.

## Paragraph 3: Call to action (direction)

Purpose: make the click the obvious next step.

Elements: speak to the reader ("If you [real situation]..."), restate their pain, say what they will see next (for example "a 3-minute video"), give one clear action, and use a button that names the benefit ("Watch The Free Video", not "Click Here").

Urgency must be true: use a real deadline, or the reader's own cost of waiting. Never invent scarcity.

# Advanced techniques

## Message match

The page must feel like a continuation of the ad. Reuse the ad's words, method and promise. If the ad says "5-minute setup", the page says "5-minute setup". Any gap and they leave.

## The unique mechanism

Give the method a clear name and a simple reason it differs. Formula: [Name] + [simple explanation] + [why it is different]. The test: if you can swap in a competitor's name and the page still works, it is not specific enough.

## The mechanism tease

Say what it is and what it is not, and give true hints, but keep the full answer behind the click. Never promise a reveal the next step does not deliver.

## "Nothing to do with" pattern

Remove old assumptions so the idea feels new: "This is not about [A], [B] or [C]." Only use it when it is true.

## Vivid language

Make the problem felt: "stuck for hours" over "inefficiency". Use sensory, concrete words. Do not overstate harm.

## The 60-second story hook

For pages that open with a personal story: Identity, Struggle, Discovery, Result.

> "Hi, I'm [name]. I run [real role] in [place]. For years I struggled with [real problem]. Then I found [method]. Now [real result]."

Use only the founder's real story.

# Headline formulas

The headline sits above the body and sets up the three paragraphs. Neutral shapes:

- **Authority + method + benefit:** "[Real expert]: Do This To [real benefit]"
- **Number + discovery:** "[Real number] Of [people] Miss This [Topic] Detail"
- **Method + timeframe:** "A Simple [N]-Minute [Method] For [Benefit]"
- **Question:** "Can [method] Help You [benefit]?"
- **Story:** "How [Method] Cut My [Problem] From [Before] To [After]"

Use a number or time only when it is real.

---

# Formatting rules

Desktop: big paragraph spacing, bold on key phrases, paragraphs of 2-3 sentences.
Mobile (most traffic): large font, shorter paragraphs, a tall, wide, high-contrast button right after the last paragraph, and no wall of text.
Button text: benefit-led. Test "Watch The Free Video" against "Watch Now".

---

# Output format

Always give **2-3 variations**, each testing a different angle, labelled so the user can A/B test:

```
## Variation 1: [Angle name]

### Headline
[headline]

### Body
[Paragraph 1: discovery]

[Paragraph 2: proof]

[Paragraph 3: call to action]

### Button text
[button text]
```

Angles: Authority Discovery, Social Proof First, Story/Testimonial, Mechanism Mystery, Problem Vivid.

After the variations, add:

1. **Facts to supply:** every placeholder, and where the user can find the real fact.
2. **Claims to check:** any line that needs evidence or compliance review.
3. **Next step:** which destination the button links to, and how to test (change one thing at a time, such as the headline, and compare click-through rate).

---

# Voice and anti-patterns

Sound like a friend sharing a find, or a plain news report. Not a sales pitch, a textbook or a generic marketing line. Read it out loud.

Ban AI tells: delve, comprehensive, robust, cutting-edge, utilize, leverage, unlock, supercharge, game-changer, "in today's fast-paced world", "are you ready to". Use firm, plain language without wimpy hedging, but never overclaim.

Aim for reading grade 6 or lower. Short sentences.

# Classic principles (reference)

- **Eugene Schwartz:** match copy to awareness level. Most markets have heard simple claims, so a clear, true method helps you stand out.
- **David Ogilvy:** 5x as many people read the headline as the body. Write the way you talk. Tell the truth, but make it fascinating.
- **Gary Halbert:** find a starving crowd. Write to one person. Tell stories.
- **Clayton Makepeace:** every line should pull toward the click.
- **Joanna Wiebe:** use the customer's own words for the problem.
- **Robert Collier:** enter the conversation already in the customer's mind.
- **Joseph Sugarman:** every element has one job: get them to read the next element.
