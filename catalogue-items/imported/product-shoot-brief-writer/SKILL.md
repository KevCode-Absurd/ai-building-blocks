# Product visuals

Produce a photo/video brief in which each shot is tied to a finding — most with a measured effect, a few marked † as practitioner tips. Format, gaze, the viewer's sense of touching the product, and composition (shine, spacing, side, props) move sales and willingness to pay more than lighting taste does.

## When to use

- Writing a shot list or creative brief; choosing video vs stills or between candidate images
- Reviewing why a product image or ad creative underperforms

## When not to use

- Page layout, CTAs, variants, price position → the Product page auditor (`product-page-auditor`)
- Description text → the Product description writer (`product-description-writer`)

## Inputs

Collect, or state your assumption:

- The product; can it be picked up and held? Is it premium?
- One main function (energy drink) or many?
- Worn, and by whom (womenswear vs menswear)?
- Where the visuals run (product page, ads, social); audience reading direction

> **Classify the product first.** *Hedonic* = bought for enjoyment, pleasure, or the experience (chocolate, fashion, decor, travel, spa, perfume). *Utilitarian* = bought to do a job, judged on function and value (detergent, appliances, insect repellent, tools, accounting software). If it's mixed, decide by what the buyer is optimizing for at the moment of purchase. If you still cannot tell, use this default: a full set of stills, one first-person hand shot, and one short optional video marked "test". Ask the user to confirm the product type before the brief is final.

## Before you start

Have ready: a product photo or spec sheet, the sales channels (product page, ads, social), and the target buyer's reading direction.

## Procedure

Run in this order: (a) collect the Inputs, (b) classify the product as hedonic or utilitarian, (c) work through steps 1 to 10 in number order, format first, then the composition rules.

Every effect size below (for example +79%, +22%, +30%) is a result from one study, and the studies are listed in reference.md. Figures marked † have no study behind them. Treat all of them as a guide, not a promise.

1. **Format.** Hedonic → video, GIF, or slideshow is recommended (willingness to pay 79% higher in the study, Roggeveen et al., 2015). Utilitarian → stills are enough. Premium hedonic → add a slow-motion action shot (+22% sales) †. Video should feel personal and human, not corporate. †
2. **Gaze.** Hedonic → model looks away from the camera (sun hat: +30% sales in one A/B test, To & Patrick, 2021). Informational or negative-emotion messages → model looks at the camera.
3. **Touch.** A first-person hand touching, holding, or using the product (+16% purchase likelihood across 15 experiments, Luangrath et al., 2022).
4. **Side.** Graspable product toward the right of the frame, angled for a right hand (Maille et al., 2019). If it can't be held, add a graspable object beside it (handedness doesn't change with reading direction — don't flip this). Future-oriented products right, vintage left; flip *that* for right-to-left readers.
5. **Shine.** Light or polish for a visible reflection; a digital reflection is fine only if it stays representative of the real finish (a matte product shown glossy misleads buyers). Never brief "no reflections" (+23% attractiveness, +32% purchase likelihood, Sharma & Kumar, 2022).
6. **Spacing.** Clear empty space between products in multi-product shots and grids (liking +11.4%, sales nearly doubled, Sevilla & Townsend, 2016). In ads, wide margins with related products clustered in the center. †
7. **Multiples.** Product bought for one specific function (an energy drink for energy, a stain remover) → several copies together (+10.7% perceived effectiveness) †. Not a rule for treats or decor.
8. **Props.** Complementary items around the focal product, varied in shape and color; the props' own sales are unaffected (Radon et al., 2021).
9. **Context.** Womenswear → a fitting setting, not white background. Menswear → irrelevant.
10. **Saturation.** High to look bigger, low to look smaller (Hagtvedt & Brasel, 2017). Pretest candidates with a poll where cheap. †

| 1 | Hero video, [LENGTH] s | [SHOT DIRECTION FOR THE USER'S PRODUCT] | Hedonic → video; premium → slow-mo † | Willingness to pay +79% in the study (example row, shape only) |

In the Effect column, give the study name for each figure. Each figure comes from one study in one setting, so say so beside it. A rule marked † has no study on file, so write "practitioner tip" instead of a number.

The effect figures come from the studies in reference.md. Check each one against its source before it goes into a brief for a client or a team, and never present it as your own result.

Then list what *not* to do for this product (e.g. no direct gaze, no matte finish).

When candidate images are supplied, score each one against the rules above and pick the one that follows the most. This gives the brief a clear next action before it goes to the photographer.

## Common mistakes

- Making video "secondary" for a hedonic product — it is the highest-effect item.
- Briefing "avoid reflections" — shine raises attractiveness and purchase intent.
- Centering a graspable product — it belongs toward the right.
- A single can for a single-function product — show several.

## Quick reference

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| Format | Hedonic → video; utilitarian → stills |
| Premium hedonic | Slow-motion action shot |
| Gaze | Away for hedonic; at camera for info or negative emotion |
| Hand | First-person hand touching |
| Position | Graspable product right (never flipped); future-right/past-left flips for RTL |
| Finish | Visible shine |
| Layout | Space apart; wide ad margins |
| Single-function | Multiple copies |
| Props | Complementary, varied |
| Womenswear | Contextual background |
| Size cue | High saturation = bigger |

† No primary citation on file for this rule — a practitioner tip, not a measured finding.
