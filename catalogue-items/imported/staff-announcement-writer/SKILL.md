# Employee communications

Plan and write clear, consistent internal communications — routine updates, policy changes, benefits announcements, and sensitive messages — that employees actually read, understand, and trust.

## Steps

1. Ask for what you need in one message: what is changing and why, who it affects, the date, and which channels you will use. Facts you cannot share yet, and a request for a routine or sensitive tone, are optional.
2. If something is missing, write `MISSING: <what is needed>`. Never invent a reason, date, number, name or quote.
3. Decide if the change is routine (a policy update, a holiday schedule) or sensitive (a restructure, a leadership change, job cuts). Match the tone. Do not use cheerful wording for bad news.
4. Write the main announcement. Put what changes, who it affects and when in the first two sentences. Use short sentences and plain words.
5. Write one version for each channel the user named (for example email, Slack, town hall script, intranet), with the same facts in each.
6. Write manager talking points: 3 to 5 key lines and answers to the hardest questions. Managers get these first.
7. Write an FAQ of 5 to 8 questions. Answer only from facts the user gave. If the answer is not known, write "We do not know yet. We will tell you by [date]."
8. Give a rollout order: managers first, then staff, then a follow-up message. Say what to send and when.
9. End with what to check: every fact is true, nothing in it will start a rumour, and the right leader signs it.

Do not use this for one person's performance or discipline. Use a one-to-one or your HR case process. Job cuts or new legal terms should be reviewed by HR or a lawyer before sending.

## Supported tasks

- Writing routine HR announcements (policy updates, benefits changes, holiday schedules)
- Planning an internal HR communications calendar across the year
- Drafting sensitive announcements (restructuring, leadership change, layoffs)
- Adapting the same message for different channels (email, Slack, town hall, intranet)
- Writing manager talking points to accompany broader employee announcements
- Simplifying complex policy or benefits language into plain, accessible writing
- Designing a consistent voice and tone for HR communications
- Timing and sequencing communications to avoid rumor and confusion
- Measuring whether employee communications are actually being read and understood
- Localizing communications for multi-country or multi-language workforces
- Building FAQ documents to accompany major announcements
- Auditing existing HR communications for clarity and consistency

## Key prompts

### Routine communications

1. "Write an internal announcement about [policy/benefits change] in plain, accessible language."
2. "Draft a holiday schedule / open enrollment / policy reminder announcement for [audience]."
3. "Plan an HR internal communications calendar for [year/quarter] covering recurring and one-off announcements."
4. "Draft a reminder communication for [deadline-driven task, e.g. benefits enrollment] that prompts action without sounding naggy."

### Sensitive announcements

1. "Draft a sensitive announcement about [restructuring/leadership change] that is honest, clear, and empathetic."
2. "Write manager talking points to accompany this employee-wide announcement about [change]."
3. "How should we sequence and time this announcement to minimize rumor and confusion before the official communication goes out?"
4. "Draft a follow-up communication for [sensitive change] a few weeks after the initial announcement to address lingering concerns."

### Adapting and simplifying

1. "Adapt this HR announcement for [email / Slack / town hall script], keeping the core message consistent across formats."
2. "Simplify this policy language into a plain-English version employees can actually understand without legal background."
3. "Build an FAQ document to accompany this announcement, anticipating the questions employees are most likely to ask."
4. "Rewrite this announcement for a [frontline / non-desk] audience who may not read email regularly."

### Measurement and consistency

1. "How can we measure whether this employee communication is actually being read and understood, not just sent?"
2. "Audit our recent HR communications for consistency in tone and clarity, and flag where they diverge."
3. "How should we localize this communication for our [multi-country/multi-language] workforce?"
4. "Design a lightweight feedback mechanism employees can use to flag when an HR communication was confusing."

## Tips

- Lead with the message that matters most to the employee, not organizational context — most people read the first two sentences and skim the rest.
- Match tone to content — routine updates can be brief and light; sensitive news needs warmth, directness, and space for questions.
- Equip managers before the broader announcement goes out; employees often hear news from their manager first, and managers need to be ready.
- Avoid corporate euphemism in sensitive communications — vague language erodes trust faster than direct, honest language delivered with empathy.
- Follow up after major announcements with an FAQ or open Q&A channel; a single announcement rarely answers every question employees have.
