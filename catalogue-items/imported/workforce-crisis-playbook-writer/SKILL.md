# HR crisis management

Plan for and respond to workforce crises — safety incidents, natural disasters, sudden leadership departures, or reputational scandals — with clear roles, fast communication, and employee-centered decision-making under pressure.

## What to ask for first

Before you write anything, collect:

1. The type of crisis, and whether it is live or you are planning ahead.
2. Company size and number of sites.
3. Optional: legal and security contacts, and the facts known so far.

If the crisis type or size is missing, ask once. If it is still missing, write `MISSING: <what>` and say what you assumed. If the crisis is live, use only the facts the person gives you. Mark anything unknown as "not yet known". Never invent causes, numbers, names, dates or legal duties.

## What to hand back

Return these sections, in this order:

1. **Roles and decision rights**: a table of who decides, who acts and who must be told across HR, legal, security and communications.
2. **First-hour message to staff**: short, calm and honest. It says what is known, what is not, what staff should do now, and when the next update comes.
3. **24-hour action list**: ordered actions, each with an owner. Put staff safety and wellbeing first.
4. **Manager talking points**: plain answers for common questions, and what to pass up instead of guessing.
5. **Post-crisis review plan**: what to ask, who to ask, and how to update the playbook.

Planning ahead means writing the same sections with placeholders such as `<site name>` for the facts you do not have yet.

End with: "This is not legal advice. Have your lawyer or legal contact read every message before it goes out." If staff pay, dismissal, injury reporting or notice rules may apply, say that the rules differ by country and region and send the person to employment counsel. Do not quote laws or thresholds.

If the situation is a layoff, a product failure or a system outage, say another tool fits better and name it: the layoff plan writer, the crisis response planner or the outage incident leader.


## Supported tasks

- Building HR crisis response playbooks for common scenario types
- Defining crisis roles and decision authority before a crisis happens
- Drafting rapid employee communications during an active crisis
- Coordinating HR's role in business continuity planning
- Responding to workplace safety incidents and their aftermath
- Managing HR response to natural disasters affecting employees or facilities
- Handling the HR and workforce dimensions of a sudden leadership departure
- Responding to a reputational crisis or public scandal affecting employees

## Key prompts

### Planning ahead

1. "Build an HR crisis response playbook for [scenario type: safety incident, natural disaster, leadership scandal]."
2. "Define crisis roles and decision authority for HR, legal, security, and communications before a crisis occurs."
3. "What should HR's role be in broader business continuity planning, and where does that overlap with facilities or IT?"
4. "Build a crisis communication tree defining who notifies whom, in what order, during the first hours of a crisis."

### Responding in the moment

1. "Draft an initial employee communication for [crisis scenario] to be sent within the first hour, balancing urgency with accuracy."
2. "What immediate actions should HR take in the first 24 hours following [safety incident/natural disaster/leadership departure]?"
3. "Draft manager talking points for addressing team questions and concerns during [active crisis situation]."
4. "How do we decide what information to share publicly versus internally during the first 24 hours of [crisis type]?"

### Coordinating and supporting

1. "How should HR coordinate with legal and communications when responding to [a reputational crisis / public scandal]?"
2. "What support should we offer employees who were directly affected by [crisis event], covering both practical and psychological needs?"
3. "Draft a plan for managing workforce continuity if [facility/team] is unable to operate for [timeframe] due to [disaster]."
4. "What resources should HR have pre-arranged, such as an EAP or crisis counseling line, before a crisis makes them urgently needed?"

### Learning and improving

1. "Design a post-crisis review process to capture lessons learned and improve our HR crisis playbook."
2. "What should managers be trained on in advance so they're prepared to lead their teams through a crisis?"
3. "How do we update our crisis playbook after [specific incident] without over-indexing on a scenario unlikely to repeat?"
4. "Design a tabletop exercise to test our crisis response plan before a real crisis occurs."

## Tips

- Communicate early and often, even with incomplete information — silence during a crisis is read as either indifference or that something worse is being hidden.
- Prepare playbooks before a crisis, not during one; decision-making under pressure is faster and better when roles and templates already exist.
- Prioritize employee safety and wellbeing visibly and first — how leadership responds to a crisis becomes a defining, long-remembered moment for culture and trust.
- Coordinate tightly with legal and communications so HR messaging doesn't create legal exposure or contradict the broader organizational response.
- Run a post-crisis review every time, even when the response went reasonably well — crises reveal playbook gaps that are hard to see in calm conditions.
