# Aaron Ross: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Ross was employee number 150 at Salesforce.com, where he helped build the outbound sales development team [S10]. The company he founded says that process, which it calls "Cold Calling 2.0," helped add $100 million in recurring revenue at Salesforce [S10]. He wrote it up as *Predictable Revenue*, in print by late 2011 [S1], and later co-wrote *From Impossible to Inevitable* with SaaStr's Jason Lemkin [S11][S12]. His core argument is that growth comes from splitting the jobs: dedicated prospectors find new deals, closers close, and different people handle inbound leads and existing customers [S2][S13]. Prospecting itself should be polite and useful. Aim at a narrow ideal customer, ask for a referral instead of pushing a pitch, keep the first message tiny, and track results rather than raw activity [S3][S5][S12].

## Core ideas

### Specialise the sales roles

Ross argued that one of the biggest drains on sales is asking the same person to qualify inbound leads, prospect, close and manage accounts [S2]. He named four roles. Inbound reps answer leads from the website and marketing. Outbound reps, or sales development reps, work target accounts to create new deals. Account executives close. Account managers handle deployment, renewals and customer happiness [S2]. A team of generalists is costly, can't be expert at anything, muddies the numbers and hides problems [S2].

How he applied it: he said to specialise sooner than you think, with as few as two salespeople [S2]. In his interview with David Skok he went further. The second sales hire can already be a dedicated lead generator, and when a side job eats more than about 20% of a role, split it out [S13]. Splitting also builds a career ladder: prospecting becomes a place to learn the business before moving into closing, and home-grown talent often becomes the strongest [S2].

### Give someone the job of prospecting

In a 2011 post he argued that salespeople don't skip prospecting just because they are lazy [S1]. They fear rejection, lack the skills, see no quick payoff, and are buried in deal support, account service, data entry and reports [S1]. If most of a team fails at it, the cause is the system [S1]. His most important fix was a dedicated outbound rep, ideally two, who only prospects and never closes or handles inbound leads [S1].

How he applied it: he said outbound reps should spend no more than about 5 to 10% of their time on inbound leads, or the outbound work fades [S5]. He argued that new graduates can be trained to hold smart conversations with senior people, by phone or email [S1].

### Ask for a referral, not a sale

Cold calls fail, the blog argued, because people dislike being interrupted, busy people get too many calls, and callers jump to the pitch with no friendlier way in [S3]. The answer was to ask for help. Call or email to ask who the right person is, open by asking if it is a bad time, and explain simply what you do [S3]. He compared it to asking a waiter what to order in a strange restaurant [S3].

How he applied it: in the Skok interview he described a small Salesforce test. A standard sales pitch drew no replies, while a short email asking for a referral to the right person drew answers [S13]. A 2012 post set out a "Talk To The Right Person" call. Start high, with the president's assistant or a front desk, ask for a moment, be honest about why you are calling, let them ask what you do, check for fit, and ask for a referral. The tone should be humble, like a lost lamb, not a bulldog [S4]. The post claimed this gets useful information or a referral from cold accounts 80% of the time or more, which is a self-reported figure [S4].

### Nail a niche before you try to grow

The first chapter of his 2019 book argues that lead generation is the main lever on revenue growth, and that many firms are not ready to grow because they have not "nailed a niche" [S12]. Niche here does not mean small. It means a specific pain, for an ideal target customer, solved in a believable and repeatable way, with predictable methods to find and interest those buyers [S12]. He lists warning signs: growth mostly from referrals and word of mouth, weak outbound or inbound results, a reliance on old relationships or a known brand, being good at too many things, and quality appointments that rarely buy [S12]. He names a risk for firms with a wide range of products, "Portfolio Attention Deficit Disorder," where staff and buyers don't know what to sell or buy first [S12].

How he applied it: his test is whether you can sign unaffiliated, paying customers, meaning people with no prior tie to you. At EchoSign, ten such customers paid about $200 a month in total, which he called "beer money," yet he treated them as proof you can reach twenty, then a hundred [S12]. His advice is to double from ten to twenty, then forty, rather than chase a thousand [S12]. He also cites firms that began narrow, such as Salesforce with sales force automation and Amazon with books [S12]. Earlier blog posts made the same point as a rule of thumb: only about 3 to 10% of target companies are ready to buy at any time, so a narrow profile shrinks the field and sharpens your message [S5].

### Respect the tiny window of attention

Ross says early adopters, who trust you, are about 15% of the market, and mainstream buyers, who don't, are about 85% [S12]. Strangers give you little time: he guessed 0.3 to 3 seconds for a cold email or ad, 3 to 30 seconds for a cold call, and 15 minutes to an hour for a referral. He labels these windows non-scientific [S12]. He calls the difference the Trust Gap and draws it as the "Arc of Attention" [S12].

How he applied it: write to a cold stranger as if they were a third or fourth grader, with a message that is simple to grasp and easy to answer [S12]. Keep first touches short. Borrow what catches the "dinosaur brain" before conscious thought: newness, contrast, movement, surprise, detail and visuals. Be intriguing without overpromising, because people learn to ignore teasers that don't deliver [S12].

### Measure results, not dials

Ross told teams to track results such as completed scoping calls per week, and to stop counting dials [S1]. Keep the CRM simple, with a few key numbers like new pipeline per month and qualified leads per month [S1]. Separate roles also make the numbers clearer, because you can see inbound volume, qualification rates and conversion at each step [S2][S13].

How he applied it: a 2012 company post on copying the Salesforce model said to judge prospectors by qualified opportunities that closers accept, not just meetings booked. It gave benchmarks such as 10 to 20 qualified opportunities per rep per month for a world-class team [S9].

### Fix the system before blaming the people

In a 2013 post he rejected the idea that sales has to be miserable to work [S5]. If many prospectors are unhappy or missing target, look at the system: the manager, the goals, the career path, the pay [S5]. Unhappy reps leave fast and rarely offer ideas, which makes a team hard to grow [S5]. Good recruiting, onboarding, training, coaching and management are what let a team scale [S5].

How he applied it: a 2013 series of short videos named three fatal CEO mistakes: hiring the wrong sales leader, managing in a rigid top-down way, and setting goals the team can't meet [S7].

### Grow the deal, not just the activity

The blog argued that growing average deal size is often the easiest and healthiest way to grow revenue [S6]. Small deals cover costs and teach you, but bigger deals drive growth. Bigger customers often pay up front, stay longer and get more value [S6]. Doubling deal size can double what a team brings in, which is often easier than doubling activity [S5].

How he applied it: the post gave steps to go bigger. Ask what it would take for a client to spend ten times more, find which clients would need that, slow down pilots at large firms until more senior people are on board, and use prospectors to reach senior leaders and other divisions. It warned early companies to chase mid-sized deals rather than huge, complex ones [S6][S8].

### Be patient, and count the cost

Ross warned in 2013 that a new prospecting team usually takes 6 to 12 months to bring in regular revenue. Companies that build it slowly, halfway or on the cheap tend to waste time and money. He also said not every company can get top results [S8].

How he applied it: the company's 2012 return-on-investment guide estimated two new customers a month per prospector, times a year, times the value of a top customer. It noted the model fits product companies with over $1 million in revenue selling direct to businesses, and may not hold for services or relationship-driven markets [S9].

## How he decides

- Split the jobs as soon as two people can share them, and when a side job passes about a fifth of someone's time [S2][S13].
- Give one person the only job of prospecting [S1].
- Ask for the right contact before you pitch [S3][S4].
- Define the ideal customer narrowly, then prove it with unaffiliated paying customers [S5][S12].
- Keep the first message short enough for a stranger to take in [S12].
- Track qualified results, not dials [S1].
- Look at the system when many people fail [S5].
- Plan for 6 to 12 months before revenue shows [S8].

## Real decisions

### Splitting prospecting out of the sellers' job

At Salesforce, Ross says combining prospecting, inbound qualification, closing and account care in one seller destroyed focus and blurred the numbers. His answer was a dedicated outbound team that creates opportunities and hands them to closers [S13][S10]. His stated reason was focus and clean metrics. The company credits the wider process with $100 million in added recurring revenue, a self-reported figure [S10].

### Testing a referral email against a pitch

In a small Salesforce experiment, he compared a conventional sales pitch with a short email asking to be pointed to the right person. In that test the referral email drew answers and the pitch did not [S13]. He reads this as proof that a polite ask opens doors. Our note warns that response rates are specific to that setting and should not be promised for a new campaign [S13].

### Treating ten cold customers as proof

At EchoSign, the early product had about ten paying customers who had no tie to the founders, worth around $200 a month. The money did not cover the bills, but Ross read it as proof the product stood on its own with strangers [S12]. His call was to double that number again and again instead of widening the vision. The first such customer was a sales manager in debt consolidation, yet her use case matched most of the customers who came later [S12].

### Refusing to hand inbound leads to outbound reps

In a 2011 post he argued against giving inbound leads to the outbound team. His reason, as we recorded it earlier, was that outbound reps should spend only about 5 to 10% of their time on inbound, or prospecting fades [S2][S5]. The tradeoff is that someone else must own those leads, which is why he named a separate inbound role [S2].

### Telling a CEO a new team takes a year

When firms wanted fast results from outbound, he told them a new team usually needs 6 to 12 months and that cheap or half-built versions waste time and money [S8]. He said some firms would not get top results at all [S8]. The reason was honesty about the ramp, since leaders who expect results in a month cut the programme before it works.

## Where he stood

**Should closers prospect?** No. Let closers close and prospectors prospect [S2].

**Are salespeople who don't prospect lazy?** Usually not. The system sets them up to fail [S1].

**Is cold calling dead?** The old pitch-first call fails. A polite call or email asking for a referral works [S3][S4][S13].

**Does niche mean thinking small?** No. It means focus on one customer and one pain, which is how big firms started [S12].

**More activity or bigger deals?** Often bigger deals, which can double revenue with the same effort [S5][S6].

**Who is outbound for?** Mostly companies selling to businesses with enough revenue and deal size to pay for a team [S9].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Big claims, mostly self-reported.** The $100 million figure and client results come from his own company's pages [S10][S8]. We found no independent check.
- **Not for every business.** His own company's guide says the model fits business-to-business product companies of a certain size, and that results vary widely elsewhere [S9].
- **Slow to pay off.** He admitted a new team takes 6 to 12 months to show revenue and that not every company gets top results [S8].
- **Marketing mixed with advice.** Some posts double as sales pages for his programs [S8]. Read the advice apart from the offer.
- **Little outside criticism found.** Our sources are friendly. That is a gap, not proof that the method has no flaws.

## Blind spots to watch

- Splitting roles adds handoffs. A small team can lose context between prospector and closer.
- Outbound to strangers depends on local rules for email and calls. Check the law before you start.
- His world is software sold to businesses. A shop selling to the public, or a services firm, may need a different approach [S9].
- The attention windows and the 15 and 85 percent split are his own rough numbers, not studies [S12].
- Results-based numbers can still turn into a quota grind. Keep the engagement points from his own 2013 post [S5].
- Referral asks work best when honest. Don't turn the "lost lamb" into a trick.
