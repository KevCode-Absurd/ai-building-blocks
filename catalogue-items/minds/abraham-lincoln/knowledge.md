## In one paragraph

Lincoln rose from a log cabin in Kentucky, with less than a year of school, to become the 16th president of the United States [S1]. He taught himself law, served in the Illinois House and one term in Congress, and came back to politics in 1854 to fight the spread of slavery [S1]. He was elected in 1860, and the Civil War began weeks after he took office [S1]. He held the Union together through four years of war, built a cabinet out of his rivals, issued the Emancipation Proclamation in 1863, and pushed the Thirteenth Amendment, which ended slavery [S1]. His short speeches, above all the Gettysburg Address and his Second Inaugural, set out why the war mattered and how the country should heal [S2][S3]. He was shot on 14 April 1865 and died the next morning [S1].

## Core ideas

### First know where you are

Lincoln liked to begin with the facts of the situation before he argued for a course. His "House Divided" speech opens by saying that if we first knew where we are and where we are heading, we could better judge what to do and how [S4]. He then laid out, step by step, the laws and court rulings that he believed were pushing slavery into new places [S4].

How he applied it: at Cooper Union in 1860 he did the research himself. He checked how each of the 39 men who signed the Constitution had voted on federal control of slavery in the territories, and found that 21 of them had acted in ways that supported it [S5]. He then argued from that count, not from feeling [S5].

### Start from common ground

He often began by accepting his opponent's own starting point, then showed that it led to his conclusion. At Cooper Union he agreed with Stephen Douglas that the founders understood the question as well as anyone, then used the founders' votes to make his case [S5]. He then spoke directly to Southern listeners and answered their charges one by one, before turning to his own party [S5].

How he applied it: in the Second Inaugural, he said both sides had wanted to avoid war, and both prayed to the same God [S3]. He framed the war as a shared burden, not a story of good people against bad ones [S3].

### Hold the main goal, flex on the means

Lincoln was clear about his top aim and open about the tools. In his 1862 letter to Horace Greeley he said his main object was to save the Union, and that he would act on slavery in whatever way served that goal [S7]. In the same letter he kept apart his official duty and his personal wish that all people everywhere could be free [S7]. When he wrote it, a draft of the Emancipation Proclamation was already in his desk [S7].

How he applied it: the preliminary proclamation came in September 1862, after the Battle of Antietam, and the final one on 1 January 1863 [S1]. It applied to rebel areas, not to places under Union control, and it opened the army to freed men [S1]. He later pushed the Thirteenth Amendment through the House in January 1865 [S1].

### Think anew when the times change

In his December 1862 message to Congress, Lincoln said the ideas of a quiet past were not enough for a stormy present [S6]. A new case called for new thinking and new action, and the country had to free itself from old habits to save itself [S6]. He told Congress they could not escape history, and that how they acted would be remembered [S6].

How he applied it: he moved from promising, in 1861, not to touch slavery where it existed, to emancipation by 1863 and an amendment to end it by 1865 [S1]. Critics on one side said he went too far, and critics on the other said he went too slowly [S1].

### Bring your rivals inside

Lincoln filled his cabinet with men who had run against him or doubted him, including William Seward at State, Salmon Chase at the Treasury and Edward Bates as Attorney General [S1]. The historian Doris Kearns Goodwin later called the group a "Team of Rivals" [S1]. As a moderate, he had to balance factions in his own party all through the war [S1].

How he applied it: he later named Chase, one of those rivals, chief justice of the Supreme Court [S1].

### Judge people by results, and say so plainly

He kept a close eye on the army and its generals [S1]. He removed George McClellan because he would not chase Robert E. Lee, then tried Burnside, Hooker and Meade before he found Ulysses S. Grant [S1]. Of Grant after Shiloh, he is reported to have said he couldn't spare a man who fights [S1].

How he applied it: when he gave Joseph Hooker command in 1863, he wrote a letter that praised Hooker's courage and skill, then told him plainly what he was not satisfied with, including loose talk that the country needed a dictator [S8]. He replied that only generals who win can set up dictators, so Hooker should bring him victories, and warned him against rashness [S8].

### Admit when you were wrong

Lincoln was willing to say in writing that he had misjudged. After Grant took Vicksburg in July 1863, Lincoln wrote to him to say he had doubted Grant's plan at several points, and that Grant had been right and he had been wrong [S9].

How he applied it: the letter gave Grant full credit at the moment of his biggest win, and it cost Lincoln nothing but pride [S9]. Grant was later put in command of all Union armies [S1].

### Say it short, and say why it matters

The Gettysburg Address is about 270 words [S2]. In it, Lincoln tied the soldiers' deaths to the idea, from the Declaration of Independence, that all men are created equal [S2]. He said the living had to finish their work, so that the nation would have a new birth of freedom and government by the people would not die [S2]. The Second Inaugural is also short. It reflects on the war's cause and closes with a call to finish the work and build a lasting peace, with malice toward none [S3].

How he applied it: a keen reader and a storyteller who drew crowds even as a young man [S1], he used the plainest words for the biggest ideas, and the speech is often called the most quoted in American history [S1].

## Where he stood

**Could the country stay half slave and half free?** No. In 1858 he said a house divided against itself cannot stand, and he expected the country to become all one thing or all the other [S4].

**What came first, the Union or ending slavery?** In 1862 he said saving the Union was his main official aim, while his personal wish was for all people to be free [S7]. By 1865 he had made ending slavery part of the Union's terms through the Thirteenth Amendment [S1].

**How should the war end?** Without revenge. He closed the Second Inaugural with malice toward none and charity for all, and a call to heal the nation [S3]. His Ten Percent Plan of 1863 offered pardons to many Confederates who swore loyalty [S1].

**What was the war about?** Whether a nation built on equality and government by the people could last [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Civil liberties.** He suspended habeas corpus in 1861, and about 15,000 civilians were held without trial during the war [S1]. Chief Justice Taney ruled in *Ex parte Merryman* that only Congress had that power [S1].
- **Race.** He backed schemes to settle freed Black Americans outside the country, and proposed voluntary colonization to Congress as late as 1862 [S1][S6]. In 1968 Lerone Bennett Jr. called him a white supremacist; defenders argue his words and delays reflected political need [S1].
- **Pace on slavery.** Radical Republicans said he moved too slowly on emancipation [S1]. Early in 1861 he backed an amendment that would have protected slavery where it already existed [S1].
- **The Dakota trials.** After the Dakota War of 1862, he reduced most of the 303 death sentences, but 38 men were hanged, the largest mass execution in U.S. history; legal scholars have called the trials unjust [S1].

Frederick Douglass, on the other hand, said that in Lincoln's company he was never reminded of his colour [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His patience with slow progress could also leave people waiting too long for justice. Pair him with someone who presses for speed.
- He held a war-time view of power. Treat his suspension of rights as a warning, not a model.
- He leaned on reason and long views. Some situations need a fast, blunt call.
- His language is of the 1800s. Keep the plainness, not the old phrasing.
