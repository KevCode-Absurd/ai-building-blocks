# Adam Grant: what he knew and believed

This data set comes from his own website and book pages [S2][S4][S5][S6][S7], a 2026 newsletter essay [S8], his reader Q&A archive "Wondering" (2017 to 2018) [S10], a 2021 review of *Think Again* [S9], and reference pages [S1][S3]. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after 2026.

## In one paragraph

Grant is an American organisational psychologist, born in Michigan in 1981, with a PhD from the University of Michigan [S1]. He joined Wharton in 2009 and became its youngest tenured professor at 28 [S1]. His research covers motivation, job design, helping behaviour and procrastination [S1]. His books include Give and Take (2013), Originals (2016), Option B (2017, with Sheryl Sandberg), Think Again (2021), Hidden Potential (2023) and Vibe (2026) [S1][S3]. He hosts the podcasts WorkLife and Re:Thinking and writes the Granted newsletter [S1][S2]. His site sums up his outlook: the most meaningful way to succeed is to help others succeed [S2]. Across his work runs a set of linked ideas. Generous people can win, if they protect themselves. Good thinkers rethink. Original ideas need people willing to speak up. And potential depends more on how well you learn than on where you start [S4][S5][S6][S7].

## Core ideas

### Givers, takers and matchers

Give and Take sorts people by how they trade help at work [S4]. Takers try to get as much as they can from others. Matchers aim for an even trade. Givers help others without expecting anything back [S4]. Grant argues that givers land at both ends. Some get exploited and burn out, while others achieve extraordinary results in many fields [S4].

How he applied it: in 2017 he co-founded a company, Give and Take, Inc., to help organisations put these ideas to work [S1].

### Give, but don't become a people pleaser

In a 2026 essay, Grant called himself a recovering people pleaser [S8]. He argued that chronic people pleasing is not really about other people. Its root is a wish to be accepted and a fear of being disliked [S8]. It even hurts others. Staying quiet to avoid an awkward moment means you don't tell people what they need to hear [S8]. He framed saying no as self-respect and self-preservation, not selfishness [S8].

How he applied it: he gave a small example. He didn't tell a driver the car was freezing, so the next passenger had to shiver too [S8]. He also drew a line between where a habit comes from and what it does for you now. People pleasing often starts early, for example when keeping the peace felt like the way to stay safe. Grant argued that knowing the origin is useful, but the real question is what purpose the habit serves today, and whether it still helps anyone [S8].

### Think like a scientist

Think Again argues that the ability to rethink and unlearn can matter more than raw intelligence [S5]. People tend to prefer the comfort of being sure over the discomfort of doubt [S5]. Being a skilled thinker can even make rethinking harder, because you are good at defending what you already believe [S5]. His remedy is to treat views like a scientist would: keep an open mind and revise them when the evidence calls for it [S9].

How he applied it: his guiding rule is to argue as if you're right but listen as if you're wrong [S5]. The book draws on cases such as a debate champion, a musician who talks people out of hate groups, and a specialist who works with people unsure about vaccines [S5].

### Seek out people who challenge you

Grant says we should look for people who challenge our thinking, not just those who agree with us [S5]. Knowing what we don't know, he writes, is a kind of wisdom [S5]. Humility and curiosity are the habits that make rethinking possible [S5].

How he applied it: Originals carries the same idea into teams, showing how leaders can build cultures that welcome dissent [S6].

### Champion original ideas

Originals is about how to champion new ideas and fight groupthink [S6]. It covers how to recognise a good idea, how to speak up without being silenced, how to build allies, how to choose the right time to act, and how to manage fear and doubt [S6]. It also gives advice for parents, teachers and leaders who want to nurture originality [S6].

How he applied it: his research found that moderate procrastination went with more creative ideas, in a 2021 study with Jihae Shin [S1]. Another study, from 2011, linked creativity to intrinsic motivation when people also cared about helping others [S1].

### Too much of a good thing

With Barry Schwartz, Grant argued in 2011 that many positive traits follow an "inverted U" [S1]. A trait helps up to a point, then starts to hurt at high levels [S1]. Generosity that turns into people pleasing fits this pattern [S8].

How he applied it: the same thinking shows in his advice that givers need limits to avoid burnout [S4][S8].

### Purpose drives effort

Grant's early research looked at what keeps people going in hard jobs [S1]. A 2008 study of firefighters and university fundraising callers linked caring about others, together with enjoying the work itself, to more persistence and productivity [S1].

How he applied it: his site says his research has raised performance and cut burnout among engineers, teachers and salespeople, and has encouraged safety behaviour among doctors, nurses and lifeguards [S3].

### Hidden potential

Hidden Potential argues that people can achieve more than their starting advantages suggest [S7]. We tend to overrate natural talent and underrate how much people can learn [S7]. Progress depends more on how well you learn than on how hard you work, and growth comes from the character you build, not the genius you were born with [S7]. He also argues for building systems that open doors for people who have been overlooked [S7].

How he applied it: the book's line is that when opportunity doesn't knock, you can build a door [S7]. Its view is that we can all improve at improving [S7].

## How he decides

- Treat your views as hypotheses, and ask what would change your mind [S5][S9].
- Argue like you're right, listen like you're wrong [S5].
- Look for people who disagree, and listen to them [S5].
- Help others, but set limits so you don't burn out [S4][S8].
- Say no when yes comes from fear of being disliked [S8].
- Watch for good traits pushed too far [S1].
- Make it safe for people to speak up with new ideas [S6].
- Judge potential by how fast someone learns, not by their head start [S7].

## Real decisions

### He rewrote the opening of Give and Take after an editor pushed back
A sceptical editor challenged a moralising opening of his book proposal. He moved the paradox up front: givers appear at both the bottom and the top of performance. The argument got stronger for a doubtful reader [S10]. This is the start of his two-circle method: ask a support network for early ideas, then a challenge network to attack finished work [S10].

### He modelled vulnerability to unlock feedback
In a 2006 class, students sat silent during 360 feedback. He had one student go first and evaluate themselves aloud, and the room opened up. His reason: people hold back to avoid hurting you, and naming your own flaws removes the fear [S10].

### He sets two tests for trusting feedback
Trust feedback when the field is predictable and the source has relevant expertise and direct knowledge of you [S10]. He also suggests keeping a second score for how well you receive feedback [S10].

### He argues that givers must choose
Asked if giving clashes with essentialism, he said no: successful givers are deliberate about who they help, when and how [S10]. His book shows givers at both ends of results, some exploited and burned out and others on top [S4].

### He judges the process, not only the result
He tells leaders to reward good processes even when the result was bad, and to question good results that came from bad processes [S10].

### He corrected himself in public
He had called people pleasing selfish. An audience member said it was survival. He conceded both of them were partly wrong and moved to "self-focused," since people please to avoid their own discomfort [S8]. He backs that with research on fear of rejection and shaky self-worth [S8].

### He does not hire only for fit
He worries about too much alignment with a company's purpose, which can create zealots, and argues for keeping some disagreeable givers and cultural misfits [S10].

## Where he stood

**Do nice people finish last?** Some givers do, but others finish first. The difference lies in how they give [S4].

**Is intelligence enough?** No. The ability to rethink and unlearn can matter more [S5].

**Is saying no selfish?** No. He called it an act of self-preservation [S8].

**Is talent fixed?** No. He argues people underrate how much they can learn and grow [S7].

**Is procrastination always bad?** Not always. A moderate amount was linked to more creative ideas in his research [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Borrowing others' research.** A 2021 review noted that Grant is sometimes criticised for taking other scholars' ideas and turning them into popular books, though the reviewer saw this as his strength [S9].
- **Too simple.** A reader replying to his people-pleasing essay argued that it needed more nuance, since caring about others' wishes can come from love and values, not just fear [S8].
- **Self-reported claims.** Much of his public profile, such as rankings and sales, comes from his own site [S3]. Wikipedia flags its article on him as needing factual checks [S1].
- **Thin critical record here.** Our sources hold few detailed reviews of his books. Treat that as a gap, not a clean record.

## Blind spots to watch

- He is optimistic about people. Some situations call for more caution about takers than his tone suggests.
- His tools assume people can speak up safely. In some workplaces they can't, and that needs fixing first.
- Popular summaries of research can make findings sound more settled than they are.
- He focuses on individuals and teams. Pair him with someone who sees pay, power and structure.
- Rethinking everything can stall action. Sometimes a team needs to decide and commit.
