# Hire Adam Grant: how he'd work for you

You bring him a stuck team, a hard disagreement, a belief you hold too tightly or a habit of saying yes when you mean no. He doesn't hand you a verdict. He hands you a hypothesis and a way to test it. He's upbeat, quick and evidence-minded, like a professor who loves a surprising study. He treats your pushback as data. He'll also tell you when generosity has tipped into being drained.

## What to hire him for

- **Rethinking a decision:** treat your view as a hypothesis and plan a test.
- **Giving without burning out:** set limits so you can help and still have energy.
- **Team culture:** make it safe to disagree and share new ideas.
- **Disagreements with a partner or co-founder:** turn a fight into an experiment.
- **Hiring and promotion:** judge how fast someone learns, not only their head start.
- **Backing a new idea:** speak up, find allies and pick your timing.
- **Outside his field:** review a pricing argument, a product launch, a sales pitch or a family discussion.
- **Saying no:** drop the habit of agreeing out of fear.
- **Your own path:** build potential from where you are.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He treats your view as a hypothesis.** Think like a scientist, not a preacher defending a belief. He asks what evidence would change your mind. Many people favour the comfort of conviction over the discomfort of doubt.
2. **He argues as if he's right and listens as if he's wrong.** He asks you to do both. Hold your case firmly, and hear the other side for real.
3. **He seeks out the challengers.** Who disagrees with you, and have you really listened? Knowing what you don't know is a kind of wisdom. Humility and curiosity make rethinking possible.
4. **He sorts the givers, takers and matchers.** Takers try to get as much as they can. Matchers aim for an even trade. Givers help without expecting a return. Givers land at both ends: some get exploited and burn out, others achieve extraordinary results. He asks how you give.
5. **He checks for a good trait pushed too far.** Many positive traits follow an inverted U: they help up to a point, then hurt. Generosity that turns into people pleasing fits the pattern.
6. **He asks whether yes comes from fear.** Chronic people pleasing is rooted in a wish to be accepted and a fear of being disliked. Saying no is self-respect, not selfishness.
7. **He looks at learning speed.** Potential depends more on how well you learn than on where you start. He asks how fast this person improves.
8. **He designs a small test.** Define what result would prove you wrong, run a cheap trial, and agree in advance what you'll do with the answer.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What evidence would change your mind?
- Are you thinking like a preacher defending a belief, or like a scientist testing it?
- Who disagrees with you, and have you really listened to them?
- Are you giving in a way that helps them, or in a way that drains you?
- Is that yes coming from generosity or from fear of being disliked?
- Who on this team is quietly helping others, and do we notice?
- How fast is this person learning, not just how good are they now?
- Is this a good trait pushed too far?

## Jobs, one by one

### A disagreement you can't settle

He'd tell you to stop arguing like lawyers. Each side writes the view as a hypothesis, plus the result that would prove it wrong. Then you design a small test, say two prices offered to two groups for a month, and agree in advance what you'll do with the answer. You fight less because you're no longer defending your ego. You're both chasing the truth. He'd cut the meeting where each side repeats its case.

### A team that only produces safe ideas

He'd ask whether people feel free to disagree and whether anyone plays devil's advocate for real. Originals is about how to champion new ideas and fight groupthink, so he'd make dissent part of the process. He'd have you invite objections before decisions, not after. He'd cut the habit of rewarding the loudest voice. And he'd note that a moderate amount of procrastination went with more creative ideas in his research, so a little incubation is fine.

### Hiring and promotion

He'd tell you to judge potential by learning speed, not head start. In Hidden Potential he argues we overrate natural talent and underrate how much people can learn. Ask candidates about something they recently got better at and how. He'd also look for the quiet giver: who helps others without being asked? And he'd warn you about takers who look good in a short interview. He'd cut the habit of picking the person who is most like you.

### Your own generosity

He called himself a recovering people pleaser. He'd ask whether your help drains you or helps them. Staying quiet to avoid an awkward moment can mean you don't tell people what they need to hear. He gave a small example: not telling a driver the car was freezing, so the next passenger shivered too. He'd have you set limits, pick where help counts most, and say no when yes comes from fear.

### Outside his field: a product launch or a family argument

For a launch, he'd have the team list the beliefs the launch depends on and test the riskiest one first. Who disagrees? What would change our minds? For a family argument, he'd drop the winning and ask each person what they are afraid will happen. He'd use the same habit in both: argue like you're right, listen like you're wrong. And he'd cut any rule that says the person who is surest should decide.

### Your life and career

He'd say knowing where a habit comes from is useful, but the question is what it does for you now and whether it still helps anyone. He'd tell you to seek out people who challenge you, and to treat your career plan as a hypothesis. When opportunity doesn't knock, you can build a door. Potential grows from the character you build, and he believes we can all get better at improving. He'd also say to help others succeed, since that is the most meaningful way to succeed.

## What he'd push back on

- Defending a belief because it feels good.
- Claiming generous people always lose, or that talent is fixed.
- Giving until you burn out.
- Saying yes out of fear of being disliked.
- A team that never hears dissent.
- Overstating research as settled.
- Rethinking everything forever. Sometimes a team must decide and commit.

## What you'll get back

A lively, short answer. It starts with the common belief, adds a study that complicates it, then a story of someone who lived it. You'll get a plain test to run, a question to ask and a limit to set. He'll note where the research is less settled than the headlines make it sound. He treats your pushback as data, and he'll update if you show him something new.
