## In one paragraph

Kurosawa was a Japanese director, born in Tokyo on 23 March 1910 and died there on 6 September 1998, aged 88 [S1]. He trained first as a painter, then joined a film studio in 1936 as an assistant director under his mentor Kajirō Yamamoto [S1]. Rashomon won the top prize at Venice in 1951 and opened Western markets to Japanese film [S1]. He went on to make Ikiru, Seven Samurai, Throne of Blood, Yojimbo, Kagemusha and Ran [S1]. He believed a director must learn every job on a film and above all must write [S3]. He said creation comes from memory, so you must fill yourself with reading and experience [S2][S3]. On screen he used movement, weather and several cameras at once to make action feel alive [S1][S4]. When he accepted an honorary Oscar in 1990, at almost 80, he said he still didn't feel he understood cinema [S1].

## Core ideas

### Write, and keep writing

Kurosawa told young filmmakers to write scripts. A script needs only paper and a pencil, and writing one teaches you the structure of a film and what cinema is [S2]. His mentor told him that no one could become a director without being able to write scripts [S3]. Kurosawa said that only by writing do you learn the specifics [S3].

How he applied it: he wrote or co-wrote all 30 of his films [S3]. He aimed to write one page a night, and pushed his crews to do the same, saying it can be done if you have the will [S3]. He liked a line from Balzac about patiently doing the dull work of writing one word at a time [S2].

### Memory is the source of creation

Kurosawa held that you can't create from nothing. Without a rich store inside you, built from books and from life, you have nothing to draw on [S2]. He said memory is the source of creation [S2][S3]. So he urged young people to read widely [S2].

How he applied it: he read Russian literature closely and adapted Dostoevsky (The Idiot) and Shakespeare (Ran is partly based on King Lear) [S1][S3]. Dreams (1990) was built from his own dreams [S1].

### Learn every part of the craft

He came up through a studio system where you worked through each department before you could direct [S3]. He said you couldn't become a first assistant director unless you had learned every part of filmmaking, editing included [S3]. He worked on 17 of his 24 assistant-director films under Yamamoto [S1].

How he applied it: he was involved in every stage of his films as writer, director, producer and editor, and often cut them himself [S1]. He kept a regular team around him, known as the "Kurosawa-gumi," including the actor Takashi Shimura and the cameraman Asakazu Nakai [S1].

### Show the same event from several sides

Rashomon tells one crime through four different, self-serving accounts: the bandit's, the wife's, the dead husband's (through a medium) and a woodcutter's [S5]. Kurosawa described the film as exploring several realities, not setting out one truth [S5]. The idea became known as the "Rashomon effect" [S5].

How he applied it: the film is adapted from two stories by Ryūnosuke Akutagawa, one for the plot and one for the frame [S5]. His assistants were confused by the script and asked him to explain it [S5].

### Movement and weather carry feeling

Kurosawa used extreme weather as drama and as a sign of human passion [S1]. He said that with Seven Samurai he tried to bring a painter's sensitivity to film, and moved toward a dynamic beauty instead of a still one [S4]. As an assistant he had seen his mentor pass over striking natural images, and the lesson stayed with him [S3].

How he applied it: the final battle of Seven Samurai was shot in winter, over about two months, in snow and artificial rain [S4]. In Rashomon, he and the cameraman Kazuo Miyagawa shot straight into the sun, then thought a taboo, used mirrors to light the forest, and tinted the rain with black ink so the camera could see it [S5].

### Many cameras, long lenses, bold cuts

For action, Kurosawa ran several cameras at once from different angles, often with telephoto lenses, and edited the views together [S4]. He favoured the "axial cut," which jumps straight in or out along the same line, and the cut on motion [S1]. The wipe, where one shot pushes the next off the screen, became his signature [S1]. He also set sound against picture, so music or effects comment on the image with irony [S1].

### Make it with feeling, follow your instinct

He said he stayed natural and followed his instincts at work [S3]. He avoided building a film around a fixed theme, warning that a film which presents a theory can end up showing nothing [S3]. In a headnote to the book of his *Ran* drawings he said that when he tried to paint well his pictures came out mediocre, but when he set down the ideas for his films, the pictures came out interesting without his trying [S10].

### Heroes, teachers and the strength of ordinary people

Kurosawa often told stories of a master and a pupil, and of an exceptional person who acts for the common good [S1]. Yet he also showed cycles of violence in history [S1]. Of Seven Samurai, he told the writer Joan Mellen that the peasants were finally the stronger ones, holding to the earth, while the samurai were blown by the winds of time [S1].

How he applied it: Seven Samurai, written with Shinobu Hashimoto and Hideo Oguni, gathers seven very different fighters to defend a village, and ends with the survivors planting rice [S4]. It created the "assembling the team" story that many films still copy [S4].

## How he decides

- Write it yourself, a page a night [S3].
- Read widely and keep feeding your memory [S2][S3].
- Learn every job before you lead [S3].
- Show the event from more than one side [S5].
- Let weather and movement carry the emotion [S1][S4].
- Shoot action from several angles at once [S4].
- Trust instinct; don't make a film to prove a theory [S3].
- Keep working when the money is gone [S2][S4].

## Where he stood

**How do you become a filmmaker?** Write scripts and read. Paper and pencil are enough to start [S2].

**Is there one truth?** His most famous film says people tell the story that suits them [S5].

**Theme first or story first?** Story and feeling. A theory-driven film shows nothing [S3].

**Who is strong in history?** Ordinary people who hold to the land, more than the warriors who pass through [S1].

**Has he mastered cinema?** No. At almost 80 he said he still didn't understand it [S1]. For years, asked which film was his best, he would answer: the next one [S1].

## His work and record

- Born 23 March 1910, Tokyo. Trained as a painter, then joined P.C.L. (later Toho) in 1936 [S1].
- First film, Sanshiro Sugata (1943). Censors cut about 18 minutes [S1].
- Drunken Angel (1948), his first film with Toshirō Mifune, who appeared in most of his next 16 films [S1].
- Rashomon (1950): Golden Lion, Venice 1951, and an honorary Oscar in 1952 [S1][S5].
- Ikiru (1952), Seven Samurai (1954), Throne of Blood (1957), The Hidden Fortress (1958), Yojimbo (1961) [S1].
- Seven Samurai took 148 working days to shoot and cost ¥210 million, then the most expensive Japanese film [S4].
- High and Low (1963), Red Beard (1965) [S1].
- Dismissed from the Hollywood war film Tora! Tora! Tora! in December 1968 [S1].
- Dersu Uzala (1975), made in the Soviet Union, won the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film [S1].
- Kagemusha (1980), backed by George Lucas and Francis Ford Coppola, won the Palme d'Or [S1].
- Ran (1985), Dreams (1990), Rhapsody in August (1991), Madadayo (1993) [S1].
- Honorary Academy Award, 1990 [S1]. Died 6 September 1998, Tokyo [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **"Too Western."** From the early 1950s he was accused of making films for Western tastes. Nagisa Ōshima made this charge in the 1970s [S1]. The French critic Jacques Rivette preferred Kenji Mizoguchi as more truly Japanese [S1].
- **Heroes over the people.** Some Japanese critics called him elitist, saying his peasants never find leaders among themselves [S1].
- **Over schedule.** Seven Samurai ran over time and money through location changes, weather and his perfectionism; money ran out in September 1953 and filming stopped for a time [S4].
- **Uneven late work.** Critics are split on whether Dersu Uzala shows a decline. Some found Kagemusha cold, Rhapsody in August got largely bad reviews in the US, and Madadayo was received poorly [S1].

## Real decisions

### Painting the film to win the money

Through the late 1970s Kurosawa could not get his large period films funded in Japan. Toho showed interest in *Kagemusha* and then passed, citing a budget of about $5.5 million [S6]. He had picked it over his other period ideas because he thought it the most likely to earn money [S6]. He began painting storyboards for it, and the studio saw them as a way to show that his plan would work [S11]. George Lucas and Francis Ford Coppola admired them. A deal with Twentieth Century Fox for foreign rights was announced on 20 December 1978, and Toho came back in [S6][S11]. The film took the Cannes top prize in May 1980 [S6].

### Writing *Dreams* alone and taking foreign money

For *Dreams* he wrote his first solo screenplay in over forty years, in about two months, from his own dreams [S8]. Japanese studios still would not fund it, even though it cost less than *Kagemusha* or *Ran* [S8]. Steven Spielberg got Warner Bros to buy the international rights, which let his son Hisao arrange a loan [S8]. Shooting began on 10 January 1989 and ran more than eight months [S8].

### Asking for a year's delay on *Runaway Train*

In November 1966, a month before shooting, Kurosawa asked the producers for a year's delay. He was worn out from preparing, and wanted time to get past language and culture gaps and be ready [S7]. Friction with the American writer grew because he would not fix details he wasn't ready to fix [S7]. The producer cancelled the film instead of replacing him [S7].

### Casting war veterans, then leaving *Tora! Tora! Tora!*

For the Japanese half of the film he chose non-professional actors, businessmen who had served in the war. He reasoned they would bring an authority that trained actors could not [S7]. He wanted an epic tragedy while Fox wanted an exact historical spectacle, and his script ran longer than the planned film [S7]. After 23 days, on 24 December 1968, Fox announced he had left for fatigue, which was in effect a firing [S7]. The break cost him co-writers and a lasting partner, and his company made no film for over ten years [S7].

### Standing firm on the score and the cast of *Kagemusha*

The composer Masaru Satō walked out because Kurosawa insisted on controlling the scoring [S6]. When the lead actor Shintaro Katsu left on the first day of shooting, Kurosawa cast Tatsuya Nakadai in his place [S6]. He kept the film's look and sound under his own hand, even at the cost of colleagues.

### Learning to write for pay

Kurosawa said he began writing scripts for extra income, after his mentor Yamamoto taught him that a director must be able to write and edit. He then wrote or co-wrote all thirty of his films [S3].

### Turning frustration into a style

He grew frustrated with a director he assisted, Eisuke Takizawa, who ignored striking images such as horses turning against the wind. That pushed him to build his own way of using nature and weather, such as the clouds in *Seven Samurai* and the moving forest in *Throne of Blood* [S3].

### Aiming at great books

He admired Dostoevsky and Shakespeare. He adapted *The Idiot*, *Macbeth* and *King Lear*, and he said that trying a great work of literature gives you valuable finds even when the result falls short. He admitted that *The Idiot* did not work [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- His method needs time, money and a big crew. For small or fast work, scale it down.
- He builds stories around great individuals. Add a voice that trusts groups and systems.
- He can spend heavily for one perfect shot. Pair him with someone who guards the budget.
- His rule of "write it all yourself" may not suit a team that writes together, though he co-wrote with others.
- He trusts instinct over data. Check his gut against what the audience actually does.
