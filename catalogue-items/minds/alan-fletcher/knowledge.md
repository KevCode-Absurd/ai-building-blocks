## In one paragraph

Fletcher was a British graphic designer, born in Nairobi, who moved to England at five and studied at art schools in London and then at Yale [S1]. In America he got freelance work through Paul Rand and cold-called Saul Bass for more [S2]. Back in London he co-founded Fletcher/Forbes/Gill in 1962, which grew into the design firm Pentagram in 1972 [S1][S5]. He left in 1992 and became art director for the publisher Phaidon [S1][S3]. He was known for bold colour and humour, and for marks like the V&A logo, where an ampersand completes a letter A [S1][S4]. His central belief was that every job needs an idea, and that the rare qualities in a designer are economy of thought, the oblique reference, charm and, above all, wit [S2].

## Core ideas

### Every job needs an idea

Fletcher believed design means reading between the lines of a brief. Once you have the idea, he said, the rest is just layout [S2]. He even treated a colour combination as a possible idea [S2].

How he applied it: IBM asked for a simple notice saying a painting would arrive for a wall in its new Paris office. He made a series of posters, each giving a different writer's or artist's definition of art, and put the line about the painting in tiny type along the bottom. A literal answer, he said, would have been a plain placard [S2].

### Wit is the rarest skill

In his view, a portfolio shows whether someone has ideas and craft. The rarer qualities are economy of thought, the oblique reference, charm and wit [S2]. He liked ideas with extra layers, even private jokes [S2]. A 1993 review noted how much Pentagram's work was built on visual humour [S8].

How he applied it: for Polaroid's new colour film he proposed a colour Rorschach test, the inkblot used by psychologists. When a client asked what it meant, he just shrugged and smiled. He enjoyed the ambiguity [S2].

### Make one mark do two jobs

Fletcher's best-known marks pack two ideas into one simple form. In the V&A logo, set in Bodoni, he removed part of the A and let the stroke of the ampersand stand in for the missing bar, so V, & and A merge into one symbol [S4]. The Reuters logo he designed was built out of 84 dots [S1].

How he applied it: the V&A job began as a signage system. While that was under way, the museum wanted to tidy up its many logo versions. His team spent a week or two testing weights and redrawn ampersands, and the answer came the morning of the presentation [S4]. The logo has stayed in use for decades [S4].

### Drop your crutches

He also said the designer sheep are separated from the goats by whether they push a job to the edge, and that you have to throw away the crutches [S2].

Fletcher warned designers against repeating what they do well until it becomes a habit. Do that, and you become a graphic cliché [S2]. He said many designers claim to be free but make work that looks the same, and he counted himself among them [S2].

How he applied it: he even admitted that his own handwriting style might have become a crutch, and that a critic who said so would probably be right [S2].

### Writing is drawing

Fletcher began writing headlines by hand instead of setting type, partly to save money [S2]. He thought of writing as drawing, with each letter a symbol to play with, and not as fine calligraphy [S2]. He found that the more raw the lettering, the more interesting it got [S2].

How he applied it: his publisher said his hand-made style of handwriting, cut-up letters, found objects and collage was very hard to copy. Foreign publishers who tried to imitate his book jackets produced poor results [S3].

### Look sideways and keep notes

He reduced things to their essence so he would not get trapped in a style, and said his curiosity was insatiable even though he was not given to self-analysis [S2].

*The Art of Looking Sideways* grew out of notes Fletcher made over many years when something caught his eye, from a flower in a crack in the pavement to a basket's print in the earth [S6]. It has no thesis and no beginning, middle or end [S9]. It mixes sayings, his own art and odd facts, grouped under themes like seeing and meaning [S6]. He described it as opening little windows [S6]. Its excerpts are grouped as tools, seeing, thinking and imaging. They hold the idea that we do not think in words, and that looking slowly means setting aside the label for the thing you see [S7].

How he applied it: in the 1990 interview he said the book was really a scrapbook sorted into pigeon-holes such as taste, perception and imagination, and that he was using words like pictures [S2]. He called himself driven by curiosity [S2]. In the book he gathered thinkers who all say creativity comes from joining unlike ideas [S6].

### Ask why before you start

Fletcher pushed hard on purpose before acting. At Phaidon, his publisher said, he would ask why a book was being made, what it was about and what the point was before designing a thing [S3]. This slowed things down at first, which frustrated the publisher, but made him a true editorial designer [S3].

How he applied it: he designed the jacket for *The Art Book* in 1994, a low-priced guide with one page per artist. It became one of Phaidon's best sellers, and the jacket helped draw in readers who might have been put off [S3].

### Peers keep you honest, pride keeps you going

Fletcher valued the informal critique at Pentagram. Partners would pass by and give opinions he had to take seriously. That stopped him fooling himself that a job was good when it was only 80% there [S2]. He said what drove the partners was pride in the work, not how much a job earned [S2].

How he applied it: paying clients funded the projects he enjoyed most, such as calendars and the Pentagram Papers [S2]. Pentagram itself was set up with five partners, each in charge of their own work but sharing resources [S5].

**Ego, and a sense of not being good enough.** In the interview he says what drove him was a feeling of inadequacy, since he always found someone doing it better. He also says a designer needs ego, because the job means showing your work to clients who may not share your enthusiasm [S2].

**Solo violin, not orchestra.** He sees himself as a single author, not a conductor of a team. He admires art directors who coordinate others, but says he could not work that way [S2].

**Push past solved.** Solving the client's problem is not enough for him. He wants the work taken to the edge. Most clients will not notice, but sensitive viewers will, and it gives the work extra energy [S2].

**What he prizes in a portfolio.** He lists sensitivity, thoughtfulness, ideas, craft, economy of thought, oblique reference and charm, and puts wit above all [S2].

**Looking before knowing.** In the excerpts he chose for the book, looking comes before knowing: time and attention are what let you see, and seeing sometimes means forgetting the name of the thing in front of you. These are lines from artists and writers he collected, not his own sentences, but they show the habit he valued [S7].

## Real decisions

### Answering the IBM placard with a poster series
IBM wanted a simple notice that a painting was coming to its new Paris office. He said that if he had answered the brief as given, they would have got a straightforward placard. Instead he made posters on the meaning of the word art, and put the line about the painting in 6 point type along the bottom [S2].

### Giving Polaroid an inkblot
For Polaroid's colour film he made a Rorschach-style poster in colour. He liked that it was open ended, and was happy when a client asked what it meant and he just shrugged and smiled [S2].

### Starting to letter by hand
He began writing headlines by hand because it saved money, not because he planned a style. He then found that every letter is a symbol, so you can play games with it [S2].

### Making the V&A mark from a missing bar
The museum job began as signage. Once the team saw the many logo versions, they spent a week or two testing weights and redrawn ampersands in Bodoni. The answer came the morning of the presentation: let the ampersand's stroke complete the A [S4].

### Cold-calling his way into work (late 1950s)
At Yale on a scholarship, he got freelance IBM work from Paul Rand. He says connections matter but you have to pursue them, and he rang Saul Bass from a phone box, asked to show his portfolio, and got freelance work though he had no money [S2].

### Starting a firm in London (early 1960s)
Back in London he carried a full-colour portfolio when most British design work was black and white. Bob Gill got in touch, and with Colin Forbes they opened a small office. The first month brought no jobs. Then came Penguin jackets, which they sometimes sketched over coffee in a café [S2].

### Asking why before he designed at Phaidon
After leaving Pentagram in 1992, he asked what each book was for and what the point was before he drew anything. His publisher found this slow at first, then valued it [S3][S1].

### Splitting his time, and keeping a rude check on himself
He called himself a split personality. Large, complex identity jobs paid for small projects of his own, such as calendars, Christmas books and the Pentagram Papers, and he thought clients were sometimes drawn to Pentagram by that work. He valued partners who glance at his desk and say what they think, an "irritating self-protection system" that stopped him accepting work that was only 80 percent there [S2].

## How he decides

- Find the idea first. Layout comes after [S2].
- Read between the lines of the brief [S2].
- Look for the twist that adds a little magic [S2].
- Make one simple mark carry two meanings [S4].
- Drop any device you're leaning on out of habit [S2].
- Ask why the job exists and what it's for [S3].
- Let peers critique the work before you call it done [S2].
- Keep a notebook of what catches your eye [S6].

## Where he stood

**Does every design need an idea?** Yes. Without one, it's just layout [S2].

**Should design be funny?** Wit was the quality he prized most, along with economy and charm [S2].

**Who is the work for?** He solved clients' problems, but admitted he treated clients as raw material for his own ideas, though he'd never tell them [S2].

**Is a signature style a good thing?** Only up to a point. A style can harden into a cliché, including his own [S2].

**What drives good designers?** Self-doubt, curiosity and enough ego to stand up to clients. He said his own drive came from feeling not good enough [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Humour can turn into cliché.** A 1993 review of Pentagram's work said many of its visual humour devices had become clichés [S8]. Fletcher himself warned of the same trap, and admitted his handwriting style might be one [S2].
- **Slow to start.** His publisher found him slow at first, because he questioned every book before designing it [S3].
- **Many ideas, few keepers.** The same tribute said most of his ideas were not useful, though a few were brilliant [S3].
- **A hard book to read.** One reviewer found *The Art of Looking Sideways* frustrating to read straight through, with some ideas dropped in without being developed [S6].
- **Clients as material.** His admission that he used clients as raw material for his own ideas could worry a client who wants a plain answer to a plain brief [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- Wit is risky in some settings. A serious message, a crisis or a safety notice may need plainness more than a twist.
- His approach depends on time to play and a partner to critique. A rushed solo job may not allow it.
- He worked by instinct and eye. He offers little on testing designs with users or on large design systems.
- His love of private jokes can leave some viewers out. Check that the main message lands even if the joke doesn't.
