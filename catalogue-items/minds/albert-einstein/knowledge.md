# Albert Einstein: what he knew and believed

The own-words sources here are two of Einstein's public-domain books in English translation: his popular account Relativity (first published 1916; Lawson's translation, revised 1924) [S2], and the 1920 and 1921 lectures collected as Sidelights on Relativity [S3]. Many "Einstein quotes" in circulation are misattributed, and Wikipedia itself flags disputed ones [S1]. This file uses only what its sources show.

## In one paragraph

Einstein was a theoretical physicist, born in Ulm, Germany, on 14 March 1879, who died in Princeton, New Jersey, on 18 April 1955 [S1]. He studied at ETH Zurich, then worked as a patent examiner in Bern, where in 1905 he published four papers on light quanta, Brownian motion, special relativity and mass-energy equivalence, E = mc² [S1][S5]. In 1915 he extended relativity to gravity with the general theory, confirmed by eclipse observations in 1919 [S1]. He won the 1921 Nobel Prize for the photoelectric effect [S1][S5]. He moved to the United States in 1933 and spent his later decades on an unsuccessful search for a unified field theory, while doubting that quantum theory was complete [S1]. His method relied on thought experiments: idealised pictures, such as riding on a beam of light or standing in a falling lift, that expose contradictions and test principles [S4]. He insisted that a physical idea means nothing until there is a way to check it [S2].

## Core ideas

### Think it through as a picture

Einstein's thinking was highly visual. He used imagined scenarios to expose contradictions, test principles and build intuition [S4]. At about 16 he pictured himself chasing a beam of light and asked what he would see; late in life he called this the seed of special relativity, though historians note his memory may have been coloured by hindsight [S4].

How he applied it: in 1907 he pictured a person in free fall, who feels no weight. He later called this the happiest thought of his life, and it led to the principle of equivalence and general relativity [S4]. Around 1912, a spinning disc showed him that gravity bends geometry, and that he needed new mathematics, which he learned with help from his friend Marcel Grossmann [S4].

### A concept must be testable

In Relativity, Einstein argues that the word "simultaneous" has no meaning for a physicist until there is a way to decide, in a real case, whether two events are simultaneous [S2]. He then defines time with synchronised clocks at different places [S2]. In the same spirit, his 1921 lecture says geometry becomes a physical science only when it is tied to real measuring rods, and whether space is Euclidean is a question for experience [S3].

How he applied it: his train and embankment example shows two lightning strikes that are simultaneous for someone on the embankment but not for someone on a moving train. Simultaneity depends on the observer [S2][S4].

### Remove distinctions that experience doesn't support

Einstein disliked theories that treat two cases differently when no experiment can tell them apart. In 1905, a magnet moving past a wire and a wire moving past a magnet produce the same current, yet the theory of the day described them differently. He later wrote that this asymmetry was unbearable to him [S4]. In 1920 he argued that giving the ether a state of rest created an asymmetry that served no physical purpose, so the idea of motion should not be applied to it [S3].

How he applied it: special relativity rests on two principles, that the laws of physics are the same for all observers moving steadily, and that the speed of light is constant, and it needs no ether [S5].

### Mathematics is a free creation; reality decides

In Geometry and Experience (1921), Einstein wrote that as far as the laws of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and as far as they are certain, they don't refer to reality [S3]. Axioms are free creations of the human mind [S3]. Only geometry combined with physics can be tested against experience [S3].

How he applied it: he showed, with pictures of discs on a sphere and shadows on a plane, that a finite but unbounded space can be imagined, so our intuition is not trapped in Euclid's geometry [S3]. He proposed checking the question with observations of star motions [S3].

### Explain it so anyone with patience can follow

Einstein wrote Relativity for readers with no training in theoretical physics, asking only for a school-leaving education and a fair amount of patience and force of will [S2]. He repeated himself on purpose for clarity, and set elegance aside, quoting the physicist Boltzmann that elegance should be left to tailors and cobblers [S2].

How he applied it: the book is full of everyday images: a railway carriage and an embankment, a raven flying past a train, a man walking along a moving carriage, clocks and measuring rods [S2]. He used the train and lightning example as his favourite way to explain simultaneity to the public [S4].

### Same effect, same cause: the man in the chest

In Relativity, Einstein describes a man in a closed chest far out in space, pulled steadily upward by a rope. He feels pressed to the floor and sees dropped objects fall at the same rate, so he may fairly conclude he is at rest in a gravity field [S2]. Since no experiment inside can tell the difference, Einstein treated acceleration and gravity as equivalent, which also explains why inertial and gravitational mass are equal [S2].

### Doubt the theory even when it works

Einstein accepted that quantum mechanics made correct predictions, but believed a deeper description was possible [S1]. He doubted that its randomness was fundamental, saying God is not playing dice [S1]. In 1930 he challenged the uncertainty principle with a "photon box"; Bohr answered using Einstein's own general relativity [S4]. In 1935, with Podolsky and Rosen, he argued quantum mechanics was incomplete, in a paper that anticipated entanglement and became one of the most cited in physics [S4].

### Revise when the evidence changes

In 1917 he added a cosmological constant to his equations so the universe could stay static [S1]. After Hubble's 1929 findings that galaxies are moving apart, he dropped the static model, calling the constant theoretically unsatisfactory [S1]. The story that he called it his "biggest blunder" rests on George Gamow's account, which the astrophysicist Mario Livio has doubted [S1].

## How he decides

- Picture it. Build an idealised thought experiment [S4].
- Ask how you would check the idea in a real case [S2].
- Drop any distinction no experiment can detect [S3][S4].
- Keep the principles few and general [S5].
- Separate the certainty of logic from the uncertainty of the world [S3].
- Explain it with ordinary things: trains, clocks, rulers [S2].
- Talk it over with a trusted friend; Michele Besso was his sounding board [S5].
- Change the model when observation changes [S1].

## Where he stood

**Is mathematics reality? (1921)** No. Where it is certain, it is not about reality [S3].

**Is space Euclidean? (1921)** A question for experience, not logic [S3].

**Is quantum mechanics complete?** No, he believed, to the end of his life [S1].

**God? (1954)** He felt sympathy for Spinoza's impersonal God, did not believe in a personal god, and called himself agnostic, not an atheist [S1].

**The atom bomb letter? (1954)** He told Linus Pauling that signing the 1939 letter to Roosevelt was the one great mistake of his life, with some justification in the fear of a German bomb [S1].

**How do you learn?** He said, of teaching himself the violin, that love is a better teacher than a sense of duty [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Quantum mechanics.** Most physicists sided with Bohr in their debates, and Einstein grew isolated from mainstream physics in later years [S1][S4].
- **Unified field theory.** His long search for it failed [S1].
- **Static universe.** His cosmological constant was added to keep the universe static, which observation later contradicted [S1].
- **Memory and myth.** His late account of the light-beam thought experiment may be coloured by hindsight [S4]. The "biggest blunder" line is disputed [S1].
- **The bomb.** Some argue that signing the 1939 letter went against his pacifism; he later called it a mistake [S1].
- **Credit.** Historians debate how much his first wife, Mileva Marić, contributed to the 1905 work [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He trusts beautiful principles. Check them against messy data before acting.
- He resisted a theory that worked because it felt wrong. Don't let taste block good evidence.
- Thought experiments can hide wrong assumptions. Test the picture against reality.
- He worked alone or with one friend for long stretches. Teams may need more structure.
- His physics is a century old. Check modern science before using his views as current fact.
