# Hire Alberto Cairo: how he'd work for you

You bring a chart, a dataset or a claim you are tempted to repeat. He helps you read it literally, inspect the missing context and build a visual explanation that earns its conclusion. Expect both a careful critique and a usable repair. These are original applications of his public work, not promises of his participation.

## What to hire him for

- **Check a chart before you share it:** Get a plain account of what it shows, what its source supports and which attractive conclusions remain unproved.
- **Redesign a presentation chart:** Get an annotated draft whose display, headline and comparison answer the same audience question.
- **Write an uncertainty caption:** Get a short explanation of what a range means, where it came from and which conclusions it cannot support.
- **Explain a household bill change:** Get separate comparisons of money, usage and time so a higher payment does not automatically become a claim about waste.
- **Read a career salary comparison:** Get a sheet of comparable variables and questions to investigate before treating a group average as a personal prospect.
- **Review a survey graphic:** Get accurate respondent language, visible sample information and a clear boundary around claims about a wider population.
- **Test dashboard interpretation:** Get a reader task, observations of misunderstanding and a short list of changes ranked by their effect on comprehension.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Name the conversation the graphic must support:** Identify the decision or question and who needs to understand it. A presentation that compares options needs a different emphasis from an exploratory display. Write one sentence describing the intended comparison before choosing a chart type.
2. **Trace each variable back to its definition:** Find the source, dates, population, unit and calculation. Distinguish a count, a rate, a percentage and an estimate. Mark anything you cannot verify. A confident headline cannot repair a variable whose meaning nobody has checked.
3. **Describe the marks without the story:** Translate axes, positions, lengths, colors and sizes into a literal account. Separate that account from causal explanations, predictions and judgments. Inspect whether the unit of the display matches the unit of the claim.
4. **Look for the comparison that is missing:** Examine the time window, denominator, subgroup, distribution or baseline that could alter the apparent message. Choose the addition that matters to the reader's question. Do not bury a straightforward comparison in unrelated detail.
5. **Redraw and explain the uncertainty:** Sketch an alternative and write its caption at the same time. Make units, scope and relevant limitations visible. Explain what a band or range describes; do not invent precision or assume a familiar-looking symbol explains itself.
6. **Listen to a reader and revise the claim:** Ask someone from the audience to explain the graphic and answer its intended question. Record their actual interpretation. Change the headline, display or accompanying explanation where it encourages an unsupported conclusion. Deliver the narrow conclusion the evidence can carry.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What do you need to decide after reading this?
- Who is the reader, and what will they already know?
- Where did the numbers come from?
- What does one observation stand for?
- Are the dates and units comparable?
- Which conclusion made you want to share it?
- What does the shaded area or error bar mean here?
- Have you heard someone explain this chart without help?

## Jobs, one by one

### Check a chart before you share it

You bring a screenshot claiming that a new service doubled satisfaction. He first looks for the source and asks what the vertical scale actually records. Suppose it measures positive reviews rather than the share of customers who are satisfied. More reviews could come from more customers, a different invitation process or changed participation.

The output is a short sharing note: the literal observation, the strongest supported interpretation and the missing denominator. If the original source is unavailable, the verdict stays limited. He would push for withholding the sweeping claim until the measure is understood. He would cut an accusation of deliberate dishonesty unless there is evidence of intent. You may still share the chart with a corrected description and a clear question.

### Redesign a presentation chart

You bring twelve regional averages under a headline saying the business improved everywhere. He asks for comparable periods, the size of each region and whether the average is weighted. If some regions declined, the headline changes even if the overall total rose.

The output is a comparison sketch with regions in a useful order, clear units and a title that distinguishes total improvement from uneven local results. A second view may be needed if size changes explain the aggregate. He would push you to retain information that makes the story less triumphant but more useful. He would cut decoration that hides the comparison and any demand to choose colors before the data question is settled.

### Write an uncertainty caption

You bring a project forecast with a band around the central line. He asks how the endpoints were produced. Suppose they are three planning scenarios selected by the team, not a statistical interval. Calling the band “95% confidence” would misrepresent the work.

The output names the scenarios, the assumptions that separate them and the consequence for the decision. For example, a team might reserve capacity for a slower delivery scenario while keeping its central plan visible. He would push for a caption beside the display so the explanation survives a screenshot. He would cut a precise-looking percentage that has no calculation behind it. If a qualified analyst supplied a model interval, retain their definition and check that the wording describes that quantity correctly.

### Explain a household bill change

You bring two electricity bills and want to prove somebody is using too much. He separates cost from consumption and checks the number of billed days. A longer billing period or a higher tariff can change the payment without the same change in daily use.

The output is a small table: billing period, days, recorded usage, usage per day where calculable, and total cost. The explanation marks estimated readings and missing information. He would push for the comparison that can guide a household conversation, rather than a blaming headline. He would cut a chart of payments labeled as physical energy. If usage is missing, the immediate result is a cost comparison and a request for the meter data, not a guessed consumption trend.

### Read a career salary comparison

You bring a chart that appears to make another occupation the obvious next move. He checks whether it mixes locations, experience levels, employment types or years. A national occupation average may say little about an entry role in your city.

The output is a comparison sheet with the published figures preserved, their definitions and a column for unresolved differences. Add questions for a recruiter or someone working in the role: typical entry level, hours, progression and local opportunities. He would push for a range or distribution when available. He would cut a promised personal salary increase or a return calculation that ignores training costs and the chance of finding work. The method supports your inquiry; it does not choose your career from one average.

### Review a survey graphic and test its reading

You bring a neighborhood survey with 40 voluntary responses. Its title says that the neighborhood favors a proposal. He changes the unit of the statement to respondents and asks how they were invited. If 30 supported it, report 30 of 40 respondents without silently extending the result to everybody nearby.

The output includes the response count, question wording, dates and an appropriate description of participation. Then ask a reader what population they think the percentage describes. If they still say “all residents,” revise the title and annotations. He would push for a visible limitation because it changes the claim. He would cut a decorative confidence interval that treats a voluntary convenience sample as a representative random sample.

## What he'd push back on

He would challenge a predetermined headline in search of a chart, a claim about individual people based only on group averages, or a forecast presented without its assumptions. He would also challenge the idea that every complex display must be stripped down until it loses the comparison people need. The task determines the useful level of detail.

He would ask you to distinguish a design correction from a substantive finding. A cleaner chart may reveal a question; it does not resolve a causal dispute or establish a study's quality. Use a domain specialist when the decision requires expertise beyond reading and communicating the data.

## What you'll get back

Expect a brief interpretation memo and an annotated visual draft or table. It identifies the audience question, source and definitions, literal observation, unsupported inference, missing comparison and proposed repair. The final caption should travel with the graphic. Where reader testing is possible, include one observed misunderstanding and the change made to address it. Where verification is incomplete, state exactly which conclusion remains open.
