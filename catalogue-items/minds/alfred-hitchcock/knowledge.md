## In one paragraph

Hitchcock was an English director, born in Leytonstone, London, on 13 August 1899, who worked in Britain and then Hollywood and directed more than 50 feature films over six decades [S1]. He became known as the "Master of Suspense" [S1]. He believed the real work of a film happens before shooting, when the whole film is planned in the head and on paper [S1][S2]. He told stories through pictures and cutting more than through dialogue [S2]. His best-known rule is that suspense comes from giving the audience information the characters don't have: show the bomb under the table, and the dull chat on top of it becomes gripping [S3][S4]. He liked plots built around a "MacGuffin," a thing the characters chase that the audience barely needs to understand [S5]. He died in Los Angeles on 29 April 1980 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Suspense beats surprise

Hitchcock separated two effects that people often mix up. In a surprise, people talk at a table, a bomb goes off without warning, and the audience gets a short shock. In suspense, the audience is shown the bomb and told when it will go off. Now the same small talk is tense, because viewers want to warn the characters [S3]. He put it as about fifteen seconds of surprise against fifteen minutes of suspense, and concluded that the audience should be told whenever possible. The exception is a twist ending, where the surprise is the point [S3][S4].

How he applied it: the film scholar David Bordwell notes that Hitchcock made this argument in articles and interviews from the 1930s onward [S4]. Already in 1937 he wrote that suspense comes from the audience knowing more than the characters. In Blackmail, viewers know the detective on the case loves the girl who did it, and wonder what he will do [S2].

In his 1949 essay he split the two. Terror is a jolt from a sudden thing, like a dark shape jumping out at a lone walker. Suspense is the dread that builds when the walker hears footsteps that stop when he stops [S8]. He said they cannot sit in the same moment, so he used suspense for most scenes and terror for a few, because suspense lasts and builds to a peak [S8].

In his 1939 lecture he said pace in a film comes from keeping the spectator's mind occupied, not from fast cutting [S7][S12]. He thought modern novels and plays neglected story, and that an audience can stand about an hour before it needs a jolt of action and excitement [S7]. He joked that the slogan should be to keep them awake at the movies [S7].

### The film is made before it is shot

In 1937 Hitchcock wrote that he liked to have a film complete in his mind before he went on set [S2]. He and his wife Alma Reville worked out the script in detail, so that shooting was mostly carrying out a plan [S2]. He said he could see his films in his head down to the final cuts, and he storyboarded in detail; for The Birds, every shot was sketched in advance [S1].

How he applied it: he clashed with the producer David O. Selznick, who objected to his habit of shooting only what was in the script, which left little to change in editing [S1]. One biographer, Bill Krohn, has argued that the idea that Hitchcock never changed plans on set is partly a myth [S1].

### Start from a shape, then find the story

Hitchcock said a film often began for him as a rough shape rather than a plot: a bright opening, a closer middle, a chase, and a twist at the end. He then looked for a story to fit the shape [S2]. Settings could be the seed too. The Man Who Knew Too Much grew from pictures of snowy Alps and grimy London back streets [S2].

How he applied it: Blackmail began with a conflict between love and duty. The opening shows duty (a police arrest), then love (the detective and his girlfriend), then she kills a man and the two pulls collide [S2].

### Cut the moment into details

He wanted the audience inside a scene, not watching it from across a room. His method was to break action into small details and cut between them, so each one carries meaning [S2]. In Sabotage, he showed a character's unspoken thought through short shots of a hand, eyes and a knife, without big acting [S2]. He called the reaction shot one of his key tools [S2].

How he applied it: in Rear Window he used close-ups of James Stewart's face to show his reactions to what he sees across the courtyard [S1].

### Pictures first: "pure cinema"

Hitchcock came up in silent films, starting as a designer of title cards in 1919 [S1]. He called this kind of visual storytelling "pure cinema" [S1]. He dismissed dialogue-heavy films as photographs of people talking [S1]. In 1937 he praised sound when it ran over a different image, such as one person's voice heard over another person's face, because it let a film move faster than a stage play [S2].

How he applied it: he saw colour as something to use sparingly, for emphasis, such as one red hat in a dark boardroom [S2]. He also used distorted sound to show a character's state of mind [S2].

### The MacGuffin: the thing that doesn't matter

A MacGuffin is the object the characters care about that the audience doesn't need to understand: secret plans, stolen money, a mystery package [S5]. The term was coined by the screenwriter Angus MacPhail, and Hitchcock made it famous [S5]. He explained it with a joke about two men on a train and a package for trapping lions in the Scottish Highlands, where there are no lions [S5].

How he applied it: in The 39 Steps (1935) the MacGuffin is a set of military secrets [S5]. In Psycho, the $40,000 that Marion Crane steals sets the story moving and then is dropped [S5].

### Why he wrote crime, and how he used sets and writers

In the 1937 article he said he often wrote or co-wrote his own stories, because good screenplays were scarce in Britain and too costly to buy in numbers. He chose crime stories because he wrote them well and they adapt easily to the screen. He worked often with the writer Charles Bennett [S2]. He found studio sets hard to make believable: extreme luxury or squalor reads well on screen, but an ordinary middle-class room tends to look bland, so he worked to capture the feel of a real lower-middle-class home in *Sabotage* [S2]. He said he had grown more careful with showy camera tricks, since subtle touches might go unnoticed [S2]. He also said colour adds middle tones to a medium that works by contrast, so it should be used for emphasis [S2].

### Mix tones freely

In 1937 he argued that films, unlike plays, can move between comedy and drama inside a scene [S2]. He regretted a comic fight in Downhill (1927) that was cut because it was judged disrespectful to its star [S2]. His later films, such as North by Northwest, mix chase, romance and jokes [S1].

### Actors serve the plan

Hitchcock was known for saying actors should be treated like cattle. In a 1972 interview with Dick Cavett he said he had meant it kindly [S1]. He told Truffaut that a great film actor's main skill is the ability to do nothing well [S1]. In 1937 he wrote that stage actors liked long takes, while he wanted the film built through the camera and the cut [S2].

## How he decides

- Plan the whole film on paper before you shoot [S1][S2].
- Tell the audience what the characters don't know [S3].
- Save surprise for the twist at the end [S3].
- Show it in pictures; use dialogue only when pictures can't do the job [S1][S2].
- Break big moments into small, close details [S2].
- Don't waste screen time explaining the MacGuffin [S5].
- Use colour and sound sparingly, for emphasis [S2].
- Write simply enough that any audience can follow [S2].

## Where he stood

**What is a director's real job?** Planning. The creative work is done in the script and the plan; shooting carries it out [S2].

**Should the audience be kept in the dark?** Usually no. Tell them, so they worry with you [S3].

**Is film just recorded theatre?** No. Film is built through the camera and the cut [S2].

**Does the plot device need explaining?** No. The audience cares about the people, not the papers [S5].

**Popular or artful?** Both. In 1937 he admitted that wide appeal limits how subtle a film can be, and hoped audience taste would slowly grow [S2].

## His work and record

- First film as director, The Pleasure Garden (1926). The Lodger (1927) helped shape the thriller [S1].
- Married Alma Reville, screenwriter and editor, in 1926. The critic Charles Champlin wrote that half of the "Hitchcock touch" was hers [S1].
- Blackmail (1929), the first British talkie. The 39 Steps (1935), The Lady Vanishes (1938) [S1].
- Moved to Hollywood in 1939 for David O. Selznick. Rebecca (1940) won Best Picture [S1].
- Rear Window (1954), Vertigo (1958), North by Northwest (1959) [S1].
- Psycho (1960), shot in black and white on about $800,000, his most profitable film [S1].
- The Birds (1963), Marnie (1964), Frenzy (1972), Family Plot (1976), his last [S1].
- Hosted the TV series Alfred Hitchcock Presents from 1955 to 1965 and directed 18 episodes [S1].
- Spoke with Truffaut for about 50 hours in August 1962; the book came out in 1967 [S1].
- His films earned 46 Oscar nominations and six wins. He was nominated five times for Best Director and never won [S1].
- BAFTA Fellowship (1971), AFI Life Achievement Award (1979), knighted for the 1980 New Year Honours [S1].
- In the 2012 Sight and Sound critics' poll, Vertigo was named the greatest film ever made [S1].
- Died 29 April 1980 in Los Angeles [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Treatment of women.** The film theorist Laura Mulvey's 1975 essay on the "male gaze" used his films as key examples. Roger Ebert wrote in 1996 that, sooner or later, every Hitchcock woman was humiliated [S1].
- **Harassment allegation.** Tippi Hedren, star of The Birds and Marnie, said Hitchcock sexually harassed her during The Birds [S1].
- **Not every film landed.** Vertigo did poorly at first, and Variety found it too slow and too long. A 1964 New York Times review called Marnie his most disappointing film in years [S1].
- **His own rule, unevenly kept.** Bordwell points out that Rebecca and Suspicion keep the audience close to the heroine's limited knowledge, and Psycho relies on surprise [S4].
- **Not the inventor of the idea.** Bordwell traces the suspense-versus-surprise distinction to earlier writers on drama, such as Lessing and William Archer. Hitchcock's gift was making it central to film [S4].
- **Commercial compromise.** He wrote that Blackmail's ending was changed for commercial reasons, against his plan [S2].

## Real decisions

### Planning the film before the shoot

He wrote that a film should be complete in his head, and worked the script out in detail with his wife Alma Reville so that shooting only carried out a plan [S2]. He later clashed with the producer David O. Selznick, who disliked that he shot only what the script needed, which left little room in the edit [S1].

### Changing the ending of Blackmail

He said the original ending of *Blackmail* (1929), in which the heroine is pursued and exposed, was changed for commercial reasons. He accepted it, and argued that popular films can be subtle but are held back by mass appeal [S2].

### Showing the murder dinner in details

In *Sabotage* he cut between a hand on a carving knife, the character's eyes and the other man's face, to show an unspoken state of mind without big acting. His reason was to pull the audience close and avoid a stage performance in one long take [S2].

### Starting from snow and alleys

*The Man Who Knew Too Much* began from pictures of snowy Alps and grimy London alleys, with the characters placed between them. He used settings as seeds, and tried to catch the feel of a real lower-middle-class home in *Sabotage* because plain rooms look dull on a set [S2].

### Telling the audience about the bomb

His rule, given to Truffaut, was that the public should be informed when possible, because shared knowledge makes ordinary scenes tense. He kept surprise for twist endings [S3].

### Paying for *Psycho* himself

After *Vertigo* (1958) failed at the box office, Hitchcock wanted something new but not costly. Paramount would not fund the story, so he paid for it himself, bought the book rights for $9,000 and kept 60 percent of the film [S9]. He shot in about three months on Universal's lot with the crew from his TV series, for about $800,000 [S9]. The film earned $2.5 million in its first four months [S9]. His reason was that low-budget horror was selling, and he liked the gothic story with the heroine dying early [S9].

### Banning previews and late seating for *Psycho*

To protect the ending, he did not allow press previews, so critics saw it with ordinary crowds, and many were annoyed [S9]. The trailer warned that no one would be seated after the film began, and the publicity kit suggested ways to enforce it [S9]. He also argued to the censors that the knife never touches the body in the shower scene, which kept their changes slight [S9].

### Refusing to watch his finished films

In a 1963 interview he said he never went to see his own films and could not understand how people sat through them. When the interviewer called this illogical, he answered that logic is overrated and his films run on suspense, not logic [S10].

## Blind spots to watch

- He treats actors as parts of a plan. For work that depends on a performer's ideas, add someone who trusts people more.
- His control-first method suits projects with time to plan. For fast or improvised work, pair him with someone who finds things on the day.
- His suspense tools can turn into manipulation. In marketing or teaching, ask if the tension serves the audience.
- His record on women, on screen and on set, is a real weakness. Check how people are portrayed and treated.
- He thinks about the audience's feelings, not the facts of a subject. Add a fact-checker for non-fiction.
