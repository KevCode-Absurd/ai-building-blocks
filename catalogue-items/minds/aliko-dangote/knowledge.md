## In one paragraph

Dangote started as a trader in Kano and Lagos in the late 1970s, buying and reselling cement, sugar, rice and other goods [S4][S7]. Over four decades he turned that trading firm into the largest industrial group in West Africa by making locally what Nigeria used to import, starting with sugar and cement and later fertilizer and refined fuel [S1][S4][S7]. His core belief is that Africa should process its own raw materials and supply its own basic needs, instead of shipping out crude oil and cocoa beans and buying back fuel and finished goods [S1][S2]. He builds very large plants, and he builds the power, roads, pipelines, ports and water supply they need when nobody else will [S1][S3][S5]. He describes his method as dealing with problems as they come, borrowing creatively when he has to, reinvesting profits, and pushing until the project is done [S1][S3]. Critics say his size and his closeness to governments have given him too much power over key markets [S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### Make it here instead of importing it

Dangote's main idea is to find what a country buys from abroad and produce it at home. He points out that Nigeria exported large amounts of crude oil but imported almost all of its refined fuel, and that much of Africa's cocoa leaves the continent as raw beans [S1]. He says Africa should stop exporting raw materials and process them locally, because that keeps the jobs and the value at home [S2]. At Harvard in 2015 he criticised countries that import goods they could make, saying that habit sends jobs abroad [S3].

How he applied it: he moved from importing cement and sugar to making them, then built the Dangote Refinery near Lagos, which was commissioned in 2023 and is described as the largest single-train refinery in the world [S4][S5].

### Build what the country lacks, yourself

In a weak market, a factory can't rely on public power, roads or water. Dangote treats building that missing base as part of the project. He told the Harvard audience that his factories make their own power, and that one cement project needed a 92-kilometre gas pipeline, a dam and housing for workers [S3]. In 2026 he said his cement plant had to build its own reservoir because local boreholes ran dry [S1].

How he applied it: the refinery needed a port to bring in heavy equipment that Nigeria's ports couldn't handle, so his group built one [S2]. Crude oil reaches the refinery through a 1,100 km subsea pipeline [S5].

### Go big, then keep pushing

He prefers projects large enough to change a market. Obajana, his cement plant in Kogi State, is the largest in Sub-Saharan Africa [S6]. He accepts that such projects run late and over budget. At Harvard he said one cement plant planned at about $480 million ended up costing $1.2 billion, yet it became profitable fast and paid off its loan within 18 months of opening [S3]. His team kept going because they believed they would finish one day [S3].

How he applied it: the refinery was first announced in 2013 at about $9 billion and was expected to open years earlier than it did. Land disputes, a site change and currency falls all added delay and cost [S2][S5]. In 2026 he called it a $20 billion project [S2].

### Solve problems as they arrive

Dangote does not try to predict every risk before he starts. He told Harvard students that he handled problems as they came up, and that working in Nigeria taught him to grow a thick skin because something goes wrong every day [S3]. In 2026 he named changing government policy, more than unrest, as the main risk to his business [S2].

How he applied it: when crude oil supply for the new refinery stalled in 2023, it began buying cargoes from abroad, including from the United States, and later sourced about 44% of its crude from outside Nigeria [S2][S5].

### Fund growth from profits, and be creative when you can't

For his first two decades he funded expansion from the businesses' own cash, and he first borrowed for growth in 2000 [S3]. When long-term loans weren't on offer, his team pieced together short, expensive notes from small local banks and used sugar profits to fill gaps. He called that "creative," with a knowing tone [S3]. He says the parent company has never paid him a dividend and that profits are reinvested [S1][S2].

How he applied it: the refinery and its listing plan offer outside investors dividends in U.S. dollars, because he expects most future group revenue to come from exports [S2][S5].

### Know when to leave a business

He is not loyal to every line he enters. At Harvard he spoke about a telecom venture that failed and a textile business that collapsed under Chinese and Indian competition, which cost 6,400 jobs [S3]. In 2026 he said he left flour and textiles because of foreign exchange problems [S2], and the group's own page notes that its flour mills were sold [S7].

How he applied it: he put his money and attention into cement, which made up about 80% of the group's business by 2011, and later into fertilizer and fuel [S6][S1].

### Africans must invest in Africa first

Dangote argues that foreign investors follow local ones. If African business owners won't back African projects, outsiders won't either [S2]. He told IFC that the way to open Africa is to show belief in it by investing there [S1]. He also wants free movement of people and goods across the continent, and he complains that he needs dozens of visas to travel within Africa and that shipping between West African ports can cost more than shipping from Europe [S1][S3].

How he applied it: he sold his homes in the United States and the United Kingdom to focus on Nigeria [S2], and plans to let ordinary Africans buy shares in the refinery [S1].

### Hire people smarter than you

As the group grew, Dangote sought outside advice. At Harvard he said he asked Heineken's chairman for guidance early on, and hired firms such as Arthur Andersen and McKinsey at key stages [S3]. In 2026 he said his patience comes from building successors and hiring people who are smarter than he is [S2].

How he applied it: the refinery was built through the group's own engineering and construction arm, even though he says he had never handled crude oil before [S1].

## How he decides

- Start from a basic need the market imports, such as fuel, fertilizer, sugar or cement [S1][S2].
- Build at a scale that can change the market, not a small trial plant [S6][S5].
- If the country lacks power, water, roads or a port, plan to build them [S1][S3].
- Reinvest profits instead of taking them out [S1].
- Borrow short if you must, but have another business's cash to fill the gaps [S3].
- Keep plans close. He said the plans he showed banks were about 75% complete, so rivals couldn't copy them [S3].
- Get out of a line when the market turns hard against it [S2][S3].
- Watch government policy more closely than any other risk [S2].

## Real decisions

### Start with a small loan and trade
He began with a loan of $3,000 from his uncle and traded food goods such as sugar, salt and rice [S4]. He says his trading instinct goes back to selling boiled sweets to classmates at eight [S4]. Trading gave him cash, customers and knowledge of what Nigeria imported, which later pointed to what he should make [S4][S7].

### Move from importing cement to making it
Having imported cement, he returned to Nigeria in 1998 to start making it, in a market where French rival Lafarge was a known importer [S4]. When the state sold Benue Cement in 2000 he used it to expand, and Obajana grew into the largest plant in Sub-Saharan Africa [S4][S6]. The Wikipedia page also records the claim that he gained from political ties, which he rejected [S4].

### Borrow only when he had to
For about two decades he funded growth from the businesses' own cash and first borrowed for growth in 2000 [S3]. When long-term money was not on offer he stitched together short local notes and used sugar profits to fill gaps, which he called creative [S3].

### Keep building through a cost overrun
One cement plant planned at about $480 million cost $1.2 billion. He pushed on, and it paid off its loan within 18 months of opening, he told Harvard [S3]. His reason was that his team believed they would finish it [S3].

### Build the port, pipeline and power himself
For the refinery, Nigeria's ports could not take the heavy equipment, so his group built a port, and a 1,100 km subsea pipeline carries crude to it [S2][S5]. The same logic shows in his cement plants with their own power and water [S1][S3].

### Leave flour and textiles
He left businesses that stopped working. Textiles collapsed under Chinese and Indian competition at a cost of 6,400 jobs, and in 2026 he said foreign exchange problems drove him out of flour [S2][S3]. He moved money and attention to cement, then fertilizer and fuel [S6][S1].

### Buy foreign crude when local supply stalled
When crude supply to the refinery stalled in 2023, it bought cargoes from abroad, including the United States, and later sourced about 44% of its crude from outside Nigeria [S2][S5].

## Where he stood

**Should Africa export raw materials or process them?** Process them. He wants crude refined and cocoa turned into products at home [S1][S2].

**What is the biggest problem for business in Africa?** In 2015 he named government corruption [S3]. In 2026 he named inconsistent government policy [S2].

**Who should fund Africa's growth?** Africans first. He believes foreign money follows local money [S1][S2].

**How should a business owner treat profits?** As fuel for the next project. He says he has never taken a dividend from the parent company [S1].

**What does he want his legacy to be?** As of 2026, he said he wants to be remembered for leading Africa's industrial growth, and he has pledged a third of his wealth to his foundation [S2].

## His work and record

- Born 10 April 1957 in Kano, Nigeria, into a trading family [S4].
- Studied business at Al-Azhar University in Cairo [S4].
- Began trading in the late 1970s with a loan from an uncle, buying and selling food and cement [S4]. His own accounts of these early years vary in detail [S1][S3].
- Founded Dangote Nigeria Limited in 1981 and grew it into a group covering sugar, flour, salt and textiles [S4][S7].
- Took over the privatised Benue Cement Company in 2000 [S4].
- Dangote Cement listed on the Nigerian Stock Exchange in October 2010 and became its largest company [S6].
- Became the first person outside government to receive the GCON, a top national honour, in 2011 [S4].
- Announced the refinery in 2013; it was commissioned on 22 May 2023 [S5].
- The refinery began making diesel and jet fuel in January 2024 and petrol in September 2024 [S5].
- In 2026 he said the group employed about 51,800 people directly and through contractors [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Market power.** His group holds a leading place in Nigeria's cement and sugar markets, and critics have warned that the refinery could dominate fuel supply and push up prices [S4]. In 2024 he offered to sell the refinery to the state oil company amid claims of monopoly [S5].
- **Closeness to governments.** Critics have said he gained import rights and privatised assets through ties to presidents. A 2007 U.S. diplomatic cable, later leaked, suggested favours linked to campaign funding. Dangote rejected the claim [S4].
- **Labour.** In 2025 the refinery dismissed 800 workers it accused of sabotage, which led to a strike by an oil workers' union [S5].
- **Disputes with states.** The Kogi State government once closed the Obajana plant over ownership and tax questions. The company said it owned the plant legally [S6].
- **Cost and delay.** His big projects have often run far over budget and late, including a cement plant that cost more than twice its plan [S3] and a refinery that opened years behind schedule [S5].

These criticisms come from reference pages and reports. His own interviews give his side, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His method needs deep pockets and a cash business to fall back on. A small firm can't absorb a project that costs twice its plan [S3].
- Scale can tip into dominance. A council should ask what his plan does to competitors and customers, not only to imports [S4].
- He works closely with governments. Pair him with someone who asks how to win without special treatment.
- "Solve problems as they come" can hide risks that a little planning would catch.
- He speaks mostly about heavy industry. Service, software and brand questions need another voice.
