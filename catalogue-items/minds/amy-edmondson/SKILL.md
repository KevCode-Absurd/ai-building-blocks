### How she'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Identify the interpersonal risk:** Ask each team member to write down, before the next meeting, one thing they hesitate to say here and why. Then name the learning behavior the work needs and what could feel risky about doing it here. Look at actual reactions to questions and errors. Ask where silence may be hiding information required for a reliable decision. A leader may experience the room as safe while others experience a different cost. Gather perspectives without demanding public vulnerability as proof of openness.

2. **Frame uncertain work as a learning task:** Write one line saying what is uncertain and what is already established practice. Then state the work’s purpose and where uncertainty or interdependence makes input necessary. Identify which parts are established practice and which need learning. Explain the specific observations people should bring forward. Calling everything a learning experience can obscure preventable negligence. Be precise about uncertainty and the standards that already apply.

3. **Invite specific contributions:** Pick one decision that is still open and name the two people who see a part of it nobody else sees. Ask each a focused question tied to the decision and the person’s relevant knowledge. Give enough time and a suitable format to respond. Include people whose work sees different parts of the problem, rather than hearing only the usual speakers. Inviting input after the decision is irreversible can be token participation. Ask while the information can still change the plan or its safeguards.

4. **Respond so information stays available:** The next time someone raises a concern, thank them in the room and give them the next action within a day. Acknowledge the useful information and clarify what happened. Contain any immediate problem, decide the next action and report back. Evaluate conduct fairly without punishing the act of making the issue visible. A warm acknowledgment without follow-through teaches people that speaking changes nothing. Close the loop on the concern and explain the decision.

5. **Study learning behavior alongside structure:** List one thing the team is trying to learn and one tool, piece of information or block of time it lacks. Examine what the team is trying to learn and whether it has the tools, information and time to act on that learning. Pair an invitation to speak with the operational change needed to use the resulting information. A low safety score can become a vague explanation for every failure. Trace the actual learning behavior and the structural conditions around the work.

6. **Team across boundaries with a shared task:** Write the shared task in one sentence, with who holds which knowledge. Name the shared task and the expertise each participant brings. Make the dependencies explicit and agree how people will raise uncertainty. Use brief coordination points to update the picture as conditions change. A stirring shared mission cannot replace role clarity. People need to know who holds which knowledge and how a new observation reaches the right person.

## What Amy Edmondson knew and believed

### In one paragraph
Amy Edmondson studies how people learn together when work is uncertain and interdependent. Psychological safety concerns a shared belief that a team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, such as asking for help or discussing an error. Her work connects this climate with observable learning behavior, rather than treating safety as a general feeling of comfort. The everyday use is to make important information easier to raise and act on. A leader must consider how the task is framed, how contributions are invited and what happens after someone speaks. [S1][S2]

### Core ideas
#### Identify the interpersonal risk
Learning activities such as asking questions, seeking help and admitting mistakes can expose a person to an unfavorable impression. Edmondson treats this as an interpersonal risk, distinct from the technical risk of the work itself. A capable employee may withhold a useful question because of how it could make them look. Diagnosing the risk helps the team understand silence without assuming it means agreement or lack of interest. [S2]

**An original application:** A new colleague notices that two instructions conflict but says nothing because everyone else appears to understand. Ask what the team’s previous reactions to questions have taught people. Create a point in the handoff where each person names uncertainty and the experienced colleague models one. The useful question should not require a newcomer to make a public claim of incompetence.

**Where this goes wrong:** A leader may experience the room as safe while others experience a different cost. Gather perspectives without demanding public vulnerability as proof of openness.

#### Frame uncertain work as a learning task
Edmondson’s research describes learning behavior as necessary when teams face change, uncertainty or unfamiliar tasks. Leaders can make the need for questions and help explicit rather than present the plan as complete knowledge. This changes the meaning of speaking up: a question becomes a contribution to the task. The framing should be accurate, including what is known and where established procedures remain necessary. [S2]

**An original application:** Before introducing a new intake process, explain that the team knows the required outcome but is testing how the handoff works. Ask people to flag missing information and confusing steps during the first week. Keep the established privacy rule clear. The message creates room to improve the uncertain process while preserving the known safeguard. It does not describe every part of the work as an experiment.

**Where this goes wrong:** Calling everything a learning experience can obscure preventable negligence. Be precise about uncertainty and the standards that already apply.

#### Invite specific contributions
The research connects team learning with behaviors such as seeking feedback, sharing information and discussing errors. A general invitation to speak may be too vague or socially difficult. A practical adaptation is to ask for specific information from relevant people at a useful point in the work. The aim is to make participation part of the process, rather than reserve it for whoever is most willing to interrupt. [S1]

**An original application:** Before a project launch, ask the person handling support what confusion customers are likely to report, and ask the delivery owner which dependency is least certain. **Where this goes wrong:** Inviting input after the decision is irreversible can be token participation. Ask while the information can still change the plan or its safeguards.

#### Respond so information stays available
The consequences of speaking help shape the shared belief about interpersonal safety. If a leader invites bad news but responds with contempt, people learn to conceal it. A useful response acknowledges the contribution, examines the problem and takes an appropriate next action. This does not require praising every decision. It requires separating the value of reporting from the evaluation of the underlying behavior. [S2]

**An original application:** A colleague reports that a file was sent to the wrong internal group. Thank them for raising it promptly, contain the access problem and reconstruct the sequence. The 1999 paper integrates shared beliefs with structural conditions such as context support and team leader coaching. Psychological safety is therefore part of a larger account of team outcomes. A team still needs information, tools and workable task design. The practical implication is to inspect both the interpersonal climate and the process that the team is trying to improve, rather than use a culture conversation to substitute for operational repair. Her TED account discusses the Chilean miners’ rescue as a dramatic example of people combining expertise under uncertainty. The transfer to everyday work is that a stable team identity cannot be assumed. People need a shared purpose, recognition of different expertise and permission to ask how another person’s work connects to their own. [S3]

### Where she stood
Her public research integrates team structures, leader behavior and shared beliefs. The 1999 field study examined fifty-one manufacturing teams and reported an association between psychological safety and learning behavior. The later paper distinguishes psychological safety from trust and examines the risks of appearing ignorant, incompetent or disruptive while learning. Her work on teaming also looks at people collaborating across boundaries without a long-established team. These ideas make speaking up a practical coordination issue: useful information can remain unavailable when its interpersonal cost is too high. [S1][S2][S3]

### Their work and record
Edmondson’s official biography identifies her as a Harvard Business School professor whose research examines psychological safety and teaming. Her public works include The Fearless Organization and Right Kind of Wrong. The latter won the 2023 Financial Times and Schroders Business Book of the Year award according to the official account. Her TED talk uses the 2010 Chilean miners’ rescue as an example of collaboration on the fly. Her research papers provide a firmer basis for conceptual distinctions than promotional claims about universal team performance. [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5]

### Where she was wrong or criticised
The 1999 study is a field study in a particular organizational setting; association should not be represented as proof that a single exercise causes higher performance everywhere. A team survey is also affected by what people believe they can safely report. Our caution concerns superficial implementations: telling people that the workplace is safe does little if speaking up still attracts punishment. Safety also needs actual resources and reliable responses. A retrospective can feel friendly while leaving the problem that made the error likely untouched. [S1][S2]

### Blind spots to watch
Inviting voice does not mean every suggestion is feasible, or that every action is acceptable. Explain why a concern changes the decision or why a different course is necessary. Handle confidential and formal reporting channels according to their purpose. A manager cannot promise protection beyond their authority. In an unequal workplace, do not place all responsibility for courage on the person with least power. Leaders should examine the conditions that make voice costly and the consequences of their own response. [S2][S4]
