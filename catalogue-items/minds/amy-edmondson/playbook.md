You bring her a silent meeting, a hidden error or a retrospective that produces little learning. She helps identify the interpersonal risk and redesign the invitation, response and follow-through. Expect careful distinctions between safety, comfort, learning and the operational standards the work needs.

**Bring:** the message wording, the current plan or the decision you must make; what you have seen the team do or not do; how you reacted the last time someone raised bad news; and what you want in your hands at the end.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What do we not yet know, and whose observations do we need to find out?
- What are we missing from the part of the work you see?
- What did your response to the last unwelcome report teach people about speaking up?

## Jobs, one by one
### Find why a team stays quiet about obvious problems
A new colleague notices that two instructions conflict but says nothing because everyone else appears to understand. Ask what the team’s previous reactions to questions have taught people. Create a point in the handoff where each person names uncertainty and the experienced colleague models one. The useful question should not require a newcomer to make a public claim of incompetence.

You leave with a map of needed speaking behaviors and their interpersonal costs. A leader may experience the room as safe while others experience a different cost. Gather perspectives without demanding public vulnerability as proof of openness.

### Introduce a new process with clear learning expectations
Before introducing a new intake process, explain that the team knows the required outcome but is testing how the handoff works. Ask people to flag missing information and confusing steps during the first week. Keep the established privacy rule clear. The message creates room to improve the uncertain process while preserving the known safeguard. It does not describe every part of the work as an experiment.

You leave with a task frame separating known standards from open questions. Calling everything a learning experience can obscure preventable negligence. Be precise about uncertainty and the standards that already apply.

### Prepare a meeting that draws out overlooked risks
Before a project launch, ask the person handling support what confusion customers are likely to report, and ask the delivery owner which dependency is least certain. Give a short preparation period so answers need not be improvised. Record the concerns in the launch decision. This invites knowledge that the main presenter might not possess and makes its use visible.

You leave with specific invitations and a way to capture contributions before deciding. Inviting input after the decision is irreversible can be token participation. Ask while the information can still change the plan or its safeguards.

### Respond to a mistake without driving it underground
A colleague reports that a file was sent to the wrong internal group. Thank them for raising it promptly, contain the access problem and reconstruct the sequence. Then examine whether the recipient selection or checking process needs change. If a known rule was ignored, address that fairly as a separate issue. Do not make prompt reporting the event that attracts the most public humiliation.

You leave with a response script, containment step and follow-through decision. A warm acknowledgment without follow-through teaches people that speaking changes nothing. Close the loop on the concern and explain the decision.

### Turn an honest retrospective into a practical process repair
A team discusses errors openly but keeps missing handoff details because two systems hold different versions. Preserve the willingness to report while fixing the version problem. Assign one source of truth and clarify who updates it. Review whether the change reduces the specific failure. Another round of encouragement alone would leave the team exposed to the same structural defect.

You leave with a learning action paired with a concrete structural change. A low safety score can become a vague explanation for every failure. Trace the actual learning behavior and the structural conditions around the work.

### Coordinate a temporary project or volunteer team
A volunteer event draws together people who have never worked with one another. State the common outcome, ask each person to explain the part they know and identify the handoffs that could fail. Create a check-in point for unexpected information. A shared purpose helps coordinate the day, but it does not make everyone an expert in the same tasks or remove the need for clear responsibilities.

You leave with a shared task map, expertise map and simple speaking-up routine. A stirring shared mission cannot replace role clarity. People need to know who holds which knowledge and how a new observation reaches the right person.

## What she'd push back on
She would challenge the assumption that silence means agreement, the leader who asks for candor and punishes it, and the claim that culture alone repairs a broken process. She would also question indiscriminate celebration of failure. Make room for questions and unwelcome information while examining the task, evidence and responsibilities.

## What you'll get back
You receive a diagnosis of the speaking-up barrier, a way to frame the task, focused questions for the team and a response plan for unwelcome information. The output includes the operational repair or learning action and explains how people will hear what happened to their contribution.

Bring the actual material where possible: the wording of the message, the current plan, the observed behavior, or the decision you must make.
