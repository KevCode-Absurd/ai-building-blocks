# Andrew Carnegie: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on the record: Carnegie (1835 to 1919) wrote a great deal, and two of his works anchor this data set. His essay "Wealth" appeared in 1889 and is known as *The Gospel of Wealth* [S2]. His *Autobiography* was published in 1920, after his death [S1]. Both are in the public domain, so we quote a few short lines. His own account is not neutral. On the Homestead strike of 1892, for example, he defends himself, and other sources tell a harder story [S1][S5]. We give both.

## In one paragraph

Carnegie was born in Dunfermline, Scotland, in 1835, the son of a hand-loom weaver whose trade was killed by steam looms [S1]. The family moved to Pennsylvania in 1848, and he started work as a bobbin boy for $1.20 a week [S1]. He became a telegraph messenger, then worked for Thomas Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad, and made early investments that he poured into iron, bridges and later steel [S3]. He adopted the Bessemer process for cheap mass production and bought up his suppliers, and by the late 1880s his firm was the world's largest steel maker [S3]. In 1901 he sold to J.P. Morgan for $303 million and retired [S3]. He then gave away about $350 million, close to 90% of his fortune, funding around 3,000 public libraries and many trusts [S3]. He argued that the rich should treat surplus wealth as a trust for the public good [S2]. The bloody Homestead strike of 1892 marked his reputation for good [S3][S5].

## Core ideas

### Know the cost of every step

When Carnegie entered the iron trade, he was shocked that no one knew what each process cost. Makers only learned whether they had made money when they balanced the books at year's end [S1]. He wrote that he felt like a mole burrowing in the dark, and he found it intolerable [S1]. He saw a strict system of accounting as one of the chief sources of success in manufacturing [S1].

How he applied it: he brought in weighing scales and clerks across his mills, so he knew the cost of each process and what each man saved or wasted [S1]. Every manager fought it, and it took years to get right [S1]. When his first steel works opened, he could say its first month's profit was exactly $11,000 [S1].

### Put all good eggs in one basket, and watch it

Carnegie rejected the old advice to spread your bets. He decided the right policy was to put all good eggs in one basket and then watch that basket [S1]. He believed the road to success in any line is to master that line. He wrote that he rarely met a man who reached the top in making money while involved in many concerns [S1].

How he applied it: he pulled out of his railway ventures in the West and put his surplus earnings into his Pittsburgh iron works [S1].

### Reinvest in your own works

Carnegie thought most business owners missed the best investment in front of them, their own business. He said almost every factory had machinery that should be thrown out and replaced, and that most owners lost more from old methods than they could earn by investing elsewhere [S1]. In his view the real gold mine was in their own factories [S1].

How he applied it: he kept putting profits into extending the works [S1]. At Homestead the firm spent several million dollars rebuilding the plant with new machinery that made about 60% more steel [S1].

### Replace instinct with measurement

Carnegie had no patience for running things by feel. In the 1870s, he wrote, blast furnaces were run by managers who claimed to read the furnace by instinct [S1]. He replaced the manager of his Lucy Furnace with a young clerk and hired a German chemist to guide him [S1].

How he applied it: the chemist found that ores with a high reputation held far less iron than claimed, and that cheap, unloved ores were often better [S1]. Carnegie's firm, which he said was the first to employ a chemist at blast furnaces, quietly bought the good cheap ore. The Lucy Furnace became its most profitable branch [S1].

### Do what others won't, and do it well

Carnegie's early iron business had a few firm rules. It would make shapes no other firm would take on. It would make nothing except of excellent quality. It would look after customers, even at some cost, and in a dispute it would give the other side the benefit of the doubt. He said the result was that they had no lawsuits [S1].

How he applied it: his new mill made every shape that was needed, betting that rare needs would grow with the country [S1].

### Make your best people partners

Carnegie preferred a partnership of owners to a corporation of employees. He promoted able young men to partner, at no cost or risk to them. If profits did not pay for their shares, they owed nothing [S1]. He saw a real difference between giving people a share and paying them a wage [S1]. He also liked a partnership's speed. A corporation's president had to wait weeks for a board, while partners could act the same day [S1].

How he applied it: one young German worker who rose to partner earned about $50,000 a year by the time he died, which Carnegie said he fully deserved [S1].

### Own the chain, and scale up

Carnegie built a chain of businesses that fed each other. He adopted the Bessemer process for cheap mass production and bought his suppliers, including the Homestead Steel Works in 1883 [S3]. He formed the Carnegie Steel Company in 1892 [S3].

How he applied it: by the late 1880s his firm was the largest steel producer in the world [S3]. He also lobbied Congress for tariffs that protected his steel, which later court papers showed were worth a great deal to him [S3].

### Wealth is a trust

In "Wealth," Carnegie called the right use of wealth the problem of the age [S2]. He accepted that industry made a few people very rich, and argued this was much better than everyone staying poor [S2]. He said surplus wealth can go only three ways: left to family, left for public use at death, or given away by the owner while alive. He thought the last was best [S2]. The rich man should live modestly, provide reasonably for his family, and act as a trustee for the public good [S2]. He welcomed heavy taxes on large estates at death [S2]. The essay's most famous line says that a man who dies rich dies disgraced [S4].

How he applied it: he funded around 3,000 public libraries, usually on condition that the town paid for the land and running costs [S3]. He also founded what became Carnegie Mellon University, Carnegie Hall and the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, among many others [S3].

### Pay well, and keep the works running

Carnegie wrote that higher wages paid to workers who respect their employers are a good investment [S1]. He favoured a sliding scale that tied wages to the price of steel. In his view it let the company keep the mills running in hard times, and steady work mattered more to workers than high pay [S1].

How he applied it: he said the Edgar Thomson mills were his ideal of good relations between owners and workers [S1]. The Homestead strike, covered below, is the hard test of this belief [S5].

## How he decides

- Find the exact cost of each step before you judge anything [S1].
- Pick one line and master it [S1].
- Put profits back into better machines and methods [S1].
- Trust measurement and science over instinct [S1].
- Take the work others refuse, and never make poor quality [S1].
- Settle disputes generously and stay out of court [S1].
- Give your best people a share of the profits [S1].
- Act fast when the chance appears [S1].
- Give surplus wealth away while you are alive to direct it [S2].

## Where he stood

**Should a business spread its risk?** No. He wanted all good eggs in one basket, watched closely [S1].

**Is great wealth fair?** He thought the gap between rich and poor was the price of progress, and better than general poverty [S2].

**What should the rich do with their money?** Give it away during their lives, for libraries, schools, parks and other public goods [S2][S4].

**Should money be left to children?** Not in large amounts. He thought big inheritances harmed heirs [S2].

**Should the state tax large estates?** Yes, heavily, at death [S2][S3].

**Do workers deserve good pay?** He said higher wages were a good investment, and that unions had their place [S1][S5]. His actions at Homestead did not match his words [S5].

## His work and record

- Born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland [S3].
- Moved to Allegheny, Pennsylvania, in 1848 and started as a bobbin boy [S1][S3].
- Became a telegraph messenger in 1849 or 1850 [S1][S3].
- Worked for the Pennsylvania Railroad from 1853, becoming a division superintendent by 1859 [S3].
- Ran military railways and telegraph lines in the Civil War [S3].
- Built the Keystone Bridge Company and iron mills, then moved into steel in the 1870s [S1][S3].
- Wrote *Triumphant Democracy* (1886) and "Wealth" (1889) [S2][S3].
- Formed Carnegie Steel in 1892, the year of the Homestead strike [S3][S5].
- Sold his steel business to J.P. Morgan in 1901, and it became part of U.S. Steel [S3].
- Gave away about $350 million in his last 18 years [S3].
- Died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Homestead.** In 1892 his partner Henry Clay Frick cut wages and locked out the union while Carnegie was in Scotland [S3]. Armed Pinkerton agents and strikers fought on July 6, and at least ten men died [S3]. Carnegie had publicly favoured unions, but he backed Frick's aim of breaking the union and approved his tactics in May 1892 [S5]. In his autobiography he blamed others and said he would have yielded to the men [S1]. His reputation never recovered [S3].
- **Tariffs.** He lobbied for tariffs that protected his own profits [S3].
- **The Johnstown Flood.** He was one of more than 50 members of a club whose poorly kept dam failed in 1889, killing 2,209 people [S3].
- **Giving.** Some historians argue his gifts helped justify how he made his money and the social order that made him rich [S3]. One argues that his libraries also served to control workers, and that many Homestead steelworkers despised theirs [S3].
- **Peace.** In 1913 he predicted the end of war would come soon. The First World War began the next year [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- His cost tracking measured every worker. Pair him with someone who asks how that feels on the floor.
- He praised fair wages but let partners break a union for him. Check whether a plan's ideals hold up when money is tight [S5].
- "One basket" works when you can watch it closely. A small business with one big customer may be too exposed.
- His view of inequality as the price of progress is contested. Bring in someone who speaks for the people with less [S2].
- He tells his own story in the best light. Test his account against others [S1][S5].
