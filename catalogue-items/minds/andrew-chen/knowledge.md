# Andrew Chen: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Andrew Chen is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from six of his essays on andrewchen.com, written from 2012 to about 2018, a review of his 2021 book *The Cold Start Problem*, and reference pages. Several essays show no date on the page, so we date them by clues such as image paths and say "about." We could not open the book itself, so its ideas come from the Charter review [S7]. We found no reliable source for his birth year. Treat each view as "as of" the essay it comes from. He is an investor, so this data set covers his frameworks only, never advice on what to invest in.

## In one paragraph

Chen is a writer and investor who has spent his career on one question: how do products get users and keep them? He led rider growth teams at Uber and is now a general partner at Andreessen Horowitz [S8]. He has published more than 650 essays [S9]. In 2012 his post on how Airbnb used Craigslist spread the term "growth hacker" to a wide audience [S1][S10]. His essays argue that every marketing channel decays as it scales [S2], that most products lose most users in the first month [S4], and that a startup leaning on paid ads can get stuck [S5]. His book adds a model for network products: start with the smallest network that works, win the hard side, then grow one network at a time [S7].

## Core ideas

### Growth hackers blend marketing and engineering

In 2012 Chen argued that a new kind of marketer was replacing the classic VP of Marketing. This person runs tests, builds landing pages, designs sharing into the product and measures everything [S1]. Before product/market fit, they build sharing into the product. After fit, they scale what works [S1]. He said huge platforms such as Facebook and Apple now give new products reach to hundreds of millions of people, so distribution runs through code more than through press or agencies [S1]. Sean Ellis, who coined the term in 2010, credited this post with making it popular [S11].

How he applied it: he took apart Airbnb's tool that let hosts post listings to Craigslist, which had no public way to connect. He argued that a classic marketer would never have thought of it. It took someone who thought like an engineer to spot a busy channel with little competition [S1].

### Every channel decays

Chen named this his law of "shitty clickthroughs" (his own blunt title): over time, every marketing strategy ends up with poor click rates [S2]. The first banner ad, in 1994, had a click rate of 78%. Facebook's average banner in 2011 had 0.05% [S2]. Three forces drive the decline. Novelty wears off, rivals copy what works, and as you scale you reach people who care less [S2].

How he applied it: he told teams to keep rotating ads and testing new places to slow the decay, to offer useful content rather than pure ads, and above all to find the next untapped channel [S2]. He warned investors to doubt results from small tests [S2].

### Retention beats the next feature

Chen named the "next feature fallacy": the hope that one more feature will make people use a product [S4]. His sample funnel starts with 1,000 visitors. About 200 sign up, and only about 20 are still daily users after 30 days [S4]. New features usually aim at engaged users, who are a small group, so they can't move the overall numbers [S4]. Features behind what he called an "engagement wall," which ask users to create or set up something first, reach even fewer people [S4].

How he applied it: he said to focus on new and casual users, improve the landing page and the first visit, and find the few actions that make someone active. Twitter's change from a blank feed to suggested accounts is his example of taking a clear stance on how a product should be used [S4].

### Paid ads can trap you

Chen argued that many startups die from an addiction to paid marketing [S5]. Early ads look profitable, so spending and fundraising rise. Then costs climb, payback stretches past a year, money dries up and layoffs follow. He called this the "paid marketing local max" [S5]. Averaging all acquisition costs together hides the true cost of each channel, because early organic users make the blend look cheap [S5]. Ads are also easy for rivals to copy, while sharing loops built into a product are hard to copy [S5].

How he applied it: he pointed to Dropbox, which found paid search unprofitable and grew instead through folder sharing and a referral program that gave extra storage [S5]. He tentatively suggested capping paid acquisition at 30 to 40 percent of the top of the funnel (his wording, "perhaps") and building a second or third channel [S5]. It is a hint, not a rule.

### The supply side is king

In about 2018 Chen explained why many "Uber for X" startups failed. Recruiting workers was costly in every case. Ride requests come all day, so drivers can earn steadily. Services such as car washing or valet parking have demand that comes in spikes, so workers sit idle and earn less [S6]. Marketplaces also need a long stretch of losing money on each transaction before they reach enough density [S6].

How he applied it: he told founders to start from the worker's point of view. Does the work suit them, can they do it close to full time, and when can subsidies stop? [S6].

### Start with the atomic network

*The Cold Start Problem* (2021) argues that network products struggle at launch because a product with few users is worth little to the next person [S7]. The fix is to start with an "atomic network," the smallest group that makes the product useful. Slack began with single teams, Tinder with college groups and Uber with set times and places in San Francisco [S7]. Google+ is the warning: it launched to everyone without first building small networks [S7].

How he applied it: the book reports (as the book's account of Airbnb, which we have not checked against Airbnb itself) that about 300 listings, with 100 reviewed, was a key level for a market to grow [S7]. It also tells how Reddit's founders filled the front page with links by hand at first [S7].

### Win the hard side, then expand

Every network has a hard side, the small group whose effort creates most of the value. Examples are Wikipedia's core editors and Uber's busiest drivers [S7]. Early work should go to them, through incentives, events or hand-holding [S7]. Once one network works, you grow into the next one nearby until growth runs on its own, which the book calls the tipping point [S7]. The book's five stages are the cold start, the tipping point, escape velocity, hitting the ceiling and the moat [S7].

How he applied it: the book names three network effects. Engagement grows as more users create more uses, acquisition grows as users bring in others, and the economics improve with scale [S7].

### Expect the trough, and don't restart

In 2012 Chen wrote about the "trough of sorrow," the hard months after launch buzz fades and before the product works [S3]. Nearly every startup goes through it. Restarting or a wild pivot only delays the same problem [S3]. He told founders to find the bottleneck first: is it retention, onboarding or growth? Marketing can't fix a product people don't stick with [S3].

How he applied it: his advice was to limit how much is new at once, make the value easy to explain even if it sounds boring, win small battles, and keep teams under about six people [S3].

## How he decides

- Find the bottleneck first: retention, onboarding or growth [S3].
- Look at the funnel in real numbers, from visit to day 30 [S4].
- Fix the first visit before building features for power users [S4].
- Assume any channel that works today will decay [S2].
- Don't trust results from small tests [S2].
- Break blended acquisition costs into costs per channel [S5].
- Build growth into the product, where rivals can't copy it easily [S5].
- In a marketplace, start with the supply side's economics [S6].
- Start smaller and more specific than feels comfortable [S7].

## Where he stood

**Can a new feature save weak retention?** Rarely. Most features reach too few users to move the numbers [S4].

**Is paid marketing bad?** No, but relying on it is risky. It can make sense to kick-start a network, as Facebook did in new regions, or with deep engineering behind it [S5].

**How should a network product launch?** In one small, dense network first, then the one next to it, never everywhere at once [S7].

**What should founders do when nothing seems to work?** Keep going, find the bottleneck and resist starting over [S3].

**Does marketing still run through press and agencies?** Less and less. By 2012 he said distribution ran mostly through platforms and product integrations [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Uber and harms of scale.** The Charter review said the book underplays Uber's controversies and YouTube's problems with extreme content [S7].
- **Local limits.** The same review noted that network effects are often local, as Uber found city by city, which the book's model can understate [S7].
- **Crypto.** The review said he stressed crypto's importance without testing how it affects his own arguments [S7].
- **Growth hacking hype.** Sources cited on Wikipedia argue that growth hacking is not free and is just effective marketing [S10]. Ellis also warned that tactics can drift from the product's core value [S11].

## Blind spots to watch

- He writes mostly about consumer tech and network products. A shop or service with no network may get less from the cold start model.
- His view comes from Silicon Valley, with venture money behind it. Advice to lose money per sale until you reach density assumes you can raise it [S6].
- Growth metrics can push aside brand, craft and the effect on workers. Pair him with a brand, design or ethics mind.
- His own law suggests his tactics also decay once everyone uses them [S2].
