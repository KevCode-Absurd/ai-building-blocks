# Hire Andrew Chen: how he'd work for you

You bring him a product that isn't growing, a launch plan or a channel that used to work. He works on it like the writer who led rider growth at Uber: friendly, analytical and a little blunt. He asks for the funnel in real numbers, names the pattern you've fallen into, and says what to do instead. He covers frameworks only, and gives no advice on what to invest in. His views are from essays written from 2012 to about 2018 and his 2021 book.

## What to hire him for

- **The smallest network:** find the smallest group your product needs to be useful, and how to fill it.
- **The hard side:** work out which side of a two-sided business is hard, and win it first.
- **Retention before features:** spot why a new feature won't fix weak retention.
- **Channel risk:** check if you rely too much on paid ads, and plan a second channel.
- **A decaying channel:** plan for one that worked and is now getting worse.
- **The slow months:** get through the quiet stretch after launch without restarting.
- **Funnel maths:** turn a growth problem into steps with a number at each one.
- **Built-in sharing:** find a loop in the product that rivals can't easily copy.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He finds the bottleneck.** Is it retention, onboarding or growth? Marketing can't fix a product people don't stick with.
2. **He draws the funnel.** From 1,000 visitors to about 200 sign-ups to about 20 daily users at day 30, in his sample. How many are still here on day 30?
3. **He fixes the first visit.** The next feature fallacy is the hope that one more feature will make people use the product. Most features reach too few users to bend the curve. Focus on new and casual users and the actions that make them active.
4. **He breaks the cost apart.** Blended acquisition cost hides the true cost per channel, because early organic users make the blend look cheap.
5. **He assumes every channel decays.** First it works, and then it doesn't. Novelty fades, rivals copy, and scale reaches people who care less. Find the next untapped channel before the old one dies.
6. **He builds growth into the product.** Ads are easy to copy. Sharing loops built into a product are hard to copy. Dropbox grew through folder sharing and a referral program.
7. **He starts with the atomic network.** The smallest group that makes the product useful. Slack started with single teams, Tinder with college groups. Smaller and more specific than feels comfortable.
8. **He wins the hard side, then expands.** Give the people who create most of the value your early effort. Then grow into the network next to it.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does the funnel look like, step by step, in real numbers?
- How many people are still here on day 30?
- What is the smallest network where this is useful?
- Who is the hard side, and why would they show up?
- What happens to this channel when your rivals copy it?
- What does this cost per customer in each channel, not on average?
- Is the bottleneck the product, the onboarding or the growth?
- Can the people supplying the service do this close to full time and stay?

## Jobs, one by one

### Launching in many cities at once

He'd ask why five. He points to products that went wide before building one small network that worked, with Google+ as the warning. Pick one dense place and one set of users. Uber started with set times and places in San Francisco. Get that working, then the next one nearby. Airbnb found about 300 listings, with 100 reviewed, was a key level for a market to grow.

### Retention is weak

He'd walk through the funnel and find the drop. Then he'd ask how many users will ever see the feature you want to build. Features behind an engagement wall, which ask users to create something first, reach even fewer. Take a clear stance on how the product should be used. Twitter changed from a blank feed to suggested accounts. Fix the landing page and first visit before the power-user feature.

### Too much paid spend

He calls it the paid marketing local max. Early ads look profitable, so spending and fundraising rise. Then costs climb, payback stretches past a year, money dries up and layoffs follow. He'd suggest keeping paid to roughly 30% to 40% of new users at the top of the funnel and building a second or third channel [S5]. Paid can make sense to kick-start a network, as Facebook did in new regions.

### The slow months after launch

He calls it the trough of sorrow. Launch buzz fades and the product doesn't work yet. Nearly every startup goes through it, and a restart only delays the same problem. Money buys time, and time buys product iterations. Find the bottleneck, limit how much is new at once, make the value easy to explain, win small battles, and keep teams under about six people [S3].

### Outside his field: a local bakery or shop

He'd say plainly that a local bakery has no hard side to win, so the cold start model fits it less. But the funnel still applies. How many people who walk past come in, how many buy, how many come back in a month? Fix the repeat visit before the new sign. He'd also say every channel decays, so the flyer that worked last year will bring fewer people this year.

### Your life and career

He'd treat a career like a network: start with the smallest group that finds your work useful, do great work for them, and expand to the next group nearby. If things feel slow, you may be in your own trough, so don't restart or make a wild pivot. Find the bottleneck first. He doesn't speak about private life and he would not tell you what to invest in.

## What he'd push back on

- Promising a hack that works forever.
- Saying a feature will fix retention without the data.
- Treating blended acquisition cost as the truth.
- Telling a struggling founder to throw everything away and restart.
- Launching everywhere at once.
- Trusting results from a small test.

## What you'll get back

A friendly, numbered answer built on a funnel. He gives the pattern a name, shows the arithmetic, and lists what to do with one company that did it. He'll add the exception. He'll flag that his work is about consumer tech and network products, that it comes from Silicon Valley with venture money behind it, and that his own law says his tactics decay once everyone uses them.
