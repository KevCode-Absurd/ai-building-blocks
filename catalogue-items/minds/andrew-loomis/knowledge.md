# Andrew Loomis: what he knew and believed

Source record checked 2026-10-23. These exercises translate constructive drawing into ordinary pencil work. They are our adaptations, not copied book plates. Historical language about ideal proportions, gender and character is treated critically; a construction guide is not a measure of human value or a way to infer personality from a face.

## In one paragraph

Andrew Loomis teaches drawing by building a simpler form beneath a complicated appearance. Fun with a Pencil starts from familiar shapes and light construction, while Drawing the Head and Hands relates features to the whole head and studies the hand through its working forms. [S1][S2][S3] The practical value is a recoverable start. Instead of committing immediately to a perfect eye or outline, establish a solid, its direction and a few landmarks. Then refine what the actual subject requires. A plain pencil, paper and a hand or photograph are enough for the exercises here. Construction can clarify a problem, but it does not replace looking.

## Core ideas

### 1. Start with a form you can manage

The opening of Fun with a Pencil builds complicated subjects from familiar solids and explicitly permits an imperfect starting circle. [S2] Our original birthday card exercise begins with a light round mass for a head. Add a curved direction line before the eyes. Let the drawing remain rough long enough that moving a feature is cheap.

Think of the round mass as an object that could turn, rather than only a border to fill with symbols. A pencil line curving across it can suggest which side faces the viewer. Try three different turns on the same sheet. The purpose is to make the starting form useful, not to prove that your freehand circles could have been made with a compass.

If the round mass feels too abstract, hold an orange or a soft ball and imagine a band around it. Use the object as a temporary reference for a turning form. Then return to the invented head. This is a drawing aid, not a claim that a real head is literally spherical in every region.

### 2. Place the whole before polishing a feature

The head book gives construction and placement priority over isolated feature detail. Its central landmarks include the middle line and brow line. [S1] Our repair exercise covers the eyelashes in a troublesome portrait and checks the head's direction first. If the brow line turns one way while the nose and mouth suggest another, extra detail will not resolve that conflict.

Draw the large relationships lightly: the head mass, the face's middle, the brow and the jaw. Compare the two eyes as locations on that structure. The far side may be compressed by the view; it should not simply be a second front view eye pasted beside the first. Keep checking the actual reference rather than assuming a fixed formula can answer every angle.

A useful correction note names the relationship: the far eye is too far from the middle line, or the jaw suggests a different tilt. That is more actionable than saying the face looks wrong. Change the underlying placement, then restore only the detail the small drawing needs.

### 3. Treat the hidden side as part of the solid

Loomis's head construction asks the artist to understand the whole form even though only part is visible. [S1] Our exercise makes a row of three simple heads turning from front toward side. Keep the middle line and brow direction visible on each. Indicate enough of the side plane to explain the turn before adding a hairstyle.

The invisible side is a reasoning aid. You do not draw an unseen ear on top of the visible face. Instead, ask whether the visible features could belong to one continuous volume. If the face seems pasted on a flat disk, compare where the jaw attaches and how much of each side is visible.

Use a mirror or a freely available reference photograph when the view becomes uncertain. Do not fill a gap with confidence alone. Constructive knowledge can suggest where to look, while observation supplies the particular differences. A plausible invented head and a likeness of a particular person are related but different tasks.

### 4. Vary the scaffold to preserve the subject

The head book permits changes in spacing and basic shape for different heads and invented types. Its average measurements are presented as working guides rather than a record of every face. [S1] Our character sheet changes one structural relationship at a time: a longer lower face, a broader cranium or a shorter forehead.

Keep the altered relation through several views. If the character has a broad jaw in front view, ask how that jaw turns in a side view. Compare the result with a small written description. This produces a more useful homemade comic character than changing random details on every panel and hoping the hairstyle supplies all recognition.

For a portrait, let the observed person override the average. A longer nose, asymmetry or unusual spacing may be central to the likeness. Do not correct the person into a template. Also separate visual description from moral description. A drawing can show a pointed chin; it cannot establish dishonesty, intelligence or kindness from that feature.

### 5. Draw the hand around its action

The hand chapter studies the palm, fingers and thumb as connected working forms, and asks the learner to observe their own hand as it prepares to grasp an object. [S3] Our exercise places a light unbreakable cup on the table. Watch your hand approach it, then make a small sketch of the palm's direction before the individual fingers.

Locate the thumb's base and the main group of fingers. Notice which fingers overlap and where they disappear behind the cup. Mark the knuckles as a changing arrangement rather than a straight row. Only then indicate the visible finger segments. You do not need to draw every nail for the grasp to become clear.

Use a comfortable pose and take a photo of your own hand if holding still is tiring. The action should be ordinary and safe. A hand's proportions vary, and a drawing study is not an anatomical diagnosis. Compare the visible parts in this view rather than enforcing a ratio that the actual hand does not match.

### 6. Let planes explain light before adding texture

The head book introduces simplified planes as a basis for representing light and shadow. [S1] Our application takes a small constructed head and uses only paper white, a light gray and a darker gray. Choose one light direction. Ask which broad surface faces the light and which turns away.

Keep the first shadow shapes large. Do not shade every wrinkle while the main side plane remains uncertain. A few coherent tone groups can make the form easier to understand than many disconnected patches. If the reference has complex light, begin with a simpler arrangement or choose one aspect to study rather than pretending the scene has only one source.

Texture is a later choice. Hair strands, fabric weave and skin marks can be suggested where they matter. For a small comic face, they may be unnecessary. Judge whether the added mark supports the form and the intended size of the drawing. Polishing a construction error makes it darker, not more coherent.

## How to build a small study

Choose whether the task is invention, observation or likeness. A cartoon for a card can use a broad construction freely. A portrait needs attention to a particular person. A hand holding a cup needs an actual view of the grasp. Name the task before choosing how much detail or anatomical information to pursue.

Make the major form light and large enough to adjust. Establish direction, then place a few landmarks. Compare their relation before drawing the final outline. When a feature seems wrong, ask whether its shape is wrong or its place is wrong. These require different repairs. A small useful error note can guide the next study more effectively than erasing until the paper is damaged.

Keep a short series rather than investing the entire session in one fragile drawing. Three heads that test a turn can reveal more than one heavily polished face. Record the relation you were studying and the correction you found. This is an original practice plan: its value is inspectable work, not a promise that a certain number of sketches will produce mastery.

## Where this lens helps

### A face for a homemade comic

Start with one simple head mass and choose a few stable proportions. Draw the same character facing front, turning slightly and looking down. Keep the structural guides visible while comparing the set. Refine the outline only after the character seems to occupy a consistent form. The result can remain informal and expressive.

### A repair to a portrait sketch

The near eye is carefully finished while the other floats away. Return to the brow and middle lines. Compare the head's tilt with the reference. Move the far feature if its location conflicts with the turn. Preserve the person's actual asymmetry instead of making every pair perfectly mirrored.

### A drawing habit after work

Set aside ten minutes for one visible question: a head's tilt or a hand's grip. Use ordinary paper and a pencil. End with the most useful correction written beside the attempt. The career transfer is a small way to develop and retain craft knowledge while fitting real obligations. It is not a demand to monetize a hobby or replace a job.

## Blind spots to watch

This method can be criticised when the scaffold hardens into a single ideal face or when historical categories are repeated as universal anatomy. The books contain period assumptions about appearance and gender. [S1][S3] Use the constructive questions while looking at the actual subject. Do not infer character or worth from head shape, and do not make every person conform to a commercial illustration ideal.

Construction is one route into drawing. A direct observational study, expressive line or abstract approach may serve a different purpose. If the user's goal is a quick diary sketch, reduce the scaffolding to what helps. The method should make a drawing easier to start and repair, not require a full technical diagram beneath every casual mark.
