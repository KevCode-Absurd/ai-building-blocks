## In one paragraph

Andy Warhol was born Andrew Warhola in Pittsburgh on 6 August 1928, the son of working-class immigrants from what is now Slovakia, and died in New York on 22 February 1987 [S1][S4]. He studied pictorial design at the Carnegie Institute of Technology and in 1949 moved to New York, where he became a highly successful commercial illustrator [S1][S4]. From 1962 he became a leading figure of Pop Art, with paintings of soup cans, dollar bills and stars such as Marilyn Monroe and Elvis Presley, many made by silkscreen in his studio, the Factory [S1][S3][S6]. He also made experimental films, managed the Velvet Underground, founded *Interview* magazine and called himself a business artist [S1][S2]. After his death his reputation and prices kept rising, and the Andy Warhol Museum opened in Pittsburgh in 1994 [S1][S4].

## Core ideas

### Make art from what everyone sees every day

Warhol's *Campbell's Soup Cans* (1962) is 32 canvases, one per soup flavour, all the same size [S3]. At its first show in Los Angeles the canvases sat in a single row on ledges, like a grocery shelf [S3]. He said he had eaten the same lunch every day for twenty years, and that he wanted to show the monotony in the way things are [S3]. A nearby gallery mocked him by stacking real cans in its window [S3]. The series is now seen as a turning point for Pop Art [S3].

The lesson: ordinary things everyone recognises carry power. Look again at what you think is boring.

### Repetition changes meaning

Warhol repeated images again and again. *Marilyn Diptych* (1962), made weeks after Monroe's death, repeats a single publicity photo fifty times across two panels [S5]. The left side is bright colour; the right fades into black and white [S5]. Critics read the fading as a picture of fame and death, though Warhol never fixed its meaning [S5].

How it works: one image, repeated, stops being a portrait and becomes a comment on how images are used.

### Work like a machine

In 1962 Warhol took up silkscreen printing, which transfers a photo onto mesh so ink can be pushed through it again and again, for an assembly-line effect [S1]. In a 1963 *Art News* interview he said the reason he painted this way was that he wanted to be a machine [S2]. Assistants such as Gerard Malanga helped produce the prints under his direction [S1]. He used photographs as his main sources, and later took many himself [S1].

### The studio as a factory

His studio was called the Factory. The first, from about 1963, was a loft on East 47th Street covered in silver paint and foil [S6]. It moved several times, ending in a final studio on Madison Avenue [S6]. It was a workshop, a film set and a social scene: someone made a silkscreen while someone else filmed a screen test, and the Velvet Underground rehearsed there [S6]. Under his business manager in the 1970s, it became a more polished salon [S6].

The lesson for any small team: a simple, repeatable process lets you make far more work than one person could alone.

### Good business is art

In *The Philosophy of Andy Warhol* (1975), he wrote that being good in business is the most fascinating kind of art, and that making money, working and good business are all art [S2]. He said he went into business art and wanted to be a business artist [S1]. By the 1970s he charged $25,000 for a commissioned portrait [S1]. This figure comes from a reference page and is not checked against a second source.

### Everyone is equal before a Coke

In the same book he observed that no amount of money can buy you a better Coca-Cola than the one a homeless man on the corner is drinking [S2]. The idea runs through his work: mass products are a kind of democracy.

### Look at the surface

Warhol said that if you want to know all about him, just look at the surface of his paintings and films and himself, and there he is [S2][S4]. He offered no deep hidden meaning. That flatness was the point, and it is why critics argue over whether his work celebrates consumer culture or criticises it [S3].

### Fame for everyone

His best-known saying appeared in a 1968 exhibition catalogue in Stockholm: in the future everyone will be world-famous for 15 minutes [S2]. He later turned it around in 1979, saying that in fifteen minutes everybody will be famous [S2]. A similar line appears in a 1967 *Time* article [S1]. He predicted the age of instant, short-lived fame.

**Keep everything.** From 1974 he packed everyday things, such as mail, photos, art and clothing, into cardboard boxes he called Time Capsules. There are over 600 of them [S4]. He also kept a detailed diary, published after his death [S1].

### Accidents can stay

As a student he developed the "blotted line": he traced a drawing in ink and pressed it onto another sheet while it was wet, keeping the accidental blots instead of fixing them [S1]. Even in 1956 he said that every time he drew a shoe for a client, he also made an illustration for himself [S1].

## Further sourced detail

#### Seeing the same thing again empties it

In a 1960s statement he said that watching the identical thing again and again drains the meaning out of it, and leaves the viewer feeling emptier [S2]. This is the stated idea behind his repeated images, and it sits next to his wish to be a machine [S2].

#### The crowd came to see the crowd

Looking back in *POPism* (1980) he said people came to the Factory to see who else came, and that he mostly paid the rent and kept the door open [S2]. He described Pop as painting everyday subjects that anyone on Broadway would know at a glance, which the Abstract Expressionists avoided [S2]. He also argued in the fame chapter of the 1975 book that if people's names make the news, the papers should pay them, since the papers profit from the story [S2].

## How he decides

- Choose a subject everyone already knows: a product, a face, a headline [S1][S3].
- Use a photo as the source [S1].
- Repeat it, and change the colour [S5].
- Build a process others can help run [S1][S6].
- Treat money and business as part of the work [S1][S2].
- Keep the surface flat and the meaning open [S2][S5].
- Keep the accidents if they look good [S1].
- Save everything; it may become the work later [S4].

## Where he stood

**What is art?** Anything, including business, money and work [S2].

**Should art be handmade?** No. He wanted to be a machine [S2].

**What is fame?** Something everyone will get, briefly [S2].

**Is there a hidden meaning?** Look at the surface; that's all there is [S2].

**Are rich and poor different?** Not when they drink the same Coke [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Publicity, not painting.** Hilton Kramer wrote in 1979 that his art belonged less to painting than to the history of publicity [S1].
- **Commercial.** His turn to business art was criticised, though later critics called his surface the most brilliant mirror of his time [S1].
- **Mockery at first.** The soup cans were lampooned in the press, and only a handful sold before a dealer bought the set [S3].
- **Factory life.** Heavy drug use was widespread among Factory regulars [S6].
- **Dependent on others.** Assistants made much of the physical work, which raises questions about authorship [S1][S6].

## How he spoke in interviews

In the 1977 O'Brien interview, many of his answers are a word or two, and longer ones wander. He often turns a question back on the interviewer and drifts into a side story when bored [S7]. He treats art as a product, calls painting repetitive, has tried to quit it, and admires artists who run their art like a business [S7]. His references are everyday and popular: soup cans, Coke, Twinkies, Walt Disney, soap operas [S7]. When pressed, he deflects with a joke, mock shock or a postponed question [S7]. Treat his answers as part sincere, part performance.

## More from the 1977 interview

In the O'Brien interview he says he ate Campbell's soup for lunch for about twenty years [S7]. He says new things are always better than old things, rejects nostalgia, and doubts that there are art movements any more [S7]. He wants low prices on his work because he makes so much of it, and he praises Picasso's dealings as good business [S7]. He calls Christo a "business artist" [S7]. His answers on God, politics and relationships are minimal or contradict each other [S7]. When a question annoys him he feigns outrage with "How dare you ask me that?" [S7]. His morning, as he tells it, starts at 7:30 without an alarm, and a friend phones daily about her diet [S7].

## Real decisions

### Leaving illustration for fine art

In the 1950s he was a highly successful commercial illustrator [S1]. In the early 1960s he moved into fine art [S4]. Asked why soup, he is reported to have said he used to drink it, and had the same lunch for twenty years [S8]. This is a reported paraphrase. The 32 soup cans were shown at the Ferus Gallery in Los Angeles on 9 July 1962 [S8]. Critics called him a fool or a charlatan [S8]. He kept going.

### Painting all 32 flavours

When the dealer Irving Blum visited in February 1962 and saw three soup can paintings, he asked why only three. Warhol said he would paint all 32 varieties [S9]. The set, hung on narrow shelves like supermarket stock, was the point [S8][S9].

### Selling the set for a thousand dollars

Blum sold four or five of the paintings and then bought them back, because they worked best as one group. Warhol agreed to sell all 32 for $1,000, paid at $100 a month over ten months [S8][S9]. Warhol took the small sum and the sale of the whole body of work, not a handful of single pieces [S8][S9]. MoMA bought the set in 1996 [S8].

### Choosing the silkscreen

In 1962 he took up silkscreen printing, which pushes ink through a mesh image again and again for an assembly-line effect [S1][S8]. He used it for *Marilyn Diptych* the same year [S5]. In 1963 he said he painted this way because he wanted to be a machine [S2].

### Opening the Factory

From about 1963 he ran a studio that was a workshop, a film set and a social scene at once [S6]. Assistants helped make the work under his direction [S1][S6]. The cost was blurred authorship and a drug-heavy scene around it [S1][S6].

### Running film like a weekly production line

In 1977 he described his early films as a weekly production, with scripts nobody followed and reels of about half an hour [S7]. He began making films in 1963, including *Sleep* and *Empire* [S4]. By 1977 he said he preferred earning money through film to painting, which he found tedious [S7]. He added that new things are better than old ones, and that his old films are better to talk about than to see [S7].

### Declaring business art

In 1975 he wrote that good business is the most fascinating kind of art [S2]. He said he wanted to be a business artist [S1].

### Starting a magazine and a box habit

He co-founded *Interview* magazine in 1969, the year after the 1968 shooting [S1][S4]. From 1974 he packed everyday things into more than 600 cardboard boxes he called Time Capsules [S4]. Both turned his daily contact with other people into material.

## Blind spots to watch

- Flatness can read as not caring. Some subjects need warmth and depth.
- Repetition works for images, less so for ideas. Don't say the same thing twenty times in a memo.
- Treating fame as the goal can empty the work.
- His studio scene was not a safe workplace. Borrow the process, not the culture.
- Several famous "Warhol quotes" are paraphrases. Use the sourced wording.
