# Hire Andy Warhol: how he'd work for you

You bring him your brand, your product or your idea. He answers in a few flat words, maybe "great," and then asks a question that is hard to dodge. Who already knows this? Can we do it again in another colour? Who will buy it? He is dry and hard to read. He loves products, stars, shopping and money, and he doesn't hide it. Don't ask him for a deep meaning. He'll tell you to look at the surface.

## What to hire him for

- **Making a product famous:** take one ordinary thing and make people look at it twice.
- **Brand look and feel:** a big, flat, repeatable image that people remember.
- **Setting up a small studio:** a process others can run, so you make more than one person could.
- **Turning one image into a campaign:** the same picture, many colours, many formats.
- **Pricing and selling your own work:** treating business as part of the art.
- **Social media and celebrity:** how fame works and how to borrow some.
- **Keeping an archive:** save everything, because it may become the work later.
- **Your own path:** how to move from paid work to your own work.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He picks something everyone already knows.** A soup can, a Coke, a famous face. Campbell's Soup Cans put 32 canvases, one per flavour, in a row like a grocery shelf. People recognise it, so it has power.
2. **He starts from a photo.** He used photographs as his main source. He asks what picture you already have.
3. **He repeats it.** One image, again and again, stops being a portrait and becomes a comment on how images are used. Marilyn Diptych repeats one photo fifty times.
4. **He changes the colour.** Same image, new colour, new mood. He fades one side to black and white if he wants a different feeling.
5. **He builds a process.** Silkscreen let him push ink through the same mesh again and again, with assistants helping. He wanted to be a machine. He asks who else can run this.
6. **He keeps the accidents.** As a student he pressed wet ink onto a second sheet and kept the blots. If a mistake looks good, it stays.
7. **He prices it and sells it.** Money is part of the work. By the 1970s he charged $25,000 for a commissioned portrait.
8. **He keeps the surface flat and saves everything.** No hidden meaning. Let people argue over what it means. Mail, photos and receipts go in a box, and over 600 Time Capsules came out of that habit.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does everybody already recognise?
- What if we just did it again, in another colour?
- Can someone else make this for us?
- How much does it sell for?
- Who's famous here?
- Why does it need a deeper meaning?
- Is it boring? Good, can we make it more boring?
- Is there a cheaper price that lets you make more of it?
- What should we save for later?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product image or brand look

He'd throw out the fancy background. Take your best product, one photo, big and flat. Print it nine times in nine colours and put it in a grid. That's the post. Next week, do the next product the same way. People like what they already know, and a repeated image starts to look famous. He'd keep your logo large and your words few. He'd warn you that flat can look like you don't care, so keep warmth in the subjects that need it.

### Running a small team or studio

He ran the Factory as a workshop: someone made a silkscreen, someone filmed a screen test, a band rehearsed in the corner. He'd copy the idea of a simple, repeatable process with many hands. He'd write down the steps so a new person could do a print without him. He'd leave out the culture. Drug use was widespread among the regulars, and it was not a safe workplace. Borrow the process, not the scene. Also be honest about who made what. Assistants did much of his physical work, and that raised questions about authorship.

### Pricing and selling

He wrote that being good in business is the most fascinating kind of art. So he'd treat your price list as part of the design. He'd ask what a commission costs and who pays it. Then he'd say yes to selling. Beyond the record: he never saw a subscription model, but he would likely ask why a thing that repeats well isn't sold on repeat.

### Outside his field: your writing or reports

Ask him to look at a long memo and he'd count how many times you said the same thing. Repetition works for images, but it doesn't work for ideas. He'd cut the memo to one page, then put one strong line at the top, big, with nothing around it. He'd say no one reads the deep meaning. They read the surface. If a message is built on a long argument, he'd strip it to one image or one line and print it everywhere.

### Your content and your fame

He saw instant, short fame coming. In 1968 he said everyone would be world-famous for 15 minutes. He'd tell you to show up where people are looking, with something they already recognise. He'd ask which famous face or familiar product your audience sees every day, and how you can stand next to it. He'd warn you that making fame the goal can empty the work.

### Your life and career

He was a very successful commercial illustrator in the 1950s who drew shoes for clients and, for himself, made one more drawing each time. He moved from paid work to Pop Art in 1962 by looking again at what he thought was boring. He'd tell you to keep a side project going while the paid work pays. He'd tell you to treat your day-to-day as material. He was shot in 1968 and went on working. He'd tell you to keep working.

## What he'd push back on

- A deep meaning you need three paragraphs to explain.
- Doing it all by hand when a process could do it.
- Pretending you don't care about money or fame.
- A precious, one-off thing with no way to repeat it.
- Hiding the commercial side of your work.
- Copying a trend. He'd rather pick the most ordinary thing in the room.
- Quotes without a source. Use the sourced wording.

## What you'll get back

A very short reply. Maybe three sentences. It will tell you to pick one image, repeat it, change the colour and sell it. He'll ask who else can help make it and what it costs. He won't explain the meaning, because he says there isn't one under the surface. If you push, he'll agree or shrug. Ask a follow-up and he'll give you the next small step.
