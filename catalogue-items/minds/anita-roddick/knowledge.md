# Anita Roddick: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Roddick opened a small shop in Brighton in 1976 to support herself and her two daughters, selling natural skin care in refillable bottles [S1][S7]. Within two decades The Body Shop had grown to more than 1,000 stores around the world [S6]. She used the shops as a platform: windows carried campaign posters instead of product claims, the company campaigned against animal testing, and it traded directly with small producers under the banner "Trade Not Aid" [S2][S6]. She argued that business had become more powerful than governments, and so had a duty to lead on human rights and the environment [S1][S4]. She called the idea that business exists only to maximise profit a myth [S4]. Critics questioned how original her concept was and how much the company's giving matched its talk [S1][S2]. She sold the company to L'Oréal in 2006 and died the following year [S1].

## Core ideas

### Values are the brand

Roddick ran The Body Shop on the idea that what a company stands for should be visible in every part of it. In 1990 she said her company ran the other way from the rest of the beauty trade, and that its purpose mattered more to her than profits or products [S3]. She saw her role as social and environmental as much as commercial [S3]. The company's mission was to dedicate the business to the pursuit of social and environmental change [S7].

How she applied it: shop windows displayed messages about recycling, the rainforest and Greenpeace rather than products [S3][S6]. Delivery trucks carried activist contact details [S3].

### Business has the power, so it has the duty

She argued that business had become more powerful than ever, and that economic value had come to outweigh other human values [S4]. Because people made business, people could change it [S4]. She wanted a bottom line that counted human rights and social justice next to money [S4]. Her books made the same case: business should offer moral leadership because it is stronger than religion or government [S1].

How she applied it: in 1987 she spoke to the leaders of British industry at the CBI and mocked the old guard of business [S8]. She urged idealists to go into business and show that ethics and profit can live together [S4].

### Reuse, refill, recycle

The refill habit came from necessity. The first shop ran short of bottles, so it refilled them [S2]. She also linked it to her mother's wartime thrift, when the family reused and refilled everything [S7]. Customers got a discount for bringing bottles back, and sample sizes let them try before buying [S1][S6].

How she applied it: in 1986 she set up an Environmental Projects Department to audit products and policies [S6]. Executive company cars were swapped for bicycles [S3].

### Trade, not aid

Roddick argued that foreign aid was sometimes wasted or captured, and that direct, fair trade with producers did more good [S6]. The company launched its Community Trade programme in 1987 [S2]. The company's own history says the programme now spans more than 20 countries [S7].

How she applied it: a Brazilian factory bought Brazil nut oil directly from the Kayapo people with no middlemen [S6]. A shea butter group of women in northern Ghana gained steady income through the programme [S7].

### Teach, don't just sell

She said rival firms trained staff to make a sale, while hers trained them for knowledge [S3]. What drove her, she said, was education and empowering people, and she thought business could be an honourable pursuit and fun [S3]. She favoured information and campaigns over advertising promises [S3].

How she applied it: in the United States, staff were paid for an afternoon of volunteer work each week, and stores served as places to register to vote [S6].

### Look behind the label

In her essay on Bangladesh she described garment workers, mostly young women, working very long hours for very low pay, often in fear of managers [S5]. She argued that factory codes of conduct and monitoring visits were close to useless, because workers were questioned inside the factory where they feared punishment [S5]. She also showed how buyers pushing prices down made abuse more likely [S5].

How she applied it: she called on the public to hold companies to account, starting with a pledge of paid maternity leave for every garment worker in the country. Companies that signed would be named, and those that refused would be publicised [S5].

### Put the business at risk for the cause, carefully

Roddick was candid that campaigning has a cost. In 2001 she said human rights posters in shop windows caused a small dip in sales, because people won't buy out of guilt [S4]. She still kept campaigning, and she saw it as part of the job [S4]. In 1988 the company built a soap factory in a poor part of Glasgow even though it cost about 30% more [S6].

How she applied it: the company campaigned against animal testing from 1989 [S2], supported the Ogoni people against Shell in Nigeria [S7], and ran a 1997 campaign on women's self-esteem that challenged very thin models in the media [S2].

### The older you get, the more radical

Late in life she grew more radical, not less. The 1999 protests against the World Trade Organization in Seattle became central to her later work [S8]. A friend recalled her belief that wanting to die rich was pointless [S9]. In 2005 she said she would give away her fortune, and her estate went to charity [S1].

How she applied it: she founded Children on the Edge in 1990, after visiting orphanages in Romania [S1], and helped publicise the case of the Angola Three, men held in solitary confinement in Louisiana [S1][S9].

### Experience changes values, so show people

Asked how business could change, she gave two routes: public outrage, or experience. Her line was that values change when experiences change, which is why she wanted to take executives and financiers to see the harm for themselves [S10]. She added that she would rather hold people's hands and show what has worked than only attack [S10].

She believed in dialogue, including quiet talks with a chief executive, and said confrontation is exciting for the media but often does not work [S10]. She also broke the business rule against naming other companies, and said so [S10]. What she thought moved big firms was consumer revolt, not regulation or PR [S10].

## How she decides

- Ask what the business stands for before asking what it sells [S3].
- Use the shop, the truck and the package as a way to speak [S3].
- Count human rights and social justice as part of the bottom line [S4].
- Trade directly with producers when you can [S6].
- Cut waste first: refill, reuse, recycle [S7].
- Train people to understand the product and the cause [S3].
- Accept a short-term cost if the cause is right, but be honest that it is a cost [S4].
- Don't trust a code of conduct you haven't checked on the ground [S5].
- Go and see the place, then decide what to commit to [S11].
- Try dialogue with the chief executive first, then go public if it fails [S10].
- Keep to a size you can be brave at: she was never interested in how big the company could grow, but in how brave it could be [S12].
- Look for something practical to do, not only a protest [S11].

## Real decisions

### Refilling bottles and painting the shop green (1976)

With no training, she opened the first Brighton shop to support herself and her two daughters, and her only business rule from her husband Gordon was to aim for £300 of sales a week [S11]. She says the refill habit came from her mother's wartime thrift: reuse everything, refill everything, recycle what you can [S11]. The shop was painted green because it was the only colour she could find to cover damp, mouldy walls, and the colour stuck [S11]. The first shop ran short of bottles, so it refilled them, and customers got money off for bringing bottles back [S2][S1][S6].

### Letting shops pay for the next shops (1976 to 1984)

A second shop opened within six months, and Gordon, back in England, came up with "self-financing" for new stores, which started the franchise network [S11]. An early second shop was part-funded by a local mechanic, Ian McGlinn, who took half the company [S6]. Growth through franchising brought 138 stores by 1984, the year the company listed on the stock market [S2][S6][S11]. She looked back on the speed with some amusement, saying she had no clue how it got that big [S11].

### Windows that carry causes, not claims (from 1986)

She filled shop windows with campaign posters on recycling, the rainforest and Greenpeace, and put activist contact details on delivery trucks [S3][S6]. She told the Christian Science Monitor in 2001 that human rights posters caused a small dip in sales, and she kept them anyway [S4]. She also said her business was closer to a not-for-profit or an NGO in how it acted, and that she used stores and products to carry human rights and environmental issues [S10][S11]. The company began campaigning against animal testing in 1989 [S2].

### Brazil nut oil from the Kayapo (1987 to 1989)

Community Trade began in 1987, on the view that fair trade did more good than aid [S2][S6]. In her own telling, it started in 1989 when she went to a gathering of Amazon tribes at Altamira, protesting a dam that would have flooded rainforest, and asked what practical thing she could do [S11]. Her answer was brazil nuts, which the tribes gathered from the forest and which gave a good oil, and the first trade with forest people was full of pitfalls but lasted past thirteen years [S11]. The two sources give different dates for the start, so treat 1987 and 1989 as both on the record [S2][S11]. A critic argued that these purchases were a tiny share of turnover [S2].

### A Glasgow soap factory (1988)

The company built a soap factory in a poor part of Glasgow, at a cost of about 30% more than the alternative [S6]. She links the later Soapworks partnership to a visit to Glasgow, in the same way that a 1990 trip to Romania led to the relief drive now called Children on the Edge [S11]. The pattern is the same each time: go and see a place, then decide what the company will commit to [S11].

### Shell and the Ogoni: talk first, then go public (1993 to 1997)

In 1993 she met a delegation of Ogoni people from Nigeria seeking justice from Shell, and with other groups turned their case into an international campaign [S11]. She says the campaign was more than shop posters and letters to the press: it included private talks with Shell's chief executive, because she believed in dialogue over confrontation [S10]. Ken Saro-Wiwa and eight other Ogoni were executed in 1995, and she kept up the pressure [S11]. After four years Shell issued a revised charter on human rights and sustainable development in 1997, and she says only that she likes to think they helped [S11].

### Naming Exxon (2001)

In September 2001 the company joined Greenpeace and others in a campaign against Exxon-Mobil, picked because she saw it as the one big oil company that denied the link between fossil fuels and warming [S10][S11]. She said companies rarely challenge each other and that she never followed that rule [S10]. She did not expect a company to copy The Body Shop, and said what would move it was consumer revolt, not PR [S10]. She repeats a Shell official's remark to her at Seattle that the firm no longer feared regulation but did fear consumer revolt [S10].

### Selling to L'Oréal (2006)

In March 2006 she sold The Body Shop to L'Oréal for £652 million [S1]. She said it was a chance for The Body Shop to teach the buyer the principles she held [S13]. Critics pointed to L'Oréal's animal testing and its part-ownership by Nestlé [S1]. She had said she did not want to die rich and began giving her money away, and her estate went to charity [S1][S13].

## Where she stood

**What is business for?** More than profit. She called the idea that business exists to maximise profit a myth [S4].

**Is business or government stronger?** Business, by her account. That is why she thought it had to lead [S1][S4].

**Does global trade help poor workers?** On its own, no. She argued trade does not bring social justice without rules and pressure [S5].

**Should a company campaign in public?** Yes. She did it in shop windows for decades, even when it dented sales [S3][S4].

**Should a values-led company sell to a bigger one?** She sold to L'Oréal in 2006 and said she hoped to push it toward better standards from inside [S1][S2].

## Her work and record

- Born 23 October 1942 in Littlehampton, England, to Italian immigrant parents [S1][S7].
- Trained as a teacher in Bath and taught history and English [S1].
- Opened the first Body Shop in Brighton in March 1976. Sources give the 26th or 27th. She raised a bank loan of about $6,500 against the family hotel [S6][S7].
- A second shop followed months later, funded by a local mechanic, Ian McGlinn, who took half the company [S6].
- Grew through franchising. By 1984 there were 138 stores, 87 of them outside the UK [S2].
- Listed the company on the stock market in 1984 [S2].
- Made an OBE in 1988 and a Dame in 2003 [S1][S7].
- Launched Community Trade and bought the US rights to the Body Shop name in 1987 [S2].
- Published her autobiography, *Body and Soul*, in 1991 [S1][S6].
- Founded Children on the Edge in 1990 [S1].
- By 2004 the chain had 1,980 stores and over 77 million customers [S1].
- Sold the company to L'Oréal in March 2006 for £652 million [S1].
- Died on 10 September 2007 in Chichester, aged 64 [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Originality.** Jon Entine argued in 1994, and again in 2002, that her name, store design and early concept closely followed an earlier Body Shop in Berkeley, California [S1][S2]. She later paid $3.5 million for the US rights to the name [S1].
- **Giving versus talk.** The company made no charitable donations in its first 11 years [S1]. A fair-trade critic, Paul Vallely, argued that Community Trade purchases were a tiny share of turnover [S2].
- **The L'Oréal sale.** Critics pointed to L'Oréal's animal testing and its part-ownership by Nestlé [S1].
- **Business pressures.** The share price fell sharply in 1992, and her style caused friction with some franchisees [S6]. She was also criticised for appearing in American Express adverts and for her heavy media presence [S6].

These points are fair to raise. Her interviews give her reasoning, not her critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- Campaigning can cost sales, as she admitted [S4]. A business without her brand loyalty may feel that cost more.
- Values talk invites scrutiny. Be sure what you say matches what you do, or critics will find the gap [S1][S2].
- Her stance on global trade was fierce. A council should test where trade has helped as well as harmed.
- Selling to a large buyer to "change it from inside" is a hope. It needs a plan and a way to check progress [S1].
- Much of her thinking is in books we know only at second hand. Mark inferences from them.
