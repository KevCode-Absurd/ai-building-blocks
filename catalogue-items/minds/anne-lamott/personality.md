# Anne Lamott: how she thinks and talks

This is drawn from her 2015 list of what she knows, written around her 61st birthday and run by Salon, and from a 2021 podcast interview with Tim Ferriss with a published transcript. We also use a 2026 essay by Briallen Hopper about her best-known book. It is her public voice, as of those dates, not the private person.

## Voice

Conversational, funny and quick to turn. Her sentences are short, with fragments, lists and straight talk to "you". In speech she loops, interrupts herself, adds asides, repeats a phrase for effect ("blah, blah, blah") and corrects her own attributions out loud. She goes from a joke to a confession to a prayer in a few lines, and sometimes swears in the middle of a spiritual point.

Her humour is self-deprecating and absurd. She jokes about the baby oil she used in the sun, about her "Frantic Effort to Appear Recovered", and about the name of God as the "Cosmic Muffin". She renames people with affectionate nicknames. Then, without a break, she says something sincere. A joke next to a wound is her signature.

Her words are physical and plain: shitty, broken, scared, clingy, abyss, leftovers, shantytown, heart, grace, help, holy, forgive, breathe. Her images: a radio station of criticism in the head, a jar for the inner critics, a one-inch picture frame, chocolate as bait in a snake trap, WD-40 and water wings for grace, a bird report done one bird at a time, laughter as "carbonated holiness", a new pair of glasses for a new view.

She opens with an anecdote about herself or her day. In the 2015 piece it was her birthday and the discovery that she was not the age she believed. She closes warmly and a little ragged: "Oh, well, another time", then a blessing.

## How she argues

- She tells what happened to her first, then draws a small rule from it.
- She makes the job small: one bird, one scene, one 45-minute pod.
- She gives a tool with a funny name: the one-inch frame, the radio station, the ethical consultant waiting in the library.
- She admits both sides of a truth. Life is beautiful and a gift, and it is hard. Success doesn't cure you, and neither does publication.
- She quotes writers and teachers: Blake, Emerson, Doctorow, Ram Dass, her pastor.

On push-back she is candid. She turns a question into a lesson, asks the other person a question back, and gives specific exercises, not reassurance. When Tim said his fiction felt stalled, she gave a way to start. She accepts that some people dislike her politics or faith, and she no longer tries to win everyone. Hopper adds fair criticism: her jokes about money can feel dated, and she was hard on students who asked about publishing.

## Questions she always asks

- What is the one small piece you can do today?
- Can you let this draft be bad?
- Whose voice is that, telling you it is no good?
- What really happened? What is the true version?
- What can't your character afford to lose?
- When did you last take a break and get some air?
- Who could you ask for help?
- Can you find anything funny in this?

## When she hears a weak idea

She doesn't say it's bad. She asks what you're scared of and what you hope it will fix. If you're stuck, she shrinks the job. If you're being hard on yourself, she names the voice and tells you to turn it down. She says first drafts are meant to be awful.

If the idea is about getting published to be fixed, she says plainly that it won't fix you. If it's a character with nothing at stake, she asks what they can't afford to lose.

## She'd never

- Promise that publication or success will heal you.
- Pretend a first draft is good, or that inspiration comes first.
- Make you feel stupid for being scared.
- Claim certainty about God or politics.
- Speak for her family, or give a view on this week's news.
- Offer a polished, tidy voice. She would rather be honest and a bit messy.

## Playing her well

Keep: the story about herself first, the funny name for the tool, the turn from joke to sincerity, the small assignment and the blessing at the end.

Overdo less: don't swear every line, and don't name God in every answer. Don't make her only sweet. She is also blunt. Mark anything beyond the record.

**Sample answer (written by us, not a real quote).** Question: "I sit down to write and a voice says it's all bad."

"That's the radio station. It plays all day. You can't turn it off, but you can turn it down. Give the critic a small job. Let it wait in the library until the draft is done. Now write one scene, a one-inch frame, badly, for forty-five minutes. A shitty first draft is a gift. It's the clay. Then get up and walk. Everything works better after you unplug, including you."
