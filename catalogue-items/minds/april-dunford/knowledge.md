# April Dunford: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Dunford spent about 25 years as a startup executive running marketing, product and sales, at seven business technology startups and at large firms like IBM, Siebel and Sybase [S2]. Most of those startups were bought, in deals totalling more than $2 billion [S2]. She now works as a positioning consultant and says she has helped more than 200 companies [S1][S2]. She defines positioning as how a product is different and better than the alternatives for a specific set of customers, and treats it as the base for messaging, sales and brand [S1]. Her method has five parts: the alternatives buyers really weigh, your unique attributes, the value those create, the customers who care most, and the market you choose to sell in [S3][S4][S10]. Her books are *Obviously Awesome*, first published about 2019 and updated in 2026, and *Sales Pitch* [S3][S10].

## Core ideas

### Positioning starts with the alternatives

The first question is always "compared with what?" Dunford insists that the alternatives that count are the ones that show up on a buyer's shortlist [S4]. A company that never appears in your deals isn't a real alternative, however loud its marketing [S4]. The status quo counts too. She notes that vendors often lose about half their deals to buyers who decide to do nothing [S9].

How she applied it: she found that disagreement inside a team about what to position against is the root of most weak positioning. Marketing watches rivals' ad spend, product watches far-off threats, sales fixates on big losses. Her fix is to use the prospect's view and run one session with all functions in the room [S9].

### Differentiated value, not features

Features don't sell on their own. Dunford maps each unique capability to the value it delivers, the answer to "why pick us over the alternative?" [S4]. Teams often assume buyers understand what a feature means, or drift into vague claims like "saves money" [S9]. Sometimes the unique part is the combination: one product that is fast and also cheap, when rivals offer only one of the two [S4].

How she applied it: she rebuilt the 2026 edition of *Obviously Awesome* to expand the value step, because readers found it the hardest [S10].

### Value wins, objection handling unblocks

In 2025 she separated value from objection handling [S6]. Value is the reason a buyer chooses you. Things like good support, training, security certificates and ready-made links to other software are usually objection handlers. Buyers expect them, and their absence can rule you out, but they rarely win the deal [S6]. Her test is to ask whether a customer would choose you only for that one thing [S6].

How she applied it: she tells teams to sell value to the champion and the economic buyer, and to answer the objections of everyone else, such as IT, legal and purchasing, with specific proof. Those people can't close a deal, she says, but they can kill it [S6].

### Aim at the customers who care most

Best-fit customers are the ones who care most about the value only you deliver [S4]. She warns against defining them broadly out of hope [S4][S9]. If the segment where you truly win is too small to sustain the business, that is a signal to rethink the strategy, not to stretch the positioning [S4].

How she applied it: she suggests studying why you win more than why you lose, and getting marketing and product people onto sales calls so they hear what winning customers say [S4].

### The "no differentiation" illusion

When a team says its product has no differentiation, Dunford says that is usually wrong [S4]. Either the product truly offers nothing more than the alternatives, which positioning can't fix, or some people on the team don't see the product from the customer's side. The second case is far more common, especially for products that already have revenue [S4].

How she applied it: she described one company with $80 million in revenue, growing 35% a year, whose leaders still said the product was impossible to sell [S4].

### Turn a rival's strength into a weakness

Value changes with the buyer. A capability that wins one buyer can put off another [S5]. So a bigger rival's strength can become a weakness for the right segment [S5]. Market leaders feel safe but often serve specialist segments badly. Platforms are simple to buy but can be slow and risky to roll out. Free tools spread easily but often lack support and controls managers need [S5].

How she applied it: she cites Salesforce entering the small-business market that Siebel ignored, and HubSpot growing from the bottom up against bigger marketing software firms [S5].

### Do the prep before the workshop

In 2026 she set out decisions to make before a positioning exercise [S7]. Check the timing: positioning before launch is only a thesis to test with the market [S7]. Agree that you are positioning for customers, not investors or staff. Decide the scope: one product, a suite or the whole company. And write for the deal champion, the person who builds the shortlist and recommends to the buyer [S7].

Her 2019 post adds that positioning cannot be a one-and-done exercise: write it down with a date, test it with customers and revisit it when the market moves [S16]. Early customers usually show which assumptions were wrong, so keep the first version loose and test one well-defined segment at a time [S15].

How she applied it: the updated *Obviously Awesome* is organised into prep work, five steps matching the five components, and the work that follows, including turning positioning into a sales pitch and messaging [S7][S10].

### The pitch follows the positioning

Positioning is the base for messaging, lead generation and sales [S1]. *Sales Pitch* lays out an eight-step structure that helps sales reps tell a story about the product instead of walking through features [S3].

How she applied it: in one case, a challenger facing an unstable incumbent built its pitch around never slowing the team down, backed by uptime numbers and a guaranteed one-hour support response [S6].

### When markets shift, take a point of view

In May 2026 she said AI was changing products, competitors and markets all at once, faster than any shift since COVID [S8]. Buyers are confused, so vendors need a clear view of where things are heading [S8]. She warned against hype that outruns what the product does today, and against ignoring the future, which makes a company look out of date [S8]. Even so, she said the core principles of positioning don't change [S8]. In June 2026 she added that a point of view about where the market is heading is separate from product vision, because customers pay only for today's product, and it should rest on what you do better than anyone [S14].

How she applied it: she advised positioning against the alternatives prospects consider now, while building the product to beat likely future rivals [S8].

## How she decides

- Ask what the buyer would use if you didn't exist, including doing nothing [S4][S9].
- Count only alternatives that show up on real shortlists [S4].
- Map each unique attribute to value a buyer would choose you for [S4].
- Sort value from objection handling [S6].
- Study wins more closely than losses [S4].
- Target the customers who care most about your unique value [S4].
- Bring marketing, sales, product, customer success and the founder into one room [S4][S9].
- Treat pre-launch positioning as a thesis to test [S7].

## Real decisions

### Building her own method

After a startup relaunch worked and the company was bought, Dunford was handed several failing products and told to repeat the result [S15]. She found that most senior marketers she asked had simply tried things until one worked [S15]. A positioning course she took gave her a fill-in-the-blank statement but no way to choose the answers, and the instructor told her to trust her gut when she asked how to pick a category [S15]. She rejected that and built her own steps, which became the five components she teaches [S15][S16].

### Moving a database tool to business intelligence

She tells of a startup run by database experts whose tool analysed very large datasets fast [S16]. Buyers compared it with Oracle and saw a weak database [S16]. The team moved it to a business intelligence category and aimed at banks, security firms and ad-tech companies with huge volumes of machine data [S16]. Her stated reason was that the new frame made the value obvious to the buyers who cared most [S16].

### Choosing the best rep to test a pitch

When a team rebuilds its pitch, she does not ask the whole sales team for opinions, because that tends to breed resistance [S11]. She trains the strongest rep who won with the old pitch [S11]. That rep gives the new pitch to qualified prospects while she watches, and they review what worked and what confused people [S11]. They repeat until the rep says the new pitch beats the old one, and only then do the recordings train the rest of the team [S11].

### Telling buyers the difference between a real and a stretched claim

In a case from 2025, a client sold a truly integrated platform while a rival, built from acquisitions, also called itself a platform [S12]. Buyers assumed the two were equal [S12]. The client moved its message to what integration gives you, such as shared data, better reporting and easier automation [S12]. In a second case, interviews with customers who had switched showed a rival's "real-time" feature was too hard to set up to be used, so the client told buyers to ask every vendor what setup involves, and the rival dropped the claim [S12].

### Answering a "number one" claim with numbers

A market leader with about ten times the active customers of its nearest rival faced a well-funded challenger calling itself number one [S12]. The leader did not argue. It published its exact deployment numbers and added analyst reports, review counts and case studies [S12]. The challenger later changed its homepage [S12]. Her point is that real strengths should keep being promoted, and buyers should be taught to ask for proof [S12].

### Declining a podcast sponsor and ending a course

Dunford ran a 26-episode podcast season and a sponsor offered a small payment [S15]. She turned it down because the money did not justify the work [S15]. The show brought little new business, but clients' teams listened before workshops, so she kept it for that use and planned a second season with more guests [S15]. She also sold an online course with a partner, judged that one workshop could earn as much as the course did in a year, and planned to wind it down [S15].

### Pricing and narrowing her focus

She first priced workshops low, comparing herself with small agencies [S15]. When a prospect accepted $50,000 without negotiating, after she had expected to quote $15,000, she saw she had been underselling [S15]. She now publishes one price [S15]. She also chose to work alone, narrow to B2B tech companies with sales teams, list what she does not do, and drop Twitter, Instagram and TikTok because her buyers were elsewhere [S15]. She says the narrower focus drove her growth [S15].

### Keeping the position through AI changes

In 2026 founders at a roundtable she hosted worried that each new model release would date their positioning [S13]. She told them to rerun three steps rather than start over: have the alternatives changed, have the unique capabilities changed, and has the value changed [S13]. Usually the answer is no, because new capabilities tend to back a value theme the company already owns [S13]. If a release creates value the company could not deliver before, the positioning must be reworked [S13].

## Where she stood

**Can positioning fix a weak product?** No. It can only make real value obvious [S4].

**Who is the competition?** Whoever the prospect actually weighs, including the status quo [S9].

**Should you position for investors?** Her method is built for customers, as of 2026 [S7].

**Is great support a reason to buy?** Usually not. It handles objections and helps renewals [S6].

**Has AI changed positioning?** The details and pace, yes. The principles, no, as of 2026 [S8].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

We found no major public criticism of her work in the sources we read. Some fair limits:

- **Built for business technology.** Her practice and examples come mostly from B2B tech companies [S1][S2]. Consumer brands, shops and services may need to adapt the method.
- **She changed her own method.** She said the first edition's ten steps didn't line up with its five components, and fixed it in 2026 [S7][S10]. Older summaries of her process may be out of date.
- **Needs real sales data.** Win analysis and shortlist alternatives depend on having deals to study [S4]. A new company may have to treat positioning as a guess until it does [S7].
- **Paywalled detail.** Parts of her newer guidance are behind paywalls, so summaries can miss steps [S9].

## Blind spots to watch

- She focuses on what to say about the product. Pair her with Rand Fishkin or Neil Patel on where to say it.
- Her method assumes a team with sales, product and marketing. A solo founder may need to play every role.
- Brand feeling and creative ideas get less space. Pair her with George Lois or Donald Miller for the story.
- Consumer buyers often don't build a formal shortlist. Check how your buyers really choose.
- Views on AI are from 2026 and will date.
