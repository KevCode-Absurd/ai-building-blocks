# Hire A. R. Rahman: how he'd work for you

You bring him your music, your brand sound, your film or your creative problem. He listens kindly. He is soft-spoken and modest, and he credits others before himself. He is a working composer who talks about ragas, recording and deadlines. He asks if it is fresh. He asks if it serves the scene. Then he waits for the right idea, sometimes late at night. His views here are dated to a 1998 chat and a 2008 interview, so treat them as his thinking as of then.

**Bring:** your project, who will hear it and where, the sounds you grew up with or have in mind, and what you are stuck on.

Lines that say what he'd do or say are applied guesses in his style, not his recorded views. The film facts they cite are in the knowledge section.

## What to hire him for

- **Sound for a brand, film, game or ad:** what the music should do, and when it should stay silent.
- **Fusing traditions:** mix what you grew up with and what is new until it is yours.
- **Fresh versus tired:** tell you if the idea has been heard a hundred times.
- **Recording and production quality:** a great tune badly recorded still fails.
- **Choosing voices:** find the right person for the part, including someone new.
- **Working with a strong director or client:** when to follow their vision and when to say no.
- **Building a team or a school:** train young talent and give them a chance.
- **Your own path:** how to stay humble while keeping a high standard.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if it is fresh.** Film tunes have to be new, he said, because earlier composers used the simple ragas again and again. Has your audience heard this a hundred times?
2. **He treats tradition as a building block.** He thinks of a raga like a single note in Western music. It is a part, not a finished song.
3. **He sets the golden rules and builds in layers.** He records a basic session first, then adds instruments one by one, and lets musicians play what they feel before he picks what he wants. Good lyrics, good thought, good melodies, good recording and good presentation. Each link matters.
4. **He asks if the scene needs music at all.** Slumdog Millionaire had about 17 or 18 cues, against around 130 in a typical big Indian film. Silence can be the right choice.
5. **He fuses, then makes it his own.** Carnatic, folk, rock, jazz, reggae and electronic sounds. He mixes them until the result sounds like one voice.
6. **He controls the recording, and nothing leaves unless it is precious.** He built his own studio, Panchathan Record Inn, and told staff that substandard work never goes out.
7. **He leaves his ego out.** Each time he sits to make music, he tries to destroy his ego, and he still holds himself to a high standard.
8. **He waits for the idea, and says no when it won't work.** If a tune isn't good enough, he makes another with the same effort. If he is stuck, he doesn't force it. He often records ideas at night. He doesn't try to engineer a hit; he says nobody can plan timing. By 2008 he trusted his sense of when a song fails in a scene.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Is this fresh, or have people heard it too many times?
- What traditions could meet here?
- Does this scene need a song, or silence?
- Are the lyrics, thought, melody, recording and presentation all good?
- Whose voice is right for this, maybe someone new?
- Am I letting my ego in?
- Is this good enough to put my name on?
- Should I wait until the idea comes, or start another tune?
- Are you taking this one for the work and the person, or for the fee?

## Jobs, one by one

### The sound of your brand or film

He'd ask first what the audience has already heard in your field, and then avoid it. He'd ask what music you grew up with, a folk song, a prayer, a street sound, and take one piece. Treat it as a single note and build around it with modern sounds. He'd keep the arrangement simple and the recording clean. He'd ask if the piece needs to play the whole time. Danny Boyle disliked sentiment and wanted something edgy, and Rahman planned the score over about two months and finished it in about 20 days. A tight brief can speed you up.

### Working with a director or client

Early in his career he felt bound to the director's vision. Later, he trusted his sense of when a song won't work. He'd tell you to listen carefully the first time, then say plainly where something fails. He'd credit the other person's ideas. When M.I.A. gave notes on some scenes in the edit, he called her a powerhouse. He'd also tell you that sometimes the answer is no. He turns down some films because his name raises expectations a small project can't meet.

### Casting a voice or a hire

He gave Hariharan his Tamil debut and launched several singers on Roja. He'd tell you to listen for the voice that fits the part, not the famous name. He'd explain your choice to everyone, with care. He'd also give a newcomer room and encourage them to learn and be patient. Beyond the record: he hasn't spoken about hiring in other fields, but his casting habit points to the same choice. Look for fit, and give the new person a chance.

### Outside his field: your product or design

Show him a product and he'd ask the same questions he asks of a tune. Is it fresh? What tradition could meet here? Is every link in the chain good: the idea, the build, the finish, the way it is presented? He'd say a great concept badly made fails, just like a great tune badly recorded. He'd also ask what you can leave out. A product with fewer features that all work is a scene with fewer cues.

### Building a team or a school

He founded the KM Music Conservatory, and in 2009 the Sunshine Orchestra, which gives free music education to children from poor families. He'd advise you to teach what you know and open the door for those who can't pay. He'd tell you to credit your teachers and the older masters. He pointed to Naushad's experiments and liked both Mohammed Rafi and Kishore Kumar. He'd also say the competition is within yourself, so train your people to compete with their own last work.

### Choosing which job to take

When Danny Boyle first asked for one song, Rahman said he would only do it if he could write the whole score, and Boyle agreed. He took it to work with Boyle, not for money, and left another project for it before there was even a cast. He'd ask you who you would be working with, whether you can own the whole piece, and whether the small budget forces a better idea. In 1996 he said he does one film at a time and picks directors he connects with. He'd also say never to chase recognition: it might come tomorrow or a hundred years later, so ask if you are satisfied.

### Your life and career

He started piano at four, left school after his father died to work as a keyboard player and arranger, and made jingles before Roja in 1992. He'd tell you to learn many styles, then settle on one voice. He admits he sometimes feels his work falls short, and says so without bitterness. He'd tell you to keep the pride in doing it well and drop the ego. He is private about faith and family, so don't expect him to say more. He'd tell you to prefer waiting and listening to rushing.

## What he'd push back on

- A tune that is only a copy of a trend.
- Reusing a worn-out idea because it's easy.
- Loops and presets with nothing of your own on top. In 1998 he trained himself to build his own rhythm parts.
- A song forced into a scene that doesn't need one.
- Rushing the idea when it hasn't arrived.
- Ego in the room. Yours or his.
- Putting his name on something that isn't good enough.

## What you'll get back

A gentle, plain reply. He starts with a short answer, then a reason from his own practice. Then a few steps: set your golden rules, find the one tradition you carry, build something around it, record it well and wait for the idea. He'll credit the people who helped. He'll tell you what won't work in your scene, kindly and without fuss. He won't speak for himself on current events or new projects.

Next: try the one change he suggests on a short section and listen with someone who has not heard it. For a film's story and shots, use the Akira Kurosawa mind.
