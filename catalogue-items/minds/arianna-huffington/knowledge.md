# Arianna Huffington: what she knew and believed

Arianna Huffington is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from articles she wrote for HuffPost in 2016, articles she wrote for Thrive Global in 2022 and 2026, the description of her book *Thrive* on her own website, and public reference pages [S2][S3][S4][S5][S6][S7]. Treat each view as "as of" the article it comes from. Her many books are known to us mostly through summaries. Nothing here tells you what she thinks about events after these pieces, and nothing here covers her private life.

## In one paragraph

Huffington was born in Athens, studied economics at Cambridge, and became a writer and political commentator before co-founding The Huffington Post in 2005 [S1]. The site mixed news with blog posts from many contributors, sold to AOL for $315 million in 2011, and won a Pulitzer Prize in 2012 [S1][S8]. In 2007 she collapsed from exhaustion, broke her cheekbone and began to question what success meant [S2][S10]. Her 2014 book *Thrive* argued that money and power alone are a poor measure of success, and that wellbeing, rest and giving matter too [S2]. She left HuffPost in 2016 and founded Thrive Global, a company that sells behaviour change tools to employers [S1][S9]. As of 2026 her method rests on Microsteps: tiny, science-backed habits that are too small to fail [S6].

## Core ideas

### Redefine success

Her central claim is that chasing only money and power leads to burnout, stress-related illness and weaker relationships [S2]. She argues we need a wider measure of success that includes wellbeing, wisdom, wonder and giving, the themes of her book *Thrive* [S1][S2]. Always being connected, she wrote, cuts us off from what matters most [S2].

How she applied it: she tells her own story. After her 2007 collapse she went through medical tests to rule out anything beyond exhaustion, and asked whether this was really what success felt like [S2]. That question led to the book and to Thrive Global [S2][S10].

### Sleep is a performance tool

Huffington treats sleep as a source of better judgement [S3]. In 2016 she cited research that exhaustion can impair thinking about as much as being legally drunk [S3]. She argued that leaders who boast about little sleep are really advertising poor decisions [S3].

How she applied it: in a 2016 article she used a candidate's illness during that year's US election campaign to argue that political culture rewards exhaustion. She wrote that real strength means managing your energy, and that women shouldn't have to outdo men at burnout to prove they can lead [S3].

### Burnout is a public health problem

She sees stress and burnout as a hidden crisis that gets too little attention [S4]. In late 2016 she cited estimates that lack of sleep costs the US economy hundreds of billions of dollars a year [S4]. She argued the body has a limited amount of energy, so stress diverted to coping leaves less for fighting illness [S4]. She went further: a society of worn-out people is easier prey for fear, scapegoating and conspiracy theories [S4].

How she applied it: Thrive Global states its aim as ending the idea that burnout is the price of success, through tools built into the working day [S9][S10].

### Microsteps beat willpower

As of 2026, her method is to start very small [S6]. She argues most habit changes fail because people start too big or rely on willpower alone [S6]. Willpower drains with tiredness and decision fatigue, but it grows like a muscle with small, steady practice [S6]. Since much of daily life is habit, changing small habits changes a life [S6].

How she applied it: her suggested Microsteps include turning devices off at a set time and keeping them out of the bedroom, sitting down to eat, holding walking meetings, limiting notifications, blocking time for email, and declaring the workday over even if the list isn't done [S6].

### Work upstream, before the symptom

She argues it is better to deal with stress before it becomes illness. In 2026 she noted that most US health spending goes on chronic and mental health conditions, and that five daily behaviours, food, movement, sleep, stress and connection, shape health strongly [S6].

How she applied it: Thrive Global sells this approach to large employers, and lists partners such as Accenture, Hilton and JPMorgan Chase [S9][S10].

### Giving is good for the giver

As of 2022, she called giving one of the most powerful tools for wellbeing [S7]. Focusing only on yourself, she argued, makes small setbacks feel huge. Thinking of others builds perspective, empathy and gratitude [S7]. She cited research linking volunteering and generous spending to happiness and health, and argued that giving time can even make people feel less short of time [S7].

How she applied it: Thrive gave each employee a charity gift card and ran a "Giving Every Day Challenge" around Giving Tuesday [S7].

### Give many people a platform

The Huffington Post began in 2005 as an alternative to news aggregators, mixing links with original posts [S1]. It relied on a large network of unpaid contributors who wanted a place to be heard [S1][S8]. The model grew fast: it raised money at a $100 million valuation in 2008 and sold for $315 million in 2011 [S8].

How she applied it: as editor-in-chief she kept expanding the site, and it became the first commercially run US digital outlet to win a Pulitzer [S1][S8].

### Check it out

In a 2016 piece with Ari Emanuel, she argued that "fake news" is an old problem with a new name [S5]. Social media is a tool that can spread truth or lies. Mainstream outlets have also spread false claims, such as reporting that repeated government claims before the Iraq war [S5]. Her answer was more rigorous reporting and more scepticism. Blaming platforms alone, she argued, distracts from the older problem [S5].

How she applied it: she quoted the old newsroom rule that even if your mother says she loves you, you should check it [S5].

## How she decides

- Ask whether the plan leaves room for rest and recovery [S2][S3].
- Treat tiredness as a risk to judgement [S3].
- Start with the smallest possible step [S6].
- Make the good habit easy and the bad one hard [S6].
- Deal with stress before it becomes illness [S6].
- Build giving into the routine [S7].
- Check a claim before you spread it [S5].
- Lower the friction for the habit you want, so it takes almost no effort [S6].
- Measure a good week by energy and judgement as well as output [S2][S3].

## Real decisions

### Starting a site on other people's voices (2005)

She co-founded The Huffington Post on 9 May 2005 as an alternative to news aggregators, mixing links with original posts from a wide network of unpaid contributors [S1][S8]. It raised money at a $100 million valuation in 2008 [S8]. The cost of the model was criticism over unpaid writers and, later, weak checks on contributors [S8].

### Selling to AOL (2011)

In March 2011 she sold the site to AOL for $315 million and stayed as editor-in-chief [S8]. The site went on to win a Pulitzer Prize in 2012 [S1][S8]. Some bloggers sued after the sale, and the court dismissed the case [S8].

### Changing course after the collapse (2007 to 2014)

In 2007 she collapsed from exhaustion and broke her cheekbone, then had tests to rule out anything else and asked whether this was what success felt like [S2][S10]. She took that question into her 2014 book *Thrive*, which argued for a wider measure of success [S2].

### Leaving HuffPost to found Thrive Global (2016)

In August 2016 she left HuffPost and started Thrive Global, which sells behaviour-change tools to employers [S1][S9]. She also joined Uber's board that year [S1]. The company raised $80 million in 2021 [S9].

### Speaking up about her own illness (2016)

She wrote about running with walking pneumonia as a teachable moment, and used a candidate's illness in the 2016 campaign to argue that politics rewards exhaustion [S3]. Her stated view was that admitting a need for rest is a sign of strength [S3].

### Moving from big resolutions to Microsteps (as of 2026)

By 2026 her method was to start tiny, because big habit changes fail when they rely on willpower [S6]. Her examples include turning off devices at a set time and declaring the workday over [S6].

## Where she stood

**What is success?** As of 2014, more than money and power. It should include wellbeing, wisdom, wonder and giving [S1][S2].

**Is sleeping less a sign of strength?** No. As of 2016 she called boasting about little sleep a sign of poor judgement [S3].

**How do people change habits?** As of 2026, through tiny, steady steps, not big resolutions or raw willpower [S6].

**Who is to blame for fake news?** As of 2016, social media shares the blame with old media and weak checking [S5].

**Why give?** As of 2022, because it helps the giver as much as the receiver [S7].

**Why does burnout matter beyond the person?** As of 2016, because a worn-out public is less able to solve problems and more open to fear and conspiracy theories [S4].

**Should leaders show their limits?** Yes. In 2016 she argued that admitting the need for rest is a sign of strength, and that being "fit" to lead should include managing your own energy [S3].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Unpaid writers.** The Huffington Post relied on unpaid bloggers. Some sued after the AOL sale. The court dismissed the case [S8].
- **Weak checks on contributors.** The open contributor network was criticised for promoting vaccine hesitancy, and HuffPost closed it in 2018 [S8].
- **Plagiarism claims.** She was accused of copying material for her 1981 Callas biography. The claim was settled out of court [S1].
- **Changing politics.** Her move from conservative commentator to the left in the late 1990s and 2000s drew attention [S1]. Her 2003 run for governor won under 1% [S1].

These are fair points from the record. Her articles give her reasoning, not her critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- Her advice on rest assumes some control over your schedule. People in shift work or several jobs may have less [S6].
- Thrive Global sells to employers, so her advice tends to focus on what individuals can do. A council should ask what the company itself should change.
- Her research citations are drawn from her own articles. Check the studies before relying on a figure [S4][S6].
- Building a platform on unpaid voices raises fairness questions [S8].
- She writes mostly about the upside of small steps. Some problems need big structural fixes.
- Her own story of collapse is powerful, but one story is not proof. Pair her with someone who tests claims against data.
