# Aristotle: what he knew and believed

A note on method: Aristotle may have written around 200 works, and about 31 survive [S1]. The polished books he wrote for the public, including dialogues, are almost all lost [S1][S5]. What we have reads like lecture notes, drafts and research files for his own students [S1]. Many were edited by later followers. His logic books, for example, were gathered into one set around 40 BC [S5]. The *Nicomachean Ethics* seems to be lecture notes, and three of its books are the same as three books of his other ethics, the *Eudemian Ethics* [S11]. The *Rhetoric* is also a set of lecture notes, built up over many years, and its third book may have been joined to the first two later [S3][S10]. So the texts can be terse, and some arguments seem to jump. We used W. D. Ross's translation of the *Nicomachean Ethics*, first published in 1908 [S9], and W. Rhys Roberts's translation of the *Rhetoric*, published in 1924 [S10]. Quotes from them are short.

## In one paragraph

Aristotle was a Greek thinker who studied for twenty years in Plato's Academy, tutored the young Alexander of Macedon, and then founded his own school in Athens, the Lyceum [S1]. He worked in almost every field: logic, nature, animals, ethics, politics, speech and poetry [S1][S5]. He built the first formal system of logic [S1]. In ethics he argued that the goal of life is to live well, that a virtue is a settled habit of hitting the right middle between too much and too little, and that we become good by doing good things [S2][S7]. In the *Rhetoric* he set out how speakers persuade, through their character, the feelings of the audience and the argument itself [S8]. Medieval Muslim scholars called him "the First Teacher," and Thomas Aquinas called him simply "the Philosopher" [S5].

## Core ideas

### Start from what appears and what people believe

Aristotle began an inquiry by setting out how things appear and what respected people believe about them. Then he worked through the puzzles those views raise [S1]. He used this kind of reasoning from accepted opinions, called dialectic, to work towards first principles [S1].

How he applied it: he spent about two years studying the sea life of Lesbos, especially the Pyrrha lagoon [S5]. He described how one arm of the male octopus is used in mating, which people doubted until the 19th century [S5]. In ethics, he set out the views of others, including his teacher Plato, before giving his own. He said that as a lover of wisdom he had to honour truth above his friends [S6].

### Everything aims at some good

The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens by saying that every skill, inquiry and action aims at some good [S6]. Some goods are wanted for the sake of others. The highest good, wanted for its own sake, is *eudaimonia*, usually translated as happiness or living well [S2].

How he applied it: he asked what the function of a human being is, the way a craftsman has a function. His answer was the activity of the reasoning soul, done well [S2][S6]. So happiness is a way of acting over a whole life, more than a passing feeling. As he put it, one swallow does not make a summer [S6].

### Explain by four causes

To explain a thing fully, Aristotle asked four questions: what it is made of, what form or shape it has, what brought it about, and what it is for [S1]. He held that natural things have purposes built into them without anyone designing them, a view that is still argued over [S1].

How he applied it: his work on animals keeps asking what each part is for, as well as what it is made of [S1][S5].

### Logic: what follows from what

Aristotle built the first system for testing whether a conclusion follows from its premises, known as the syllogism [S1]. He also studied arguments about what must be and what might be [S1].

How he applied it: in speeches he used a looser version, the enthymeme, which he called a rhetorical syllogism [S8]. It starts from beliefs the audience already holds and leaves out steps, because a crowd can't follow a long chain of reasoning [S3].

### Three means of persuasion

Aristotle defined rhetoric as the ability to see the means of persuasion available in each case [S8]. He compared it to medicine. A doctor can know the craft and still fail to cure every patient [S3]. There are three means [S3][S8]:

- **Character (ethos):** the speaker must come across as sensible, good and well-meaning, through the speech itself. He said character may be the speaker's most effective means of persuasion [S8].
- **Feeling (pathos):** people judge differently when they are angry or calm, afraid or confident [S3].
- **Argument (logos):** proof, or what seems to be proof, by example or by enthymeme [S8].

How he applied it: Book II of the *Rhetoric* studies the emotions one by one, including anger, fear, shame, pity, envy and gratitude, and how young, old, rich and powerful audiences tend to think [S3].

### Know which kind of speech you're making

He sorted speeches by time and goal [S8]. A political speech looks to the future and asks what will help or harm. A courtroom speech looks to the past and asks what was just or unjust. A ceremonial speech praises or blames, mostly about the present [S3][S8].

How he applied it: for political speech, he listed the subjects an adviser must know: money, war and peace, defence, imports and exports, and laws [S8]. Like Socrates, he wanted advisers to know the facts.

### Virtue is a middle, set by the case

A virtue is a settled state of character that involves choice and lies in a middle point [S7]. Courage sits between cowardice and rashness. Generosity sits between waste and meanness [S7]. The middle is relative to us, so it isn't an average [S2][S7]. The right amount of food differs for the wrestler Milo and for a beginner [S7]. There's no formula. The middle is what a person of practical wisdom would choose [S2].

How he applied it: he gave three practical tips [S7]. Move away first from the extreme that is worse. Pull yourself towards the opposite side, the way you straighten a bent stick. And be most on guard against pleasure [S7]. He added that anyone can get angry, but being angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, is hard [S7].

### We become good by practice

Moral virtue comes from habit [S7]. We become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts, the way we learn a craft by practising it [S7]. Habit gives a rough start. Full virtue also needs practical wisdom, the skill of seeing what each situation calls for, and that only grows with experience [S2].

How he applied it: he said a young person is not a fit student of politics, because they lack experience, and because the aim of the study is action, not knowledge [S6].

### Humans are political animals

Aristotle held that people are by nature political animals who want to live together [S4][S5]. The city comes into being so that people can live, but it exists so that they can live well [S4]. He sorted governments by whether one, a few or many rule, and by whether they rule for everyone or only for themselves [S4].

How he applied it: for real cities that fall short of the ideal, he recommended a large middle class between rich and poor, as the most stable and least split by faction [S4]. He also argued that many people judging together can sometimes judge better than a few experts [S4].

## Real decisions

### Deliberate about means, not ends
In Book III he separates what we weigh up from what we take as given. A doctor does not deliberate whether to heal, nor an orator whether to persuade. They fix the goal and then search for the means, preferring the easiest and most creditable, and working back until they reach the first thing they must do. The last step of the analysis is the first step of the doing. If the search hits an impossibility, such as needing money you cannot get, they drop the plan. For large matters we bring in advisers, since we distrust our judgement alone. [S12]

### Do not deliberate about everything
He also says we do not deliberate about eternal things, or about small facts of sense, such as whether this is bread or is cooked, because endless deliberation never ends. Deliberation fits matters that follow general rules but have uncertain results in a given case. After deliberation, the option preferred is what he calls moral choice. [S12]

### Put friendship inside the study of how to live
In Books VIII and IX he stops the argument to treat friendship, saying nobody would choose to live without friends even with every other good. He gives practical reasons: the rich need friends to use and keep their good fortune, the poor and unlucky need a refuge, the young are kept from error, the old are helped, and people in their prime act better in pairs. He adds that rulers care about friendship more than about justice, and that where people are friends, little justice is needed. [S12]

### Test the claim against what is said
In Book I he sets out the leading opinions on what the good life is (pleasure, honour, wealth) and weighs each against what people actually value before giving his own account, which he ties to doing things well over a whole life, not a moment. [S6]

### Start an ethics lecture from how to live
He opens the *Ethics* by asking what the good is at which all action aims, and settles for the level of precision the subject allows. He then says the aim of the study is action, not knowledge. [S6][S12]

## How he decides

- Ask first what the thing is for. Judge each part by that end [S2][S6].
- Gather the main opinions and the puzzles before giving your own view [S1].
- Expect only as much precision as the subject allows. Ethics holds true "for the most part" [S2][S6].
- Look for the middle, as it applies to this person and this case [S7].
- Lean away from the extreme you're most tempted by [S7].
- Know the facts of the matter before you advise [S8].
- Fit the argument to what the audience already believes [S3].

## Where he stood

**What is happiness?** Living and acting well, in line with virtue, over a full life. Friends, health and some wealth help, and luck plays a part [S2].

**Is pleasure the highest good?** No. Pleasure comes with activity and completes it, but pleasures must be ranked by the worth of the activity [S2].

**What makes a friendship?** There are three kinds: friendship for use, for pleasure, and between good people who wish each other well for their own sake. The last kind is best and rare [S2].

**Can we know the good and still do wrong?** Yes, partly. Against Socrates, he held that strong feeling can weaken or bypass reason, though the weak-willed person grasps the rule only partly at the moment of acting [S2].

**Is rhetoric just a trick?** No. Truth and justice tend to win, so if a jury gets it wrong, the speakers are to blame [S8]. It's odd to be ashamed of being unable to defend yourself with your body but not with speech [S8].

**What is the best life?** In Book X, the life of study, since it is most self-sufficient. A life of public action comes second [S2].

## His work and record

- Born 384 BC in Stagira, in northern Greece. His father, Nicomachus, was a doctor to the king of Macedon [S1][S5].
- Joined Plato's Academy in Athens at about seventeen and stayed until Plato died in 347 BC [S1].
- Spent about three years at Assos and about two on Lesbos [S1].
- From 343 BC, tutored the young Alexander, probably for two or three years [S1].
- In 335 BC, returned to Athens and founded the Lyceum. His followers were called Peripatetics, probably after a covered walkway at the school [S1][S5].
- Left Athens in 323 BC, after Alexander's death, as feeling against Macedon grew [S1].
- Died in 322 BC at Chalcis, on the island of Euboea [S1].
- Main surviving works include the logic books later called the *Organon*, the *Physics*, works on animals, the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the *Politics*, the *Rhetoric* and the *Poetics* [S1][S5].
- His *Rhetoric* is still called by many the most important single work on persuasion [S10].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Slavery.** He defended the rule of masters over people he called natural slaves, whom he said lacked the power to deliberate. He gave no evidence for this [S4].
- **Women.** He said women can deliberate, but that their judgement lacks authority, so they need men's rule. Modern readers find this unconvincing [S4].
- **Physics.** He held that heavier objects fall faster. Later thinkers, including Galileo, challenged this [S5]. He also placed thought in the heart, not the brain [S5].
- **Harsh verdicts.** Bertrand Russell said almost every serious advance in thought began with an attack on Aristotle [S5]. The biologist Peter Medawar dismissed much of his science as hearsay [S5]. Modern observers have also confirmed several of his more surprising claims about animals [S5].
- **The middle.** Critics say the doctrine of the mean fits choices about amount, such as how much to spend, better than choices between two duties [S2].
- **Greeks first.** He advised Alexander to lead the Greeks but rule other peoples as a despot [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- He sorts and classifies. That can make a messy problem look tidier than it is.
- He trusts the views of respected, experienced people. That can bake in the bias of the group he respects.
- His social views came from a slave-owning society that excluded women. Leave them out of modern advice.
- "Find the middle" can become an excuse for a weak compromise. The middle is the right answer for the case, and sometimes that is an extreme.
- He is strong on structure and weaker on doubt. Pair him with Socrates, who keeps asking if we really know.
