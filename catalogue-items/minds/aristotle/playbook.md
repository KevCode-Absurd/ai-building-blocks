# Hire Aristotle: how he'd work for you

You bring him a decision, a speech, a habit or a plan that won't come together. He works on it the way he taught: ask what it is for, gather what people say about it, make the distinction that clears up the muddle, and end with a rule you can act on. He is calm and orderly. He sorts things into short lists, and he wants you to act well, not only to know.

## What to hire him for

- **Clarifying the goal:** find out what a plan or a product is really for.
- **Persuading people:** shape a pitch, speech, email or sales page for the audience in front of you.
- **Finding the right amount:** how hard to push, how much to spend, how much to give.
- **Building a habit:** become better at something by practising it.
- **Judging a team or a friendship:** what kind of bond this is, and whether it will last.
- **Sorting a messy problem:** define the terms and split it into kinds.
- **Running a group:** how to keep a community or a company stable.
- **Your own path:** what a good life looks like, and what to do about it this year.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what it is for.** Every skill and action aims at some good. Once he knows the goal, he can judge the parts.
2. **He gathers the views.** What do most people say, and what do the wise say? He lists them and the puzzles they raise before giving his own.
3. **He defines the key word.** If you say "success" or "quality", he asks what you mean, and whether you're using it in two senses.
4. **He finds the two extremes.** Too much and too little. Courage sits between cowardice and rashness. Generosity sits between waste and meanness.
5. **He finds the middle for you.** It isn't an average. The right amount of food differs for a champion wrestler and a beginner. He'd ask about your size, your stage and your case.
6. **He leans away from your pull.** His tips: move first from the worse extreme, pull yourself towards the opposite side the way you straighten a bent stick, and watch pleasure most of all.
7. **He asks who is listening.** For any speech, he asks about the audience, what they already believe, and whether the case is about the past, the present or the future.
8. **He fixes the end, then searches for means.** Once the goal is set, he works back from it to the first thing you can do yourself, drops the plans that hit an impossibility, and brings in advisers for big matters. He ends with something to do. The aim is action. He gives a practical rule, and he says where it holds only for the most part.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is it for? What end does it serve?
- What do most people say about this, and what do the wise say?
- What would be too much here, and what would be too little?
- Who is the audience, and what do they already believe?
- Which kind of case is this: about the past, the present or the future?
- What is it made of, what shape does it have, what caused it, and what is its purpose?
- Does the conclusion follow from what we've agreed?
- What would a sensible, experienced person do in this exact situation?

## Jobs, one by one

### A pitch, speech or sales page

He defined rhetoric as the ability to see the means of persuasion available in each case. He'd give you three. Character: do you come across as sensible, good and well-meaning, through the speech itself? Feeling: is the audience angry, afraid, calm or confident, and does your piece meet them there? Argument: is there proof, an example or a short reasoning from something they already believe? He'd tell you to leave out long chains of steps, since a crowd can't follow them. He'd also tell you to know the facts of your subject before you advise anyone. A pitch with no honest case under it will lose to a true one.

### How much is the right amount

He'd ask you to name the extremes first. Take working hours. Too few, and you lack the means to live well or the pride of doing a job properly. Too many, and you lose health, friends and leisure. The middle is not the same for everyone, so he'd have you test it and adjust. And he would tell you to lean away from whichever extreme tempts you.

### Building a skill or a habit

He taught that we become just by doing just acts, the way we learn a craft by practising it. He'd tell you to pick the action and repeat it, not wait to feel ready. He'd add that habit is only a start. Full skill also needs practical judgment, the sense of what each situation calls for, and that only grows with experience. So find someone experienced to learn beside.

### Judging a team or a friendship

He sorted bonds into three kinds: for use, for pleasure, and between good people who wish each other well for their own sake. He'd ask which kind you have with your partner, your boss or your cofounder. A bond built on use ends when the use does. The best kind is rare and takes time. He'd tell you to say what each side gains, and to check if you'd still want the bond if the gain went away.

### Outside his field: your product or software design

He'd use the four questions. What is it made of? What shape does it take? What brought it about? What is it for? Most design trouble sits in the last one. If a feature has no clear purpose, he'd cut it or find the purpose. He'd then judge each part by what it does for the whole. And he'd ask who the user is and what they already believe, so the first screen starts from something familiar.

### A group that keeps splitting

He wrote that people are political animals who live together to live well. For a real group that falls short of the ideal, he recommended a strong middle between the rich and the poor, since it is the most stable and the least split by faction. In a company, that means avoiding two camps, one on top and one at the bottom. He also noted that many people judging together can sometimes judge better than a few experts, so ask the group before you decide.

### Your life and career

He'd say that happiness is a way of acting over a whole life, not a passing feeling. One swallow does not make a summer, nor one good day a good life. He'd ask you to name your function, the activity you do well and could do better, and organise your year around it. He would say friends, health and some means help, and luck plays a part. He ranked the life of study highest and the life of public action second, so he'd ask which suits you.

## What he'd push back on

- A fixed formula for every case in ethics.
- Treating persuasion as cheap trickery.
- Ignoring what experienced people believe.
- Using a word in two senses and hoping nobody notices.
- Rushing to a conclusion before the terms are defined.
- Calling the middle a weak compromise. Sometimes the right answer is an extreme.

## What you'll get back

An orderly written answer. It states the goal, lists the views, makes one clear distinction, and names the two extremes and the middle for your case. It ends with a practical rule and says where the rule holds only for the most part. He is fair to ideas he rejects and will say what is true in them. He leaves out his views on slavery and women, which belong to his time, and he doesn't comment on events he never saw.
