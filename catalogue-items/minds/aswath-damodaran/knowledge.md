# Aswath Damodaran: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-21. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1]. He is living, so every view is dated to what he had published by October 2026 and may have changed since. His past valuations and trades are history, not advice.

## In one paragraph

Aswath Damodaran is a finance professor at NYU's Stern School of Business, where he has taught since 1986 [S1]. He is known for valuation: working out what a business is worth from the cash it can make, how fast it can grow and how risky it is [S1][S3]. He teaches full classes at NYU, opens them to the public each year, and gives his data and spreadsheets away online [S25]. His main teaching is that every valuation is a story backed by numbers, and every number should trace back to a sentence in the story [S15]. He keeps value (what the cash flows justify) apart from price (what buyers will pay today) [S2][S3]. He posts his valuations with dates, so his misses are public, and he names his own errors [S16][S25].

## Core ideas

### Value is cash, growth and risk

An asset is worth the cash it will pay out, adjusted for risk and for how long you wait [S3]. This is discounted cash flow, or DCF. He is open about its limits: nobody sees the "true" value, so a DCF is an act of faith that value is tied to cash flows, growth and risk [S3]. It has many inputs, so it is easy to bend toward the answer you want [S2]. Assets with no cash flows, such as gold or collectibles, can be priced but not valued [S2].

How he applied it: he teaches that if all the DCF variants use consistent assumptions they give the same value, so a fancy new model that promises more precision should make you suspicious [S3].

### Story first, then numbers

He says people split into storytellers and number crunchers who distrust each other, and he wants each to learn the other's skill [S15]. A story with no numbers is only talk. Numbers with no story are unmoored [S15]. In his chain, a one-sentence story becomes a market size, market growth, market share, the slice of revenue the company keeps, a mature operating margin, how much it must reinvest, a cost of capital that fades as the firm matures, and a separate chance of failure [S13]. Each number has to point back to a line of the story [S13].

How he applied it: for Uber in June 2014 he wrote the story as city-by-city car service with local network effects, then built the nine inputs from it [S13]. He also says big gaps between two valuations nearly always come from different stories, not from arguing over decimals [S13].

### Possible, plausible, probable

Each block of a story goes into one of three bins. Possible means it could happen, with no clear path. Plausible means there is a credible path. Probable means the evidence points that way [S12]. Value depends on where you draw the lines [S12]. For any new market he asks three things: is there a reason to switch, can users overcome habit and inertia, and will the old players fight back [S12].

How he applied it: on Uber in 2014 he judged taxi-type car service probable, mass transit and suburbs plausible, and car ownership only possible, which he priced as a small option [S12][S13].

### Price and value are different games

Price comes from supply, demand, mood and momentum. Value comes from cash flows, growth and risk [S2][S3]. The two games have different rules and different players. In the pricing game the aim is to guess where the price goes next. In the value game the aim is to buy below value and sell above it [S3]. People get hurt when they think they are playing one and are in the other [S2]. Relative valuation, which he calls pricing, is not worthless: it fits when there are many comparables and a short horizon. But a low multiple is not a bargain until you know why it is low, and it can hide weak assumptions [S2][S5].

How he applied it: in December 2025 he kept apart a stock that is highly priced and one that is overpriced, and tested the first by asking what revenue the price needs [S22]. For the Facebook IPO he set his own value beside the offer price instead of taking the offer as a fact [S9].

### Growth has to be earned

Growth adds value only if the money put in earns more than the cost of capital. If the return equals the cost of capital, growth leaves value unchanged. Below it, growth destroys value [S7]. Growth also has a cost: the business must reinvest to get it [S7][S8]. High growth is easy to buy. High quality growth is rare, and it is harder to keep as a company gets bigger [S7].

How he applied it: in his "Inferno" slides, a business making $120 million after tax stays worth $1,200 million whether it grows 0%, 2% or 3%, as long as its return on new investment equals its 10% cost [S7]. He also shows that a mature firm which chases growth at a poor return loses value, which he calls the cost of denial [S18].

### Terminal value has rules

The terminal value is the value at the end of the forecast, when the business is treated as stable. For young firms it can be 90% or more of today's value [S6]. His rules: stable growth can never beat the economy, and he uses the risk-free rate as the cap; reinvestment in the terminal year must match growth; and you do not close the model with a peer multiple, which he calls a Trojan Horse because it quietly turns a valuation into a pricing [S8].

How he applied it: his Amazon valuation in February 2000 had a stable growth rate of 6% on a 20% return on capital, so the business had to reinvest 30% of its operating income forever [S4][S8].

### The cost of capital comes from the market

He takes the big inputs from market data instead of guessing them. The equity risk premium (the extra return stocks are priced to earn over safe bonds) is worked out from today's index level [S20][S23]. Betas (a measure of how much a stock moves with the market) come from the business mix, not a noisy regression [S7]. He warns against adding small-company or illiquidity premiums on top, because they overlap and come from noisy history [S7]. He also does not want you to fuss over the last decimal. Most firms sit in a narrow band, so a rate far outside it goes on his suspect list [S21].

How he applied it: he dropped the old habit of treating the US Treasury yield as default-free after Moody's cut the US rating in April 2025, and now subtracts a small default spread [S20].

### Young firms: a few inputs and a chance of failure

A young firm has no history to extend, so he builds from the top: market, share, revenue growth, a target margin and sales per dollar of capital [S4][S6]. The chance that the firm does not survive is its own number. It is not hidden inside a higher discount rate [S6][S9]. He says the venture capital method (an exit multiple discounted at 50% or so) blends pricing, survival and time into one rate, and hides how bad the odds are [S6].

How he applied it: Uber 2014 carried a 10% failure chance, and a cost of capital that began at 12% and faded to 8% as the firm matured [S13].

### Bias and complexity are the enemies

His opening truths for the class: every valuation is biased, the bias grows with who pays you, and simple models beat complex ones [S2]. Noise, he says, is real uncertainty and not a flaw in the model [S9]. His fixes are to keep the inputs few, build checks that tie revenue, margin and reinvestment together, put the work in front of people who disagree, and be open about your own bias [S2][S9][S15].

How he applied it: he posts his valuations with dates and spreadsheets so others can change the inputs, and says he would rather be transparently wrong than opaquely right [S16][S25].

## How he decides

- Start by asking if you are valuing or pricing, and keep the two answers apart [S2][S3].
- Write the story in a few sentences. Then pull only the inputs it moves: growth, margin, reinvestment, risk, failure chance [S13].
- Check that the inputs fit. Fast growth with low spending and low risk needs a reason [S7][S9].
- Test each piece as possible, plausible or probable [S12].
- Take market inputs from the market, and cap terminal growth [S8][S20].
- Never put survival risk into the discount rate [S6][S9].
- Show a range, not a point, and say which input moves it most [S9][S4].
- If you use multiples, define them the same way for every firm, look at the whole spread and not only the sector, and compare firms with similar growth, risk and cash flow [S5].
- Read a price backwards to the revenue and margin it needs, then judge that story [S13][S22].
- Count shares, options and stock pay carefully. Stock pay is a real cost, not a non-cash add-back [S10].
- Don't staple a flat "control premium" onto a value. Control is worth something only where you can change how the firm is run [S7].
- For a private business, pay the owner a market salary, value the key-person risk and discount for being hard to sell [S11].
- For a business in decline, value it shrinking, and ask if selling parts beats keeping them [S8][S18].
- Write what would change your mind, and redo the work when it happens [S16].

## Real decisions

### Uber, June 2014, and the debate with Bill Gurley

In June 2014 Uber had raised money at a price near $17 billion. Damodaran valued it at about $5.9 billion: a $100 billion urban car service market, a 10% share, 20% of fares kept as revenue, a 40% mature margin, 12% fading to 8% cost of capital and a 10% chance of failure [S13]. He added $2 to 3 billion for the chance that Uber would grow beyond taxis into car ownership [S12][S13]. To reach the price he showed that the market had to be roughly three times bigger, or the share above 20% [S13]. Bill Gurley, an Uber investor, argued that the market was far larger than a taxi market [S12]. Damodaran answered by valuing each extra segment one by one with the possible, plausible, probable test, and he granted that inside knowledge could move parts of Gurley's story from plausible to probable and that he might not know management's plans [S12]. His reason was that different stories, not different arithmetic, explained the gap [S13].

### Amazon, February 2000

He valued Amazon at about $35 a share when it traded at $84 [S4]. Revenue growth began at 150% and faded to 6%, reaching about $39 billion in year ten, and the pre-tax margin was lifted from deeply negative to about 10% as for a specialty retailer [S4]. Reinvestment came from a sales-to-capital ratio of 3.0, and he netted out the value of employee options [S4]. He also published a grid of margins and growth rates showing that the $84 price needed far stronger assumptions than his own, such as revenue growth near 50% a year and margins of 14% to 16% [S4]. His stated reason: say which scenario justifies the price and then ask the odds of it, and base decisions on expected values, not best cases [S4].

### Facebook's IPO, May 2012

On the IPO date, 17 May 2012, he valued Facebook at about $25.39 a share against the $38 offer [S9]. The model had 40% revenue growth for five years that tapered to 2% by year ten, a margin sliding to 35%, a sales-to-capital ratio of 1.5 and a cost of capital above 11% that fell as the firm aged [S9]. He uses the case in his talk "Living with Noise" to make a point: the noise in a young company valuation is real uncertainty, not a fault in the model, and cruder tools such as price to sales only hide it [S9][S4].

### Tesla across the years, and his admitted mistakes

In September 2013 he judged Tesla overpriced, treating it as a luxury carmaker with steady-state revenue near $64 billion [S16]. In November 2021 his value was about $571 a share (about $190 after later splits) against a price above $1,200, built on $400 billion of revenue, a 16% operating margin and a 6% cost of capital [S16]. In January 2023 his base case was about $130 and his simulation median about $120, against a price near $143, with the same revenue and margin but a 10.15% cost of capital, because the median US cost of capital had risen from about 5.6% to about 9.6% [S16]. He named three errors: he capped its growth by calling it luxury, he modelled it as a low-margin manufacturer instead of partly electronics and software, and he assumed heavy plant spending when it grew with less [S16]. He also bought in June 2019 and sold in January 2020 after roughly a fourfold gain, then it kept rising [S16]. He calls revisiting a stock "the scene of the crime", and he responded with simulations instead of a single value [S16].

### Nvidia, June 2023: selling half

In June 2023 he valued Nvidia at about $240 a share against a price near $409, using a roughly $267 billion revenue path by 2033 and a margin of 35% to 40% by 2027, with the price near the 95th percentile of his value range [S17]. He had bought in 2018. He sold half to lock in a gain and kept half for the chance of new markets [S17]. He called himself a pragmatist, not a purist, and pointed out that the move did not fit his own value rule of selling all above value [S17].

### Yahoo, December 2015: sum of the parts

By December 2015 most of Yahoo's value sat in stakes in other companies. He added up its holdings of $40.3 billion (Alibaba $32.0 billion and Yahoo Japan $8.4 billion), plus $5.9 billion of cash, less $2.2 billion of debt, to get about $44.0 billion against a $32.4 billion market cap, a gap of about 29% [S19]. He judged the odds of a turnaround of the operating business low and said the better paths were to sell it, return cash or run a holding company [S19]. He rejected the "bet the farm" criticism: shareholders own the farm [S19].

### The 2025 and 2026 AI breakeven tests

In December 2025 he worked backwards from market caps to the revenue needed to earn the cost of equity. At a $5 trillion cap, Nvidia would need about $483 billion of revenue in steady state, or about $678 billion if it first grows for five years, at a 53% net margin [S22]. Tesla, at a net margin of about 5.3%, would need over $2.2 trillion of revenue, which is growth of about 86% a year for five years [S22]. He also noted that the AI model makers were priced at $1.5 trillion or more on less than $100 billion of revenue, and that each might pass the possible and plausible tests, while all of them together could not reach their breakeven [S22]. His label was the "big market delusion", and he added that highly priced is not the same as overpriced [S22]. In October 2026 he wrote that nobody yet knew whether AI spending would earn more than its cost of capital, and that markets would react before accounting showed it [S23].

## Where he stood

Dated as of 2026.

- **Is the market in a bubble?** In January 2026 he saw US stocks as not obviously overpriced, with the implied equity risk premium in the middle of its historic range, and some AI excess [S20]. By 1 October 2026 the implied premium was 3.70% over the Treasury yield, below 4% for the first time that year, because yields jumped while stock prices held [S23].
- **What is the risk-free rate?** From 2025, the US Treasury yield minus a small default spread (0.23% at the start of 2026) [S20]. At the start of October 2026 he gave 5.07% [S23].
- **Can AI earn its keep?** Undecided. The spenders carry the cost now, the suppliers earn the profits, and the returns are unproven [S23].
- **What did he do with his own money?** In October 2026 he wrote that most of his holdings followed market consensus, including five of the Magnificent Seven but not Tesla or Nvidia, and that new money went into short-term Treasuries [S23]. This is his stated position, not a suggestion.
- **Can a young company be valued?** Yes, with a story, few inputs and a failure chance [S6][S13].
- **Is there one best way to invest?** No. The right philosophy depends on your temperament, time horizon and what you believe about market mistakes [S14].
- **Is stock pay a cost?** Yes [S10].
- **Is a big terminal value a flaw?** No. For a going concern most of the value comes from the end. What matters is that the end year is realistic [S8].

## His work and record

- Born in Chennai in 1957; degrees from Loyola College Chennai, IIM Bangalore and UCLA Anderson, with an MBA in 1981 and a PhD in 1985 [S1].
- Visiting lecturer at Berkeley 1984 to 1986, then NYU Stern from 1986, now holding the Kerschner Family Chair in Finance Education [S1].
- Books include Damodaran on Valuation (1994), Investment Philosophies (2003), Investment Fables (2004), The Dark Side of Valuation, The Little Book of Valuation (2011), Investment Valuation, Applied Corporate Finance, Narrative and Numbers (2017) and The Corporate Life Cycle (2024) [S1][S15].
- Free online classes on corporate finance, valuation and investment philosophies, a corporate life cycle class added in 2024, and prep classes in accounting, statistics and finance basics [S25].
- Free data: implied equity risk premium, country risk, industry betas and costs of capital, and a debt-rating table, refreshed each January, with a 2026 update series that ran from January to February [S20][S21][S24].
- Winner of the Herbert Simon Award in 2013 [S1].
- Writes the Musings on Markets blog, with posts that are dated and often built on earlier ones [S23][S25].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

He is unusually open about his misses. On Tesla he admitted three modelling errors, a late update in 2023, and a sale in January 2020 that was too early [S16]. In the Uber exchange he accepted that inside knowledge could make parts of Gurley's case more likely than he had assumed [S12]. He sold half of Nvidia and said it contradicted his own rule [S17]. On Amazon in 2000 his own grid showed the price was possible under strong assumptions, and he published the grid [S4]. The pattern in his own record is that he caps growth and margin for companies that keep growing, then raises the inputs afterwards; Tesla is the clearest case [S16]. His answers also swing a lot with the discount rate: his Tesla cost of capital moved from about 6% to 10% in two years [S16]. His defense is to show the work, keep inputs few and invite others to change them [S2][S25].

## Blind spots to watch

- A model is only as good as the story behind it, and a weak story can look rigorous once it sits in a spreadsheet.
- He tends to cap growth and margin for firms that turn out to be exceptions, and updates later.
- He teaches a method, not a verdict, so he won't give you the answer you may want.
- He works from public facts. For a small private business the adjustments (owner pay, key person, hard to sell) widen the range a lot [S11].
- His three drivers (growth, margin, reinvestment) plus risk leave out governance and politics, which can matter [S9].
- Pair him with a sceptic of models and with someone who judges people and markets.
