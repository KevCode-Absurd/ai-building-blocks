# Aswath Damodaran: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-21. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1]. He is living, so every view is dated to what he has published up to October 2026 and may change.

## In one paragraph

Aswath Damodaran is a finance professor at NYU's Stern School of Business, where he has taught since 1986 [S1]. He is known for valuation: working out what a business is worth from the cash it can make, the growth it can reach and the risk it carries [S1][S2]. He gives away his classes, spreadsheets and data online, and describes himself as a teacher first [S2]. His main teaching is that every valuation is a story backed by numbers, and every number should trace back to a story [S7][S8]. He keeps value apart from price, because one is set by fundamentals and the other by supply, demand, mood and momentum [S5][S6]. He is open about his biases and says he expects to be wrong, then fixes the story [S7].

## Core ideas

### Value is the cash a thing will make, adjusted for risk

In his teaching, the value of any asset is the present value of the cash it is expected to produce, discounted at a rate that reflects risk, plus a value at the end of the forecast [S1]. Three things drive it: cash flows, growth and risk [S6]. Discounted cash flow is one tool for this, not the only route to intrinsic value [S6]. His seminar walks through it in order: pick an approach, estimate the inputs, forecast cash flows and growth, estimate the terminal value, then handle special cases [S9].

### Every valuation is a story backed by numbers

He argues that valuation is neither pure modelling nor pure story. People drift into "storytellers" or "number crunchers" and learn to distrust each other, and he sees that split as the barrier to good valuation [S8]. His book Narrative and Numbers tries to bridge it: a number cruncher should be able to build a story, and a storyteller should be able to turn a story into numbers [S8]. A good valuation, he says, is a marriage of the two, and every number needs a story behind it [S7]. He also warns that adding decimal places to a forecast gives a false sense of precision [S8].

### Possible, plausible and probable

A story is not worth much until it is tested. He sorts each building block into three bins: possible (it could happen, path unclear), plausible (there is a credible path) and probable (likely to happen) [S4]. Not everything possible is plausible, and not everything plausible turns probable [S4]. Value depends on where you draw those lines [S4]. In his 2014 reply to Bill Gurley on Uber, he tested the claim that Uber could grow the market past taxis by asking three questions: is there a reason to switch, can inertia be overcome, and will incumbents fight back [S4]. He called the bigger case plausible but not yet probable, and said he was not calling Uber overpriced, only working out what drives its value [S4].

### Value and price are different games

Price is set by demand and supply, and mood and momentum can push it away from value [S6]. In the pricing game, price is the only number you can act on, and the aim is to guess which way it moves [S5]. In the value game, every asset has a worth that can be estimated with error, and the aim is to buy below it and sell above it [S5]. He does not rank one above the other. He says people get hurt when they misunderstand which game they are playing [S6]. His Twitter point from 2013 sums it up: it can be a good trade and a bad investment at the same time [S5]. He valued Twitter near $10 billion, while a pricing approach based on users gave a figure more than twice that, and the market landed near the higher one [S5][S6].

### Pricing done well

Pricing means attaching a number to an asset by looking at how similar assets are priced. It takes three steps: scale the price by a common measure such as earnings, revenue or users, find comparable companies, then adjust for differences in growth, risk and returns [S6]. He calls much of pricing as practised sloppy, because it throws data away [S6]. His rules: judge a multiple against the spread of the whole market, not a fixed rule; treat book value with suspicion; check whether a cheap stock deserves to be cheap; and look at what is already priced in [S6].

### Young and loss-making companies

He says the hard part of valuing a young company is uncertainty, not a better model, and about nine tenths of it is economic uncertainty that no homework removes [S7]. Past numbers cannot be projected forward, so the story must drive the estimates [S7]. He keeps the model short: revenue growth, target operating margin and sales-to-invested-capital for the business, and cost of equity, cost of debt and probability of failure for risk [S7]. Failure risk should be modelled directly, not buried in a higher discount rate, which he calls a blunt tool [S7]. Terminal value often supplies most of the value, so he does not set it as a multiple of earnings, and he keeps long-run growth below the economy's [S7]. For ranges he runs simulations in place of single guesses [S7].

### The cost of capital comes from the market, not a guess

He takes macro inputs from the market instead of estimating them to false precision [S7]. Each month he publishes an implied equity risk premium, the return stocks seem to be priced to give, and at the start of October 2026 it stood at 3.70% [S2][S3]. For the discount rate, he looks at the spread of costs of capital across tens of thousands of public companies, and he reported a median of about 6 to 7 percent in US dollars at the start of one year [S7]. He also describes an "intrinsic" risk-free rate as expected inflation plus real growth [S3].

### Bias is the one thing you cannot remove

He lists three challenges: uncertainty, complexity and bias. The first two can be managed, but bias cannot be removed [S7]. His fix is to admit your biases rather than claim to be objective, avoid falling for your own story, and share the work with people who disagree [S7]. His seminar names bias and preconception as a major challenge to valuation [S9]. His stated test of success is being less wrong than others, not being right [S7].

### The story changes with the life cycle

In his book's framing, young firms live on big-picture narratives, growing firms must deliver numbers that back the story, mature firms have a mostly written narrative, and declining firms must aim for a happy ending without rewriting history [S8]. He applies it to Uber, Apple, Amazon, Vale, BlackBerry and JC Penney [S8]. A later book looks at the corporate life cycle [S1][S2].

## How they decide

- Start with the story, then say which numbers it moves [S7][S8].
- Test each part of the story as possible, plausible or probable [S4].
- Keep the model to a few inputs that matter [S7].
- Check that growth, reinvestment and risk fit together; big growth with low reinvestment and low risk needs strong support [S7].
- Say which game you are playing, pricing or valuing [S5][S6].
- Take inputs for the market from the market [S7].
- Write down your bias, and ask someone who disagrees [S7].
- Change the story when facts change, and say so [S7][S8].

## Where they stood

- **Can a young company be valued at all?** Yes. He says there is nothing fundamentally different about it except thin history and large uncertainty [S7].
- **Does price converge to value?** He treats it as a belief of investors that can fail: even a correct valuation may not see price move toward it [S5].
- **Should a venture investor care about profit?** In a September 2026 post he argued that venture capitalists often price companies rather than value them, judging success by entry and exit prices, and that scale and profit are not opposites [S3].
- **Can traders and investors both be right?** Yes, and he does not look down on traders who make money [S5][S6].
- **What about the AI boom?** As of October 2026 he laid out best, worst and middle cases, noted that capital spending rose about 36% in a quarter, and said markets will price AI outcomes before accountants write assets down [S3].
- **What about ESG?** His site calls it a concept oversold and overhyped by its backers [S2].

## Their work and record

- Born in Chennai in 1957; degrees from Loyola College Chennai, IIM Bangalore and UCLA Anderson, with an MBA in 1981 and a PhD in 1985 [S1].
- Visiting lecturer at Berkeley 1984 to 1986, then NYU Stern from 1986, now holding the Kerschner Family Chair in Finance Education [S1].
- Books include Damodaran on Valuation (1994), Investment Philosophies (2003), Investment Fables (2004), The Dark Side of Valuation, The Little Book of Valuation (2011), Investment Valuation, Applied Corporate Finance, Narrative and Numbers (2017) and The Corporate Life Cycle (2024) [S1][S2].
- Free online classes on valuation, corporate finance and investment philosophies, plus short prep courses on finance foundations, minimalist accounting and statistics [S2].
- Free datasets: historical returns, monthly and yearly implied equity risk premiums, country risk premiums and industry averages, plus spreadsheets to value companies [S2].
- Two-day valuation seminar with worked examples such as Amgen, Tata Motors and Amazon in 2000 [S9].
- Awards include the Herbert Simon Award in 2013 [S1].
- Writes the Musings on Markets blog [S3].

## Where they were wrong or criticised

Wikipedia carries no criticism section [S1], so this part is thin and sourced to his own words. He has said that valuation always carries error and that bias cannot be removed [S7]. In the Uber exchange he allowed that insider data could move parts of Gurley's story from plausible to probable, and that he might not know of management plans [S4]. In 2013 the market priced Twitter near the higher, pricing-based figure rather than his lower value [S5]. His own point is that a correct valuation can sit far from price for a long time [S5]. His answer to the worry that inputs are too uncertain is to show the working, keep inputs few and invite challenge [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- A model is only as good as the story behind it, and a weak story can look rigorous once it sits in a spreadsheet.
- He teaches a method, not a verdict, so he will not give you the answer when you want one.
- His examples are mostly public companies. Small private businesses need extra adjustments for the owner's role and for being hard to sell, and the method gives wide ranges.
- He favours long horizons, which can feel slow if you need a decision this week.
- Pair him with a sceptic of models and with someone who judges people and markets.
