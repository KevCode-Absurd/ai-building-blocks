# Barbara Corcoran: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Corcoran grew up in New Jersey, the second of ten children, was labelled the slow kid at school and later learned she was dyslexic [S7]. She got straight Ds in high school and college and had about 20 jobs by the age of 23 [S4]. In 1973 she borrowed $1,000 from her boyfriend, quit waitressing and co-founded a small New York real estate firm, which grew into The Corcoran Group [S4][S7]. In the mid-1970s she began publishing a newsletter on New York property trends, the Corcoran Report [S7]. She sold the company in 2001 for $66 million [S7]. She is an original investor on Shark Tank, with well over 100 deals [S6][S7]. Her core message is that success comes on the back of failure [S3]. A great idea isn't enough. You need resourcefulness, good people and the ability to get back up after a setback [S4]. Her practical advice is to test ideas on strangers, find one paying customer, trust your gut and get started before it's perfect [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Failure comes before the win

Corcoran's central belief is that her best results followed her worst moments. On her website she says that everything in her life came on the heels of failure [S3]. She advises people to say thank you when someone tells them no [S3]. One of her talks treats resilience as a skill you can learn, and promises to teach the art of the comeback [S5].

How she applied it: she tells the story of building a business that later sold for $66 million after someone doubted she could make it on her own [S3][S7].

### More than a great idea: resourcefulness and reading people

She argues that ideas are cheap. What counts is resourcefulness, good people and the ability to get back up after a setback [S4]. Her own start had few advantages: poor grades, many low-level jobs and a $1,000 loan [S4][S7].

How she applied it: she now teaches founders on building teams, leading, hiring and bouncing back, drawing on her own business [S4][S5].

Her own resourcefulness began at work. Corcoran credits waitressing with teaching her to read people fast, make them feel important and close a sale [S3]. She says she carried those lessons into real estate and into building her company [S3].

How she applied it: she built a sales business in one of the toughest property markets in the world, starting from a single small office [S4][S7].

### Test your idea on strangers

When a reader asked how to launch a product, Corcoran gave simple steps. Make a prototype, or at least a 3D drawing, so people can see it [S1]. Then take it to strangers on the street and ask if they'd buy it and what they'd pay [S1]. Don't ask family and friends, she said, because they are too polite [S1]. If you want an honest critic, she joked, try your mother-in-law [S1].

How she applied it: in the same post she said the best proof an idea works is one customer willing to pay [S1].

### Say what makes you exceptional

In a crowded market, she told an IT consultant, work out what makes you exceptional and market that clearly [S1]. Your reasons need to be persuasive. They don't have to be proven yet [S1]. Then build trust before the first sale by sharing free, practical tips on social media [S1].

How she applied it: the Corcoran Report, her newsletter on New York property trends from the mid-1970s, put her firm's knowledge of the market in front of readers [S7]. (That it worked as marketing is our reading.)

### Trust your gut

Asked what she'd tell her 21-year-old self, Corcoran said to trust her instincts, which she describes as the sum of everything you've learned [S1]. She had 22 low-level jobs before starting her business at 23, and said she didn't fully trust her gut until about 30 [S1]. Her instinct told her to start young, and she overrode the logical reasons not to [S1].

### Get it going, not right

Her new-year message for 2025 was that progress matters more than perfection. You don't have to get it right, she wrote, you have to get it going [S2]. She frames a new year as a clean slate to set the right goals, whether you are launching, growing or pausing to refocus [S2]. She invites people to share goals, on the view that people do better working together [S2].

### Count the cost of starting

She is honest about what a business takes. She compares starting one to having a baby, because it eats time, focus and energy [S1]. She asks would-be founders whether they have the time and support to focus fully [S1]. She warns that, as a business grows, the team will compete with the rest of your life for attention [S1].

How she applied it: she told one reader going through a hard time that a new business could give her momentum, as long as she was steady enough to take it on [S1].

### Hunger helps

Corcoran argues that people who grow up with less have a natural hunger. They take more risks, want what they don't have and take pride in earning [S1]. She grew up one of ten children, worked early and was allowed to fail [S1]. She believes the value of money is hard to teach to someone who has never had to earn it [S1].

How she applied it: she advises parents and mentors to let young people stumble and not rescue them at once, and she points to cold-calling as a good school for handling rejection [S1].

### Bad times are a chance to move ahead

One of her talks covers how she adapted through downturns: high interest rates, a New York financial crisis, the subprime crash and 9/11 [S5]. The talk's message is that bad times are the best times to move ahead [S5].

How she applied it: she also teaches how to spot and recruit talent, drawing on having hired, and fired, thousands of people [S5].

## How she decides

- Treat a no as the start of the next attempt [S3].
- Check you have the time and support before you start [S1].
- Make something people can see, then ask strangers to judge it [S1].
- Look for one paying customer as proof [S1].
- Say clearly why you're exceptional, then give value away to earn trust [S1].
- Trust your gut once you've done the work [S1].
- Start before it's perfect [S2].
- Keep moving when the market turns [S5].

## Real decisions

### Starting a firm on a $1,000 loan

In 1973 she was a receptionist for a New York real estate company when she co-founded her first firm with her boyfriend, who lent her $1,000 [S7][S8]. In her telling the deal gave him 51 percent [S8]. Seven years later he left her for her secretary [S7][S8]. She split the company and started again, which she uses as her proof that recovery is most of life [S8].

### Running a same-price sale to sell 88 apartments

She tells of a sale of 88 apartments at one price, framed as picking from the litter, which sold out in about three hours [S8]. The point she draws is that scarcity and a clear rule make people act fast [S8].

### Advertising one empty desk

When recruiting, she ran an ad for a single empty desk rather than a general job notice [S8]. Her reason was scarcity: one seat made it feel like a prize [S8].

### Grading buyers A, B and C

She sorted buyers by how soon they needed to move and dropped the C's [S8]. This saved her agents' time for people who would act [S8].

### Fighting the commission lawsuit

She says she fights only when a moral principle and real money are both at stake, and first asks how much energy a fight will take and for how long [S8]. She won a commission dispute with a well-known developer, and the returned flowers marked "Rejected" are a story she tells with relish [S8].

### Selling the firm in 2001

She sold the Corcoran Group to NRT for $66 million in 2001 and left the company soon after [S7]. In 1973 she had wanted to be her own boss, and she did not stay on as an employee [S7].

## Where she stood

**Is a great idea enough?** No. It takes resourcefulness, good people and resilience [S4].

**Who should you ask about your idea?** Strangers, not family and friends [S1].

**What proves an idea?** One customer who will pay [S1].

**Is it better to grow up with less?** In her view, it builds hunger and a taste for risk [S1].

**When should you start?** Now. Get it going and fix it along the way [S2].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

The sources we opened contain little criticism of her. Points to weigh:

- **By her own account,** she held back from fully trusting her judgement until about 30 [S1].
- **Shark Tank results are mixed.** Her site highlights winners [S6], which says little about deals that failed. Treat success stories as a partial picture.
- **Her advice is personal.** It comes from one founder's path in one city's property market [S4][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- "Failure leads to success" is easier to say once you've succeeded. Pair her with someone who checks the downside.
- Gut instinct works best with deep experience. A beginner may need more data.
- Her view that growing up with less builds hunger can overlook how hard poverty makes it to take risks.
- She speaks from sales and real estate. Product, design and technology questions need another voice.
