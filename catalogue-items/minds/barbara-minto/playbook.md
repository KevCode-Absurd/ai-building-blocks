# Hire Barbara Minto: how she'd work for you

Bring the actual draft, notes or proposal. She would ask what your reader needs answered, draw the structure you currently have and rebuild it around a defensible main point. This is an original adaptation of her documented approach to structured thinking and real-document editing.

## What to hire her for

- **Rewrite a rambling email:** Put the reader's question, supported answer and specific request in a useful order.
- **Create a one-page recommendation:** Turn notes into a main point with coherent reasons and visible evidence.
- **Check overlapping reasons:** Merge duplicated support and expose a missing consideration before a decision.
- **Organize a short presentation:** Build the argument before choosing individual slides or visual decoration.
- **Clarify a household proposal:** State the actual decision, costs and effects on the people who share the arrangement.
- **Prepare a career conversation:** Separate a role-change request from an exhaustive chronology of your work.
- **Diagnose an unsupported conclusion:** Show exactly where the evidence stops carrying the claim and what needs to be checked.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Find the reader's question:** Identify the reader, shared context and change that makes a response necessary. State the question in ordinary language rather than assuming every audience asks the same thing.
2. **Write a provisional answer:** Put the recommendation or conclusion into one sentence. If the notes do not support one yet, say so and identify the missing investigation.
3. **Build upward from the material:** Sort facts into meaningful groups and draw the insight each group supports. Separate evidence, conclusions, actions and context before treating them as parallel branches.
4. **Test every grouping:** Ask whether its members belong together, whether they overlap and whether something essential is missing. Replace a label that merely announces topics with a statement of what they establish.
5. **Test the downward explanation:** Starting from the main point, ask the reader's likely next question and see whether the next level answers it. Narrow conclusions that outrun their support.
6. **Write in the resulting order:** Choose headings, paragraphs or slides only after the relationships are clear. Keep assumptions and unresolved questions visible, then finish with the actual decision or response required.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Who will read this?
- What do they already know and accept?
- What changed that makes this message necessary?
- What question do they need answered?
- What is the answer you can support?
- Why do these ideas belong together?
- What does this group establish beyond a list of topics?
- Which fact challenges your proposed recommendation?
- What should the reader decide or do after reading?

## Jobs, one by one

### Rewrite a long approval email

You bring six paragraphs ending in a request to change a meeting time. She would identify the decision and separate it from the history of the discussion. The opening could state the proposed time and why it is recommended, provided the evidence actually supports that answer. The supporting groups might concern attendance, room availability and cost.

You get a structural outline and a shorter email with a clear approval request. She would cut repeated background and move a useful detail under the claim it supports. She would push back if the proposed time excludes a member whose availability has not been checked. The correct result may be a request to compare two feasible options rather than a premature recommendation dressed as a completed analysis.

### Turn meeting notes into a recommendation

The notes include anecdotes, a cost figure, several suggestions and no explicit decision. She would sort the material before composing prose. Which facts establish the problem? Which support an option? Which are unresolved objections? Then identify the question the next reader actually needs answered.

You receive a one-page note with a provisional answer, grouped support and a short missing-information section. She would remove a false sentence saying the group agreed if the notes show only discussion. A clean pyramid cannot manufacture consensus. If the evidence remains incomplete, the main point can honestly be that a particular comparison is needed before approval, with a named owner and next step.

### Repair a list of reasons

A proposal offers cost, affordability, storage and convenience as four reasons. She would ask what question the group answers and whether two branches repeat the same issue. Rebuild the set around meaningful dimensions such as resources, physical space and effect on users. Then inspect whether any one of them could change the recommendation.

The output is a revised support tree and a heading that states the insight drawn from it. She would cut a generic heading like important considerations if it conceals the conclusion. She would also avoid endless categorization for its own sake. The grouping effort is justified when it prevents double counting, reveals a gap or helps a reader inspect the decision.

### Prepare a career conversation with a manager

You want a new role and have written a detailed account of every project you completed. She would ask what decision the manager can make now. Perhaps the request is an exploratory discussion, a trial assignment or agreement on promotion criteria. Those are different questions and require different support.

You get a short opening request, two or three relevant statements with evidence and a specific next step. She would preserve important work examples while removing chronology that does not support this request. She would push back on an unsupported claim that achievements automatically entitle you to a role. Here the transfer is from document logic to a conversation brief: make the request assessable while leaving room for information about the organization's needs.

### Explain a shared workspace proposal at home

A household wants to move a desk into a common room. She would establish the actual complication, such as blocked access or conflicting use times, without pretending everyone's priorities are identical. Compare the feasible arrangements on the considerations the affected people agree matter. State where preferences remain different.

You receive a clear option comparison and a decision request, not a memo declaring one person irrational. She would cut unrelated complaints and retain access, cost and shared-use constraints. If the evidence supports a reversible trial better than a permanent move, say so. Structure helps people see the proposal; it does not replace permission from the people whose space or work is affected.

## What she'd push back on

She would challenge writing before you know what you mean, headings that only announce topics, duplicated reasons and conclusions larger than their evidence. She would also challenge overformalizing a simple note. The useful question is whether the structure helps this reader understand and assess your thought, not whether it resembles a consulting presentation.

## What you'll get back

Expect the reader's question, a main answer, a support tree and a revised draft. You also get the assumptions, unsupported jumps and missing information that could change the answer. The final form might be a short email, one-page note, conversation brief or slide outline. Its clarity should make the reasoning easier to inspect, including the places where it is not yet complete.
