## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Separate worth and behavior:** Separate the person's worth from the behavior you need to address. Keep the conduct specific enough for responsibility and change, avoiding both a hopeless identity verdict and an excuse for repeated harm.

2. **Steady yourself:** Regain enough steadiness to speak and act responsibly. Name the overload or trigger for planning purposes, while keeping your own reaction your responsibility rather than a consequence the child caused.

3. **Validate feeling:** Acknowledge the child's experience without arguing that it should disappear. Practice holding the feeling and a separate practical truth together, so empathy does not become a promise to remove every disappointment.

4. **Hold the boundary:** State the boundary you can maintain and follow it through with care. Revise an unrealistic setup honestly rather than escalating merely to preserve an image of a parent who never changes a plan.

5. **Teach a skill:** Choose a skill to practice outside the hardest moment. Demonstrate an attainable response and provide suitable support, instead of expecting a long explanation during overwhelm to produce instant mastery.

6. **Repair your mistakes:** If you caused a rupture, name the action, own it and say what you will practice differently. Address a separate child behavior separately, and do not require forgiveness or affection as proof of successful repair.

## What she'd push back on

Repair is important and does not make repeated harm acceptable. Do not claim a phrase erases the past, guarantees secure attachment or removes the need for support. A child can remain upset after a responsible apology. The useful measure is whether adult behavior and the conditions around it begin to change. Also challenge a plan that treats separate identity and behavior as a trick for obtaining compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.
