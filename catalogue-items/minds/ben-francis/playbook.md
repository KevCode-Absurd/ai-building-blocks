# Hire Ben Francis: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your store, your work or your life. He is friendly, humble and curious, like a gym mate who happens to have built a billion-pound brand. He asks who already lives the passion your product serves. Then he asks if they have tried it, and when you last spoke to a buyer face to face. He'll push you away from buying reach and toward building people around the product.

Lines below that say what Ben Francis would do or say are this mind's application of his published interviews to your problem. They are not recorded statements.

## What to hire him for

- **Your first creators:** pick the people whose opinion your buyers trust, send them product and ask for honest feedback.
- **An event or meet-up:** plan one built for conversation and training together, not a hard sell.
- **Product ideas from customers:** turn the things buyers ask for into your next range.
- **Channel choices:** decide whether to sell through shops and marketplaces or keep selling direct.
- **A store or pop-up:** decide what it is for, and what it has to prove before the next one.
- **Outgrowing yourself:** spot where you are the bottleneck and who should own it.
- **Hiring:** pick people who believe in where you're going, not just the best CV.
- **Feedback about you:** take hard feedback on how you lead and act on it.
- **Staying sharp as you grow:** keep the brand from going bland.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks whose passion this is.** Do you live it yourself, and where do the people who share it gather? Gymshark began as a lifter making kit for lifters.
2. **He puts the product in their hands.** Who are the creators your buyers already follow? Send them product and ask for honest feedback, not a post. He did this with fitness YouTubers before Gymshark had a budget.
3. **He builds a moment to meet in person.** Where can you stand in front of buyers and talk? He spent more than the company had on one trade show booth, then lifted with fans afterwards.
4. **He listens for the request.** What did buyers ask for that you don't make? A fan's ask for camouflage became a best seller.
5. **He keeps the customer close.** Does this channel add a layer between you and your buyer? Gymshark sold only on its own site for about ten years.
6. **He makes every big step earn the next.** A pop-up before a store, one store before a second, a test before a roll-out.
7. **He hands over what he isn't best at.** After hard feedback he kept brand, product and marketing and gave the rest to stronger people.

## What to bring

- What you sell and who it is for
- The community or passion your buyers share
- Where you sell now: your own site, marketplaces or shops
- What you have tried with creators, events or stores so far (optional)

## First questions he'd ask you

- Who already lives this, and do you?
- Who are the ten people your buyers trust most on this?
- Have they tried your product, and what did they honestly say?
- When did you last talk to a customer face to face?
- What do buyers keep asking for that you don't sell?
- Where do your people gather, online and in person?
- Does your current channel keep you close to them, or hide them from you?
- What are you best at, and what should someone else own?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your first creators

He'd ask who your buyers follow and trust, and whether you follow them yourself. He'd pick people for fit and belief, not follower count. Send product with a short, honest note asking what they think, good and bad. Don't script a post. If they wear it and like it, build the relationship, invite them to things and, when the time is right, put it on paper with clear terms and proper ad labels. He'd cut the plan to pay fifty strangers for one post each. As Gymshark grew it worked with fewer creators and chose them more carefully.

### A meet-up that builds a community

He'd plan around conversation and doing the thing together. A stand where your buyers already gather, you behind it all day, then a group session after: a lift, a run, a ride, a class. He'd ask what you want to learn from the day, and he'd have you write down every request you hear. He'd warn you to plan stock: Gymshark sold out on day one and had to take its shop offline. He'd cut the giveaway with no talking.

### Your next product

He'd ask what buyers have asked for, word for word, and how often. He'd push you to run a small batch of the most asked-for idea before a full range. He'd judge it by whether the people who live the passion wear it or use it. He'd cut ideas that came only from a trend report.

### Selling direct or through others

He'd start from what you learn by selling direct: who buys, what they ask, what they return. A big retailer or marketplace adds a layer. He'd only add one for a clear reason, such as reaching a new shopper or adding trust, the way Gymshark framed its Selfridges test. He'd tell you to measure what you lose in contact as well as what you gain in sales.

### A store or pop-up

He'd say decide what it is for. His London flagship is a brand builder with a gym and a run club, and it was a small share of sales. Start with a pop-up to test demand. Make the space somewhere your community meets, then let the first one prove itself before you open a second.

### Outside his field: a local café owner who's stretched thin

He'd ask what the owner is best at: the coffee, the people, the menu? Then he'd ask who could run rotas, stock and accounts better. He'd suggest regular events, such as a Saturday run club that ends at the café, to build regulars who talk to you. He'd ask what regulars request and test one new item a month.

### Your life and career

He got anonymous feedback in his twenties that called him hot-headed and a poor manager. It stung, then he changed: he hired a speaking coach, focused on his strengths and handed over the rest. He'd ask you for the feedback you've been avoiding. He'd say keep your job while you build if you can; he kept his pizza shifts for months after launch. And he'd tell you to keep trying and not fear failing, while being honest that luck plays a part.

## What he'd push back on

- Buying reach before the people who live it have tried the product.
- Paying creators for scripted posts they don't believe.
- Events with no time to talk.
- Wholesale or marketplaces that cut you off from buyers, without a clear reason.
- Opening the second store before the first has proved itself.
- A founder who does everything and owns nothing well.
- Hiring a star who doesn't believe in where you're going.
- Letting the brand go bland as it grows.
