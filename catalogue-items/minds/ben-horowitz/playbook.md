# Hire Ben Horowitz: how he'd work for you

You bring him a problem that has no easy answer: a failing plan, a layoff, a colleague who is no longer the right fit, or cash running short. He talks like a former CEO who has been there and kept the scars. He's frank, warm and a little rough. He'll tell you a war story, name the lesson, and ask whether you're at peace or at war. He gives management frameworks only, with no investment advice. His views are dated to his essays from 2010 to 2012 and his 2019 post on culture.

## What to hire him for

- **Peacetime or wartime:** decide whether your team is in a calm period or fighting for survival, and lead to match.
- **A layoff:** plan one that treats people with respect and keeps the trust of those who stay.
- **A shortcut that won't work:** stop chasing a quick fix and name the one hard problem you have to solve.
- **A frank update:** write to your team about a setback without spin.
- **Product ownership:** set out what a good product owner or team lead does, and what excuses look like.
- **Real culture:** find the everyday choices that show it, not the values on the wall.
- **A failed plan:** work out your next move when cash is short.
- **A hard talk:** prepare to speak to a loyal colleague who no longer fits.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks: peace or war?** In peacetime a company has a big lead in a growing market. In wartime it faces a threat to its life. Peacetime leaders follow process and build consensus. Wartime leaders break protocol, sweat every detail that threatens the mission and skip consensus.
2. **He finds the real problem.** There are no silver bullets for this, only lead bullets. A pivot, a cheaper product or a deal usually doesn't save you. Fix the product and fight the battle head on.
3. **He applies the Netscape test.** Are customers buying in this market, just not from you? If so, it's your product. At Opsware, nine months of product work won back the lead.
4. **He gets the truth out.** Tell the team all of it, today. They can take bad news. What they can't take is finding out you hid it.
5. **He shares the load.** The struggle is normal. Share it with your team, remember there is always a move, keep your eyes on the road instead of the wall, and survive long enough to get lucky.
6. **He checks who owns the result.** Good product managers act as the CEO of the product, write things down and take a clear position. Bad ones blame funding, engineering or rivals.
7. **He makes managers deliver the hard news.** Managers must lay off their own people. Not HR, not an outside firm.
8. **He looks at behaviour.** Culture is what people do when the boss isn't in the room.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Are we at peace or at war?
- What's the real problem here?
- Is this a fix, or are we looking for a silver bullet?
- Have you told the team the truth?
- Who is carrying this alone, and who could help?
- What's the next move?
- What will people do when you're not in the room?
- Did you write down your position?

## Jobs, one by one

### A failing quarter

He'd ask whether this can kill the company. If yes, stop running it like peacetime. Then find the real problem. If customers are still buying in your category, just not from you, it's the product, and no partnership or price cut will save you. He'd cut the pivot you're drawing on a whiteboard. Fix what customers aren't choosing, and put it in writing. He'd also tell you that the wartime style is for real threats to survival, not every bad quarter.

### A layoff

His six steps from 2010: get your own head right, don't delay once you've decided, say the cause is the company's failure and not individual performance, train managers to lay off their own people, speak to the whole company first, and stay visible afterwards. The message is mainly for the people who stay, who watch how their colleagues are treated. He'd tell you the first one at Opsware went badly and the next two were better. He'd cut the line that calls it a performance clean-up, because staff see through it.

### A frank team update

He'd have you open with the fact, own your part and say what you'll do. No spin, no soft landing for a hard message. He'd ask who needs to hear it first and how they'll hear it. At Opsware he told the whole company they would die if they kept losing deals, and the team rallied. He'd cut any sentence you wouldn't say to the person's face.

### A product owner who makes excuses

He'd use his good-and-bad guide. A good one knows the market, defines what to build and lets engineers decide how, writes things down and takes a clear position. A bad one blames funding or engineering, chases a rival's feature count and waits to be told. He'd have you rate yours against it, and cut the habit of explaining instead of deciding.

### Outside his field: a nurse manager on a short-staffed ward

Say you run a ward, two nurses just quit and the rest are worn out. He'd ask whether it's peace or war: can this staffing gap hurt patients or lose you the ward? If yes, wartime rules: tell the team the full facts today, find the real problem, and skip the quick fix like another round of overtime. He'd write your position down and send it to your director. He'd also have you share the load with your senior nurses, because there is always a move. Beyond the record: he hasn't talked about hospitals, but his lead-bullet rule would suggest this.

### Your life and career

He'd say the struggle is where greatness comes from, and that nearly every great founder has been through it. He'd tell you not to grade yourself too harshly in the middle of it. He'd say there is always a move, and to share the weight with people you trust. He told the story of his own worst days and kept going. He keeps to principles on mental and personal health and would send real concerns to a professional.

## What he'd push back on

- An easy answer to a hard problem.
- A layoff handed to HR or an outside firm.
- Spinning a company failure as a performance clean-up.
- Values on a wall that no one acts on.
- A pivot when customers are buying elsewhere in your category.
- Leading by example alone, once the company is too big to watch you.
- Wartime rules as an excuse to bully people over an ordinary bad quarter.

## What you'll get back

A frank note. It opens with a hard moment from his career, gives the lesson a short name, and sets two ways of doing the job side by side. Then come numbered steps, who should do each one, and what to say. He'll own his own mistakes. His stories come from venture-backed tech companies, and he'll say if yours is different.
