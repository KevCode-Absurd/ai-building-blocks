## In one paragraph

Graham grew up poor after his father died and the Panic of 1907 hit his family, and he spent his life working out how to invest without being ruined by the market's moods [S1]. With David Dodd he wrote *Security Analysis* in 1934, after the huge losses of the early Depression, and in 1949 he wrote *The Intelligent Investor* for ordinary readers [S1][S3]. His core ideas are simple. A share is a part of a real business. Its value can be estimated and is different from its price. Buy only when the price is well below value, so you have a margin of safety. And treat the market as a moody partner you can ignore, not a guide you must obey [S1][S4]. He taught at Columbia, where Warren Buffett was his student, and Buffett called *The Intelligent Investor* the best book on investing ever written [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Investment versus speculation

Graham drew a hard line between the two. An investment is an operation that, after careful study, "promises safety of principal and an adequate return" [S3]. Anything that doesn't meet that test is speculation [S3]. He didn't say speculation was always wrong, only that you should know which one you are doing.

How he applied it: the first chapter of *The Intelligent Investor* is about this line, and the rest of the book builds on it [S2].

### A share is part of a business

Graham taught that a stock is part ownership of a company, not a ticket that goes up and down [S1]. The question to ask is what the business is worth, not where the price will go next week [S1]. Buffett said Graham's students all think this way: they are always buying the business, not the stock [S5].

### Value is not price

Graham and Dodd separated intrinsic value, which comes from the operating business behind the share, from the price the market quotes today [S3]. In 1955 he told the Senate that a stock's real value is measured by its price relative to earnings, dividends and future prospects, and to a lesser degree its assets [S6]. Because value depends on judgment and forecasts, he said, informed people can fairly disagree about it [S6].

How he applied it: *The Intelligent Investor* describes value as expected earnings times a factor that reflects growth, management quality, financial strength and dividend record, compared with the price [S2]. The 1962 edition of *Security Analysis* gave a rough formula for this, which later readers noted leaves out interest rates [S3].

### Margin of safety

Graham and Dodd coined the term for buying at a discount to value, so that errors, bad luck or bad forecasts don't sink you [S3]. *The Intelligent Investor* calls it the central concept of investment in its chapter 20 [S2]. Buffett gave the plainest picture of it in 1984: when you build a bridge rated for 30,000 pounds, you drive only 10,000-pound trucks across it. You don't buy a business worth $83 million for $80 million. You leave a big gap [S5].

### Mr. Market

In chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor*, Graham asks you to picture a business partner called Mr. Market [S4]. Every day he offers to buy your share or sell you his, at a price that swings with his mood, from gloomy to giddy [S4]. You are free to say no, because he will be back tomorrow with a new price [S4]. The lesson is to use his moods when they suit you and ignore them when they don't, and to judge value from the business, not from the quote [S4]. Buffett has called this chapter the best part of the book [S4].

### Defensive or enterprising

Graham split investors into two types [S1]. The defensive investor wants to keep effort and worry low, and follows simple, safe rules [S1]. The enterprising investor is willing to do original research to find bargains [S1]. Each can do well. The danger is acting like one while having the time and temper of the other.

### Simple rules and groups of stocks

Later in life Graham trusted simple rules more than detailed analysis. He favoured buying a group of stocks that each met a simple test of cheapness, rather than studying single companies in depth, and said earnings tests gave the best results [S2]. In his 1976 interview he said he had lost most of his interest in the fine detail of security analysis, and that a few techniques and simple principles were enough [S7]. What matters, he said, is having "the right general principles and the character to stick to them" [S7].

### Shareholder activism as a tool

Early in his career he made his name in "The Northern Pipeline Affair". His research showed that the company held large cash and bond assets that were not being used well, so he bought enough shares to force a proxy vote to hand the assets back to shareholders [S1]. It shows the same habit as the rest of his work: look at what the business really holds before looking at the quote.

### Moods drive prices in the short run

Graham saw markets swing between hope and fear. In 1955 he told senators that the market had risen since 1953 mostly because people's mood had changed from doubt to confidence, with prices rising from about 8 to about 14 times earnings while earnings had barely moved [S6]. He warned that mood alone, "not supported by any visible change in value," can move prices a long way [S6]. He also reminded them that the Dow fell 90% from 1929 to 1932 [S6]. In 1976 he compared the cycle to a Ferris wheel or a seesaw [S7].

How he applied it: asked by the committee chairman how a cheap stock ever gets noticed, he admitted he didn't fully know. Experience showed the market catches up with value in the end, but how it happens was "one of the mysteries of our business" [S6].

## How he decides

- First ask: is this an investment or a speculation [S3]?
- Estimate value from earnings, dividends, assets and prospects, and accept the estimate is rough [S6].
- Pay well below that value. Don't cut it close [S5].
- Treat the market's price as an offer, not a verdict [S4].
- Know which kind of investor you are, and follow that kind's rules [S1].
- Prefer a few simple principles you will stick to over clever forecasts [S7].
- Don't try to forecast the market. In his experience people don't succeed at it [S7].

## Real decisions

### Taking a Wall Street job to support his mother
After graduating from Columbia at about 20, Graham chose a Wall Street job to help support his widowed mother, instead of an academic post [S1]. The reference page gives that reason and nothing more about the choice [S1].

### Forcing Northern Pipeline to release its assets
He found that Northern Pipeline held vast cash and bond assets that were not being put to good use. He bought enough shares to force a proxy vote to distribute them to shareholders, an early case of shareholder activism that involved John D. Rockefeller [S1]. The stated logic was that the assets, not the price, showed what the company was worth [S1].

### Defining investment, after the crash
With David Dodd he published *Security Analysis* in 1934 and gave a clear definition of investment: an operation that, on thorough analysis, promises safety of principal and a satisfactory return, with everything else called speculation [S1][S3]. The book came after the huge early Depression losses [S1].

### Buying half of GEICO
In 1948 the Graham-Newman fund bought a 50 percent interest in GEICO for $712,500, which became its largest gain [S1]. The Securities and Exchange Commission ordered the fund to hand the shares on to its investors. An investor with 100 shares in the fund in 1948, who kept the GEICO stock, would have had $1.66 million by 1972 [S1].

### Hiring a student who first offered to work for free
Buffett offered to work for Graham for nothing and was turned down, reportedly because Graham judged him overvalued, and Graham-Newman hired him in 1954 [S1][S5]. We read this in reference pages, not in Graham's own words.

### Telling senators what he did not know
In March 1955 he told the Senate Banking Committee that the market's rise since 1953 came from a change of mood, not of earnings: the multiple paid per dollar of earnings had gone from about 8 to about 14 while earnings barely moved [S6]. Asked how a cheap stock ever gets noticed, he said the market catches up with value but how that happens was one of the mysteries of the business [S6].

### Closing the fund and simplifying
Graham-Newman closed in 1956 when he retired from active investing [S1]. In his 1976 interview he said he had lost most of his interest in fine detail and that a few simple techniques were enough, and he suggested funds start from an index [S7].

## Where he stood

**Can anyone predict the market?** No. In 1976 he said people do not succeed in forecasting it [S7].

**Are markets efficient?** He doubted the academic theory had much to do with real results [S7]. Buffett's 1984 talk argued that Graham's students beat the market far more often than chance allows [S5]. Late in life, though, Graham did lean towards simpler, broader methods, and one reference says he moved towards the efficient-market view [S3].

**What should big funds do?** He suggested funds start from an index of about 100 to 150 large stocks, then make changes only if the managers take personal responsibility for them [S7]. He backed index funds decades before they existed [S1].

**What did he think of Wall Street?** Very little, by 1976. He said he had no confidence in how Wall Street people would behave, since greed, hope and fear would last as long as people did [S7].

**Should money be backed by more than gold?** He proposed a currency backed by a basket of commodities, an idea that drew fresh interest after 2008 [S1].

## His work and record

- Born Benjamin Grossbaum in London on May 9, 1894. His family moved to New York when he was one and changed the name to Graham [S1].
- Graduated from Columbia as salutatorian, after entering at 16 [S1].
- Ran the Graham-Newman fund with Jerome Newman from 1936. It closed in 1956 when he retired from active investing [S1].
- *Security Analysis*, with David Dodd, 1934. Graham and Dodd revised it through 1962 [S3].
- *The Intelligent Investor*, 1949, revised up to 1971–72. The 1973 edition carried a preface by Buffett [S2].
- His 1948 purchase of half of GEICO became his biggest gain [S1].
- Taught at Columbia Business School and later at UCLA. Buffett offered to work for him for free and was turned down as overvalued, before being hired at Graham-Newman in 1954 [S1][S5].
- Helped make security analysis a profession, and helped set up what became the CFA designation [S1].
- Died in Aix-en-Provence, France, on September 21, 1976, aged 82 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Too little weight on growth.** Buffett and Charlie Munger saw his methods as necessary but not enough, because he gave too little weight to a business's future growth [S1].
- **Formulas age.** His 1962 value formula ignores interest rates, which change what any stream of earnings is worth [S3].
- **He changed his mind.** By the 1970s he had lost interest in the detailed analysis that made his name [S3][S7]. Readers who treat his early methods as fixed rules miss that he kept revising them.
- **The catch-up mystery.** He could not explain how a cheap stock's price comes to meet its value, only that it usually does [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- He was built for avoiding loss. For a young business, a bold product bet or anything with no earnings yet, his tests may say "no" to things that later win big.
- His world was one of factories, assets and steady dividends. Brands, software and networks are harder to value his way.
- Cheap can stay cheap. A margin of safety guards against error, not against a business that is quietly getting worse.
- Pair him with someone who sees growth, customers and quality, so the council doesn't only buy what is cheap.
