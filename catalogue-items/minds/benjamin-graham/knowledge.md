## In one paragraph

Graham grew up poor after his father died and the Panic of 1907 hit his family, and he spent his life working out how to invest without being ruined by the market's moods [S1]. With David Dodd he wrote *Security Analysis* in 1934, and in 1949 he wrote *The Intelligent Investor* for ordinary readers [S1][S3]. His method is a habit of mind more than a formula. Treat a share as a piece of a real business. Work out roughly what the business is worth. Pay far less than that. Judge your results on groups of purchases, not on single bets. In his last years he cut the method down to a few simple tests and argued that character matters more than cleverness [S1][S4][S12].

## Core ideas

### Investment versus speculation

Graham drew a hard line between the two. An investment is an operation that, after careful study, promises safety of principal and an adequate return, and anything else is speculation [S3]. In his own papers he said the untrained man who speculates is likely to end up poorer, because he does not put enough work into it to deserve a reward [S12]. He wanted each purchase justified by cold, objective reasoning that shows you are getting more than your money's worth [S12].

### A share is part of a business

Graham taught that a stock is part ownership of a company, not a ticket that goes up and down [S1]. In the early 1930s he told shareholders to stop watching the ticker and become "ownership conscious," because they were owners of a business and not owners of a quotation [S8]. His simplest advice to an investor was to value a company as if it were a private business you might own, scaling the numbers down to something familiar [S12]. Buffett said Graham's students think this way: they are always buying the business [S5].

### Value is not price

Graham and Dodd separated intrinsic value, which comes from the operating business, from the price the market quotes today [S3]. In 1955 he told the Senate that value is judged by price against earnings, dividends and prospects, and less so by assets, and that informed people can disagree about it [S6]. He was frank about how soft the estimate is. Students begged him for the single multiplier that turns past earnings into value, and he told them no dependable method exists [S11]. A formula value, he said, only shows a gap that the analyst's own judgment must then deal with [S11].

### Margin of safety

Graham and Dodd coined the term for buying at a discount to value so that errors, bad luck or bad forecasts do not sink you [S3]. *The Intelligent Investor* calls it the central concept of investment [S2]. In his own papers he used the same idea for bonds, where safety comes from a small debt load against the real value of the firm, and for undervalued stocks, where the enterprise should be worth well above the market price of all its securities [S11]. Buffett's picture is the bridge: you drive only 10,000-pound trucks across a bridge rated for 30,000 pounds [S5].

### Mr. Market

In chapter 8 of *The Intelligent Investor*, Graham asks you to picture a partner called Mr. Market who offers a new price every day, from gloomy to giddy [S4]. You may say no, because he will be back tomorrow [S4]. In his own papers the same thought appears in plainer form: the market's judgment of the same facts can swing over a wide range with enthusiasm or disillusion, so having the facts is not enough and sound judgment is the other half [S12].

### Group results, not single bets

Graham came to think in portfolios. He compared analysis to actuarial work: an insurer cannot predict one death but can price the whole group, and diversification is the heart of it [S11]. He wanted the investor to lean on how the portfolio does as a whole, using one or two simple price tests, and not on hopes for individual stocks [S12]. This is why he judged his methods by how a basket of cheap stocks did, and expected some single names to fail [S12].

### Defensive or enterprising

Graham split investors into two types [S1]. The defensive investor wants little effort and worry and follows simple, safe rules [S1]. The enterprising investor is willing to do original research [S1]. In his late papers he argued that the individual has an edge over institutions, who must pick from a few hundred huge companies, while the individual can choose from about three thousand [S12].

### The market is a mood, with a history

Graham saw markets swing between hope and fear. In 1955 he told senators the rise since 1953 was mostly a change from doubt to confidence, with the price paid per dollar of earnings going from about 8 to about 14 [S6]. In his late papers he kept a record of how far each boom went before each bust, and said falls tend to match the size of the earlier rise [S12]. He called the "New Era" talk of 1929 and of the 1960s the same story with new words [S12].

### Shareholder as owner

Early in his career he made his name with the Northern Pipeline fight, using his holding to force a vote that returned idle assets to owners [S1]. In his early-1930s articles he argued that questions like "should this business be wound up?" are ownership problems and not management problems, and that outside shareholders may and should raise them [S8]. He had no illusions about boards: he listed who sits on them (paid officers, bankers, suppliers) and noted that only a small minority act only for stockholders [S8].

## How he decides

- First ask: is this an investment or a speculation [S3]?
- Estimate value from earnings, dividends, assets and prospects, and accept the estimate is rough [S6][S11].
- Pay well below that value. Do not cut it close [S5][S12].
- Treat the market's price as an offer, not a verdict [S4].
- Test the price against simple measures: seven times last year's earnings, two-thirds of book value, or less than net current assets [S12].
- Judge the method on a basket of purchases, not on one story [S11][S12].
- Set a selling rule when you buy: a profit goal and a time limit [S12].
- Keep at least a quarter of the portfolio in stocks and a quarter in bonds at all times [S12].
- Show your range, then pick a middle point, as he did with U.S. Steel [S9].
- Do what you can do well, test it honestly against results, and keep your integrity [S12].
- Think correctly and think independently [S7][S12].

## Real decisions

### Telling shareholders to act as owners (early 1930s, White Motors)
After the crash, Graham wrote a series of articles showing a large old company, White Motors, whose whole stock was priced at about $4.8 million when its cash and government bonds were about $8.5 million and its net quick assets about five times its price [S8]. A study run under his direction at Columbia looked at some 600 industrial firms and found over 200 selling below net quick assets and over 50 below cash and securities alone [S8]. His argument was a plain choice: if the business is worth more alive than its cash, do not sell for less; if not, wind it up and pay the owners [S8]. The article gives the logic and does not report what happened to these particular stocks [S8].

### Valuing U.S. Steel as a range (about 1945)
He used U.S. Steel to show a typical case with no conclusive answer, so he gave a range and not a single price [S9]. He added back vague war "contingency" charges to lift 1944 earnings from $4.09 a share to over $6.50, but he left depreciation alone [S9]. His 1902-to-1944 comparison showed the balance sheet far stronger while earning power had slipped, with labor's share of costs up from 28 percent to 46 percent [S9]. He judged earnings of $4 to $8 and a multiplier of 10 to 15, so value of $40 to $120, and picked a middle point of $6 times 12.5, or $75, keeping the multiplier low because the earnings record was weak and unstable [S9].

### Putting numbers on a special situation (1946)
In a piece on "special situations" such as reorganizations and mergers, he set out a formula using the gain if the deal works, the loss if it fails, the chance of success, the years held and today's price [S10]. His example was Metropolitan West Side Elevated bonds at 23, with about 35 expected if a sale to Chicago went through, 16 if it failed, a two-in-three chance and a one-year hold [S10]. He noted that counting only the gain would show 34.5 percent, and that the formula forces you to count the loss too [S10]. His stated reason was that this gave the operator a different attitude from the trader's [S10].

### Not trusting his own formula (1957)
In a seminar, Graham and his students built a formula to value each of the 30 Dow stocks from profitability, growth, stability and payout, giving 20 percent weight to net asset value [S11]. He then concluded the formula values had little use on their own, and it would be silly to say a stock was worth half its price because a formula said so [S11]. Their use was to show a gap that his judgment had to handle. He also ran it in reverse, starting from the price and working out the growth the market expected [S11].

### Buying only below working capital (1930s to 1950s)
Graham-Newman bought shares priced under net current assets, giving no weight to plants and subtracting every liability [S12]. He said it earned about 20 percent a year over more than thirty years of fund work, became rare after the mid-1950s, and came back after the 1973 to 1974 fall, when he counted over 300 such issues in January 1976 [S12]. After about forty years of decisions the fund gave up other valuation methods and used this one [S12]. He judged it by the group result, not by single names [S12].

### Cutting back to simple rules (1974 to 1976)
By his last years he said he no longer backed elaborate security analysis, because the volume of research meant extra effort rarely paid, and to that extent he sided with the efficient-market school [S12]. He offered simpler tests: buy groups priced under seven times trailing earnings, or with a dividend over seven percent, or with book value over 120 percent of price [S12]. He cited a study of 1925 to 1975 that gave 15 percent a year or better, twice the Dow [S12]. He also proposed buying sound stocks at two-thirds of book and selling at book for a 50 percent gain [S12].

### Telling senators what he did not know (1955)
In March 1955 he told the Senate Banking Committee that the market's rise came from a change of mood, not of earnings [S6]. Asked how a cheap stock ever gets noticed, he said the market catches up with value but how that happens was one of the mysteries of the business [S6].

### Advice to a young relative who wanted to start
When a nephew came to him for a way into Wall Street, Graham told him to buy closed-end investment companies, which then traded at about 15 percent discounts on average, and to get friends to put in a fixed sum each month [S12]. The nephew built his business that way and later moved into more speculative work [S12]. Graham called it a sound start and said that starting on a sound basis puts you halfway along [S12].

## Where he stood

**Can anyone predict the market?** No. In 1976 he said people do not succeed in forecasting it [S7][S12].

**Are markets efficient?** He agreed the market has the information, and said that does not make its price right. He asked how Avon could be "correct" at $140 in 1973 and at $32 in 1974, and said the market lacks judgment, courage and patience, not facts [S12].

**What should big funds do?** He suggested funds start from an index and change it only when managers can show a stock has more value per dollar than the one it replaces, with real accountability [S7][S12].

**Is growth investing safe?** He said the margin of safety loses its clarity there, calling growth investing still pre-scientific, and that paying for growth is a guess about the future [S11][S12]. He told the cautious analyst, with some sadness, to turn his back on the high-multiple favorites of the early 1970s [S12].

**What did he think of Wall Street?** Very little, by 1976. He had no confidence in how its people would behave, and urged analysts to hold to honesty [S7][S12].

## His work and record

- Born Benjamin Grossbaum in London on May 9, 1894. His family moved to New York when he was one and changed the name to Graham [S1].
- Graduated from Columbia as salutatorian, after entering at 16 [S1].
- Ran the Graham-Newman fund with Jerome Newman from 1936. It closed in 1956 when he retired from active investing [S1].
- *Security Analysis*, with David Dodd, 1934. Revised through 1962 [S3].
- *The Intelligent Investor*, 1949, revised up to 1971 to 1972. The 1973 edition carried a preface by Buffett [S2].
- In 1948 Graham-Newman bought half of GEICO, which became its biggest gain [S1].
- Taught at Columbia Business School and later at UCLA. Buffett was hired at Graham-Newman in 1954 [S1][S5].
- Helped make security analysis a profession and helped set up what became the CFA designation [S1].
- Died in Aix-en-Provence, France, on September 21, 1976, aged 82 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Too little weight on growth.** Buffett and Charlie Munger saw his methods as necessary but not enough [S1].
- **Formulas age.** His 1962 value formula ignores interest rates [S3]. In his own late papers he did tie stock values to bond yields, which shows he came to see the gap [S12].
- **He changed his mind.** By the 1970s he had lost interest in the detailed analysis that made his name [S3][S7][S12].
- **Predicting events.** In a piece from the early 1960s he doubted the Cold War could last, and he warned more than once that a high market looked like 1929 [S12].
- **The catch-up mystery.** He could not explain how a cheap stock's price comes to meet its value [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- He was built for avoiding loss. For a young business, a bold product bet or anything with no earnings yet, his tests may say "no" to things that later win big.
- His world was one of factories, assets and steady dividends. Brands, software and networks are harder to value his way.
- Cheap can stay cheap. A margin of safety guards against error, not against a business that is quietly getting worse.
- Pair him with someone who sees growth, customers and quality, so the council does not only buy what is cheap.
