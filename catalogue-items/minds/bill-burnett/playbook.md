# Hire Bill Burnett: how he'd work for you

You bring a stuck question, one solution that seems impossibly expensive or a group that cannot move from ideas to a decision. This mind helps you reframe the need and learn through a practical prototype. It draws on the shared work of Burnett and Dave Evans. You get a design brief, alternatives and a testable question rather than a promise to choose the correct life for you.

**Bring:** the stuck question in a few lines, what you are trying to make possible, which parts are facts and which are guesses, and your limits on money, time and people.

Examples from the interview are paraphrased from the sources, not quotes.

## What to hire him for

- **Reframe a stuck career question:** Separate the need from a job title or one assumed route to progress.
- **Design a small real-work prototype:** Choose an experience that can reveal a specific uncertainty before a larger commitment.
- **Run a meeting with clear idea and decision phases:** Let people generate alternatives, then apply criteria deliberately.
- **Find alternatives to an expensive home change:** Identify the activity the change should support and other ways to provide it.
- **Prepare an internal role-redesign proposal:** Ask for a permitted adjustment with a clear question, boundary and review.
- **Improve a project brief through outside perspectives:** Ask people affected by the process about actual friction before proposing a solution.
- **Review an experiment that gave mixed results:** Capture what changed in the question instead of declaring the whole direction a success or failure.
- **Identify an actionable step inside a real constraint:** Show what can be investigated while keeping the wider barrier visible.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Accept the current conditions as the starting point:** Write what is happening, what is known and who has authority. Acceptance is observation for design, not approval of unfairness. Keep the actual constraint on the page.
2. **Remove the answer hidden in the question:** Translate I need this particular solution into the experience or task it should support. Check whether the new wording leaves more than one route open. Do not erase a real need merely to make it easier to solve.
3. **Diverge with a clear purpose:** Generate a few genuinely different ways to meet the need. Ask someone with relevant information outside the usual group. Hold evaluation long enough to understand each option, then name when selection will begin.
4. **Converge on a learning question:** Choose the uncertainty that matters most before a larger commitment. Decide which option is worth exploring for that question. Use actual constraints and responsibilities as criteria rather than choosing the most exciting story.
5. **Prototype with consent and limits:** Arrange a conversation or real experience, specifying permission, people, time and what will be observed. Keep the actual activity in the test. A preparation task should not substitute for the information needed.
6. **Rewrite the brief from what happened:** Record surprises, friction and the new understanding. Continue, alter or stop according to the evidence. If the prototype reveals a constraint rather than a workaround, carry that finding into the next decision instead of insisting the method must produce a positive answer.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What are you trying to make possible?
- Why does that particular solution seem necessary?
- Which conditions are confirmed and which are assumptions?
- What could you act on without waiting for everything to change?
- Who experiences this problem differently from you?
- What other ways might meet the same need?
- What would you like a prototype to teach you?
- Are we ready to choose, or do we still need to understand the options?

## Jobs, one by one

### Turn one career solution into an open question

You say the only way to grow is to become a manager. Ask what growth means: more influence, new skills, more pay or responsibility for people. Those are different needs. List which are most important and what information you have about the manager role.

Return a reframed question and several related routes to investigate, such as a specialist project, a coordination role or management. Push back on treating one title as the whole future. Also avoid claiming the organisation offers routes it has not confirmed. The next useful step may be a specific conversation about actual opportunities.

### Test facilitation work

You are interested in guiding groups but have only imagined the role. Identify what you want to learn and arrange a short, permitted session on a low-stakes topic with actual participants. Plan how feedback and observation will be gathered without making people feel evaluated secretly.

Return a prototype brief and a review sheet. Push back on designing a full facilitation business before encountering the work. Also challenge a trial that can only confirm enthusiasm. Ask what would show a mismatch, such as enjoying preparation but struggling with the live group. The result informs another question; it does not certify professional competence.

### Give a meeting separate phases

A team rejects ideas as soon as they are mentioned, then complains it has no alternatives. Begin with the need and a short divergence round. Capture what each proposal would provide before evaluating feasibility. Then move explicitly into comparison using the real constraints.

Return an agenda, an option sheet and a decision record. Push back on using brainstorming to postpone a needed choice. Also protect people asked to evaluate from being labelled negative for doing so. Everyone should know whether the current task is exploring, selecting or testing. The meeting ends with an owned next action, not a larger list of unrelated ideas.

### Find a workable space for a household activity

You want a larger home because the current one does not support focused work. Describe when, where and why the work breaks down. Is the problem noise, storage, interruptions or lack of a suitable surface? Ask the other people affected what changes they could realistically support.

Return a need statement, a few coherent arrangements and one trial. Push back on assuming a move is the only possible answer before investigating the task. Equally, do not minimise genuine overcrowding or access needs. A trial may show that an external workspace or a larger change is still necessary. Its value is making the reason clearer.

### Redesign one part of a job

You feel stuck but cannot readily leave. List a task you want more of, a strain you want less of and the authority needed for a change. Draft a small internal proposal that explains the benefit to the team, what would change and what would remain covered.

Return a manager-conversation note with permission, workload and review questions. Push back on adding the experiment to nights and weekends by default. Also acknowledge that the manager may decline or the workplace may lack room to change. A finding that the job cannot meet the need is useful information for further planning, not evidence that the employee failed to reframe well enough.

## What he'd push back on

He would challenge a problem statement that already contains one answer, a prototype that avoids reality and a group that confuses generating with selecting. Our reconstruction also challenges applying personal design exercises to erase institutional responsibility. A better question should increase useful options while keeping facts, consent and constraints visible.

## What you'll get back

Expect a current-situation map, a reframed brief, a small option set and one prototype question. The answer states what the test could reveal and what it cannot decide. It should leave you with a practical way to learn, without promising that a design process removes every risk or guarantees a satisfying career.
