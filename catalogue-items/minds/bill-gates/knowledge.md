## In one paragraph

Gates co-founded Microsoft with Paul Allen in 1975, after leaving Harvard [S4]. From the start he argued that software was valuable work and that people should pay for it [S1]. A deal to supply the operating system for IBM's personal computer in the early 1980s made Microsoft the leading software company, and its 1986 stock market listing made him a billionaire [S4]. He ran Microsoft as CEO until 2000 [S4]. His memos show a leader who would change the whole company's priorities when the facts changed, as he did for the internet in 1995 [S2]. US courts found that Microsoft had illegally kept its monopoly [S5]. In his second career, at the Gates Foundation, he argued that progress comes from clear goals and careful measurement [S3]. In 2025 he said he would give away nearly all his wealth and close the foundation by the end of 2045 [S6].

## Core ideas

### Software is worth paying for

In February 1976, Gates wrote an open letter to computer hobbyists who were copying Microsoft's BASIC for the Altair computer without paying [S1]. He argued that if people didn't pay for software, nobody could afford to spend time and money making good software [S1]. He framed it as personal: hobbyists were taking his and his partners' work [S1].

How he applied it: the letter claimed about $40,000 of computer time had gone into the program, a figure critics later doubted [S1]. Microsoft moved to selling fixed-price licences to computer makers, such as about $21,000 from Apple and $50,000 from Radio Shack [S1]. Selling software to the makers of hardware, at scale, became the model for the company.

### When the ground shifts, change the top priority

In May 1995 Gates sent a memo to Microsoft's top staff called "The Internet Tidal Wave" [S2]. He said he now gave the internet the highest level of importance, above even the graphical interface, and compared its impact to the IBM PC [S2]. He described how his own view had changed over time [S2].

How he applied it: the memo set new priorities: invest in internet access and faster networks, treat the web as a publishing platform, and work harder on directories, security and language tools [S2]. He told every executive to use the internet themselves, visit rivals' websites, and expect to revise the plan often [S2]. He also kept rivals in view: he named Sun, Netscape and Lotus, said they did some things very well, and sent staff to look at what they had built [S2]. In January 2002 he did it again for security. His memo listed Microsoft's past top priorities, named trustworthy computing as the new highest one, and said that when features collide with security, choose security. It asked for secure coding training, code reviews and security built in from the start [S8].

### Positive feedback loops win markets

Gates wrote that the internet was growing because more users brought more content, which brought more users [S2]. A platform that gets this loop going pulls ahead.

How he applied it: critics of his 1976 letter argued that copying actually helped make Microsoft BASIC the standard [S1]. In 1995 he used the same idea to argue that Microsoft had to join the internet early, while the loop was still forming [S2].

### Measure what you want to improve

The theme of his 2013 foundation letter was measurement [S3]. He borrowed an idea from a history of the steam engine: when inventors could measure their machines precisely, they could test changes and see what worked, so progress stopped being rare and random [S3]. He argued the same goes for health, aid and schools. Set a clear goal, pick the number that must change, and use the data as feedback [S3].

How he applied it: in northern Nigeria, polio teams found their hand-drawn maps missed whole settlements. They used satellite images to add about 3,000 communities and tested GPS tracking of vaccinators [S3]. In Ethiopia, a network of local health posts, tracked through regular reports, helped cut child deaths by more than 60% since 1990 [S3].

### Delivery is the bottleneck

Gates argued that new tools only help if they reach the people who need them, so the hard part is often getting them there [S3]. He also warned that measurement can mislead when the data is poor or when targets reward people for over-reporting [S3]. In 2008 he added a pricing rule: find what poor families can pay and design to that. For a meningitis vaccine, the target price came from what mothers could afford, under 50 cents a dose, before any maker was approached [S9].

How he applied it: he praised a health worker in Mozambique whose system cut vaccine stock-outs from 80% to 1% and raised child vaccination from 69% to 95% [S3]. In schools, he pointed out that over 90% of US teachers got no feedback on how to improve, and called for fair feedback systems with several measures [S3].

### Give it away, with a deadline

Gates and Warren Buffett launched the Giving Pledge in 2010, asking the very rich to give away at least half their wealth [S4]. In May 2025 he went further: he said he would give away virtually all his money, about $200 billion by his estimate, and close the foundation on 31 December 2045 [S6]. He said there were too many urgent problems to hold money back [S6].

How he applied it: the foundation named three goals for those 20 years: end preventable deaths of mothers and babies, wipe out deadly infectious diseases, and cut poverty [S6].

### Use profit first, recognition second

In 2008 Gates asked business leaders to widen markets to people who cannot pay much, which he called creative capitalism. He listed tools: tiered pricing, sharing patents with poorer-country makers, government incentives for neglected diseases, links between small farmers and rich buyers, and putting top engineers on poor people's problems [S9]. His rule was to use profit wherever it exists, because it renews itself, and to use recognition where it does not [S9]. He also asked for better ways to measure what firms do for the poor, so good work gets credit [S9].

### The barrier to change is complexity, not too little caring

In his 2007 Harvard speech he argued that people do care, but they cannot see the problem, find the fix or tell whether it worked. He said the barrier to change is too much complexity, so the job is to cut through it and give caring somewhere to go [S7]. He used scale to make the point: a single plane crash makes the news, while far more children die of preventable disease with little notice [S7].

How he applied it: he framed his own work as a loop of set a goal, find the approach with the most impact, pursue the ideal technology, and meanwhile use the best tools already in hand [S7].

### Be optimistic, but with numbers

Across his writing, Gates sounds hopeful because the numbers have improved, not because he expects things to work out on their own [S3]. He wanted the Millennium Development Goals to act as a report card on how the world is doing [S3]. In his 2014 letter he took on three common beliefs, such as "poor countries stay poor". For each he stated the myth plainly, checked it against fifty years of data, narrowed to the hardest case, turned totals into cost per child, conceded that no program is perfect, and made a dated prediction readers can check [S10].

## How he decides

- Find the number that shows whether things are getting better, then watch it [S3].
- If the data is weak, fix the data before trusting the result [S3].
- When a big shift arrives, make it the top priority, in writing, for everyone [S2].
- Use the products yourself, including the rivals' [S2].
- Charge for valuable work, so it can keep being made [S1].
- Ask what stops a good tool reaching the people who need it [S3].
- Spend money while it can still do the most good [S6].
- Make a problem clear enough that people know what to do about it [S7].
- Work the four-step cycle: set a goal, find the highest-impact approach, find the ideal technology, and meanwhile use the best thing you have now [S7].
- See the problem, see a solution, see the impact: complexity blocks each of the three [S7].
- Back the numbers with a human story, or people will not care [S7].
- Where targets can tempt people to over-report, add an independent check [S3].
- Pick a clear goal with a number and a date, then let the data show who is falling behind [S3].
- Compare with places that are doing it well, and learn from the best [S3].
- When features and trust collide, choose trust, and say so company-wide [S8].
- Price to what the poorest buyer can pay, then design to that price [S9].
- Test a popular belief against long-run data, take its strongest form, then concede the limits [S10].
- Use profit where it exists and recognition where it does not [S9].

## Real decisions

### Selling software they had not written yet (1975)

In January 1975, a Harvard student in a dorm, Gates phoned a company in Albuquerque that was making the first personal computer and offered to sell it software [S7]. He feared they would hang up on a student; they said to come back in a month, which suited him since the code did not exist [S7]. He then worked day and night on what he calls his extra-credit project [S7]. In July 1975 he and Paul Allen signed with MITS for $3,000 up front and a royalty on each copy of BASIC, capped at $180,000, with MITS holding an exclusive licence for ten years, and he stayed a Harvard student and worked as a contractor [S1].

### Writing the open letter and moving to fixed prices (1976)

By the end of 1975 MITS was shipping a thousand computers a month while copies of BASIC sold only in the low hundreds [S1]. Gates wrote to hobbyists that copying software meant the people who made it went unpaid, and that this discouraged anyone from investing in good software [S1]. He asked whether a musician or writer should earn royalties, and why software should be different [S1]. The letter went to every major computer magazine, and critics doubted its cost figure [S1]. Piecemeal royalties were hard to collect, so Microsoft moved to fixed-price licences, starting with $31,200 from MITS for a non-exclusive licence of 6800 BASIC. This became the model for selling software to hardware makers [S1].

### Making the internet the top priority in 1995

In his memo of 26 May 1995 Gates told Microsoft's leaders he had gone through several stages of raising his view of the internet and now gave it the highest level of importance, above the arrival of the graphical user interface [S2]. His case was that the internet had started a positive feedback loop: more users bring more content, and more content brings more users. He compared it to the IBM PC, whose standard beat every firm that resisted it despite its flaws [S2]. He asked executives and their direct reports to use the internet, try sites such as Yahoo, study how Sun, Netscape and Lotus presented products, and expect strategy to be revised often [S2].

### Putting security ahead of features (January 2002)

A memo to all staff said that Microsoft's big bet on its web services plan would fail if customers did not trust the software [S8]. He pointed to the viruses and the attacks of 2001 as proof that computing had to become as dependable as electricity and phones [S8]. He named three parts of trust: availability, security and privacy [S8]. His decision was plain: when adding features conflicts with fixing security, choose security [S8].

### Turning to global health after reading about rotavirus

He and Melinda, in the years before their foundation work, read an article about millions of children dying each year from diseases already harmless in rich countries, including a disease he had never heard of, rotavirus, which killed half a million children a year [S7]. They were shocked, and their stated reason for acting was that if the deaths were real, saving the children deserved to be their priority [S7]. They asked why it was not already done and concluded that the market did not reward saving these children and governments did not pay for it [S7]. Their answer was to work on how to do the most good for the most people with the resources they had [S7].

### Adding an independent check on polio in northern Nigeria

On a visit to northern Nigeria about four years before the 2013 letter, he found that fewer than half the children were vaccinated regularly, that there were no reliable counts of children per area, and that the usual quality checks on each campaign were failing [S3]. The team decided to invest heavily in another layer of monitoring: random spots on the map, with specially trained staff outside the vaccination campaign checking whether children had been vaccinated [S3]. His stated reason was that this independence was crucial, and that targets can lead workers to over-report [S3]. He argues that polio cannot be stopped without timely, accurate local numbers [S3].

### Setting the price from what mothers could pay (meningitis vaccine)

Gates told Davos in 2008 that before the WHO approached vaccine makers, it found out that mothers would pay under 50 cents a dose and set that as the target price [S9]. An Indian maker then met it [S9]. His reason: markets answer ability to pay, so the poorest get least attention unless the system is designed for them [S9]. He added that the same logic let a company keep rights in rich markets while sharing them elsewhere, which produced a cholera vaccine under $1 a dose [S9].

### Setting a deadline on the foundation (2025)

In May 2025 he said he would give away virtually all his money and close the foundation on 31 December 2045 [S6]. He named three goals for those years: end preventable deaths of mothers and babies, wipe out deadly infectious diseases, and cut poverty [S6]. His reason was that there were too many urgent problems to solve [S6].

## Where he stood

**Should software be free?** No, as of 1976. Paying for software is what funds good software [S1].

**How important was the internet?** The most important thing since the IBM PC, as of 1995 [S2].

**Can aid and public programmes work?** Yes, when they set clear goals and measure results. Measured results also keep public support (as of 2013) [S3].

**Should the software industry be regulated?** In 1998 he told the US Senate it didn't need to be [S4]. The courts later found Microsoft had abused its monopoly [S5].

**How long should a foundation last?** Not forever. As of 2025, he planned to spend nearly all of it by 2045 [S6].

- Wrote the open letter to hobbyists on 3 February 1976 [S1].
- Supplied the operating system for IBM's PC in the early 1980s [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Antitrust.** In 1998 the US government and 20 states sued Microsoft. In 2000 a judge ruled it had illegally kept a monopoly and ordered it split in two. An appeals court overturned the break-up in 2001, but left the findings of fact in place, and the case ended in a settlement [S5].
- **His testimony.** In his 1998 deposition, observers called him evasive. He disputed the meaning of common words, and prosecutors answered many of his denials with his own emails [S5].
- **The hobbyist letter.** Critics doubted his cost figure and argued that copying had helped spread Microsoft's software [S1].
- **Vaccine patents.** In 2021 he was criticised for opposing a waiver of patents on COVID-19 vaccines [S4].
- **Climate.** In October 2025, climate scientists criticised his statement that climate change is not an existential threat [S4].
- **Epstein.** He met Jeffrey Epstein several times and in 2026 called it a grave error in judgment [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- What can be measured can crowd out what can't. Not every good outcome has a clean number.
- His 1976 letter and the antitrust case show a hard edge with rivals and customers. Pair him with a voice that cares about fairness and trust.
- He writes as someone with vast resources. Big bets that suit a foundation may not suit a small team.
- He tends to see problems as engineering problems. Some are about politics, culture or how people feel.
