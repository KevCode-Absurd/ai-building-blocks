# Bill Mollison: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Historical methods cite the interview and edited course transcript. Daily exercises are ours. A relationship diagram is a design hypothesis, not an engineering, safety or ecological certification.

## In one paragraph

Bill Mollison, who lived from 1928 to 2016, co-originated permaculture with David Holmgren and taught it internationally. Their Permaculture One appeared in 1978; Mollison's later works included Permaculture Two and the 1988 designers' manual. His practical contribution is to design relationships rather than collect isolated features: examine what elements need and provide, where they belong and how much care they require. A useful plan explains why things are placed together and what work the arrangement changes. [S3][S4]

## Core ideas

### Design the connection, not just the object

In the 1980 interview, Mollison illustrates design through relationships among a shelter, growing spaces, water and animals. The point is their functional connections rather than simply owning each element. [S1]

Our volunteer-workspace exercise lists the real sequence: obtain materials, prepare, use, clean, return and record anything missing. Identify where an object needs something another part of the arrangement already provides. A storage box beside a task is useful only if its contents, access and return process fit. The output is a relationship map rather than a shopping list. Do not assume that more connections are always better. A link can also introduce contamination, conflict, noise or extra coordination. Explain the proposed mechanism and remove any connection that creates an unsupported burden.

### An element may serve several useful functions

The course transcript describes placement that makes use of multiple functions, with observation as part of the designer's role. It distinguishes design placement from simply telling someone how to garden or construct a feature. [S2]

Our new-feature review asks what the proposed element actually does in this setting. A work surface might support preparation, sorting and a place to review the task, but those functions may conflict at busy times. List the benefits, demands and conflicts rather than praising multifunctionality automatically. Ask whether the existing arrangement can serve the purpose with less change. The output is a function card showing what the element requires, provides and displaces. Its several uses should be real and compatible, not added to the brief to justify a purchase already desired.

### Place frequent care within easy reach

The interview explains zones through frequency of attention and access. The course transcript likewise connects distance with the intensity and seasonality of use. [S1][S2]

Our tool-placement exercise records what is used daily, weekly, seasonally and rarely. Consider the person reaching, carrying and returning it. Put frequently needed items where they can be obtained appropriately, while retaining safety, access and household preferences. The closest position is not always suitable: a hazardous item may need secure storage, and a shared route must remain clear. The output is a placement proposal tied to actual tasks and a record of expected trips saved. A drawing of concentric rings is unnecessary if a simple list explains the care pattern more clearly.

### Outside conditions are part of the design

Mollison distinguishes zone planning from sector planning in the interview: one concerns attention within the arrangement, the other influences entering from outside. The course includes observation of site-specific conditions. [S1][S2]

Our garden brief notes observed light, wind, water, access and neighbouring uses, with dates and uncertainty. Ask which conditions change by season and which require expert assessment. Avoid designing from a single pleasant visit or assuming a generic climate description settles the actual site. The output is a conditions map and questions for the relevant designer or local specialist. It should not prescribe earthworks, drainage, fire protection or species introduction from historical examples. Noticing an influence makes it a design question; it does not prove the proposed response is safe or effective.

### Client needs and the site's limits must meet

The edited course transcript asks designers to gather client requirements, maps and local regulations, analyse problems and check whether the proposal meets the needs. It also emphasises protecting the site while responding to a client. [S2]

Our shared-resource brief begins with the task people want to do and the constraints they cannot ignore. A group may want more activity than its room, storage or volunteer capacity can support. Compare a different layout, reduced scope and outside support. Do not assume the answer must be a clever physical feature. The output identifies requirements, conflicts and decisions needing agreement. A design should serve the people who will live with it without pretending their preference overrides permissions, other users or environmental conditions. Explain where a desired outcome needs a different method or cannot responsibly be promised.

### Teaching should leave practical capability

Right Livelihood describes Mollison's emphasis on seminars and practical training for local people. The association memorial describes teaching and efforts to support teachers who could continue locally; ABC's tribute reports his influence as a public speaker and educator. [S3][S4][S5]

Our career-learning exercise asks a willing group what small design problem it wants help understanding. Document a task sequence, map a placement issue and prepare options under suitable guidance. Leave the group with an understandable record, not a mysterious drawing only its author can interpret. Credit existing knowledge and explain limits. The output may be a useful brief rather than a built intervention. A learner can discover whether they enjoy observation, spatial reasoning or coordination without claiming professional competence after a short project. Practical capability includes knowing what requires somebody else's expertise.

## How he decides

These working rules adapt the interview and course material into ordinary design questions. They are our synthesis and do not reproduce a complete permaculture design course. [S1][S2]

- Begin with the task, client requirements and actual setting before selecting a feature.
- List what each element needs, produces and requires in care. Look for useful matches and conflicts.
- Locate frequent attention within appropriate reach, while retaining safety, privacy and accessible movement.
- Map external influences separately from internal care patterns. Ask which site observations need better evidence.
- Explain the proposed functional connection and what effort or dependency it changes.
- Check the whole requirement and leave a usable account of responsibilities, implementation questions and limits.

Our map of a shared repair area may show that a missing cleaning station, rather than a shortage of tools, interrupts every task. Another arrangement may have appropriate storage but depend on one key holder. Those are different design problems. Examine support for important functions: can work continue appropriately if a usual person or resource is unavailable? This is our extension of functional analysis, not a claim of guaranteed resilience. A backup needs its own access and care. Buying duplicates that cannot be found or maintained may add clutter without supporting the function at all.

## Where he stood

The interview presents a design ambition of working with the setting and reducing unnecessary external effort. The award record describes permaculture as a broad design approach with stability, diversity and conservation among its aims. Those aims are different from proof that a particular arrangement achieves them. [S1][S3]

Our everyday transfer uses the modest part of that ambition. Someone can inspect repeated carrying, searching and care in an existing home or workspace. Ask what the setting supports and which change would make the task easier to sustain. A person with little space need not imitate a farm. A shared table, storage area or handover can still be examined through needs and outputs. Keep comfort and pleasure among the functions, and recognise the value of reliable services. Designing to reduce effort should not become a demand that people perform every task themselves.

## Their work and record

Right Livelihood establishes Mollison's dates as May 4, 1928 to September 24, 2016, his 1981 award and the 1978 foundation of the Permaculture Institute. It records the coauthored first book and later publications. [S3]

The Permaculture Association memorial describes his working relationship with Holmgren and his move into teaching. ABC's tribute records Holmgren's appreciation of his ecological thinking and the reach of his public explanation. The checked course pamphlet is explicitly edited from the 1981 Wilton course, not a newly written book or an unedited transcript. [S4][S5][S2]

## Where he was wrong or criticised

The 1980 interview includes very ambitious claims about production and an ideal requiring almost no maintenance. Those are historical statements of aspiration, not results that this profile can guarantee. The association memorial also acknowledges a provocative public style and that he was not universally liked. [S1][S4]

Our practical caution is stronger than the enthusiasm of some historical examples. A detailed anecdote does not establish that moving wildlife, altering water or combining animal and growing systems is appropriate today in another place. Some course passages include specific tactics that need current scientific, welfare, regulatory and technical scrutiny. [S2]

Use the functional question without copying an unsafe or unsuitable tactic. Do not equate an elegant map with actual productivity, ecological balance or safety. The checked sources are primarily an advocate's teaching and appreciative records; they are not independent validation of every technical assertion or a reason to dismiss contrary evidence.

## Blind spots to watch

An urge to find multiple functions can overcomplicate a small arrangement. Ask whether the connections work without constant supervision and who accepts the care. A simple dedicated object may be more suitable than a complicated multifunction system.

Historical descriptions can also overlook current access, welfare and environmental responsibilities. Keep the people doing the work and the wider setting visible. This portrait can produce a brief and relationship map; it cannot approve construction, certify land use, prescribe animal management or promise a self-running household. In a consequential application, competent local assessment and permissions remain part of making the design real.
