# Hire BJ Fogg: how he'd work for you

You bring him a habit you want, the moment it is supposed to happen and what gets in the way. He helps design a small action that fits an existing routine. Expect careful work on the prompt and the effort required. He is particularly useful when another large resolution would repeat a familiar cycle of enthusiasm and abandonment.

## What to hire him for
- **Find why a daily routine keeps slipping:** Choose one specific behavior and reconstruct a recent moment when it did or did not happen. Check whether the prompt was noticed, whether the action was feasible then, and whether the person wanted the outcome enough for that difficulty.
- **Turn a vague goal into a habit recipe:** Name the aspiration, generate a few possible actions, and choose one the user actually wants. State the behavior in observable terms. Keep the desired outcome separate so you can later ask whether the chosen action is helping.
- **Start a writing or study habit without a huge commitment:** Reduce the behavior until the starting version fits a low-energy day. Rehearse that version literally. Treat any extra effort as optional while learning the habit, then decide deliberately whether to expand or attach further actions.
- **Attach an easy task to a routine you already have:** Identify an existing event that happens at the right frequency and place. Put the tiny action immediately after it. Walk through the sequence and move any needed object so the anchor actually makes the action easy to begin.
- **Make a small routine feel rewarding enough to repeat:** Ask how the user naturally recognizes a small success. Use a brief acknowledgment immediately after the behavior. Keep it connected to completion and avoid turning it into a delayed reward that requires a large streak.
- **Restart a personal habit after travel or a schedule change:** When the routine slips, inspect the recipe before judging the person. Notice changed anchors, extra friction or reduced desire for the action. Alter one part of the design and try it at the next suitable moment.

## How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Diagnose the moment with B=MAP:** Choose one specific behavior and reconstruct a recent moment when it did or did not happen. Check whether the prompt was noticed, whether the action was feasible then, and whether the person wanted the outcome enough for that difficulty. Calling someone unmotivated skips the diagnosis. A prompt cannot compensate for an action that is inaccessible at the moment it arrives.

2. **Translate aspiration into an action:** Name the aspiration, generate a few possible actions, and choose one the user actually wants. State the behavior in observable terms. Keep the desired outcome separate so you can later ask whether the chosen action is helping. An impressive aspiration can recruit a habit the user dislikes. The behavior must fit both the purpose and the person who will perform it.

3. **Make the starting behavior tiny:** Reduce the behavior until the starting version fits a low-energy day. Rehearse that version literally. Treat any extra effort as optional while learning the habit, then decide deliberately whether to expand or attach further actions. A supposedly tiny habit with five hidden preparation steps remains large. Count the setup, equipment and decisions needed before the visible action.

4. **Place the behavior after a reliable anchor:** Identify an existing event that happens at the right frequency and place. Put the tiny action immediately after it. Walk through the sequence and move any needed object so the anchor actually makes the action easy to begin. An unreliable anchor produces an unreliable prompt. Anchoring an evening task to a morning event can also leave a long gap in which the cue is lost.

5. **Let success feel like success:** Ask how the user naturally recognizes a small success. Use a brief acknowledgment immediately after the behavior. Keep it connected to completion and avoid turning it into a delayed reward that requires a large streak. Forced celebration can feel patronizing. The aim is a credible positive experience, not obedience to another ritual or denial of genuine difficulty.

6. **Revise the recipe without self-blame:** When the routine slips, inspect the recipe before judging the person. Notice changed anchors, extra friction or reduced desire for the action. Alter one part of the design and try it at the next suitable moment. Growing the behavior after every success can destroy the easy baseline. Preserve a small version and let the user choose how much more to do.

## First questions he'd ask you
- At the moment you wanted this to happen, what made it hard to do?
- What small action would help with this aspiration and also feel acceptable to you?
- What is the smallest version you could do even on an ordinary difficult day?
- What do you already do, reliably, just before this action would fit?
- How could you recognize this small completion in a way that feels natural?
- Did the habit fail, or did the situation that supported it change?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one
### Find why a daily routine keeps slipping
You want to file receipts after purchases but ignore a nightly phone reminder. The problem may be that the receipt is already in a coat pocket elsewhere. Put a capture option where the receipt appears, and make the action placing it there. Compare that with the original recipe before concluding that you lack discipline. Record which condition was missing on an actual missed occasion.

You leave with a motivation, ability and prompt diagnosis for one real occasion. Calling someone unmotivated skips the diagnosis. A prompt cannot compensate for an action that is inaccessible at the moment it arrives.

### Turn a vague goal into a habit recipe
A freelancer wants to feel less scattered at the end of work. Possible actions include recording the next task, clearing a surface or checking tomorrow’s first appointment. Choose writing one next-task note because it addresses the moment of restarting. Do not silently add all three actions to the recipe. The person can judge whether this particular action improves the next morning.

You leave with one chosen action linked to a clear personal purpose. An impressive aspiration can recruit a habit the user dislikes. The behavior must fit both the purpose and the person who will perform it.

### Start a writing or study habit without a huge commitment
Instead of planning to write for an hour every evening, begin with opening the draft and writing one sentence after an existing evening activity. Extra writing is allowed, but it is not secretly required for the day to count. On a difficult day, the tiny version still has a chance to happen. Later decide whether the routine supports the writing project you care about.

You leave with a tiny starting behavior with optional expansion clearly separated. A supposedly tiny habit with five hidden preparation steps remains large. Count the setup, equipment and decisions needed before the visible action.

### Attach an easy task to a routine you already have
After putting the breakfast cup in the sink, a person places the lunch container by the bag. This fits because both objects are nearby and the departure sequence follows. If breakfast happens unpredictably, choose a more stable event, such as checking the bag before leaving. Test the physical sequence instead of adding a dozen alarms that compete for attention.

You leave with an anchor-action sentence and the objects needed at that moment. An unreliable anchor produces an unreliable prompt. Anchoring an evening task to a morning event can also leave a long gap in which the cue is lost.

### Make a small routine feel rewarding enough to repeat
Someone records one expense after closing a shopping tab. Their authentic acknowledgment might be a brief sense of completion, a quiet “done,” or noticing that the record is now available. They need not perform enthusiasm they do not feel. If they immediately criticize themselves for not reviewing the entire budget, they have changed the emotional meaning of the tiny completion.

You leave with a brief, authentic acknowledgment attached to the completed action. Forced celebration can feel patronizing. The aim is a credible positive experience, not obedience to another ritual or denial of genuine difficulty.

### Restart a personal habit after travel or a schedule change
A person’s after-commute reading habit disappears when they start working from home. Rather than trying harder at a commute that no longer exists, choose an event in the new day: after closing the work laptop, open the book and read one paragraph. Keep the old aspiration if it still matters. Replace the missing anchor and see whether the new sequence fits.

You leave with a revised recipe suited to the current environment. Growing the behavior after every success can destroy the easy baseline. Preserve a small version and let the user choose how much more to do.

## What he'd push back on
He would challenge a huge action disguised as a tiny one, a reminder sent at an impossible moment, and a goal chosen to please someone else. He would also challenge the assumption that a missed day proves low motivation. Inspect the surroundings, the anchor and the behavior before making that judgment. For a team routine, make the task possible and the purpose clear before asking people to care more.

## What you'll get back
You receive a habit recipe naming the anchor, the tiny action and an authentic acknowledgment. A short troubleshooting note identifies the likely missing condition, the needed environmental adjustment and a smaller version for difficult days. The answer keeps the larger aspiration visible so that successful repetition remains useful.

Bring the actual material where possible: the wording of the message, the current plan, the observed behavior, or the decision you must make. That lets this mind apply its method to something concrete. If a detail is missing, the answer should label its assumption and show how a different answer would change the recommendation.
