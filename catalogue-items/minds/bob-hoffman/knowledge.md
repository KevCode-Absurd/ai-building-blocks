# Bob Hoffman: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Bob Hoffman is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from his blog, The Ad Contrarian, mainly posts from December 2020 to 2021 and the sayings in its sidebar, plus his own website's biography [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5]. He has written seven books and many newsletters we didn't open, so treat this as a sample of his thinking [S5]. His birth year isn't in our sources. Treat each view as "as of" the date of its post.

## In one paragraph

Hoffman ran two independent ad agencies and the US arm of an international agency before leaving the business to write and speak [S5]. His blog, The Ad Contrarian, was named one of the most influential in marketing by Business Insider, and he has spoken in 24 countries, including to members of the British and European parliaments [S5]. His method is skepticism with a sense of humour. He asks who benefits from a claim, trusts plain logic when data is shaky, and treats advertising as a game of probabilities, not certainties [S2][S3]. His main targets are ad tech, online ad fraud, surveillance-based targeting, social media hype and marketers who believe precise but wrong numbers [S1][S3][S4]. He still believes in advertising, and in creative advertising in particular [S2].

## Core ideas

### Precise answers can be wrong

A saying on his blog sums up his stance: marketers prefer precise answers that are wrong to imprecise answers that are right [S1]. A number with decimal points feels safe. He wants marketers to ask whether it is true.

How he applied it: he closed a 2021 post by asking marketers what reason they have to believe the metrics they get [S3].

### Reason it through when data fails

When both sides of a debate wave numbers, Hoffman steps back and reasons from facts no one disputes [S3]. In his 2021 post on ad fraud, trade groups said fraud was small and shrinking while independent researchers put it above $60 billion [S3]. He listed plain facts: over $300 billion a year flows through automated systems, skilled hackers had just broken into secure government systems unseen, and no international body punishes cross-border ad fraud [S3]. From those, he concluded fraud is probably bigger and harder to spot than the industry admits [S3].

How he applied it: he was careful to say which parts were known and which weren't. Criminal theft is known; state involvement was unproven, but likely in his view [S3].

### Ask who is selling the story

He distrusts claims from parties that profit from them. In the fraud debate, he noted that the trade groups' confidence rested on detection vendors whose business depends on the problem looking under control [S3]. He criticised industry bodies and senior marketers for ignoring ad tech's harms [S1][S4].

How he applied it: he called on the leaders of US advertising trade groups and big advertisers' marketing chiefs to face the damage and reform ad tech quickly [S4].

### Ad tech has a cost beyond the ad

In 2021 he argued that ad tech collects data on people and feeds algorithms built to keep users engaged so platforms can sell more ads [S4]. Since sensational content holds attention longer, he said, these systems push people toward more extreme material and help divide society [S4]. He cited a 2020 Wall Street Journal report on an internal Facebook study as evidence [S4]. He called the advertising industry the hidden hand that pays for these practices [S4].

How he applied it: he raised his description of ad tech from "dangerous" to "disastrous" [S1][S4].

### Social media is overrated as a sales tool

His sidebar mocks social media as millions of disagreeable people looking for trouble [S1]. Another saying calls the web a much better yellow pages and a much worse television [S1]. In other words, it's excellent for finding things and weak at the broad, emotional work TV does.

How he applied it: he urged marketers to stop treating every new platform as a revolution [S1].

### Creativity matters, but only probably

In a December 2020 post he said he has no doubt creative advertising works better than dull advertising, and admitted he can't strictly prove it [S2]. Creative ads are more likely to succeed, not certain to; some brilliant campaigns have failed [S2]. Creativity without a purpose, he wrote, is self-indulgence [S2].

How he applied it: he warned against defining a creative ad as one that sold, since that makes the argument circular [S2].

### Awards are weak evidence

He said award-show judgments are scientifically weak because judges often know the agency, have read the coverage, and may know the results [S2]. He suggested a cleaner test: judge creativity blind, then compare the scores with business results [S2].

How he applied it: he admitted he had once used studies he now thinks were flawed, and said so in public [S2].

### Sales aren't the only measure

He rejected sales as the only yardstick for creative work [S2]. Advertising often holds sales and share steady rather than growing them, an idea he credited to Byron Sharp [S2]. Long-term brand effects are hard to see in short-term sales, so he defers to the long-run research of Sharp, Mark Ritson, and Les Binet and Peter Field [S2].

How he applied it: he summed it up with the rule that advertising has no always or never, only likelihoods [S2].

### Say what you can't prove

Hoffman makes his doubts part of the argument. In the 2020 post he said plainly that he could not prove his belief in creativity to a strict skeptic, then explained why he held it anyway [S2]. He qualifies claims with "in my opinion" and names his own biases [S2]. In the fraud post he split his case into facts, his own assertions, and conclusions, so readers could see which step they disagreed with [S3].

How he applied it: he proposed a better test of his own belief, blind judging of creativity compared with business results, even though no one had run it [S2]. For anyone using his method, the lesson is simple. Label each claim as known, likely or guessed before you argue for it.

## How he decides

- Who benefits if I believe this? [S3]
- Is this number precise, or true? [S1]
- What facts do both sides agree on, and where does logic lead from there? [S3]
- What's proven, and what's only likely? [S2][S3]
- Is this new channel a revolution, or a better yellow pages? [S1]
- Does the creative work have a purpose, or is it showing off? [S2]
- Judge over years, not weeks [S2].

## Where he stood

**Is creative advertising better?** Yes, as a probability, not a guarantee [S2].

**Can we trust online ad metrics?** Be skeptical. Fraud is probably bigger than reported [S3].

**Is ad tech neutral?** No. As of 2021 he saw it as harmful to society and funded by advertisers [S4].

**Do awards prove effectiveness?** Not on their own. The judging isn't blind [S2].

**Is it fair to argue without data?** Yes, if you say so. He wrote as a non-specialist using logic, and asked readers to test his steps rather than trust his credentials [S3].

**What should industry leaders do?** Face the problems their money pays for. As of 2021 he wanted trade groups and big advertisers to act fast on ad tech [S4].

**Should sales be the only test?** No. Much advertising maintains sales, and long-term effects hide in short-term data [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Logic without data.** His fraud argument is built on reasoning, not new measurements, as he says himself [S3]. A reader who wants hard numbers may find it unproven, and he agrees state involvement wasn't shown [S3].
- **One-sided on digital.** His jokes about social media and the web [S1] can undersell what targeted and digital channels do well, such as search and direct sales.
- **Strong claims on social harm.** His link between ad tech and social division [S4] rests on reports about one platform. Others may weigh the causes differently.
- **Rudeness.** Byron Sharp, a fan, joked that he wished he were brave enough to be this rude [S5]. The tone wins readers and loses some.

## Blind spots to watch

- He is better at spotting what's wrong than building a plan. Pair him with a strategist.
- His skepticism of digital can blind a team to channels that really work for them. Test, don't assume.
- He writes about the big ad industry. A small business may care less about fraud at scale and more about getting found.
- Skepticism can turn into cynicism. Keep his humour and his fairness, too.
