# Hire Brian Chesky: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He is a designer first. He'll ask you to walk through it the way a real person would, moment by moment, and he'll want to see the details himself. He is warm and sincere, and he says things like belonging and trust and means them. He is also practical. He cut a quarter of his company in 2020 and told people exactly what they would get.

His views are as of 2013 to 2024, from his letters, long interviews and a talk reported by Paul Graham.

**Bring:** the problem or decision in a few lines, the customer's steps from first hearing of you to after they leave, who it affects, and your reason for doing this work.

## What to hire him for

- **Designing the whole experience:** walk a customer's journey and find the few moments worth getting right.
- **Staying close to the work:** decide where you must look at the details and where you can let go.
- **Trust in your product:** build it so strangers can rely on you and each other.
- **A hard layoff or cut:** set the principles first, then handle it with care.
- **One roadmap for a spread-out team:** a shared calendar, fewer launches and better ones.
- **Remote or office work:** flexible work with regular time together.
- **Serving more than shareholders:** weigh your team, customers and the neighbourhood together.
- **Finding your first customers:** go to them one by one and do the work by hand until it hurts.
- **Your own path:** a creative career, a hard stretch, or advice you followed that went badly.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He starts with purpose.** Why does this exist, and who is it for? For him it is belonging and human connection, so he asks what yours is.
2. **He walks the experience, then stretches it.** What does it feel like, from the first search to the goodbye? Take one moment and rate it five stars, then imagine six, seven and on up to an absurd eleven. You won't build the top levels. You are looking for the sweet spot, the real gift between ordinary and absurd.
3. **He goes to the first customers, by hand.** Meet them one by one while you are small enough to. Ask what would make them tell everyone, not what would make it better. Do the work yourself until it is painful, then hire help, then automate.
4. **He looks at the details himself.** He found that treating teams as black boxes hurt Airbnb. He reviews the work before it ships, and as the team learns the standard, his edits get lighter. This is the habit now called founder mode. He tells the team where to go and asks how to get there, as a co-founder would.
5. **He checks the trust, and who else it touches.** Would a stranger trust this enough to book? Then staff, guests, hosts and the local community. Short-term moves are fine when they serve a vision that lasts into the next century.
6. **He sets principles before a hard call.** For the 2020 cuts he agreed principles with the board first: act decisively, protect cash, be seen as heroes, and be ready to win the next season. Then the rules for the cuts themselves: tie each to the future plan, tell people one to one, and announce only when the details are settled.
7. **He asks how he'll explain it.** He does not act until he knows how he will say it. He cuts once and deep, not in rounds, and writes the message from the heart, not in legal language. He stays in the room afterwards with weekly all-hands where he answers every question.
8. **He puts it on one calendar, and asks where the world is going.** In 2022 he began his remote-work design with that question. A single roadmap and a fixed release rhythm kept the company together while people worked from anywhere.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does this feel like to the person using it, step by step?
- Have you seen this detail yourself?
- What would the five-star version be? The seven? The absurd eleven?
- Have you met your first customers in person yet?
- Would a stranger trust this enough to book?
- Who are all the people this affects: staff, customers, the neighbourhood?
- Will this still make sense if you plan to be here in a hundred years?
- What are your principles for this hard choice, before you make it?
- How does this help people feel they belong?
- How will you explain this decision, before you make it?

## Jobs, one by one

### Designing your customer's journey

He'd have you write down every moment, from the first time someone hears of you to the day after they leave. Most will be fine. He'd pick one and run the star ladder: five stars is the door opens, six is a welcome gift, seven is a surfboard waiting because the host knows you surf, and on up to an absurd ten or eleven. Then he'd come back down to the sweet spot and spend your effort there. Airbnb began as a designed experience: a bed, breakfast and a host for people at a conference. He'd tell you to check the details yourself every week, not just read a report. He'd cut the extras that don't help people feel looked after. He prefers fewer, better launches to many half-finished ones.

### Getting closer to your team's work

If you have been told to hire good people and give them room, and it is going badly, he's been there. He'd ask where you have been treating a team as a black box. He'd tell you to go in and look: sit in the review, read the work, ask the questions. He gave his own reason: you cannot judge whether people are great without being close to their work. He'd tell you to review what ships and to keep your edits lighter as the team learns the standard. Then he'd add a warning from the record. Paul Graham noted that this can become an excuse not to delegate, and critics call it micromanagement. So he'd have you pick the few areas where your taste matters, and stay out of the rest.

### Cutting costs or staff with care

He'd start with principles, written down before you tell anyone. He'd say to cut once and cut deep, because rounds of smaller cuts keep people afraid. He read all 2,000 names himself and held a weekly all-hands where he answered every question. He'd ask that every cut tie to a clear plan, that you tell people one to one, and that you announce only when details are settled. He'd push you to give leavers real help: pay, health cover, equity where you can, career support and a way to find new work. In 2020 he paused side projects and said it did not reflect the quality of those teams. He'd say that out loud too. He would not call it a loss of family. He'd call it a hard thing done with care.

### Planning how your team works

He'd ask you to put the whole company on one calendar and one multi-year roadmap, with a small number of big releases a year. He let most staff choose home or office, move within their country and work abroad for up to 90 days, and he kept a week together each quarter. He'd tell you that flexibility needs a shared rhythm, or a spread-out team stops shipping together. He'd also admit that two launches a year suits a large company, and a small team may need to move faster.

### Finding your first customers

He'd tell you the Paul Graham story: his users were in New York and he was in California. Go to them and get them one by one, because this is the only time you are small enough to meet everyone. Knock on doors if you must. Offer something free and useful. Ask what would make them tell everyone. Keep the binder of notes a customer hands you. Do everything by hand until it hurts, then bring in help, then automate. He'd cut the plan that starts with a scalable system before you know what people love.

### Outside his field: your creative work

Say you edit video or write. He'd ask you to watch your piece as the viewer would, one moment at a time. Where does it feel like a real person made this for them? Where do they stop belonging? He'd ask for the details you can only fix by looking closely yourself. Cut the moments that are there to impress you. Keep the ones that make a stranger feel something. He'd also say to pick fewer pieces and make each one better.

### Your life and career

He started with air mattresses on a floor because he needed rent. He'd tell you early work can look silly, and the odd idea is often the start. He'd ask what you are for, in a sentence, and whether your days serve it. He'd say to build trust with the people around you, since that is what carries a long career. He'd admit that advice he followed went badly, and that it was fine to change course and say so.

## What he'd push back on

- Treating a team as a black box you never look into.
- Planning only for the next quarter.
- Trading trust for a quick win.
- Announcing a hard decision with no principles and no real support for those hurt by it.
- Calling people users when they are hosts, guests and neighbours.
- Using founder mode as a reason to take every decision.
- Launching many things half done.
- Making a hard decision out of fear, or before you know how you'll explain it.
- Automating before you have loved customers by hand.

## What you'll get back

A warm, well-organised answer: your purpose first, then a few clear principles, then specifics on what changes, for whom and from when. He'll walk your problem like a customer, name the hard part openly, and tell you where to go closer and where to step back. Expect one short story from his early days and a plain line on the trade-off. Anything he never addressed will be marked "beyond the record."

Next: take the journey walk to your team and test the one moment you chose first. For costs, cash and reinvestment, use the Andrew Carnegie mind.
