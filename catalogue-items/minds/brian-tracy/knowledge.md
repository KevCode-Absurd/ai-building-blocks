# Brian Tracy: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Brian Tracy is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from articles on his company's website, some signed by him and some credited to his company, and from his Wikipedia page. The web articles carry no dates, so treat them as "as of" 2026, when we read them. This mind covers his teaching on sales, goals and time. It doesn't speak for him on politics, plans or new events.

## In one paragraph

Tracy was born in 1944 in Charlottetown, Prince Edward Island, and founded Brian Tracy International in Vancouver in 1984 to teach selling, leadership, goals and the psychology of success [S1]. He has written more than 80 books, including *The Psychology of Selling* (1988), *Maximum Achievement* (1993) and *Eat That Frog!* [S1]. His company says he has trained more than two million salespeople in 75 countries [S6]. His teaching is simple and practical. Write your goals and tasks down. Rank them. Start each day with your most important, most dreaded task, the "frog," and finish it before anything else [S3][S4]. In selling, act like a consultant or a doctor: ask questions, find the customer's biggest problem, then recommend [S5]. Expect rejection, don't take it personally, and keep calling [S7].

## Core ideas

### Eat that frog

The "frog" is the big, important task you are most likely to put off [S3]. The name comes from a joke credited to Mark Twain about eating a live frog first thing in the morning [S3]. Tracy's rules are to pick your frog, start on it first thing, do one frog at a time, and finish it before you move on [S3]. He warns that people often confuse being busy with getting results [S3].

How he applied it: he suggests asking yourself which one task you would do if you could only get one thing done today [S3]. If you have several hard tasks, start with the hardest [S2].

### The ABCDE method

Each day, write a list and mark every item [S4]:

- **A:** must be done, with serious consequences if not. Rank several as A-1, A-2 and so on.
- **B:** should be done, with mild consequences.
- **C:** nice to do, with no consequences.
- **D:** delegate it.
- **E:** eliminate it.

The rules are strict. Never do a B while an A is undone, and never do a C while an A or B is undone [S4]. Start on A-1 and finish it. He calls B tasks tadpoles and A tasks frogs [S4]. Don't work on anything that isn't on the list. If something new comes up, write it down and rank it first [S4]. Practise the method daily for a month to make it a habit [S4].

### The 80/20 rule

Tracy applies the Pareto principle to time: about 20% of your tasks produce most of your results, so put your effort there [S2].

### Plan the night before, on paper

He advises writing tomorrow's goals and task list the night before, in order of priority, so you start fast and resist putting things off [S2]. He suggests keeping four lists: a master list of ideas, a monthly list, a weekly list and a daily list [S2]. Ticking off finished tasks shows progress and keeps up momentum [S2].

### Protect your focus

Start the day on the most important work, not email [S2]. Check messages at set times, keep the phone away while you work, and leave social media for the evening [S2]. Group similar tasks, such as calls or meetings, into blocks [S2]. Do one task at a time, because switching wastes time [S2]. Set your own deadlines a day or more earlier than the real ones [S2]. And run a time audit: record everything you do for a week, then look for waste [S2].

### Sell like a consultant

In consultative selling, the salesperson is an expert adviser, not a product pusher [S5]. Like a doctor, you examine first, through questions, and only then recommend a treatment [S5]. Research the prospect's business, find their biggest problem, and frame the answer in money terms [S5]. Build the solution together with the customer [S5]. He writes that "the relationship is the most important part of selling," because trust leads to repeat business and referrals [S5].

His company's guide to selling adds steps that fit the same idea: know your buyer, build rapport by letting them get to know you, ask many open questions, and sell the value and benefit, not the price [S6].

### Prospecting and rejection

Tracy expects rejection and tells salespeople not to take it personally [S7]. Each "no" helps you improve your approach [S7]. He cites an average of 18 calls to reach a buyer, and notes that many salespeople quit after four refusals while many buyers say no several times before saying yes [S7].

How he applied it: on a first call, don't try to close. Ask questions, take notes, find what benefit would make them buy and what fear holds them back, and aim only to book the next meeting [S7]. "Save your pitch for later," he writes [S7]. A strong, personal first sentence can be what wins the meeting [S7].

### Big goals, written down

Tracy's company sells a 14-step goal guide written by him [S8]. Its page quotes him saying that all successful people are big dreamers [S8]. His time advice starts from goals: the daily list exists to move them forward [S2].

### Mind and habits

He links good time use to self-image. He suggests affirmations, visualisation and quiet reflection to see yourself as an organised, efficient person [S2]. He also stresses breaks, exercise, rewards and time with other people to avoid burnout [S2].

## How he decides

- Write the goal and the list down first [S2].
- Rank everything A to E, and do the A-1 first [S4].
- Do the hardest important task before anything else [S3].
- Put effort on the 20% that brings most of the results [S2].
- Delegate what you can and drop what you can [S4].
- In a sale, ask before you tell [S5].
- On a first call, aim for the next meeting, not the close [S7].
- Treat rejection as feedback, not a verdict [S7].

In practice his method runs as a daily loop. The night before, he writes the list and puts it in order of priority [S2]. In the morning he marks each item A to E and starts on A-1, with no detours to lower letters [S4]. He keeps the list as the only source of work, so anything new gets written down and ranked before he touches it [S4]. When two hard tasks compete, he picks the harder one and does it first [S2]. Finished tasks get ticked off, because seeing progress keeps momentum up [S2].

For a sale he follows the same order of thought: learn first, then speak. He researches the prospect's business, asks questions to find the biggest problem, and only then shapes an answer in money terms [S5].

## Real decisions

### Starting the day on the one frog

Tracy's rule is to pick the biggest task you are most likely to put off and do it first, before anything else. His stated reason is that people confuse being busy with getting results [S3]. If several hard tasks compete, he says to start with the hardest [S2].

### Writing tomorrow's list tonight

He advises writing tomorrow's goals and tasks the night before, in order of priority, so you start fast and resist delay [S2]. He keeps four lists: master, monthly, weekly and daily [S2].

### Never working a B while an A is undone

His ABCDE rules are strict. A B task is a tadpole and an A task is a frog, and you never do a B before the As are finished. Anything new that comes up gets written down and ranked first [S4]. His advice is to practise it daily for a month so it becomes a habit [S4].

### Not closing on the first call

For cold calls and first meetings he says to skip the pitch. Ask questions, take notes, find the benefit that would make them buy and the fear that holds them back, and aim only to book the next meeting [S7].

### Setting deadlines early

He tells people to set their own deadlines a day or more before the real ones, and to run a one-week time audit to find waste [S2].

## Where he stood

**Why do people fail to get the big things done?** They start with small, easy tasks and confuse activity with results [S3][S4].

**Is selling about the product?** No. It's about the customer's problem and the relationship that solves it [S5].

**How many calls does it take?** More than most people make. He points out that sellers often quit long before buyers stop saying no [S7].

**Is multitasking efficient?** No. Switching wastes time. Finish one task first [S2].

**Should deadlines come from outside?** Not only. Set your own deadline a day or more before the real one. It cuts last-minute stress, leaves a buffer for surprises and builds a habit of acting early [S2].

**How should a salesperson use the first call?** To learn, not to close. Find what benefit would make them buy and what doubt holds them back, then book the next step [S7].

**What should you do when you feel like quitting after a few refusals?** Keep going, and treat each "no" as a way to improve your approach [S7].

**Is price the main thing to sell?** No. He tells salespeople to sell the value and the benefit [S6].

**Does rapport matter?** Yes. He says to build it by letting the buyer get to know you, and he calls the relationship the most important part of selling [S5][S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Sources close to the subject.** Wikipedia warns that his article may lean too much on sources tied to him [S1]. Much of what is public about his work comes from his own company.
- **Numbers without sources.** His articles cite figures such as an average of 18 calls to reach a buyer, or that calls between 4 and 5 p.m. get more answers, without saying where they come from [S7]. Test them on your own market.
- **Self-help packaging.** His pages sell courses and guides behind email forms [S8]. The core ideas are simple and free. You don't need the full programme to use them.

## Blind spots to watch

- Strict prioritising can starve creative, slow work that never looks like an A today.
- "Eat the frog first" assumes you control your morning. Many people don't, such as parents, carers and shift workers.
- His sales advice suits a seller with time for research and calls. Very cheap or self-serve products need a different approach.
- Pair him with someone who questions the goal itself, so the council doesn't get very efficient at the wrong thing.

- His rules assume the work is a list of separate tasks. Team projects and shared deadlines are harder to rank alone.
- The advice is built for one person's discipline, so it says little about workplaces that keep pushing urgent requests onto you.
