# Bruce Lee: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Bruce Lee was a Hong Kong-American martial artist, actor and filmmaker, and the first global Chinese film star [S1]. He trained in Wing Chun kung fu in Hong Kong, moved to the United States at 18, studied philosophy at the University of Washington and opened martial arts schools [S1][S5]. Frustrated by fixed styles, he built his own approach, Jeet Kune Do, "the way of the intercepting fist," which he named in 1967 [S2][S4]. Its core is simplicity, directness and freedom: keep what works, drop what doesn't, and adapt to the situation like water [S2][S3][S4]. His Hong Kong films, and *Enter the Dragon*, released just after his death at 32 in 1973, made him a world star [S1]. He is often called a forerunner of mixed martial arts [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Be water

Lee used water as his main image for adaptability [S2][S3]. Water has no fixed shape. Put it in a cup and it becomes the cup. It can flow or it can crash [S3]. The best fighter adapts to any style instead of following one system [S1].

How he applied it: the famous "be water" lines come from words he wrote for his character in the TV series *Longstreet* (1971), later shown in the documentary *Bruce Lee: A Warrior's Journey* (2000) [S6][S9]. His family company now uses "Be water, my friend" as its tagline [S3][S4].

### No way as way

The main principle of Jeet Kune Do, written on its symbol, is "using no way as way; having no limitation as limitation" [S4]. The symbol is a yin-yang surrounded by arrows, standing for the endless play of opposites [S4]. It is linked to the Daoist idea of wu wei, acting without forcing [S2].

How he applied it: he was reluctant to name his approach at all, because a name fixes a living thing into a form [S4]. In a 1971 *Black Belt* article he called the name "just a name used, a boat to get one across," to be left behind once you're over [S2].

### Absorb what is useful

Lee's family company quotes his method: research your own experience, absorb what is useful, reject what is useless, and add what is your own [S4]. Reference sources note that the exact wording of this maxim is hard to source [S2]. The idea runs through everything he did: test in practice, keep what works, drop the rest.

How he applied it: Jeet Kune Do drew on boxing, fencing, Wing Chun and other arts [S1][S2]. His notebooks cite Western thinkers like Plato, Hume and Descartes alongside Lao-tzu, Chuang-tzu and Musashi [S1].

### Simplicity, directness, freedom

His family company names simplicity, directness and freedom as Jeet Kune Do's guiding principles [S4]. Reference sources describe economy of motion: the least distance, time and energy for an exact strike [S2]. The approach also favours interception, meeting an attack as it begins, even reading the small signals that come before it [S2].

How he applied it: he taught kicks aimed low, at the shins and midsection, by default rather than showy high kicks, though not as a fixed rule [S2]. He trained four ranges, kicking, punching, trapping and grappling, which closely match the phases of modern mixed martial arts [S2].

### Truth has no fixed path

A line in *Tao of Jeet Kune Do* says truth has no path, because it is living and always changing [S6]. Wikiquote notes this probably comes from the philosopher Jiddu Krishnamurti, who said truth is a pathless land [S6]. Lee was especially drawn to Krishnamurti [S1]. The book also warns against rejecting the classical approach just as a reaction [S6]. The point is not to rebel but to see clearly. The 1971 *Black Belt* essay in which he describes the teacher as a guide who points and the student as the one who must find the truth is traced by Quote Investigator, which notes the magazine's 1988 reprint changed the wording [S15].

### Formlessness

The *Tao* says Jeet Kune Do favours formlessness so it can take any form [S6]. His poems, written in free verse and edited after his death, use paradox and yin-yang ideas in the same spirit [S1].

### Train the whole person

After a 1965 match with Wong Jack-man, Lee shifted to total fitness: strength, endurance, heart and lungs, and flexibility [S1]. He used bodybuilding methods but avoided extra muscle that would slow him down [S1]. He took diet seriously and began his day with meditation [S1]. The *Tao* calls training one of the most neglected parts of athletics [S1]. The Bruce Lee Foundation describes him as training his mind as he trained his body [S10].

How he applied it: a back injury in 1970 put him in bed for six months. He rebuilt his strength with his own recovery programme and used the time to write the notes that became the *Tao* [S5][S7].

### Honest self-expression

Lee saw martial arts as a means of self-expression and thought all knowledge leads to self-knowledge [S1]. His family company quotes him: always be yourself, express yourself, have faith in yourself [S4]. Asked in a 1971 TV interview whether he thought of himself as American or Chinese, he said he thought of himself as a human being [S8].

### One family

In that 1971 interview he said that under the heavens there is but one family [S8]. He criticised stereotyped portrayals of Asian people and said producers saw casting an Asian lead as a business risk [S8].

## Training and teaching rules from the sourced sayings

Wikiquote lists several lines tied to named books or interviews [S6]. A fighter, he wrote, moves through three stages: instinctive, then mechanical once technique is learned, then spontaneous when technique is no longer imposed and the fighter simply responds [S6]. He told Berton in 1971 to train every part of the body, since real fighting has no rules [S6]. For teachers, the written rules were to demonstrate what you ask of students and do more, to be patient with lack of ability but firm with lack of effort, and never to mock a student [S6]. He held that learning never ends [S6]. On fear, he wrote that accepting defeat frees the mind to flow, and that an opponent obsessed with a single aim is the most dangerous kind [S6]. In a 1967 letter to the producer William Dozier he credited *The Green Hornet* with teaching him to strip away the unessential [S6].

## In his own letters, replies and notes

**Replies to readers (1967).** After *Black Belt* ran two articles on him, readers wrote in and Lee answered several himself [S13]. A reader who said classical drills do not help in a real fight got a short agreement: practise against live opposition, hit a heavy bag, use your legs like your hands, spar. His line was that you have to get wet to learn to swim [S13]. A karate student who listed six objections got a point-by-point reply, mixing mockery with real argument. Lee even gave up one point, that karate has no passive blocks [S13]. A Wing Chun student was told to empty his cup first [S13]. A man who claimed to have beaten him got a two-word reply [S13]. A rumour that he had been killed in Hong Kong got dry humour [S13]. Rule of thumb: argue seriously with serious critics, laugh off the rest.

**Letters to Dozier and Kimura (1967).** In a thank-you to Dozier after *The Green Hornet* ended, Lee thanked him for starting his career and said part of his job on the show had been hacking away the unessential [S14]. In a September 1967 note to Kimura he reported daily shooting, said the show was doing "50/50," and promised to teach Kimura his method over a week at Christmas [S18]. The tone is warm and practical.

**The aim note (January 1969).** He wrote to himself that he would become the highest-paid Asian film star in the United States, using the word of his day, "Oriental." He set fame for 1970 and a fortune of $10 million by 1980, said he would live on his own terms and find inner harmony, and promised in return to deliver top acting and highly exciting performances. He signed it with his full name [S17].

**Daily mind drills (1968).** In a pocketbook written before his 28th birthday he copied a self-improvement creed and noted how he wanted his will, emotions, reason, imagination and memory to work. He set himself 30 minutes a day to picture the person he meant to become and 10 minutes a day on self-confidence [S16]. On yellow index cards he wrote his firmest beliefs and signed them like contracts [S16].

**The 1971 interview.** Open Culture notes that the 25-minute talk with Pierre Berton covered his Mandarin-language film success though he spoke only Cantonese, the difficulty of a career in a country hostile to China, and his work training Hollywood stars [S19].

## How he decides

- Test it in real conditions. Keep what works [S2][S4].
- Cut every wasted motion [S2].
- Meet the problem as it starts [S2].
- Adapt to the situation instead of forcing a set method [S1][S3].
- Don't reject the old just to be different [S6].
- Learn from many sources, then make it your own [S1][S4].
- Train body and mind together, every day [S1][S10].
- Use a name or method as a boat, then leave it behind [S2].

## Where he stood

**Is one style the best?** No. The best fighter can adapt to any style [S1].

**Should you throw out tradition?** No. Don't reject the classical approach as a mere reaction [S6].

**What matters more, technique or the person?** The *Tao* says the art is about spirit and body more than small techniques [S1].

**Was he religious?** He said he had no religious affiliation and did not believe in God [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Borrowed lines.** *Tao of Jeet Kune Do* was assembled after his death from his notes. Many passages paraphrase earlier writers, which Lee had copied into his notes in his own words for study. The publisher credited several fencing and combat authors as sources, and later readers traced other passages to D. T. Suzuki and Eric Hoffer [S7]. One famous line in it comes from a Chan Buddhist poem [S6]. Lee was not strictly the author of the book as published [S7].
- **Street fights.** The Hong Kong press criticised him in the early 1970s for accepting street-fight challenges [S1].
- **His death.** The coroner's verdict was death by misadventure, from brain swelling linked to a reaction to a painkiller. Other theories include heat stroke, low blood sodium and an epileptic cause. The cause remains disputed [S1].
- **After him.** He never fully codified Jeet Kune Do, so his students split into rival schools of interpretation [S2].

## The Berton interview passage

In his December 1971 interview with Pierre Berton in Hong Kong, Lee recites a passage on water: it takes the shape of a cup, a bottle or a teapot, and it can flow or crash [S11][S12]. Berton introduces the lines as Lee's philosophy, and Lee confirms them before reciting [S12]. ## Real decisions

### Cutting the show fights to the bone (The Green Hornet, 1966 to 1967)
As Kato, Lee refused to stage fights like the slugging matches common in Westerns, because his art stressed efficiency [S14]. His moves were too fast for the TV cameras, so the fights were filmed in slow motion, and he reluctantly added flashier flying kicks [S14]. He later told Dozier that part of his job had been hacking away the unessential [S14]. The series was cancelled in March 1967 [S14]. The lesson is that he wasn't dogmatic: he gave the audience a showier kick but kept the logic of the fight.

### Walking off the Batman crossover (1967)
The first script had Batman and Robin win the fight with Kato. Lee objected and walked off, and Dozier rewrote the scene as a draw [S14]. The sources give the protest and the fix, not a long reason from Lee.

### Answering critics one by one (Black Belt, 1967)
When a karate student attacked his article point by point, Lee answered each point, mocked some, defended live sparring and dummy work for timing and distance, and conceded that karate has no passive blocks [S13]. To a reader who said drills are useless in real fights he said to spar and use a heavy bag [S13]. He told a Wing Chun man to empty his cup [S13]. He answered a baseless boast with two words [S13]. Giving a real answer to real critics and none to empty claims is a rule you can copy.

### Putting a chief aim in writing (January 1969)
After The Green Hornet and a run of small TV parts, Lee wrote himself a dated note with a goal, a start year, a money target and a promise of what he would give in return [S17]. He signed it. The note named 1970 for the start of fame, and his Hong Kong films from 1971 and *Enter the Dragon* followed [S17][S1]. He did not state a method in the note, only the aim and the price.

### Training the mind on a schedule (1968)
In his pocketbook he set daily minutes for picturing his future self and for self-confidence, with the stated thought that a steadily held desire finds a practical outlet [S16]. He also asked reason to test his wishes, because unchecked emotion can be dangerous [S16]. It shows he trained attitude like a muscle, with a timetable.

### Holding out for the philosophy in *Enter the Dragon* (1973)
One account says that when the studio wanted a plain action film, Lee refused to go on set for two weeks. He argued that kung fu and philosophy were bound together, and the studio gave way [S16]. We have only this one source for it, so treat the details as reported, not proven.

### Refusing to fix a name
He was reluctant to name his approach, because a name fixes a living thing [S4]. In 1971 he called the name "just a name used, a boat to get one across" [S2].

### Writing through a back injury
A back injury in 1970 put him in bed for six months. He rebuilt his strength with his own programme and used the time to write the notes that became the *Tao* [S5][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- "Be water" can become an excuse never to commit. Adapt the method, not the goal.
- His world was one-on-one combat and film. Team, money and long-term strategy need other minds.
- Many quotes credited to him are wrong or borrowed. Check before quoting.
- Directness can turn into bluntness. Pair him with a gentler voice on people.
