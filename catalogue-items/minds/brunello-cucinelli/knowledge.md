## In one paragraph

Cucinelli grew up on a farm outside Perugia, in Umbria, in a home with no electricity or running water [S1]. He left engineering school and taught himself philosophy [S1]. In 1977 he began dyeing cashmere in bright colours in a small workshop, and in 1978 he founded the company that bears his name [S1][S2]. He based it in Solomeo, a small hilltop village he restored, and he moved the firm into its castle in 1987 [S2]. He calls his way of running it humanistic capitalism: make a profit, but ethically, with dignity and with as little harm as possible to people and the earth [S3]. He wants fair pay, fair hours and pleasant places to work [S3][S4]. His company listed on the Milan stock exchange in 2012 [S5], and by 2024 it had sales of about €1.28 billion and about 3,100 staff [S2]. He writes often about the soul, beauty, craft and measure, and he quotes old thinkers more than business books [S11].

## Core ideas

### Humanistic capitalism

Cucinelli argues that a company should make profits ethically and with dignity, while doing as little harm as possible to people and to what he calls Creation [S3]. He describes it as a wide kind of sustainability. It covers the economy and the environment, but also culture, the spirit and morals [S3]. He calls the people who run the company guardians of Creation [S3].

How he applied it: when the company listed in 2012, he said he wanted the listing to help make human work more humane, and to let the business last for decades [S5].

### Every worker has dignity

His idea of dignity starts with a memory. His father suffered an unjust offence at work, and the young Brunello saw the tears in his eyes [S8]. He says the memory taught him to try always to be just, moderate and respectful of people [S8]. His company's page links its whole approach to that hurt, which he calls an offence to human dignity [S3]. He believes in the moral and economic dignity of every person [S3].

How he applied it: he says he wanted people to earn a little more, and he pictured pleasant workplaces where people could enjoy the view outside [S3]. When he chose to become a cashmere maker, his father told him to always be a good man [S8].

### Protect people's time

In a 2012 essay he argued that work must not take over a person's life, rest and time to balance body and spirit [S4]. He drew on Saint Benedict, who advised a daily balance of study, prayer and work [S4]. He said an employer should never steal the soul of a worker [S4].

How he applied it: Solomeo has one firm rule, that no one works past the agreed hours [S4]. Staff are asked not to send work emails or be on call after their day ends [S4]. During the day, he says a phone call often works better than an email [S4].

### Fair profit, never a greedy one

He wants a profit that is healthy and fair, and never what he called preposterous [S5]. He accepts that growth matters, but he wants it to be gentle and to harm no one, or as few as possible [S5]. In his telling, his grandfather gave the first bale of wheat each year to the community, as a sign of balance between gain and giving back [S3].

How he applied it: at the 2012 listing, he told would-be investors that if they wanted profit from harming people, or growth without respect for craft, his company was not right for them [S5]. In 2018 he sold shares worth about €100 million and put the money into his family's charitable fund [S1][S2]. The company says it gives 20% of its profits to its foundation [S2].

### Use things, don't consume them

In a 2014 essay he argued that consumer habits had gone too far and that a more measured way of owning things was coming [S6]. To consume, he wrote, is to drain and use up. To use something well lets resources recover [S6]. He linked the wellbeing of a society to a fair use of things, and called slow, healthy growth more human [S6].

How he applied it: the company makes its products in Italy, mostly in Umbria, and keeps a large share of sales in its own shops [S2]. Its whole pitch rests on clothes made well enough to keep.

### Gentle luxury

In 2024 he wrote that luxury is a symbol that binds people to beauty [S7]. He called his kind "gentle luxury": creative, welcoming, respectful of others and kept within measure [S7]. It means finding beauty in simplicity, he wrote, and never crossing the line into excess [S7].

How he applied it: he treats measure as a design rule. The idea runs through his writing on beauty, the village and the clothes, rather than through any one product [S7][S10].

### Craft deserves pride and pay

Cucinelli worries that young Italians are turning away from manual trades and their heritage [S9]. His company says young people once felt shy to admit they worked with their hands, and it wants them to be proud of it [S9]. It draws on the Arts and Crafts thinkers John Ruskin and William Morris [S9].

How he applied it: in 2013 he set up a school of crafts in Solomeo's old buildings [S9][S10]. It teaches tailoring, pattern making, mending, knitting, gardening and masonry, under masters [S9]. Students are paid a monthly grant while they learn [S9].

### Build in harmony with the place

He believes a company is shaped by its place. In 1985 he had the idea of gathering the meaning of his life in Solomeo [S10]. He restored its castle and village and added a theatre, a park, a vineyard and a wood [S3][S10]. He sees the hill as a line from the spirit at the top, to culture in the village, to work and nature in the valley [S3].

How he applied it: instead of a usual investor tour before the 2012 listing, he invited investors to Solomeo to see the place, meet staff and eat with his family [S5]. Demand for the shares came to about eighteen times what was on offer [S5].

## Real decisions

### Listing the company, but on his terms
Before the 2012 listing in Milan, he skipped the usual investor tour and invited investors to Solomeo to see the place, meet staff and eat with his family [S5]. He told them that if they wanted profit from harming people, or growth without respect for craft, the company was not for them [S5]. Demand ran to about eighteen times the shares on offer [S5].

### One hard rule on working hours
Instead of a long policy, Solomeo keeps one firm rule: no one works past the agreed hours [S4]. Staff are asked not to send work emails or take calls after the day ends, and he prefers a phone call to an email when the call works better [S4]. His stated reason is that an employer should never steal a worker's soul [S4].

### Moving into the castle, then moving out to the valley
In 1987 he moved the company into Solomeo's 14th-century castle, and in 2001 he moved the main offices to the valley below [S2]. He restored the village and added a theatre, a park, a vineyard and a wood, with the aim of work, culture and nature sitting side by side [S3][S10].

### Opening a school of crafts
In 2013 he set up a craft school in Solomeo's old buildings, teaching tailoring, pattern making, mending, knitting, gardening and masonry under masters [S9][S10]. Students get a monthly grant while they learn [S9]. His stated worry was that young Italians had stopped being proud of manual trades [S9].

### Giving away part of the company
In 2018 he sold about 6% of his shares and put roughly €100 million into his family's charitable fund [S1][S2]. The company says it gives 20% of profits to its foundation [S2].

### Staging a busy trade-show booth
In the company's first years, one account says he paid some people to pose as buyers and swamp his booth with orders at menswear trade shows, to give the look of a business already doing well [S1]. It is a single line in an encyclopedia entry, and it sits oddly beside the plain-dealing image in his letters.

## Where he stood

As of his published letters, 2012 to 2024:

**What is a business for?** To make a fair profit while serving people, the community and the earth [S3][S5].

**How much should people work?** Only the agreed hours, with no email after work [S4].

**Is growth good?** Yes, if it is gentle and does little harm [S5][S6].

**What is luxury?** A link to beauty, kept within measure and open to all [S7].

**Who owns a company?** He sees himself as a guardian of the company more than an owner, and hopes future guardians share its beliefs [S5].

**Technology?** He has written several letters on artificial intelligence, framing it as a tool to keep at humanity's side [S11].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Russia.** In September 2025 a short seller, Morpheus Research, claimed the company was still selling costly goods in Moscow despite EU limits on luxury exports to Russia [S12]. The shares fell about 17% in a day [S12]. The company denied it, said its Russian stores were closed and Russia was about 2% of sales, and said it was weighing legal action [S12]. A hedge fund had made similar claims earlier that year [S12]. These are claims, not findings.
- **Self-told story.** Most of what the public knows about his values comes from his own letters and his company's pages [S3][S11]. Treat them as his view of himself.
- **Luxury for the few.** He speaks of measure and gentle luxury, but sells high-priced goods to wealthy buyers [S2][S7]. Readers can judge how far the two fit.

## Blind spots to watch

- His model needs high prices and margins. A business with thin margins may not afford the same pay, hours and buildings.
- He writes in lofty, spiritual terms. Advice drawn from him can drift away from numbers. Pair him with someone hard-nosed on cash.
- He controls his company, so he can plan for centuries. Firms with impatient owners may not have that choice [S2].
- The record shows his intentions more than outside checks on pay and conditions.
- Pair him with a marketer or a mass-market maker, and with someone who will question his story.
