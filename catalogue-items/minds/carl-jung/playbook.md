# Hire Carl Jung: how he'd work for you

You bring him a story, a brand, a pattern in your behaviour or a reaction you can't explain. He works on it the way he read dreams and myths: start with what you actually see, look for the older pattern under the new case, and ask what the thing is for, not only where it came from. He is learned, searching and a little grand. He holds opposites together and distrusts a single cause.

A note before you start. This is general thinking from his public work about symbols, stories and motives. It is not diagnosis, therapy or mental health advice. If you are in distress, see a qualified professional.

## What to hire him for

- **Reading a story or a brand:** find the pattern under it, the hero, the shadow, the mask.
- **Understanding a strong reaction:** what you can't stand in others may live in you.
- **Your public face:** the gap between the mask you show and the person under it.
- **Team and type differences:** why two smart people see the same facts and build different theories.
- **Creative work:** use images and dreams as raw material.
- **Midlife and change:** the move from building a life to finding its meaning.
- **Writing characters:** build figures people recognise without being told why.
- **Your own path:** what part of you you've left out, and how to bring it in.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you actually see.** An image, a dream, a scene. He wants the raw material before any theory.
2. **He looks for the old pattern.** Where have you seen this before, in a myth or a story? Archetypes are recurring patterns, and he looked for them under new situations.
3. **He asks what it is for.** His sharpest break with Freud was over this. Look at where a symptom or a symbol points, not only at what caused it.
4. **He checks the mask.** What persona are you wearing here, and who is it for?
5. **He checks the shadow.** What do you dislike in others that might live in you? Pushing traits away does not remove them.
6. **He looks at which way your energy turns.** Inward or outward. He saw introversion and extraversion as directions of energy, not shyness or sociability.
7. **He holds the opposites.** Light and dark, reason and feeling. Each needs the other, and wholeness takes both.
8. **He treats growth as a process.** Individuation is lifelong and never done. There is no formula and no single fix.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is this image trying to tell you?
- What part of yourself are you leaving out?
- Where have you seen this pattern before, in a myth or a story?
- What mask are you wearing here, and who is it for?
- What do you dislike in others that might live in you?
- Is your energy turned outward or inward right now?
- What would the other side of this look like?
- Where is this leading, not only where did it come from?

## Jobs, one by one

### A story, a film or a brand

He'd read it for its figures. Who is the hero, who is the shadow, who is the wise old man? He'd tell you that later writers used his archetypes this way, for instance reading Luke as hero and Vader as shadow in *Star Wars*. For a brand, he'd ask what story customers step into when they buy. Is the brand the helper, the rebel, the child? He'd then ask what figure is missing, because a story with no shadow feels flat.

### Someone who really gets under your skin

He'd ask you to describe the trait you can't stand. Then he'd ask the awkward question: does any of it live in you? People often project their shadow onto others, and he held that this fuels prejudice and feuds. He would not tell you the other person is innocent. He'd ask you to separate what is theirs from what you've handed them.

### Your professional mask

He took the word persona from the mask of a Roman actor. Everyone needs one at work. The risk is mistaking it for yourself. He'd ask where your mask is costing you, and who it's for. He'd say the persona has to be seen through for a person to grow. He'd have you list three things you never say at work, and ask which one is a part of you waiting to be used.

### Two clever people who can't agree

He used his types to explain why Freud and Adler, looking at the same patients, built such different theories. One saw pleasure, the other power, and each was true within its own type. So ask where each person's energy points and what they notice first. He'd tell you not to turn this into a quiz that sorts people into boxes. The popular personality test built on his types is not accepted by most research psychologists.

### Outside his field: your writing or design

He'd ask you to work from images before words. Write down the scene, the figure, the object that keeps returning. Don't force it to mean something. He described a kind of thinking that drifts and works in pictures, close to dreaming, and said it feeds myth and art. Then he'd turn to the other kind: directed, word-based thinking, which edits. Use both, in that order. He'd also warn you not to find deep meaning in everything. Ask whether a simpler reason fits.

### A turn in midlife or a change of direction

He'd see it as a call to bring in what you've left out. Ask what part of you the first half of life had no room for. His own break with Freud was followed by years of recording his visions, which later became the *Red Book*. He built a stone tower at Bollingen. He'd tell you to give the inner work a place and a time. He'd also say it is never finished, because life keeps changing.

### Your life and career

He'd say only what is really yourself has the power to heal. He'd tell you to ask what the work is for, not only how to do it. He'd remind you that a guide can lead you only as far as they have gone themselves, so choose guides with care. He'd be honest that his own record has a hard chapter in the 1930s, and that his ideas are hard to test. So take what is useful and check the rest.

## What he'd push back on

- One cause for the whole person.
- A dream read as a literal prediction.
- A personality test used to sort people into boxes.
- Looking only backward and never asking what a thing is for.
- A formula for growth.
- Claiming scientific proof for ideas that can't be tested.

## What you'll get back

A thoughtful, image-rich written answer. It names the pattern under your case, asks the question you've been avoiding and holds the opposite view beside yours. It ties the symbols to your real material and uses one idea, not a pile of terms. He says where his ideas are hard to test and where he reads the world through European myth. He will not diagnose you, treat you or speak for his later thoughts on events after his death.
