# Carl Richards: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Public claims cite mind.yaml. The sketches described here are our own text exercises; we do not reproduce Richards's artwork. Hypothetical examples illustrate decision processes and do not prescribe investments.

## In one paragraph

Carl Richards explains money decisions with spare sketches and short observations about behaviour. His public work includes The Behavior Gap, The One-Page Financial Plan and the Sketch Guy column. He asks what money is for, distinguishes a decision process from a lucky result and treats planning as an activity that continues when circumstances change. The useful everyday contribution is a clearer conversation: a purpose statement, a visible assumption or a pause between a frightening headline and an action. A small drawing helps expose the question; it does not remove financial uncertainty. [S1][S2][S3]

## Core ideas

### Judge the process before celebrating the result

In Process Versus Outcome, Richards tells of someone who risked everything on a roulette spin and won. He uses the story to show that a favourable result does not make the original decision sound. A sequence of outcomes may reveal more about a process than a single lucky event. [S2]

Our application: a friend made a speculative purchase and profited. Before copying it, make two columns: information available before the decision and facts known only afterwards. Then ask what could have happened if the outcome had gone differently. The point is not to deny the friend's gain. It is to stop a gain from becoming proof of a reliable method. For a nonfinancial example, a team skipped a backup and had no data loss. The same distinction applies: the quiet week did not establish that skipping the backup was a good process. Keep the evaluation tied to foreseeable consequences and available alternatives.

### Begin a plan with why

In the published interview, Richards describes a one-page financial plan headed by a statement of purpose. He gives time with family, mainly outside, as his own example. The purpose helps interpret later goals and actions. [S3]

Our application: someone says their goal is a larger balance but cannot explain what the balance would make possible. Ask what they would like to protect or change in ordinary life. A useful sentence might be to keep housing stable while making time for a qualification. That sentence does not decide amounts or products, but it clarifies why a proposed commitment belongs in the conversation. Put it at the top of the page and check whether each goal serves it. If two purposes compete, show the tension rather than choosing a pleasing slogan that conceals it. The result should be recognisable to the household, not impressive to an adviser.

### A plan and planning are different

The interview describes uncertainty, flexible goals and revisiting a plan as life changes. The plan is a current expression of the process rather than a certificate that the future has been settled. [S3]

Our application: a household's plan assumes two steady incomes, but one person begins contract work. Mark that assumption as changed. Revisit the timing of commitments, the reliability of expected income and the decisions that now need professional review. Do not call the earlier plan a failure simply because life changed. Also do not continue using its old numbers to avoid a difficult conversation. Write a version date and a reason for the revision. A useful update says what changed, which parts still fit and what needs new information. It is shorter and more honest than rebuilding an elaborate forecast while preserving the same unexamined assumption.

### Behaviour belongs inside the system

Richards discusses the impulse to react when market news causes pain and the social influence of what neighbours are doing. He frames these mistakes empathetically rather than as evidence that the reader is stupid. His planning archive also uses a rear-view-mirror image to question chasing past performance. [S3][S4]

Our application: a person checks an account whenever a headline feels alarming. Record the trigger, the action they feel pulled toward and the purpose the account is supposed to serve. Then draft a pause protocol: save the question, check what has materially changed and take it to an appropriate review. This is an information and conversation routine, not an instruction to hold, buy or sell a security. The reader's actual obligations or circumstances may have changed and deserve attention. Reducing impulsive reaction should not mean ignoring evidence. The output is a way to distinguish a frightening story from a change that requires a decision.

### Know which game is being played

The planning archive explicitly distinguishes speculation from investing and uses contrasting sketches to make the difference visible. The material is about recognising the nature of an activity, not assigning a guaranteed path to a particular reader. [S4]

Our application: examine a social-media pitch without deciding whether its asset will rise. Ask what the proposed gain depends on, which costs are stated, whether the seller is paid and what would make the claim false. If the explanation amounts to hoping a later buyer pays more, label that dependence plainly. A household can then understand the proposition rather than mistake excitement for a plan. Do not turn this exercise into a universal claim that all named categories are either safe or unsafe. The output is a claim map with assumptions and questions for qualified review, not a product ranking.

### Simplicity reveals the conversation

Richards's official biography describes the use of cardstock and a Sharpie to explain money, and identifies his short-form sketches and books. The store biography also identifies his professional background and Sketch Guy work. [S1][S5]

Our application: a team has a page of conflicting priorities. Draw two plain circles labelled what we say matters and where time goes. List examples in each before deciding where the overlap is. This is our diagram, not a reproduction of his work. Its value is that colleagues can point to a mismatch and discuss it. A picture that hides the evidence is merely decorative. Add the dates, tasks or decisions behind the labels. In a household, the same move might show a purpose at the top and three actions beneath it, with one changed assumption in the margin. Keep the page small enough to use in a real conversation.

## How he decides

The sources support examining purpose, process, behavioural pressures and uncertainty together. The public sketches are useful because they make one distinction visible at a time. [S2][S3][S4]

Our practical adaptation uses a decision note with four fields: what we knew, what we assumed, what we chose and when we will revisit it. Record the note before seeing the outcome when possible. Later, evaluate whether the information was adequate and the alternatives were considered. A disappointing result may expose a poor process, an unlucky event or both. Do not rush to a flattering explanation. Equally, do not assume every loss means someone should have predicted the future.

When the question involves another person, ask what the numbers mean to them before trying to correct the behaviour. One partner's cash buffer may represent safety; the other's concern may be a neglected goal. The useful page contains both meanings and the question that requires a shared decision. This does not make all choices equally workable. It makes the disagreement specific enough to examine without pretending one chart can resolve it.

## Where he stood

In the interview, Richards repeatedly returns to why money matters and the limits of certainty. In his process essay, he resists judging a method by one outcome. His planning archive challenges hopeful speculation and the instinct to extrapolate recent performance. [S2][S3][S4]

Our use keeps that stance concrete. If a reader wants reassurance, distinguish emotional support from a prediction. You can acknowledge that uncertainty feels difficult while refusing to invent an outcome. If a reader wants an answer immediately, show what information would make the decision meaningfully different. A one-page plan can include unknowns. Its usefulness comes from directing attention, not from looking complete.

## Where he was wrong or criticised

Our criticism concerns the limits of using a simple behavioural framework alone. A diagram about fear or past performance does not establish an appropriate allocation, explain a reader's debt terms or resolve an income shortage. The official public material is deliberately concise; the interview also acknowledges uncertainty and the limits of projections. [S1][S3][S4]

Behaviour is only one part of a financial outcome. Fees, product structure, access, unstable income and changing obligations can matter even when someone follows a process calmly. Our application must therefore ask what is outside the sketch. It should not blame a reader for reacting to a real threat or claim that greater discipline guarantees success. When detail changes the decision, add the detail or obtain suitable professional help rather than defending a tidy picture.

## Blind spots to watch

The charm of a simple sketch can make an argument feel settled before it has been tested. Ask which data would challenge it. A picture of two lines is not itself evidence for the historical path, future path or causal relationship it suggests. In our exercises, label diagrams as conceptual and keep the supporting facts separate.

For career decisions, a purpose statement can clarify what matters but cannot predict whether a new role will satisfy it. Compare the documented role with the purpose, identify assumptions about hours or responsibility and gather missing information. For household decisions, keep the page accessible to everyone involved. Simplicity should reduce the burden of discussion, not give one person a persuasive tool for overriding another.
