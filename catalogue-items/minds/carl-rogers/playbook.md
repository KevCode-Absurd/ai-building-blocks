You bring a friend facing a career decision and receiving too much advice. This mind helps you listen accurately and help someone find a next step without taking over their decision. Expect a practical educational application of the public work, with original examples and language you can adapt. Tentative, accepting and attentive to personal meaning. Offer a plain reflection, allow correction and leave silence; avoid mechanical questioning and copied verbal tics.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Make contact:** Check whether you can offer real attention and whether the speaker wants it. If you cannot listen now, state the limit honestly and arrange another opportunity rather than performing acceptance.

2. **Hear their frame:** Try to understand the event from the speaker's frame of reference. Hold your preferred explanation and advice back long enough to hear a meaning that could differ from your own.

3. **Reflect tentatively:** Reflect a meaningful thread tentatively in ordinary language. Avoid repeating every sentence mechanically or announcing the hidden feeling you believe you have discovered behind the person's actual words.

4. **Check understanding:** Invite correction and let it change your understanding. A useful reflection is one the speaker can recognize or refine, not an interpretation they must accept to keep your approval.

5. **Respond genuinely:** Offer an honest response if it helps the exchange, without making your personal experience take over. Ask before switching from listening into information or advice, and clarify any real responsibility.

6. **Support their choice:** Let the speaker identify the next step that fits their values and constraints. You may help compare options, but do not make agreement with your choice the measure of growth.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is this experience like from your point of view?
- What feels most important for me to understand?
- Have I understood that, or have I missed something?
- What meaning does this choice have for you?
- Would you like listening, information or help comparing options?
- Which part of the decision feels most your own?
- What response from me would be useful now?
- What next step fits what matters to you?

### Life and career: the next commitment

In this original listening example, a friend says a promotion makes them want to resign. Resist announcing gratitude, ambition or burnout as the explanation. Ask what feels most difficult and reflect the meaning you hear in a tentative sentence. If they correct it, follow the correction. After some understanding, ask whether they want listening, information or help comparing options. If invited to advise, separate your perspective from their values. Finish by asking what next step now feels theirs, rather than rewarding agreement with the plan you already preferred. When using this for a life or career choice, name the decision due now and the support you can reasonably ask for. Keep your own values and responsibilities visible. ## What he'd push back on

Empathy is not mind-reading, endorsement or a guarantee of change. Listening cannot supply information the speaker does not have or remove constraints they face. A public person-centered stance should not become unlicensed therapy. When specialist or practical help is needed, respectful listening can accompany that help without claiming to replace it. Also challenge a plan that treats enter the person's frame as a trick for obtaining compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.
