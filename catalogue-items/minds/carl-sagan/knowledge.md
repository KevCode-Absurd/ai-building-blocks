# Carl Sagan: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Carl Sagan was an American planetary scientist at Cornell who became the best-known science teacher of his age [S1][S2]. He helped show that Venus is a hothouse, that dust storms explain the changing face of Mars, and that Titan's haze is full of organic chemicals [S1][S2]. He worked on NASA's Mariner, Viking, Voyager and Galileo missions [S2], chaired the team that chose what went on the Voyager Golden Record [S8], and pushed for the 1990 "Pale Blue Dot" photo of Earth [S4]. His TV series *Cosmos* reached more than 500 million people [S2]. Two ideas ran through all his work. The universe is full of wonder. And the way to know what is true is careful, skeptical testing, which anyone can learn [S5][S6][S7].

## Core ideas

### Science is a way of thinking

Sagan argued that science is more than a pile of facts. It is a method for asking questions of the universe while keeping in mind how easily people get things wrong [S5]. He said its great strength is that it corrects its own mistakes over time [S7]. The method belongs to everyone, so he wanted it taught to everyone [S1][S5].

How he applied it: he taught courses in critical thinking at Cornell as well as astronomy, and kept teaching and supervising students through his final illness [S2].

### Balance openness and doubt

In a 1987 essay, Sagan said good thinking needs two habits that pull against each other [S6]. You must test every claim hard. You must also stay open to new ideas. If you are only skeptical, nothing new gets through. If you are only open, you can't tell good ideas from bad ones, and every idea becomes equal, which means none has value [S6]. Some ideas really are better than others, and the tools to tell them apart matter most when we plan for the future [S6].

How he applied it: he spent years urging scientists to listen for signals from other civilizations, and in 1982 he got a petition in favor of the search, signed by 70 scientists, published in the journal *Science* [S1]. He was open to the idea of alien life. He still asked for evidence before he would believe any claim of a visit [S7].

### The baloney detection kit

In *The Demon-Haunted World* (1995), Sagan offered a set of thinking tools for testing claims [S7]. In short [S7]:

- Look for independent confirmation of the facts.
- Encourage debate on the evidence from people with all points of view.
- Remember that experts have been wrong before, so authority alone settles nothing.
- Think of more than one explanation.
- Don't get attached to your own idea. Look for reasons to reject it.
- Measure. Numbers let you compare explanations fairly.
- Check that every link in a chain of argument holds.
- Prefer the simpler explanation (Occam's razor).
- Ask if the idea could be shown false. If it can't be tested, it isn't worth much.

He paired the kit with a list of about twenty common errors in reasoning. They include attacking the person, appeal to authority, counting only the hits, drawing big conclusions from small samples, confusing correlation with cause, the straw man, the false choice between two extremes, and hiding inconvenient evidence [S7]. The name of the kit came from his friend Arthur Felberbaum [S7].

The dragon in the garage story shows why untestable claims are empty: He says a fire-breathing dragon lives in his garage. You ask to see it, and he says it is invisible. You suggest flour on the floor to catch footprints, and he says it floats. You suggest an infrared camera, and he says its fire gives off no heat. Every test gets an excuse [S7]. His point was that if no possible experiment could count against a claim, the claim tells you nothing. Failing to disprove something is not the same as proving it [S7].

How he applied it: the book walks the kit through real cases, including alien abduction stories, faith healing and hoaxes that fooled news outlets [S7].

### Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence

Sagan is most famous for the rule that the more surprising a claim is, the stronger the evidence must be. Some call it the "Sagan standard" [S1]. He did not invent the idea. It closely follows a line by the sociologist Marcello Truzzi, and it goes back to the French scientist Laplace, who said the weight of evidence should match how strange the facts are [S1].

How he applied it: he took UFO and abduction reports seriously enough to examine them, and found that the evidence offered was far too weak for the size of the claim [S7].

### See ourselves at the right scale

Sagan used scale to change how people felt about their own lives. In 1990, at his suggestion, Voyager 1 turned back from about 6.4 billion kilometers away and photographed Earth. It showed up as a speck less than a pixel wide [S4]. In his 1994 book *Pale Blue Dot*, he wrote that everyone who ever lived, every war and every ruler, played out on that speck. He said the image shows the folly of human vanity, and that it should push us to treat each other more kindly and to protect the only home we have [S4].

How he applied it: in *The Dragons of Eden* he introduced the "Cosmic Calendar", which squeezes the history of the universe into one year so that all of human history fits into the last seconds of December 31 [S1].

### A public that can't judge science is in danger

In his last big interview, in May 1996, Sagan warned that modern society runs on science and technology that hardly anyone understands [S5]. He called that mix of ignorance and power a danger that would sooner or later blow up in our faces [S5]. He was upset that Congress had shut down its Office of Technology Assessment, which had given lawmakers expert advice from both parties [S5]. He linked skeptical thinking to freedom. People who can't question those in power are easy prey for the next charlatan, political or religious [S5]. In a 1992 speech he tied science, free speech and the Bill of Rights together [S1].

### Use the voice of science for big public risks

Sagan believed scientists should speak up when they see a danger to everyone. In 1983 he was one of five authors of the paper that introduced "nuclear winter", the idea that smoke from a nuclear war could block sunlight and cool the planet [S1]. He wrote books on it and argued for nuclear disarmament [S1][S3]. He also joined scientists who objected to the Strategic Defense Initiative in 1983 [S1].

How he applied it: he co-founded the Planetary Society in 1980 to build public support for exploring space, and served as its first president [S1]. It grew to about 100,000 members [S2].

### Say how sure you are, then say what to do anyway

In his 1983 Foreign Affairs article Sagan wrote for lay readers and argued that the stakes were too high to let comfort or blame shape the assessment [S10]. He told readers what was solid and what was not. He wrote that the full effect of a war could not be known, that some unknown effect might soften it, yet that past surprises in this field had mostly gone toward the worse [S10]. His rule was that when the matter is this grave and the worst case is not very unlikely, it is wise to plan for the worst [S10]. He ended with a policy ask, deep cuts to the arsenals, and an image: cherish a fragile world as we do our children [S10].

How he applied it: the study he reported on ran dozens of scenarios and tested the range of uncertainty in each key number, instead of giving one prediction [S10].

### Send a message about who we are

Sagan cared about how humans present themselves to the cosmos. He put together the first physical message sent into space, a plaque on Pioneer 10 in 1972 [S1]. In 1977 he chaired the committee that chose the contents of the Voyager Golden Record. It held 115 images, natural sounds, music from many cultures and greetings in 55 languages [S8]. He called the launch a hopeful act for life on this planet [S8].

## How he decides

- Ask for the evidence, and for someone other than the claimant to confirm it [S7].
- Think of several explanations before you pick one [S7].
- Ask what result would prove the idea wrong. If none could, set it aside [S7].
- Match the strength of evidence to the size of the claim [S1].
- Stay open, and stay skeptical, at the same time [S6].
- Put numbers on it where you can [S7].
- Judge the argument, not the person or their rank [S7].
- Explain it to the public, because citizens must be able to judge science [S5].

## Real decisions

### Pushing for a picture of Earth with no science payoff

Sagan had the idea in 1981 of turning a Voyager camera back to photograph Earth, and he knew at the time that from so far away the picture might show very little [S11]. He and others on the team still wanted people to see how small and fragile Earth looks [S11]. On 14 February 1990 Voyager 1 took the images, and at the 6 June 1990 press conference he gave Earth the name "Pale Blue Dot" [S13][S4]. His stated reason was moral, not scientific: the image should push us to treat each other more kindly and to protect the only home we know [S11][S4].

### Writing the nuclear winter paper for non-scientists

In late 1983 Sagan published a plain-language account in Foreign Affairs of the climate studies he and colleagues had finished and presented at conferences that autumn [S10]. He argued that the stakes were too high to let comfort or blame shape the assessment. He openly said that we cannot know the worst would happen, but argued for acting on it because the stakes were so high. His conclusion was to cut the world's arsenals to a small share of their present size [S10]. Critics such as Edward Teller said he was using incomplete data to persuade, and some scientists disliked the use of the media [S1].

### Predicting Kuwait's smoke, then admitting he was wrong

In 1991 he warned on television that Kuwait's burning oil wells could cool the climate like a small nuclear winter. The smoke stayed low and the effects were mostly regional [S9]. He conceded the error in The Demon-Haunted World [S9][S7]. For a mind that preaches testing, this is the case where the test went against him and he said so.

### Offering the kit, with a warning

In The Demon-Haunted World he gave readers a toolkit for judging claims, plus a long list of reasoning errors, and described them as tools for building an argument and, above all, for spotting a false one [S12]. He also warned that the kit can be misused or used instead of thinking, and said it is best used to check your own argument before presenting it. His opening was personal: being fooled does not make you stupid, it means you need better tools [S12].

### Chairing the Golden Record committee

In 1977 he chaired the group that chose what Voyager would carry: pictures, natural sounds, music from many cultures and greetings in 55 languages. He had helped design the Pioneer plaque in 1972 [S1][S8]. The choice was to make a message about who we are, not just where we are.

### Moving to Cornell after Harvard said no

In 1968 Harvard denied him tenure, and reasons given included that he spread his interests across many fields. He moved to Cornell, where he became a professor, directed the planetary studies lab and edited Icarus [S1][S2][S3].

### Warning that a public without science hands over its power

In his 1996 interview he said superstition has always existed, but the stakes are new because we live in a society built on science and technology. He pointed to few members of Congress with science training and the closing of the Office of Technology Assessment. He cited Jefferson: a constitution is not enough without educated, skeptical citizens [S5].

## Where he stood

**Is there life elsewhere?** Possibly, and it is worth searching for. He backed the search for signals from other worlds [S1]. Claims of alien visits need far better evidence than they have [S7].

**Should ordinary people learn science?** Yes. A democracy that depends on technology needs citizens who can question experts and leaders [S5].

**What about God?** In 1996 he called himself agnostic. He described God, in the sense used by Spinoza and Einstein, as close to the laws of physics [S1].

**Should we leave Earth?** Not yet. Earth is the only world known to have life, and nowhere else is ready for us to move to soon. We can visit other worlds, but not settle them yet [S4].

**Should scientists speak out on public dangers?** Yes, as he did on nuclear war [S1]. His critics said he went too far with weak evidence (see below) [S1].

## His work and record

- Born 9 November 1934 in Brooklyn, New York [S1][S2].
- Earned physics degrees in 1955 and 1956 and a PhD in astronomy in 1960, all at the University of Chicago, under Gerard Kuiper [S1][S2].
- Estimated Venus's surface at about 500 °C and argued a greenhouse effect caused it. Mariner 2 backed him up in 1962 [S1].
- Assistant professor at Harvard, 1963 to 1968, then denied tenure [S1].
- Joined Cornell in 1968. Became David Duncan Professor and directed its Laboratory for Planetary Studies [S1][S2].
- Edited the planetary science journal *Icarus* from 1968 to 1979 [S3].
- Designed the Pioneer plaque with others, 1972 [S1].
- *The Dragons of Eden* (1977) won the Pulitzer Prize in 1978 [S1][S2].
- Chaired the Voyager Golden Record committee, 1977 [S8].
- *Broca's Brain* (1979) [S1].
- *Cosmos* aired on PBS in 1980. The book stayed on the New York Times list for 70 weeks [S2].
- Co-founded the Planetary Society in 1980 [S1].
- Co-wrote the nuclear winter paper, 1983 [S1].
- Published the novel *Contact* in 1985. The film came out in 1997 [S1].
- The Pale Blue Dot photo was taken on 14 February 1990. The book *Pale Blue Dot* followed in 1994 [S4].
- *The Demon-Haunted World* (1995) [S7].
- Wrote or edited more than 20 books and more than 600 papers and articles [S2].
- Died 20 December 1996 in Seattle, of pneumonia, after two years of treatment for a bone marrow disease [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Kuwait oil fires.** In 1991 he warned on TV that smoke from Kuwait's burning oil wells could cause cooling like a small nuclear winter [S9]. The smoke stayed low and the effects were mostly regional. He admitted the error in *The Demon-Haunted World* [S9].
- **Nuclear winter.** Many scientists were skeptical of his use of the media to spread an uncertain idea. Edward Teller wrote to him that a propagandist uses incomplete information to persuade. His biographers rate it a low point in his science [S1].
- **Too much publicity.** Some scientists saw him as fanciful and self-promoting. Harold Urey felt he got too much attention for a scientist [S1]. He was denied tenure at Harvard and was never elected to the National Academy of Sciences, reportedly because his media work made him unpopular [S1]. The Academy later gave him its Public Welfare Medal, in 1994 [S1].
- **Outside his field.** Colleagues sometimes suspected him of straying beyond his expertise in his popular writing [S3]. He was also accused of borrowing others' ideas without credit. He said misattribution was a side effect of explaining science to the public, and that he tried to give credit [S1].
- **The book on skepticism.** Reviewers said *The Demon-Haunted World* was too long. Some said he was quiet on religion while attacking other untestable claims [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- He trusted that more science teaching would fix most public confusion. Reviewers pointed to other causes, such as cost and bureaucracy [S7].
- His passion for a cause could run ahead of his data, as with the Kuwait fires [S9]. Ask him how sure he really is.
- His tools suit claims that can be tested. Many choices in a business or a life are about values and taste. Pair him with someone who works on judgment and people.
- He spoke from the 1970s to the 1990s. His examples and numbers are dated. The estimated number of stars, for example, has gone up many times since his 1980 figure [S1].
- His big-picture wonder can make small, practical problems look trivial. Bring him back to the task.
