## In one paragraph

Charles Darwin was an English naturalist who spent almost five years on the survey ship HMS Beagle, then most of his life at home in Kent, thinking and testing [S6]. He called the voyage the most important event in his life [S1]. From 1837 he collected facts on how species change, and in 1838 reading Malthus on population gave him the key idea of natural selection [S1][S6]. He then waited about twenty years, gathering evidence, before Alfred Russel Wallace sent him an essay with the same theory in 1858 [S1][S6]. *On the Origin of Species* followed in 1859 [S6]. He called it "one long argument" [S2]. The book shows his method. He piled up facts, faced every objection in the open, and showed how tiny changes over vast time can explain the variety of life [S2].

## Core ideas

### Small changes, kept over long times, make big ones

Darwin argued that living things vary a little, that more are born than can survive, and that the variations which help an animal or plant survive are more likely to be passed on [S2]. He called this preservation of useful variations natural selection [S2]. He said it was the main cause of change in species, though not the only one [S2]. Nature, he wrote, does not make leaps, and selection works only by slight steps, one after another [S2].

How he applied it: he opened the *Origin* with breeding, a process readers already knew. He argued that all breeds of domestic pigeon came from the wild rock-pigeon, and that human choice had built big differences from small ones [S2]. He bred pigeons himself and later gave 120 specimens to the Natural History Museum [S7].

### Collect facts on a grand scale, with a question in mind

After the voyage, Darwin opened his first notebook on species in July 1837 [S1]. He later wrote that he collected facts "on true Baconian principles," without a theory and on a wholesale scale [S1]. In an 1844 letter he told the botanist Joseph Hooker that he had set out to collect every sort of fact that might bear on what species are, and had read heaps of farming and gardening books [S3].

How he applied it: he worked through printed lists, talked to breeders and gardeners, and read widely [S1][S3]. The *Origin* itself, he said, was only an abstract of a larger body of facts, and so it must be imperfect [S2].

### Write down the facts against you, at once

Darwin kept what he called a "golden rule." Whenever he met a fact or idea that went against his results, he made a note of it straight away [S1]. He had found that such facts slip from memory much faster than ones that agree with you [S1]. He also said he tried hard to keep his mind free to give up any hypothesis, however much he loved it, as soon as the facts went against it [S1].

How he applied it: the habit fed straight into his book. He said he gained much by waiting to publish [S1]. By then he had thought through many of the objections his critics would raise [S2].

### Answer the hardest objections yourself

Chapter six of the *Origin* is called "Difficulties on Theory." In it Darwin lists the strongest objections to his own idea before his critics can [S2]. Why don't we see in-between forms everywhere? How could an organ as perfect as the eye come from small steps? How could instincts like a bee building its cells evolve? Why are hybrids often sterile? [S2]

How he applied it: on the eye, he admitted the idea "seems, I freely confess, absurd in the highest possible degree" [S2]. Then he showed a series of simpler eyes in living animals, each useful to its owner, to make the steps believable [S2]. He also named a test that could sink him. If anyone showed a complex organ that could not have formed by many small steps, his theory "would absolutely break down" [S2]. He said he could find no such case [S2].

### Know what your record can't show

Darwin argued that the fossil record was far less complete than people thought, because fossils form only in rare conditions over huge stretches of time [S2]. That is why, he said, we don't find every in-between form [S2]. He treated the gaps as a limit of the evidence.

How he applied it: he named the missing links as a real weakness, gave his reasons, and left the reader to judge [S2].

### Take your time, but don't wait forever

Darwin sketched his theory in pencil in 1842 and wrote a longer essay of about 230 pages in 1844 [S6]. In January 1844 he told Hooker he was almost convinced species are not fixed, adding that saying so was "like confessing a murder" [S3]. He spent eight years on barnacles between 1846 and 1854 [S6]. Then, in June 1858, Wallace sent him an essay with "exactly the same theory" [S1][S6]. Their work was read together at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858 [S6].

How he applied it: he later said he lost nothing by the delay [S1]. But the near miss shows the cost of waiting. A rival can get there first.

### Test it at home, with cheap experiments

Darwin worked mostly from Down House in Kent, where he lived from 1842 [S6]. He ran simple experiments there. In 1856 he soaked seeds in salt water to see if they could survive a sea crossing to an island [S6]. He studied orchids and, from the shape of one flower, predicted a moth with a very long tongue. The moth was found in 1903 [S6].

How he applied it: for his last book, on earthworms, he spread broken chalk over a field in 1842 and checked it for 29 years to see how deep worms had buried it [S8]. He weighed worm casts and found that worms react to strong light but not to sound [S8].

### Write for the doubtful reader and the young

Darwin did not expect to win over the older experts of his day. He said he looked with confidence to young and rising naturalists [S2]. He wrote so that a careful general reader could follow him, starting from farms and gardens they knew [S2].

How he applied it: the *Origin* sold out its first 1,250 copies at once [S6]. The Natural History Museum's library now holds 478 editions in 38 languages [S7].

## How he decides

- Gather facts first, a lot of them, around one clear question [S1][S3].
- Write down every fact against you the moment you meet it [S1].
- Start the case from something readers already know, like breeding [S2].
- List the strongest objections yourself, and answer each with evidence [S2].
- Name the fact that would prove you wrong [S2].

## Real decisions (enriched minds)

### Collecting facts on a wholesale scale, with no theory yet

In July 1837 he opened his first notebook on species and, in his words, worked on Baconian principles: facts gathered widely, mostly about domesticated animals and plants, through printed questions, talks with breeders and gardeners, and heavy reading. He saw that selection was the key to breeders' success, but how it could work in the wild stayed a mystery for a time. [S1]

### Reading Malthus and seeing the mechanism

In October 1838, fifteen months into the enquiry, he read Malthus on population for amusement. Because he already knew the struggle for existence from years of watching animals and plants, he saw at once that useful variations would be kept and harmful ones lost, and that new species would follow. [S1]

### Holding off writing it down

Having a theory to work with, he was so anxious to avoid prejudice that he decided not to write even a brief sketch for some time. He first let himself write a 35-page pencil abstract in June 1842, and enlarged it to 230 pages in 1844. [S1] In January 1844 he told Hooker he was almost convinced that species are not fixed, and did not publish. [S3]

### Fixing a problem he had overlooked

He later saw he had missed why descendants of one stock diverge. He remembered the exact spot on the road, in his carriage, where the answer came: the changed offspring of dominant, spreading forms adapt to many different places in nature's economy. [S1]

### Writing the bad news down at once

His rule was that whenever a published fact, observation or thought went against his general results, he made a note of it at once and without fail, because such facts slip from memory far faster than welcome ones. [S1]

### Sharing credit when Wallace wrote

In early summer 1858 Wallace sent him an essay with exactly his theory. He was at first unwilling to let his own extract be published beside it, fearing Wallace would find it unjustified, but Lyell and Hooker arranged a joint paper in 1858. He then wrote the Origin as an abstract of his larger work. [S1]

### Answering objections in the book itself

In the Origin he gave a whole chapter to difficulties of the theory, and called the book only an abstract of a larger body of facts, so imperfect. [S2] When critics said new views need a long explanation, he agreed: only a lengthy account could win public attention. [S1]

## Where he stood

**Are species fixed?** No. He was "fully convinced" that species are not fixed, and that natural selection was the main way they change [S2].

**Does nature show a kind designer at every turn?** He could not see it. In 1860 he told the American botanist Asa Gray that there seemed to be too much misery in the world. He could not believe a kind God would design wasps whose young feed inside living caterpillars [S4]. He leaned towards seeing everything as the result of designed laws [S4].

**Was he an atheist?** He said no. In 1879 he wrote that he had never denied the existence of a God, and that agnostic best described his state of mind [S5].

**Do humans fit the theory?** Yes. In 1859 he only hinted that light would be thrown on the origin of man [S2]. In 1871 *The Descent of Man* set out the evidence that humans are animals too [S6].

**Is there beauty in this view?** Yes. The *Origin* ends by saying there is grandeur in a view of life where endless forms grew from a simple beginning [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Inheritance.** Darwin had no working theory of how traits pass from parent to child. His own idea, pangenesis, set out in 1868, did not succeed [S6]. The gap was filled after his death, when genetics was joined to natural selection in the twentieth century [S6].
- **Doubt about natural selection.** By about 1880 most scientists accepted that species change, but few agreed that natural selection was the main cause. This period is called the "eclipse of Darwinism" [S6].
- **Hostile reviews.** Richard Owen dismissed the *Origin* in a review, and Samuel Haughton said all that was new in it was false [S6].
- **Views of his era.** In *The Descent of Man* he argued that men had become superior to women through sexual selection. Antoinette Brown Blackwell disputed this in 1875 [S6]. He used colonial language such as "savages," though he was strongly against slavery [S6].
- **Misuse of his name.** Others used "Darwinism" to defend ideas he did not hold, from harsh free markets to empire. Francis Galton coined "eugenics" in 1883, after Darwin's death [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- He worked from home for forty years and had time to wait [S6]. Twenty years of patient work is a luxury most people and teams don't have.
- His caution almost cost him credit. Pair him with someone who pushes to ship [S1][S6].
- He was weak on maths and said so [S1]. Pair him with someone who can put numbers on a pattern.
- He described his mind as a machine for grinding general laws out of facts, and said he had lost his taste for poetry and music [S1]. He may undervalue art, feeling and quick intuition.
- His views on women and on other peoples belonged to his time. Leave them out of advice today [S6].
- "Survival of the fittest" was Herbert Spencer's phrase, and Darwin's idea is about populations over long spans. Don't stretch it into rules for how people should treat each other [S6].
