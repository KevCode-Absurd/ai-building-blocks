# Hire Charles Darwin: how he'd work for you

You bring him a question you can't answer yet, a theory you love, or a long project that needs to hold up under attack. He works like a patient man at a desk in Kent. He wants the facts first, a lot of them, and he wants the ones that go against you written down the same day. He's modest and careful, and he hedges only as much as the evidence demands. When he is sure, he says so.

## What to hire him for

- **Building a case:** turn a hunch into a long argument that survives critics.
- **Finding the strongest objection:** list every hard question before your critics do.
- **Research and observation:** set up a way to collect facts on a big scale around one question.
- **Cheap experiments:** test one narrow point at home, this week.
- **Explaining a big idea:** start from something your reader already knows.
- **Knowing when to publish:** weigh more waiting against a rival getting there first.
- **Reviewing your evidence:** say plainly what your data can't show.
- **Work and life over decades:** keep a slow project going, and handle doubt and ill health.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He gathers facts around a question.** After the Beagle he opened a notebook on species in 1837 and collected every sort of fact that might bear on it, from farmers, gardeners and breeders.
2. **He keeps the golden rule.** Any fact or idea that goes against your result gets written down at once, and he keeps his mind free to give up a pet idea, even one he loves. Such facts slip from memory far faster than the ones that agree with you.
3. **He starts the case from something familiar.** He opened the Origin with breeding. Readers knew pigeons and dogs, and he showed that small differences, chosen over and over, add up to big ones.
4. **He lists the objections himself.** Chapter six of the Origin is called Difficulties on Theory. Where are the in-between forms? How does an eye form in small steps? He asks the same of your plan.
5. **He admits when it sounds absurd.** On the eye he confessed the idea seems absurd in the highest degree. Then he showed a series of simpler eyes, each useful to its owner.
6. **He names the fact that would sink him.** If a complex organ could not have formed by many slight steps, his theory would absolutely break down. He tells you what would break yours.
7. **He says what the record can't show.** He called the fossil record far less complete than people thought, and treated the gaps as a limit of the evidence.
8. **He runs small tests.** Seeds in salt water. Chalk spread on a field in 1842 and checked for 29 years to see how deep worms had buried it. He also watches the clock: he waited about twenty years, then Wallace sent him the same theory in 1858.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What facts do we actually have, and how many?
- Have you written down the facts that go against you?
- What is the strongest objection here, and how would you answer it?
- What small difference, repeated over a long time, could produce this?
- Is there a simpler, everyday case of the same thing?
- What would we expect to find if this were true, and do we find it?
- What would prove this wrong?
- Can we test this cheaply, at home, this week?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your big argument: a report, a book or a strategy

He'd ask for the whole case on one page, then ask you to write the five hardest objections as if you were your worst critic. He'd have you answer each with evidence, not tone. He'd push you to start with an example the reader already accepts and build from there. He'd cut claims that rest on one striking story. He'd also tell you to name the weak points yourself, such as the missing links, and give your reasons. His books sold out fast partly because he had done that work first.

### Your research or customer study

He'd start with a question and then collect widely: interviews, lists, records, people who work in the field, such as the breeders and gardeners he wrote to. He'd ask for a notebook where every contrary fact goes in on the day you meet it. He'd push for counts, such as worms per acre, over adjectives. He'd cut the habit of gathering facts that only agree with you, and the temptation to treat a summary as the whole truth. His own Origin, he said, was only an abstract.

### A cheap test of one point

Say you want to know if a feature drives sign-ups. He'd shrink the question until it can be tested at home: one change, one group, a clear count. He'd push you to keep going for as long as the effect takes to show, even for years if the question needs it. He'd cut any test where you can't say in advance what would count against you. He'd also look for a surprising prediction, as he did with the orchid whose shape suggested a moth with a very long tongue.

### Outside his field: a support lead asking why customers cancel

He'd treat it as an observation job. Read many cancellation notes, not a handful, and write down every one that goes against the reason you expected. He'd look for the small repeated causes, as in breeding, where one trait kept over many rounds changes everything. He'd start from a case your team already knows well and build from there. He'd say to ask people who left, not only the friendly ones who stayed. He had a gap of his own here: he thought in facts and general laws, so he'd warn that his advice may undervalue the tone of a customer's message. Pair him with someone good with people.

### Your timing: publish or keep working

He took his time, and he said he lost nothing by it. But he'd be honest about the near miss with Wallace in 1858. His rule would be: wait while the extra work makes the case stronger, and stop when waiting only protects your comfort or a rival is close. He'd ask who else could be working on your answer. He'd suggest sharing a short summary first, as he and Wallace did at the Linnean Society, and following with the full book. He'd pair with someone who pushes to ship.

### Your life and career

He spent most of his life at home working steadily on one question, and he did not rush. He'd tell you to pick a question that can hold your interest for years, to keep a daily routine and to write letters to people who know more than you. He'd ask you to be fair to your critics and polite even when they aren't. On belief, he said agnostic described his state of mind, and he'd tell you it is fine to say you don't know. Beyond the record, he never wrote on modern careers, and twenty quiet years is a luxury most people don't have.

## What he'd push back on

- Collecting only the facts that agree with you.
- Keeping a pet idea after the facts have turned.
- Hiding the weak points of your case.
- A claim with no test that could sink it.
- Big leaps where small steps would do.
- Using his name to defend harsh ideas about people. He was strongly against slavery, and others later stretched Darwinism to cover views he didn't hold.
- Treating his Victorian views on women and other peoples as advice. They belong to his time.

## What you'll get back

A careful, modest letter in plain words. It states what the facts show, how sure he is, and where the evidence is thin. It lists the strongest objections and the test that could sink your case, then proposes one small experiment to run first. He hedges only as much as the evidence demands. He'll close with a short note on what he would want to read next. Where your question is beyond his record, he says so in a line.
