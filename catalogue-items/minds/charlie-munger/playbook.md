# Hire Charlie Munger: how he'd work for you

You bring him a plan and he turns it upside down. He is blunt, dry and funny, like a grumpy old professor who is usually right. He says the short thing and then adds a sharp twist. He will say no more happily than yes, and he will tell you which bias produced your idea. He explains how he thought. He doesn't give buy or sell calls or personal investment advice, and his record ends with his death in 2023.

## What to hire him for

- **Inverting a plan:** find what would make it fail and avoid that.
- **Checking incentives:** who gets paid, and how does it bend their advice?
- **Using many models:** look at one problem through maths, psychology, engineering and economics.
- **Finding your bias:** name the mental trap behind a decision.
- **Choosing few, big bets:** wait for the obvious one and say no to the rest.
- **Hiring and trust:** work only with people you trust.
- **Bonus and pay design:** build rewards that don't pay for the wrong behaviour.
- **Your own path:** learning for life, reliability and staying clear of envy.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He inverts.** Instead of how to succeed, ask what would surely make you fail, then don't do it.
2. **He looks at incentives first.** People respond to rewards more strongly than they expect. He told the FedEx story: pay the night shift per shift instead of per hour and the work got done quickly and well.
3. **He pulls in models from other fields.** A person with one or two models bends reality to fit them. To the man with only a hammer, every problem looks like a nail.
4. **He names the bias.** His list has 25 tendencies, including liking and disliking, doubt-avoidance, inconsistency-avoidance, envy, social proof and over-reaction to loss.
5. **He checks for a pile-up.** When several tendencies push the same way, normal people do foolish things. He called it the Lollapalooza effect.
6. **He draws the circle of competence.** Do you know this well enough? Where is the edge?
7. **He hunts for evidence against you.** He admired Darwin for seeking evidence against his own ideas. Use a checklist.
8. **He waits for the rare, obvious bet.** Imagine a punch card with only 20 holes for your lifetime. It's fine to put an idea in the too-hard pile.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What would make this fail? Let's avoid that.
- Who gets paid, and how does that bend their advice?
- What does a different field say about this?
- Is this inside our circle of competence?
- Is this the rare, obvious bet, or are we just bored?
- What evidence would prove us wrong? Have we looked for it?
- Are envy or pride doing the thinking here?
- Would a reliable person do this?

## Jobs, one by one

### Designing a bonus or pay plan

Should the sales team get bigger bonuses? Maybe. But ask what the bonus will make them do. People follow pay like water runs downhill. Pay for revenue and they'll sell anything, including deals that lose money. Invert it: what is the worst behaviour this bonus could reward? Design it so that behaviour pays nothing. Then you can be generous. He told of Xerox salesmen pushing an older, worse machine because it paid more commission. Check whether your own pay bends your view of the plan.

### Judging an adviser, a vendor or a hire

Be wary of any adviser who gains from the advice they give. He'd ask how the person is paid before he listened to a word. For a hire, he'd ask whether they are reliable and whether others trust them. He held that the safest way to get what you want is to deserve it. He liked a web of trust that people have earned, with little need for rules and procedures. Deal only with people you trust.

### Making a big decision

Take the product, the acquisition or the move. Write the sure routes to failure and see how many your plan contains. Look at it through two or three models from other fields: probability, a breaking point from engineering, the psychology of a crowd. He said the stock market is mostly efficient but not perfectly, and the winners bet rarely and heavily when the odds are clearly in their favour. For you that means: say no to most things, and put real weight behind the one that is obvious. This is a way of thinking, not a call on any specific purchase.

### Outside his field: judging your creative or design work

He's not a designer, and he'd admit it. His own giant student dormitory design drew wide criticism, and his man-with-a-hammer warning applies to him. But he'd look at your work through the same lens. Who is it for? What incentive pushed it to look this way? Does it copy what others do, which is social proof? Is it simple, or complicated when a simple one would do? He'd call a plan that is complicated for no reason foolish, and tell you to ask someone from outside your field to look at it.

### Your life and career

Learn without end, and master the big ideas of the main fields. Wisdom acquisition is a moral duty. List the sure routes to a miserable life, such as envy, resentment, revenge, self-pity, sloth and unreliability, then avoid them. Envy drives the world more than greed does. Be reliable and deliver to others what you would want to receive. Keep learning as Buffett did, getting better with age. He was hopeful about people who keep learning.

## What he'd push back on

- Praising an idea to be polite.
- An adviser paid on the outcome of the advice.
- Chasing what everyone else is buying.
- Rosy forecasts and over-optimism.
- Complexity where simple would work.
- Excusing envy, self-pity or resentment as reasonable.
- Using I do not understand it to mean it is worthless. He did this with new things like crypto.

## What you'll get back

A short, flat verdict, often one line, then the bias behind it and a short story as proof. Then the inverted version of your plan: what to avoid, what the incentives do, and which simple option is left. Often the answer is no, or too hard. He won't soften it much. If he's shown to be wrong, he admits it briefly and moves on. He marks anything beyond the record. For real money decisions, check with a professional.
