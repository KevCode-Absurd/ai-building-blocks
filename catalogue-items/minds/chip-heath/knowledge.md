# Chip Heath: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Chip Heath is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his research, an article he co-wrote in 2006, interviews he gave from 2013 to 2022, and reference pages. Most of his books were written with his brother Dan Heath, so many ideas here belong to both of them [S1][S12]. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after 2022.

## In one paragraph

Heath is a psychologist who teaches at Stanford's business school, where he is now an emeritus professor of organizational behavior [S2]. His question is why some ideas survive in the "social marketplace of ideas" and others die, and how people make choices and the mistakes they make along the way [S2]. With Dan he wrote four bestsellers: *Made to Stick* (2007) on memorable messages, *Switch* (2010) on change, *Decisive* (2013) on choices, and *The Power of Moments* (2017) on experiences people remember [S1]. In 2022 he added *Making Numbers Count*, with Karla Starr, on how to explain data [S2]. The thread through all of it is practical: take what psychology knows about the mind and turn it into steps a manager, teacher or writer can use on Monday [S7].

## Core ideas

### The curse of knowledge

Once you know something, it is very hard to imagine not knowing it. Leaders who have spent years inside a strategy pack a lot of detail into short phrases. Front-line staff don't share that detail, so they only hear a slogan [S3]. Heath and his brother argued in 2006 that this is a main reason sound strategies fail to change what people do [S3]. He makes the same point about experts with numbers: they forget that raw figures are a foreign language to most listeners [S10].

How he applied it: the 2006 article picked on lines like "unlocking shareholder value" as examples of phrases that mean a lot to the boss and almost nothing to the staff [S3].

### Six traits of ideas that stick

*Made to Stick* says that ideas people remember tend to share six traits. They are simple, meaning the core is found and kept. They are unexpected, so they get attention. They are concrete, so people can picture and recall them. They are credible. They are emotional, so people care. And they come as stories, which help people act on them [S4]. The book takes the idea of "stickiness" from Malcolm Gladwell's *The Tipping Point* and asks what makes a message stick [S4].

How he applied it: the book uses the Southwest Airlines low-price strategy as a model of a simple core that staff can use to make daily choices. Each chapter ends with a "clinic" that fixes a real message [S4]. He built a Stanford course, How to Make Ideas Stick, on the same work [S2].

### Change needs the rider, the elephant and the path

*Switch* borrows a picture from the psychologist Jonathan Haidt. The rational mind is a rider on top of an emotional elephant. The rider plans and analyses but can stall in "paralysis by analysis." The elephant is bigger and supplies the energy [S5]. Lasting change needs three things: direct the rider with a clear goal, motivate the elephant with feeling, and shape the path so the change is easy [S5].

How it was applied: a 2013 review of the book used a hospital safety campaign as the model. It set a clear goal and deadline, used a mother's story of losing her daughter to a hospital error to stir feeling, and gave hospitals step-by-step guides and weekly calls [S5].

### Widen your options, then test them

*Decisive* lays out four steps: widen your options, reality-test your assumptions, gain distance before you decide, and prepare to be wrong [S6]. Heath says people often frame a choice as yes or no. Asked about firing a weak employee, they forget to weigh moving or mentoring the person [S6]. Research he cites found that people who considered just one more option did far better than those who weighed only one [S7].

How he applied it: he points to research by Kathleen Eisenhardt showing that the fastest teams in Silicon Valley used more information and kept two or three options alive at once. What really slows people down, he argues, is having only one option or being unable to drop a costly project [S6][S7].

### Fight confirmation bias on purpose

Heath calls this tendency the spotlight effect. We look at what is already in our heads and miss what sits outside the beam [S7]. He says psychology has found about 30 ways decisions go wrong, and confirmation bias, looking for evidence we already agree with, is a big one [S6]. His fix is practical: ask why an idea might fail, seek out a devil's advocate, and talk to people outside your circle who have solved a similar problem [S6][S7].

How he applied it: he uses the story of Alfred Sloan at General Motors, who delayed a decision because everyone agreed, so the team could develop some disagreement first [S7]. He also suggests a school principal trying to speed up a lunch line should study restaurants and stadiums, not just other schools [S6].

### Prepare to be wrong, with tripwires

Our view of the future is never perfect, so Heath wants safeguards set in advance. These include contingency plans and tripwires. A tripwire is a line you pick now, such as a sales level in a test market, that will force you to stop and think again later [S6]. A second opinion works like insurance. He cites studies suggesting that doctors who are fully certain can still be wrong a large share of the time [S6].

How he applied it: for distance, he recommends Suzy Welch's 10-10-10 test, which asks how you will feel about a choice in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years [S6]. A quicker tip from the book is to ask what you would tell your best friend to do [S7].

### People remember peaks, so build them

*The Power of Moments* argues that most experiences fade, while memory clusters around peaks, beginnings, endings and transitions [S8][S9]. Defining moments tend to hold four elements: elevation, insight, pride and connection [S8][S9]. Heath thinks pride is the most underused, and he gives the marks parents make on a doorframe as a child grows as an example [S8]. Surprise comes from breaking the script people expect [S8].

How he applied it: he praises John Deere in India for a first day that includes a welcome from a friend, a desk ready on arrival and a note from the chief executive on the company's history [S8]. He uses Disney's Space Mountain to show that people forget the long wait and remember the short ride [S8].

### Translate numbers into pictures

In *Making Numbers Count*, Heath treats statistics like a language most people don't speak. Untranslated, a number confuses people and loses its force [S10]. Good translation compares a figure with something familiar, makes it concrete and ties it to feeling, because feeling is what moves people to act [S10].

How he applied it: he tells of students who sold long-life light bulbs by saying one bulb would last from the time a child learns to walk to the time they start driving lessons [S10]. He also turns "40% don't wash their hands" into "two of the last five people who shook your hand" [S10].

## How he decides

- Find the core of the idea before you write a word [S4].
- Assume your audience doesn't know what you know [S3].
- When a choice looks like yes or no, add at least one more option [S6][S7].
- Ask why the plan might fail, and find someone paid to disagree [S6][S7].
- Gain distance: think 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years out [S6].
- Set a tripwire before you start, so stopping is a plan and not a fight [S6].
- For change, give a clear goal, a reason to care and an easier path [S5].
- Aim to build a peak people will rate 9 or 10 out of 10, rather than smoothing every step to a 7 [S8].
- Make the number concrete and emotional before you show it [S10].

## Where he stood

**Why do good strategies fail?** Often because leaders describe them in abstract terms that front-line staff can't use. The curse of knowledge hides the gap from the people at the top [S3].

**Does more information slow decisions down?** Not in his reading of the research. The fastest teams used more information. A lack of options and an inability to quit slow people down more [S6][S7].

**Should we teach people about biases?** Teaching a list of biases is not enough. He wants practical steps that fix the process, and he started out studying decisions with Amos Tversky [S7].

**Can memorable moments be planned?** Yes. He argues you don't have to wait for chance, and that leaders and speakers can design them on purpose [S9].

**Do numbers speak for themselves?** No. People skip the work of translating numbers because they are tired after the analysis, and the message suffers [S10].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

Our sources record mostly praise for the books, and little direct criticism [S4]. Fair limits a reader should keep in mind:

- **Sticky is not the same as true.** His own research shows how a modest lab result became the "Mozart effect" and grew far beyond the evidence. Coverage shifted to babies, who had never been studied [S11]. He compared it to fad diets and simple business frameworks, which spread when problems are hard [S11]. The same tools that make a good idea stick can help a weak one spread.
- **Models, not laws.** The rider and elephant is a borrowed metaphor. A reviewer praised it, but it is a way to think, not a measured result [S5].
- **Success stories.** The books lean on vivid cases like John Deere, Southwest and Disney [S4][S8]. Readers see the cases that worked, not the ones that tried the same thing and failed.

## Blind spots to watch

- He is strong on the message and the process. He says less about whether the idea itself is any good, so pair him with someone who tests the substance.
- His tools come from psychology and big organisations. A tiny team may lack the time to run every step of a four-part decision method.
- Stories and emotion can be used to mislead. Keep the "credible" part of the six traits in view [S4][S11].
- Designing peak moments can tip into gimmicks if the basics are broken. A warm cookie won't fix a dirty room.
