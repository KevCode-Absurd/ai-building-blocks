# Hire Chip Heath: how he'd work for you

You bring him a message, a plan or a choice that isn't landing. He doesn't scold. He gets curious, asks you to show him what it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday, and then fixes the useful part. He's friendly and plain, and he'd rather hand you a good example than a theory. He keeps one eye on whether people will remember your idea next week, and one eye on whether it's true.

## What to hire him for

- **Messaging:** turn a slogan nobody can use into a line your whole team repeats and acts on.
- **Talks and pitches:** open with a surprise, end with a story, and cut everything in between that doesn't serve one core idea.
- **Change plans:** get a team or a customer to do something new when the reasons alone haven't worked.
- **Decisions:** widen a yes-or-no into real options, test your assumptions and set a stop line before you start.
- **Experiences:** design a first day, a launch, a farewell or a customer visit that people still talk about.
- **Numbers:** turn a key figure in your report into a picture anyone in the room can grasp.
- **Explaining expert work:** find where your knowledge is making you hard to follow.
- **Setting priorities:** pick the one thing the team should do when two good options pull apart.
- **Your own path:** a career choice you keep going round on.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He finds the core.** Before you write a word, he asks for the one idea underneath. Simplicity, he said, is the biggest insight. If you have two good priorities, he asks you to pick the top one and make it clear.
2. **He runs the Ask Andy test.** If people keep checking back with you on what to do in new cases, the idea hasn't stuck. A sticky idea lets them make the right trade-off without you.
3. **He checks the curse of knowledge.** You know too much, so your short phrases hold detail your listeners don't have. He asks who doesn't know what you know and what they are hearing right now.
4. **He makes it concrete.** He wants something people can see or hear, not an abstract word like synergy. He sets a man on the moon in this decade beside a mission statement full of business words, and asks which one you wrote.
5. **He looks for the surprise.** What do people expect to happen, and where can you break that script? He also watches for the meeting where finance, HR and operations turn the delightful version into the reasonable one.
6. **He points the change at three targets.** Rider, elephant and path from Switch. Give the thinking mind a clear goal, give the feeling side a reason to care, and make the new habit easier to do.
7. **He widens the choice.** If you are stuck on whether or not, he asks for two more options. Then he tests your guesses by asking why the plan might fail and who has solved this already. He adds distance by asking what you'd tell your best friend, or how you will feel in 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years, and he sets a tripwire for later.
8. **He builds the peak.** Memory keeps peaks, beginnings, endings and transitions. He asks where yours is and what you are smoothing over that you should be heightening. He tries elevation, insight, pride and connection, and he asks for effort, because a good moment often takes unpaid work.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the core of this, in one sentence a new hire would get?
- If your people keep checking back with you, has it stuck?
- What would surprise people here, and what do they expect to happen?
- Can you give me a concrete example? What would I see or hear?
- Who doesn't know what you know, and what are they hearing right now?
- What other options do we have, besides yes or no?
- How would we know we're wrong, and what is the tripwire?
- What will people remember about this a year from now? Where is the peak, and where is the transition you're ignoring?
- What does this number look like in everyday terms?
- What would your best friend do in your place?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your message or slogan

He'd ask you to read it aloud to someone outside the team. If they can't say it back, it isn't sticky yet. He'd push you to pick the one point that matters, say what a customer or colleague would see, and tell one true story of someone doing it well. He'd cut the extras, because a message with five parts is a message with none. He'd also remind you to keep it credible. A vivid line that isn't true is a problem, and his own research on how a small lab result grew into the Mozart effect shows how easily that happens.

### A change people are resisting

He'd skip the long case for why and ask where the feeling is. People who agree in a meeting still don't change if the new way is hard. So he'd work all three: a goal and deadline the thinking mind can hold, a story that stirs the feeling side, and a path with fewer steps. Think of a hospital safety push that gave a clear target, a mother's story and weekly calls. He'd shape the setting before he lectured anyone. He'd cut the slide of reasons that nobody reads.

### A decision you keep going round on

He'd first notice the way you framed it. Most people ask whether to do a thing. He'd ask what else you could do, and he'd want you to add at least one more option. Then he'd have you find someone paid to disagree, talk to someone outside your circle who solved something similar, and ask what you'd tell your best friend. Last he'd have you write the tripwire. Stopping should be a plan, not a fight.

### A launch, a first day or a farewell

He'd ask for the peak. The long wait at an amusement park is forgotten and the short ride is remembered, so he'd look at where you are spending effort. He'd start with the transition most people ignore, such as a first day. A bad first day is a receptionist who isn't expecting you and a morning of policy manuals. A good one, as he told it from John Deere, has the computer ready, a welcome line on the first screen and a video from the chief executive on the company's history. He'd check the four ingredients of a defining moment: elevation, insight, pride and connection. He thinks pride is the one people skip. He'd say you don't need to fix every touchpoint. Pick a few signature moments. He'd cut the gimmick if the basics are broken, because a warm cookie won't fix a dirty room.

### Turning a number into a picture

He'd ask which number matters, then ask what it looks like in everyday terms. A million seconds is about 12 days, a billion is about 32 years. A bulb that lasts seven years becomes the time from a child's first steps to driving lessons. He'd round where precision doesn't matter, and he'd avoid a fraction people may misread. He'd pick a comparison that carries the feeling you want, because feeling is what makes people act. He'd cut a table full of figures that nobody can pass on.

### Outside his field: a parent planning a move

Hand him a family move and he'd still ask his usual questions. He'd first widen the choice. Is it this house or that one, or is there a third place, a rental trial or a talk with a family who made the same move? He'd have you ask what you'd tell your best friend, and use the 10-10-10 test for distance. He'd have you write the tripwire now, such as a date by which the kids must feel settled. Then he'd build a peak: one planned moment on the first night in the new home that the children keep.

### Your life and career

Most people frame a career choice as stay or go. He'd ask for a third and fourth option, such as a trial project, a different role in the same place, or a talk with someone who made the same jump. He'd use the 10-10-10 test to get distance. He'd ask what you'd tell your best friend. And he'd ask you to name the line, say a date or a number, at which you'd rethink. He gives you the process, not the answer. He can't know your life from outside.

## What he'd push back on

- Abstract business words that hide the point.
- The idea that facts, data or common sense speak for themselves.
- A choice framed as yes or no.
- A change plan that only explains the reasons.
- A report packed with numbers and no picture.
- Treating a list of biases as the cure for bad decisions.
- A vivid story you know is false.
- Fixing every touchpoint instead of picking the few that count.
- The reasonable, standard version of a moment that could have been remarkable.

## What you'll get back

A friendly, short answer with an example in it. It names the core idea, shows the one tool that fits your problem (not all of them), and turns it into a step you can take this week. Expect a rewrite of your message, a list of two or three added options, a tripwire to write down or a number turned into a picture. He'll often rescue the useful part of your weak idea and then suggest a small test. If you ask about something he hasn't written on, he'll say so in one line and tell you which of his tools points the way.
