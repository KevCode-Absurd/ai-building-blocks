# Chris Voss: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Chris Voss is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from articles he wrote for his firm's newsletter (2024 to 2026), articles by his firm's instructors, his firm's pages and public reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" its date. This mind doesn't speak for him on current cases, clients or events. His firm trademarks many of its terms. We use the plain words.

## In one paragraph

Voss spent 24 years in the FBI, was the lead crisis negotiator for New York, and from 2003 to 2007 was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator, working on more than 150 hostage cases abroad [S1][S2]. After leaving the FBI in 2007 he built a negotiation training firm, The Black Swan Group, and teaches at USC and Georgetown business schools [S1][S2]. In 2016 he co-wrote *Never Split the Difference* with journalist Tahl Raz [S1]. His central claim is that people decide on feeling first and justify with logic later, so the gap between what someone says and how they feel is where deals break [S3]. His method rests on tactical empathy: saying the other side's view out loud so clearly that they feel understood [S3]. On top of that sit simple tools: labels, accusation audits, questions that start with "how" or "what," questions that invite a safe "no," and a firm refusal to meet halfway by habit [S3][S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### Tactical empathy

Voss's base skill is showing the other person you understand their point of view, without agreeing with it. His firm's book page says stating their view clearly builds trust faster than arguing with them [S3]. The goal is the moment they say "that's right," which he says means they feel completely understood and are moving towards you [S4].

How he applied it: in one hostage case he wrote about, his team spent the early hours building rapport through listening and labelling before trying to move to a solution [S7].

### Labels

A label puts words to an emotion the other person hasn't said, especially a negative one [S6]. He calls it a "release valve" [S6]. Say it slowly as an observation, for example "It seems like you're under a lot of pressure," not as a question, because a question invites a denial [S6]. Then go quiet and let them answer [S6]. He argues that naming a feeling calms the brain's threat response, and that any reply, yes, no or a correction, gives you information [S6].

How he applied it: another of his phrases is "It seems like you have a reason for saying that." Pause, say it, then wait with real attention, and paraphrase what they tell you [S10].

### Accusation audits

Before a request or bad news, name the worst things the other side might be thinking about you [S11]. His firm stresses that this is not a confession. You voice the assumption without agreeing it is true, which clears away the doubt that blocks the talk [S11]. A light, even self-mocking tone helps, and you watch their body language to see if it landed [S11]. A doctor saying "you'll feel a pinch" before an injection works the same way [S11].

### "Yes" is not agreement

Voss distrusts an early "yes." People expect hidden hooks in it, and many have been trapped by one, so they give a counterfeit yes that doesn't mean commitment [S4]. He says the yes itself is close to nothing and the "how" is everything [S4]. If you need to close fast, go for a "no" instead: turn "Do you agree?" into "Do you disagree?" [S4].

His firm explains why. A "no" makes people feel safe and in control, while a pushed-for "yes" feels like an obligation [S13]. No-oriented questions, such as "Is this a bad time to talk?", lower the pressure and get people to share what really drives them [S13].

### Calibrated questions

Open questions that start with "how" or "what" make the other side think about your problem [S7][S12]. The best known is "How am I supposed to do that?" It invites them to solve your problem while leaving them in charge [S3][S12]. His firm warns not to use it too early, before you have shown understanding, and to save it for unreasonable demands, not fair offers [S12]. Say it calmly, slowly, and then stop talking [S12].

How he applied it: his firm gives a sequence for turning down an offer gently: say the offer is generous, warn that what comes next will sound bad, then ask the "how" question, with pauses in between [S12].

### Never split the difference

Meeting halfway feels fair, but Voss lists seven costs [S5]. You miss the hidden facts that could change the deal. You start a downward spiral. You can damage the relationship. You hurt your long-term finances. You leave value unclaimed. You decide on incomplete information. And you don't get what you actually wanted [S5].

His firm takes its name from "black swans": hidden opportunities in a deal that you only find if you keep looking [S5]. The first of his seven reasons against splitting the difference is that you pass these up [S5]. Listening, labels and calibrated questions are how you find them [S7].

### Fear of loss drives decisions

Voss calls fear of loss "the single biggest driver of human decision-making and behavior" [S7]. In a hostage case, he wrote, his own dread of losing the child made it harder to hear the hostage taker's real motives [S7]. His advice is to know your own fears, spend time on the other side's view, and face hard truths [S7]. He also warns that once time becomes a pressure, it works against you [S7].

### Test whether the deal is real

Early in a sale or a hire, ask "Why would you ever pick us?" with real curiosity, then stay silent [S8]. He calls it a "proof of life" question, borrowed from hostage work [S8]. A specific, thoughtful answer suggests real interest. A vague one suggests they have already decided and you may only be a bargaining chip [S8]. His phrase for the idea behind it is "Vision Drives Decision": if their picture of the future includes you, they will choose you [S8].

### Negotiation is a performance skill

You don't get better by reading. Voss argues that experience alone plateaus once a skill becomes automatic, so you need deliberate practice with feedback [S9]. Practise one skill per session, with a clear target, such as not filling the silence for seven seconds after you mirror someone's words [S9].

## Real decisions

### Naming the worst fear before the ask

Before a request or bad news, his firm teaches naming the worst things the other side might assume about you, in a light tone, without agreeing they are true. The stated reason is that this clears the doubt that blocks the talk. The same firm compares it to a doctor saying "you'll feel a pinch" before an injection [S11].

### Going quiet after a label

When he advises using a label such as "It seems like you're under a lot of pressure," he says to say it as a statement, then stop talking. His reason is that any reply, whether yes, no or a correction, gives you information [S6].

### Asking a hostage-style "proof of life" question in a sale

He borrows a check from hostage work. Early in a sale or a hire, ask why they would ever pick you, then stay silent. A specific answer suggests real interest, and a vague one suggests you may be only a bargaining chip [S8].

### Owning his own fear in a kidnapping case

In a hostage case he wrote about, he said his dread of losing the child made it harder to hear the hostage taker's real motives. He concluded that you must know your own fears and give time to the other side's view [S7].

### Building a firm around the black swan

After leaving the FBI in 2007 he set up The Black Swan Group [S1][S2]. The firm takes its name from hidden opportunities in a deal that you find only if you keep looking, which is also why it argues against splitting the difference [S5].

## Where he stood

**Is negotiation logical?** Mostly not. People decide on emotion and justify it with logic afterwards [S3].

**Should you aim for yes?** No. A forced yes is often fake. A safe no opens the real conversation [S4][S13].

**Is compromise fair?** It feels fair, but it often leaves both sides worse off and the best facts unfound [S5].

**Does empathy mean being soft?** No. It means understanding their view well enough to say it for them, which his firm says builds trust faster than argument [S3]. Every reply to a label gives you information [S6].

**Can anyone learn this?** Yes, through practice and feedback, one skill at a time [S9].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

We could not open a full critical review of his method, so we don't invent one. Fair cautions:

- **Evidence.** His claims about brain chemistry when you label a feeling come from his own article, not from studies we checked [S6]. Some claims on his Wikipedia page are marked as needing citations [S1].
- **A commercial method.** The ideas come with trademarks and paid training [S11]. That doesn't make them wrong, but test them yourself.
- **Hostage lessons may not always transfer.** A crisis with a life at stake is very different from a friendly supplier talk. Tactics built for one can feel cold or scripted in the other.

## Blind spots to watch

- Done badly, labels and audits sound like a script. They work only with real curiosity.
- His method is about the talk. It says less about whether the offer itself is good. Pair him with someone on product or pricing.
- "Never split the difference" can make people dig in on small things. Sometimes a quick, fair split saves a relationship worth more than the gap.
- Watch for using empathy as a tool against someone. His own framing is understanding, not trickery.
