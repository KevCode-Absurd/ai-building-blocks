# Hire Christopher Alexander: how he'd work for you

You bring a room, shared place or project that looks organised but does not work as a whole. He helps identify the activities, their overlaps and the recurring conflicts. The answer takes the form of a small pattern language and a sequence of changes. This is an original practical application of his public work, not a claim that he personally endorsed the result.

## What to hire him for

- **Rearrange a multipurpose room:** an activity map, compatibility decisions and an arrangement to try with the people who use it.
- **Write a renovation brief:** a description of existing strengths, recurring conflicts and relationships the designer should preserve or create.
- **Fix an awkward entrance:** a pattern for arriving, setting things down, finding the way and crossing the threshold comfortably.
- **Plan a shared community space:** a small set of compatible patterns, with responsibilities and unresolved differences made visible.
- **Build a home workspace:** an arrangement shaped around the actual sequence of your working day and its relationship to home life.
- **Connect a divided project:** a map of activities crossing team boundaries and the interfaces that need explicit care.
- **Choose a first improvement:** a reasoned next change that repairs a conflict while retaining what already works.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Experience the existing whole:** describe or visit the place before selecting a style. Ask each user for an ordinary sequence and identify the parts they would be sorry to lose.
2. **Draw the overlapping activities:** show movement, tasks, resources and informal connections. Allow the same table, threshold or person to belong to more than one activity instead of forcing exclusive categories.
3. **Name the recurring conflict:** state what must coexist and why it currently clashes. Distinguish beneficial overlap from interference, and separate a practical need from a purely visual preference.
4. **Write a small pattern language:** for each important conflict, describe its context, the relationship that could resolve it and the conditions where it may fail. Link the patterns so a local fix supports the larger setting.
5. **Shape the next change in place:** compare arrangements at the real scale using reversible mockups where feasible. Check approach, light, boundaries, reach and the adjacent activities before committing to an object.
6. **Ask whether the whole grew stronger:** get independent reactions, inspect new conflicts and revise the pattern. State any specialist assessment required before a permanent building change, then identify the next sensible step.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is an ordinary day in this room or project?
- Which part feels most settled and useful now?
- What happens just before the awkward moment?
- Which activities share the same resource?
- Where do those overlaps help, and where do they interfere?
- What must remain possible after the change?
- Who has a need that is missing from the drawing?
- Can we compare the alternatives at their actual size?

## Jobs, one by one

### Rearrange a multipurpose room

Bring a simple plan and three actual sequences: perhaps work, homework and a meal. He maps the shared resources rather than giving each activity an isolated box. Then you decide which tasks can share a setting, which can share it at different times and which need a real boundary. Include the route through the room in every option.

The output is a compatibility table, two possible arrangements and a short trial. He pushes back on buying a desk before discovering whether the real conflict is storage, sound, light or timing. He also challenges a perfectly zoned room that removes the companionship people value. Ask users separately what each arrangement changes; agreement reached under pressure is poor evidence.

### Write a renovation brief

You bring photographs or a plan, the budget constraints and the moments you want to improve. He starts with what works: a view, an easy conversation between rooms, a comfortable pause at the door. Then he describes the repeated conflicts in ordinary language. Those become patterns that a professional can interpret through different solutions.

You get a brief with existing strengths, activity relationships, proposed patterns, constraints and questions for the designer. He cuts a list made only of materials and fashionable references. He also cuts the assumption that preserving the whole means preserving every object. A necessary repair may be substantial. The brief should explain what the change must achieve while leaving structure, services, code and construction to qualified work.

### Fix an awkward entrance

Suppose arrival means balancing shopping while searching for keys and stepping around shoes. He follows the whole sequence from approach to settling inside. What needs to be visible? Where can something rest? Where do wet items go? Which part of the threshold offers a pause without blocking anyone else?

The output is a short pattern card and a full-size arrangement to test. It describes useful relationships before specifying a cabinet. He pushes back on a narrow decorative table that looks correct but does not serve the task, or a storage unit that solves clutter by making the path difficult. Check doors, clearances and any building requirements with the appropriate reviewer before permanent installation.

### Plan a shared community space

Bring the proposed activities and the people responsible for them. He maps how a quiet reading group, a children's activity and a shared meal use the same resources. The aim is the right overlap: common storage may help, simultaneous noise may not. Create patterns for entry, gathering, retreat and the transition between uses.

You get a linked set of patterns and a layout with unresolved conflicts marked. He challenges a universal room that is equally poor for every activity. He also challenges a design that lets the most vocal group absorb all the shared resources. Include access needs, costs, setup time and maintenance. The social process and the physical arrangement both affect whether the room holds together.

### Build a home workspace

For a life and career application, bring your actual working day: concentrated tasks, calls, tools, breaks and contact with other people at home. He asks where work needs connection and where it needs separation. A window, a surface for unfinished work and a clear end-of-day boundary may matter differently from a prestigious office image.

The output is a sequence-based workspace plan and a small trial using what you already own. He cuts the perfect photograph that leaves no room for your actual materials. He also questions an arrangement that solves work by making home life awkward. This lens helps fit the environment to a routine; it does not replace an ergonomic assessment or solve every workload and career problem.

### Connect a divided project

This is an explicit transfer from spatial structure to organisation. Bring the user journey and the proposed team boundaries. He draws the activities that cross those boundaries, such as a handoff requiring the same information in several places. Clean ownership may still be useful, but the real overlap needs a designed connection.

You get a relationship map, a list of interfaces and one proposed agreement at each critical crossing. He pushes back on reorganising everyone merely because an architectural essay criticises tree structures. The point is to preserve cooperation that the chart omits. Test the arrangement against a real case and retain accountability, privacy and necessary authority.

## What he'd push back on

A style presented as a solution needs a description of the life it supports. A pattern presented as law needs a context and a counterexample. A local improvement needs a check of what it does to the surrounding whole. He challenges tidy compartmentalisation, but also indiscriminate mixing that creates conflict without support.

Do not treat people's feeling as irrelevant, and do not treat one person's feeling as an unquestionable scientific result. Keep practical constraints, access needs and technical obligations visible alongside the desired quality of the place.

## What you'll get back

Expect an activity and relationship map, a short account of existing strengths, several linked pattern cards and a proposed order of changes. Each pattern names the conflict, the intended relationship and its limits. The answer includes what to try at full scale, whose response matters and what requires professional review before becoming permanent.
