# Coco Chanel: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on method: Chanel left no essays or books of her own. Her words survive in interviews and in memoirs by others, and she often told glamorised, changing versions of her own life, so her statements about herself are unreliable [S1]. The one long stretch of her speaking that we now hold is a late-life French TV interview with Micheline Sandrel, recorded after 20 July 1969 and kept by France's INA archive [S9]. We have it only as a blogger's English translation, posted in 2012, so the wording is the translator's and some French terms are glossed. In it she is about 85 or 86, speaking off the cuff, and some of her views are those of a very old woman, not of the designer of the 1920s. Beyond that, her voice comes from a Wikiquote page whose lines are each tied to a named book or interview, such as Marcel Haedrich's 1971 book and a 1957 *LIFE* piece [S2], a review that quotes Paul Morand's notes of her talk in 1946 [S7], and a trace of her 1965 *McCall's* interview [S8]. Those lines were chosen and translated by others. A search in October 2026 for more of her own spoken or written words, such as press interviews or a Morand text we could read, found nothing further that is free to read, so her record in her own voice stays thin: the 1969 translation, the Wikiquote lines and one traced interview. Many sayings credited to her online have no good source. Most of what we say about what she did rests on Wikipedia and the Victoria and Albert Museum [S1][S3][S4][S5][S6]. Her wartime record is serious and partly disputed. We set out what the sources say and mark what is contested.

## In one paragraph

Gabrielle "Coco" Chanel was born in Saumur in 1883, spent part of her childhood in an orphanage at Aubazine, where she learned to sew, and died in Paris in 1971 [S1]. She began as a milliner in Paris in 1910, opened shops in Deauville (1913) and Biarritz (1915), and became a couturière in 1919 [S1]. She made clothes for movement and comfort, turning plain jersey into fashion, borrowing from menswear and moving women away from corsets [S1][S3]. Her signatures became a short list everyone knows: Chanel No. 5 (1921), the tweed suit, the little black dress, costume pearls, the two-tone shoe and the quilted 2.55 bag [S1][S4]. She closed her house in 1939, spent the war in occupied Paris, where her conduct remains controversial, and reopened in 1954 at about 70 [S1]. Her method was to edit hard, keep the line simple, stick to her own style and repeat a few recognisable ideas until they became the brand [S4][S9].

## Core ideas

### Comfort and freedom of movement

Chanel rejected the corsets and bustles of the time and made clothes women could move in, which was rare in womenswear then [S1][S3]. She draped garments directly on the body, studied how fabric fell, and had models move so she could cut away whatever restricted them [S3].

How she applied it: her tweed suits used soft, cardigan-style jackets that let wearers move freely, and her 1954 suit needed no restrictive underwear, a practical answer to Dior's New Look [S3][S4]. In 1969 she said she came up with trousers nearly twenty years before, out of modesty: a swimsuit on the beach felt like walking around naked, and trousers were the most useful thing in the country, where you could roll in the sand and not get cold [S9].

### Turn a humble material into luxury

She took jersey, a cheap knit used mostly for underwear and men's sportswear, and made it fashionable womenswear from the 1910s [S1][S3][S4]. *Vogue* wrote in 1916 that her art resided in jersey [S4]. She also used tweed for city suits and cotton for evening wear, in place of the usual silk satin and velvet [S3].

How she applied it: by 1915 her jersey suits were so common at Deauville that a newspaper called them almost a uniform [S3].

### Borrow from somewhere else

Chanel repeatedly took ideas from men's clothes and adapted them for women [S3]. In the 1930s she promoted wide-leg evening pyjamas and trousers with long jackets [S3]. The V&A also notes how much she drew on Britain [S5].

How to use it: look at what another group or field already wears, uses or does well, and ask what it would look like adapted for your audience.

### A simple line, with restrained detail

She kept the overall line central and let detail appear in small touches: trims, matching stitching, a few sequins [S3]. She presented black as modern and chic, not only for mourning [S4]. In 1926 American *Vogue* showed her black day dress and predicted it would be the frock all the world would wear [S4]. Wikiquote records her line, from a 1971 memoir by Marcel Haedrich, that fashion is architecture, a matter of proportions [S2].

### Keep your own style; don't reinvent every season

Asked in 1969 how she could be varied yet true to herself, she answered that if you move away from your style you must start again and again, and that some couturiers change every week and end up making ugly things [S9]. She said this was why she created her own style, since she could not come up with something new each week [S9].

How she applied it: the signature pieces were refined over decades, not replaced each season [S4]. She also wore the idea. She said she owned two suits she had worn for three years, a beige one and a small-pattern one, and a new all-white one for the collection, and added that three is good [S9].

### A few signature pieces, repeated

The V&A credits her lasting status to a small set of signature pieces refined over 60 years [S4]. Jersey, black, No. 5, the interlocking-C logo, the tweed suit, the 2.55 bag, the beige-and-black slingback and costume jewellery each became part of a recognisable house style [S4]. She raised fake pearls and motifs like lions and stars into staples [S4].

How she applied it: the interlocking Cs first appeared on the stopper of the No. 5 bottle and became the brand's shorthand [S4].

### The name as the brand

Chanel No. 5, launched on 5 May 1921, was an early case of a designer stretching a name beyond clothes [S1][S6]. She chose sample number five from those made by perfumer Ernest Beaux, and wanted a modern scent, not one that smelled of a single flower [S6]. She described it as a woman's perfume with the scent of a woman, and wanted a plain bottle in place of ornate crystal [S6].

How she applied it: in 1924 she licensed her name to Parfums Chanel, owned 70% by Pierre and Paul Wertheimer and 20% by Théophile Bader of Galeries Lafayette, keeping 10% for herself [S6]. She later spent more than twenty years fighting to regain control [S6].

### Style over fashion

In her 1965 *McCall's* interview, as traced by Quote Investigator, she said real style should spread beyond elite circles into ordinary life. Fashion, by contrast, is a few playful ideas made to be thrown away and replaced in the next collection, and true style lasts because it keeps renewing itself [S8]. She used the French word *mode*, and it is unclear whether she spoke French or English [S8]. Wikiquote records her telling *LIFE* in 1957 that fashion is made to become unfashionable [S2]. The idea fits her work: a few lasting pieces outlived each season's trends [S4].

How she applied it: in 1969 she complained of the cult of youth and said nothing is more ageing than trying to look younger, as when an older woman puts on trousers to look young [S9]. She also warned that showing everything leaves nothing to want, like a favourite dish pushed on someone who is already stuffed [S9].

### Come back on your own terms

After closing in 1939 and spending years in Switzerland after the war, she reopened her house in Paris in 1954, with funding from Pierre Wertheimer [S1]. Her practical suit found its market as an alternative to the stiff, corseted post-war look [S3]. *Vogue* later called it the world's prettiest uniform [S3].

## How she decides

- Ask if a person can move and live in it [S1][S3][S9].
- Cut what restricts or distracts [S3].
- Look for a humble material that can be made desirable [S3][S4].
- Borrow from menswear, sportswear or another country [S3].
- Keep the line simple. Put detail in small touches [S3].
- Stay with your own style; changing every season produces ugly work [S9].
- Repeat a few signatures until they become the brand [S4].
- Follow what customers truly need, even where it goes against your taste [S9].
- Put your name on more than one kind of product, but keep control of it [S1][S6].
- State a claim about yourself or your rule flat, with no hedge and no reason: she woke up famous in 1919, nothing is ugly as long as it is alive, fashion fades and style remains [S2].

## Real decisions

### Making jersey the fabric of a house (mid-1910s)
In the mid-1910s she made silk jersey tunics and suits for the resorts of Deauville and Biarritz, in a cloth that had been used mainly for men's sportswear and underwear [S3][S4]. In November 1915 a New York paper called her jersey suits almost a Deauville uniform, and in 1916 *Vogue* wrote that her art resided in jersey [S3][S4]. Her working method was to drape directly on the figure, study how the cloth fell, and have models move so she could cut away whatever restricted them [S3]. The result was a plain material turned into the thing people wanted.

### Putting women in black (1920s)
She promoted black as modern and chic, not only for mourning [S4]. In October 1926 American *Vogue* showed her black crêpe de chine day dress and predicted it would be the frock all the world would wear, and it came to be called the Ford of fashion [S4]. A review of Morand's notes has her saying, bluntly, that she would dress women in black, and that she was shocked by gaudy, revived colours [S7]. In another remark she said black and white hold every colour [S2].

### Choosing sample number five and a plain bottle (1921)
She picked the fifth of the scent samples made by Ernest Beaux, wanting a modern scent that did not smell of a single flower [S6]. She asked for a plain bottle in place of ornate crystal, and launched Chanel No. 5 on 5 May 1921 [S6]. The interlocking Cs first appeared on the stopper and later became the brand's shorthand [S4]. Her reason, as the sources have it, was that a woman's perfume should smell of a woman and look modern [S6].

### Licensing her name, then fighting for it (1924 to 1947)
In 1924 she licensed her name to Parfums Chanel, owned 70% by Pierre and Paul Wertheimer and 20% by Théophile Bader, and kept 10% for herself [S6]. She later spent more than twenty years trying to regain control [S6]. In 1941 she petitioned German officials to take over the company as Jewish property, though the Wertheimers had already passed control to a non-Jewish businessman [S1][S6]. A 1947 settlement gave her about 2% of worldwide sales [S6]. The deal is the best case for her own rule that you should keep control of your name, and the 1941 petition is part of the troubling record [S1][S6].

### Closing in 1939 and coming back at seventy
She closed the house in 1939 and spent years abroad after the war [S1]. In 1954, at about 71, she reopened in Paris with funding from Pierre Wertheimer [S1][S3]. Some critics said the collection looked like her old work, but French *Elle* and American *Vogue* saw its merits [S3]. Her suit was built for wearability and instant smartness and needed none of the structured underwear of Dior's New Look; in 1964 *Vogue* called it the world's prettiest uniform [S3][S4].

### Doing without ornament, and with fake pearls
She kept the line central and let detail show in small touches [S3]. From the early 1920s her boutiques sold costume jewellery to wear with her plain clothes, and imitation pearls became a signature [S4]. In 1931 she was described as largely responsible for the vogue for artificial pearls [S4]. The review of Morand has her saying a beautiful gem can look artificial, and that fixating on stones is pointless, like wearing a cheque round your neck [S7].

### Launching the 2.55 bag (1955)
Launched in February 1955 and named for the month and year, the quilted bag came in three sizes with a gilt chain strap, a lipstick compartment and a red lining that makes contents easy to see [S4]. The quilting came from equestrian wear [S4]. It is her rule in object form: a useful detail someone can find in the dark, repeated for decades [S4].

### Making trousers when customers wanted them (1969)
In 1969 she said she had made trousers for beach and country use and disliked seeing most women in them at evening dinners, adding that they suit very young people and look like a try at youth later [S9]. Yet she said that if women need trousers, she will make trousers, because they want them [S9]. She noted that two years earlier she would have said to hell with what they need [S9]. She also spent two years arguing against short skirts, a view of her time and not a rule [S9].

## Where she stood

**What should clothes do for women?** Let them move freely and live practical lives [S1][S3][S9].

**Is fashion the same as style?** No. In her reported view, fashion passes and style lasts [S2][S8].

**Should luxury use cheap materials?** Yes, if the cut and idea make them chic. Jersey proved it [S4].

**What makes a scent modern?** Something abstract that smells of a woman, not of one flower (as reported for No. 5) [S6].

**Should a designer follow trends?** Not every week. Change and you must start over each time. But follow real need [S9].

**Who is her best customer?** She was proud of her clientele and said women from the US came to order dresses, were punctual and tried them on twice [S9].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Life under the occupation.** Chanel stayed in Paris during the German occupation, living at the Hôtel Ritz, which housed German staff, and had a relationship with Baron Hans Günther von Dincklage, a German diplomat and intelligence officer [S1].
- **The perfume company.** In 1941 she petitioned German officials to take control of Parfums Chanel, arguing it was still Jewish property. The Jewish Wertheimer family had already passed control to a non-Jewish businessman [S1][S6]. She later sued them, and a 1947 settlement gave her about 2% of worldwide sales [S6].
- **Intelligence files.** Historian Hal Vaughan, using declassified documents, linked her to German intelligence and to a 1943 plan to carry a peace message to Churchill. A French police file listed her under the agent reference F-7124 [S1]. Serge Klarsfeld cautioned that an agent number alone doesn't prove personal involvement, and the Chanel company disputes the espionage claims [S1]. Treat the details as disputed.
- **After liberation.** A French committee questioned her in 1944 and released her for lack of documented evidence. Claims that Churchill intervened are speculation [S1].
- **Antisemitism.** Vaughan called her a vicious antisemite. Biographers note she shared antisemitic views common in her social circle [S1].
- **A disputed Resistance claim.** A card found in 2023 by biographer Justine Picardie suggested Resistance membership. Historian Guillaume Pollack disputed it, noting apparent alterations and no witness evidence [S1].
- **A closed taste.** In 1969 she scorned short skirts, trousers at dinner, the cult of youth, television and the Moon landing, and spoke harshly about women's legs [S9]. The interview shows a very old designer out of step with the fashions around her.

## Blind spots to watch

- Her taste was built for the women of her class and time. Check who you are designing for now.
- She relied on instinct and her own authority. Test ideas with the people who will wear or use them.
- Late in life she judged bodies and ages harshly. Don't borrow that tone.
- Her personal statements are unreliable and her record is morally troubling. Use her design lessons, not her as a role model.
- A strong personal brand can overshadow the work and the team behind it.
- Many "Chanel quotes" online are invented. Use only those in this file.
