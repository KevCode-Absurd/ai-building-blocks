### Jobs, one by one

#### Explain a household spending chart

Ask what the people involved want to compare and which records are available. Check the period and category definitions. Keep transfers, settlements and actual purchases distinct where that affects the totals. Identify irregular costs and write a neutral observation. Choose a display that supports the question and keep the full figures available. Invite the household's interpretation before proposing a change.

The output is a chart brief, a clear title and a discussion question. Push back on treating a category as evidence of someone's character. Do not prescribe a spending ratio or conceal an annual expense to make one month look representative. The exercise explains supplied records and supports a shared conversation.

#### Turn a spreadsheet update into a meeting message

Identify the decision the meeting can make. Write the main observation away from the full table, then return to verify it. Select the comparison that supports the point and preserve the necessary context. Draft a short explanation and a proportionate request. Put detailed values and analysis in a record the audience can inspect rather than forcing every exploratory step onto the slide.

The output is a message, visual plan and next question. Push back on showing everything as proof of effort. Also reject a simplified story that hides contradictory evidence. A clear recommendation should remain open to challenge, especially when the data establishes a pattern but does not establish its cause.

#### Remove chart clutter

Ask what the reader should notice and what the chart measures. Review decorative effects, repeated labels and competing emphasis. Reduce elements that do not serve understanding. Strengthen grouping and label the important relation. Keep the units, source, period, scale and qualifications needed for fair interpretation. Check that the result remains usable without relying on color alone.

The output is a concrete revision plan with reasons for each change. Push back on removing information merely to make the graph look elegant. A clean display without a denominator or an honest baseline can mislead. If the audience needs detailed inspection, a well-organized table may serve the task better than a sparse chart.

#### Compare two chart designs

Start with the source and the intended comparison. Sketch two reasonable forms or arrangements. Describe what each makes easy to see, what it obscures and what explanation it needs. Compare them against the audience's question. Choose one provisionally and test it with a reader unfamiliar with the analysis. Preserve exact values separately if the task requires them.

The output is a comparison and a reasoned selection. Push back on choosing a chart only because it is novel or available by default. Also avoid universal claims that one form is always right. The relationship, amount of data and reader's task determine whether an option earns its place.

#### Write a title and annotation

Ask what the values actually establish. Draft a title that states the supported observation in ordinary words. Add an annotation for a verified event or relevant qualification, keeping timing distinct from causation. Read the chart and text together. If the title requires information absent from the source, narrow it or mark the missing evidence before the user shares it.

The output is an editable title and contextual note. Push back on persuasive wording that overstates the pattern. A title should make the intended reading clear while leaving the evidence inspectable. It cannot convert a selected example into a general trend or make a hypothesis true through confident language.

#### Prepare evidence for a job interview

Ask what the user did, who else contributed and what results are supported. Use specific scope, actions and observed outcomes. If there is no measured impact percentage, say so and choose an accurate description instead. Connect the evidence to the skill the interview question concerns. Keep the account short enough to leave room for follow-up questions.

The output is an editable answer and a verification list. Push back on invented numbers and taking sole credit for shared work. A truthful description of a completed task may be stronger than a dramatic but unsupported statistic. This is an original career application, not a promise of interview success.

### How to make a chart brief useful

Start with the decision or understanding the reader needs. A household conversation about spending differs from a work update about a delayed task. Write the audience, the relevant period and the comparison in a few sentences. Confirm that the source records actually measure that question. A count of requests is not the same thing as the time needed to complete them, and a percentage without its denominator may conceal a meaningful change in scale.

Next write what the values establish. Separate observation from interpretation and the action being proposed. This is our original organizing exercise. A line rising across several months is an observation. An explanation about why it rose may require other evidence. A proposed response adds a judgment about resources and priorities. These layers can be communicated together while remaining distinguishable. Their clarity helps a reader disagree with one part without losing the whole account.

Choose a display for the relation. A table may be best when exact values matter. A bar comparison may make categories easier to inspect; a time series may show a change over a period. These are examples, not a universal chart-selection formula. Sketch two reasonable options and ask what each makes easier or harder to see. Include the surrounding information a reader needs to avoid a false impression.

Finally test the explanation with someone who has not followed the analysis. Ask what they think is being measured and which comparison they notice. Compare their answer with the actual source and purpose. Fix ambiguous labels, missing context or an overconfident title. Keep the detail sheet accessible and consistent with the chart. The goal is an inspectable explanation that supports a conversation, not a claim that everyone must reach the same decision.

### Blind spots to watch

The approach can be criticised when a desired action is chosen before the evidence is examined and the chart is then made to sell it. Audience awareness should clarify a truthful account, not license removing inconvenient information. A reader may understand the same data and reasonably prefer a different action because their obligations or priorities differ. Keep that possibility visible.

Another limit is mistaking communication skill for analytical validity. A useful title cannot repair a poor measure, an unrepresentative sample or missing records. General claims about memorability in an interview do not establish a guaranteed effect for this audience. AI assistance adds another need for checking, not an exemption. Return the facts, interpretation and proposed action with their limits rather than promising persuasion, accuracy or a private endorsement.
