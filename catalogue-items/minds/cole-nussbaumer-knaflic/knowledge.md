# Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Knaflic's public interviews put the audience and the purpose of communication before chart formatting. Her teaching distinguishes the detailed work of analysis from explaining a useful result, then asks where the reader should look and what the information supports. [S1][S2] The everyday application is a clear chart brief: who needs this, which comparison matters, what context is necessary and what action or question follows. A visually convincing graph remains accountable to its data. The original exercises below do not treat a polished story as proof, a recommendation as inevitable or an AI-generated visual as verified.

## Core ideas

### 1. Define the audience before building the display

In the Chicago Booth interview, Knaflic considers mixed audiences and discusses separating communications or making the relevant sections clear. [S1] An original use is a community event update. Organizers may need a decision about capacity while volunteers need instructions for their shift. The same spreadsheet can support different messages. Write each audience's actual question and identify what information answers it. Do not merely substitute different decorative titles on an unchanged data dump. If one communication must serve both groups, give each a clear route through the material and preserve the shared facts. Different emphasis should not become inconsistent evidence or contradictory claims.

### 2. Step out of the analysis before explaining it

Her interview answers distinguish knowing the data deeply from showing the audience every route taken to understand it. [S1] An original exercise starts with a long spreadsheet review. Away from the full table, write the main observation in ordinary language. Return to the values and ask whether they support it. Select the evidence needed to understand or challenge that observation. Keep the detailed record available separately. The output is a message and an evidence map, not an attempt to conceal the analysis. A listener should be able to inspect the basis of the explanation without following every exploratory dead end in the spoken update.

### 3. Direct attention without distorting the comparison

The InfoTrust interview discusses removing unnecessary clutter and using visual cues deliberately. [S2] An original application is a graph in which every category has a different bright color. Decide which comparison matters, reduce irrelevant emphasis and keep the reference categories understandable. Add labels or another cue so color is not the only signal. Retain the units, period, baseline and qualifications required for fair interpretation. A quieter design should make the intended relation easier to inspect, not erase data that complicates the preferred conclusion. If the evidence is mixed, the visual and accompanying explanation should show that complexity rather than disguise it.

### 4. Put the meaning into words beside the evidence

In the Chicago Booth conversation, Knaflic recommends titles, axis labels and words that explain the intended takeaway rather than leaving readers to guess. [S1] An original task is a monthly attendance chart. Write a title that states a supported observation, then annotate a relevant event only if its timing is verified. Distinguish that event from a causal explanation. The output might say that attendance fell during a period, while a separate note names the question about why. A title can guide attention without instructing the reader to accept an unsupported cause. If the chart cannot support the sentence, revise the sentence or obtain the missing evidence.

### 5. Connect an observation to a proportionate request

Her InfoTrust answers distinguish an interesting finding from an explicit proposed action that the audience can discuss. [S2] An original use is a chart showing repeated delays in a volunteer handoff. State the observation, the uncertainty and a limited next step, such as reviewing the instructions with the people doing the task. Do not leap from a pattern to blaming a person or promising that one change will solve it. The requested action should fit the decision authority and the strength of the evidence. A valid outcome may be investigation, a trial or a decision to gather better data rather than immediate commitment.

### 6. Use generated options with an informed reviewer

In the September 2026 transcript, Knaflic describes AI as a way to explore alternatives while emphasizing the human foundation needed to judge its output. [S3] An original application is a chart generated from a supplied table. Compare its values with the source, inspect the axes and category mapping, then ask whether the design serves the intended message. Consider another chart form rather than accepting the first polished result. This is a verification exercise, not a current ranking of products or a guarantee that a paid tool is correct. Keep private data within the user's authorized setting and do not invent information to complete a visual.

## How to make a chart brief useful

Start with the decision or understanding the reader needs. A household conversation about spending differs from a work update about a delayed task. Write the audience, the relevant period and the comparison in a few sentences. Confirm that the source records actually measure that question. A count of requests is not the same thing as the time needed to complete them, and a percentage without its denominator may conceal a meaningful change in scale.

Next write what the values establish. Separate observation from interpretation and the action being proposed. This is our original organizing exercise. A line rising across several months is an observation. An explanation about why it rose may require other evidence. A proposed response adds a judgment about resources and priorities. These layers can be communicated together while remaining distinguishable. Their clarity helps a reader disagree with one part without losing the whole account.

Choose a display for the relation. A table may be best when exact values matter. A bar comparison may make categories easier to inspect; a time series may show a change over a period. These are examples, not a universal chart-selection formula. Sketch two reasonable options and ask what each makes easier or harder to see. Include the surrounding information a reader needs to avoid a false impression.

Finally test the explanation with someone who has not followed the analysis. Ask what they think is being measured and which comparison they notice. Compare their answer with the actual source and purpose. Fix ambiguous labels, missing context or an overconfident title. Keep the detail sheet accessible and consistent with the chart. The goal is an inspectable explanation that supports a conversation, not a claim that everyone must reach the same decision.

## Where you can put the lens to work

### Explain a household spending pattern

Use actual categories and a period everyone involved understands. Distinguish purchases from transfers or account settlements if those differences affect the totals. Choose the comparison the household wants to discuss and make irregular costs visible. Draft a neutral observation before proposing a change. The output is a chart explanation and a question for the people affected. This original communication exercise does not prescribe how the household should spend or infer character from a category. A low-looking month may simply exclude an annual bill; keep the relevant context beside the visual.

### Turn a spreadsheet into a meeting update

The user has a table of activity and wants to show that a project needs attention. Ask which decision the meeting can make. Select the relevant measure, period and comparison, then write the narrow statement the table supports. Sketch the display and a request proportionate to that evidence. Keep the full table available for inspection. The output is a message, visual brief and next question. Do not use a dramatic chart as a substitute for establishing the cause, or describe an incomplete period as directly comparable with a complete one.

### Describe an accomplishment in a job interview

Ask what the user actually did and what evidence they can support. A specific scope, a completed task or an observed change may be useful without an invented impact percentage. Draft a short account connecting the situation, action and result. Name shared work fairly. The output is an editable answer and a list of facts to verify. This is an original career application of evidence-based communication. It does not turn an estimate into a measured result or assign the entire team's achievement to the person speaking.

### Check a graph before sharing it

Compare the displayed values with the supplied source. Inspect labels, units, ordering, scales and omitted categories. Ask whether the title describes what is shown and whether the annotation implies more than the evidence supports. If the source is unavailable, state the limit rather than certifying the graph. The output is a concrete review and a corrected explanation where possible. A chart can be attractive and inaccurate, or accurate in its numbers and misleading in its presentation. Both parts deserve attention before the user relies on it.

## Blind spots to watch

The approach can be criticised when a desired action is chosen before the evidence is examined and the chart is then made to sell it. Audience awareness should clarify a truthful account, not license removing inconvenient information. A reader may understand the same data and reasonably prefer a different action because their obligations or priorities differ. Keep that possibility visible.

Another limit is mistaking communication skill for analytical validity. A useful title cannot repair a poor measure, an unrepresentative sample or missing records. General claims about memorability in an interview do not establish a guaranteed effect for this audience. AI assistance adds another need for checking, not an exemption. Return the facts, interpretation and proposed action with their limits rather than promising persuasion, accuracy or a private endorsement.
