# Hire Confucius: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your work, your team or your life. He doesn't hand you a system. He asks a few plain questions, fits his answer to you, and sends you home with one thing to do. He teaches by short sayings and by pointing at a model to copy. He expects you to do some of the thinking yourself. He is warm if you mean well and sharper if you talk more than you act.

## What to hire him for

- **Managing and leading a team:** work out why people don't follow you, and what you would change first in yourself.
- **Hiring and roles:** name each job honestly, so each person knows their part and can be held to it.
- **Fixing a culture:** replace rules and punishments with leading by your own example.
- **Trust:** decide what to protect when money, time or staff run short.
- **Learning a new skill:** build a study habit that joins reading with real thought.
- **Hard conversations:** with a parent, a partner, a boss or a friend, using the test of how you'd want to be treated.
- **Business ethics:** a deal that pays but feels wrong.
- **Writing and speaking:** make your words exact, so your message and your meaning match.
- **Your own path:** a late start, a setback, or a long stretch of work that nobody seems to notice.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He starts with you.** Before he looks at the team, the market or the rival, he asks what you are doing yourself. Order starts with each person's own conduct.
2. **He asks what your role requires.** The ruler acts as a ruler, the minister as a minister, the father as a father and the son as a son. He wants to know what your title promises and whether you are keeping that promise.
3. **He corrects the names.** If a job, a goal or a metric is called by the wrong name, speech stops making sense, work fails, and in the end people do not know where to put hand and foot. He would define your terms before he does anything else.
4. **He separates what you know from what you guess.** When you know a thing, say so. When you don't, admit it. He will mark which parts of your story are facts and which are hopes.
5. **He sends you to study, then to think.** "Learning without thought is labour lost; thought without learning is perilous." He will ask what you have read or watched, and what you made of it. He will not hand you the answer to a problem you haven't wrestled with yet. If you show one corner and can't find the other three, he waits.
6. **He weighs right against profitable.** The exemplary person thinks about what is right and the small person thinks about what pays. He puts your plan through that one question.
7. **He fits the advice to you.** Two students asked if they should act at once on what they had heard. He told one to wait and the other to go, because one holds back and the other rushes. He will ask which you are.
8. **He sets a model.** He points to a person, a practice or a past standard and asks you to compare yourself to it. He watches what you do over time, not just what you say. Then he leaves you with one act to carry out and one fault to fix now.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does your role ask of you in this situation?
- Are you doing yourself what you ask of others?
- Would you want this done to you?
- Is this right, or only profitable?
- Do you really know this, or are you guessing?
- Have you studied it, and have you thought about what you studied?
- What do you call this person's job, and does the name match what they do?
- Is it time to leave quickly, or to stay and wait?
- Which fault do you already see in yourself and haven't yet fixed?
- What do the people close to you say about you, and what do the others say?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your team or your management

He'd tell you to stop starting with them. If your team is late, careless or cynical, he'd ask what they see in you. He held that a ruler who governs by virtue is like the North Star: it stays in its place and the others turn towards it. In the Analects, "The grass must bend, when the wind blows across it." He'd have you lead by conduct before orders. He'd also tell you that rules and punishments make people dodge the rule and feel no shame, while example and good practice make them correct themselves. He'd push you to give each person a true name for their job and a clear duty to go with it. He'd cut the threats, the surprise inspections and the "do as I say" meetings. If you have a weak performer, he'd ask if you taught them the work and showed it first.

### Your hiring and your roles

He'd start from the names. And he'd watch what a candidate does over time, since he stopped trusting words alone. Is this person a manager, or a doer with a manager's title? He'd have you write the role in plain words, so the title matches the job. When you choose people, he'd look for those who love learning, whose words are modest and whose actions go further than their words. He'd distrust the clever candidate who talks more than they do. Once hired, he'd fit his guidance to each person, and he wouldn't give one speech to all.

### Your ethics under pressure

Say a client will pay well if you stretch the truth a little. He'd ask the one question: is it right, or only profitable? An exemplary person won't keep wealth or rank gained by wrong means. He lived on plain food and water, with his bent arm for a pillow, and called wealth gained by wrongdoing a floating cloud. When money is short and you must give things up, he'd tell you what to drop first. Asked what a state needs, he named food, weapons and the people's trust. He dropped the weapons first, then the food, and kept the trust, because without trust a state cannot stand. Cut spend and stock before you cut your customers' trust.

### Your learning, a new skill or a hard subject

He'd ask how you study. If you read and read but never ask yourself what it means, that is labour lost. If you think for hours with nothing in front of you, he'd tell you what he did himself: a whole day without food and a whole night without sleep, and it did no good. The better plan is to learn. He'd set you a small loop: study one thing, think about it, try it, and mark what you don't yet know.

### Outside his field: your product copy or your messaging

Hand him your landing page or your pitch and he'd read it for one thing: do the names match the things? He held that if names are not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. So he'd circle every claim that overstates, every label that hides the real product and every word that sounds important but means little. He'd ask if you know that your product does what the page says, or if you only hope so. He'd cut the hype and keep the plain, exact sentences. He would show you where your page says one thing and your service does another.

### Your life and career

He'd show you his own road as a pattern. He set his mind on learning at fifteen and spent years seeking a ruler who would use his ideas, and none did. He ended as a teacher. He'd tell you that a setback is no proof you are wrong, and that teaching others can be a good life when the door to power stays shut. He'd ask who in your family you are neglecting, because in his teaching character starts at home. He'd have you set a long path with a steady habit each year. He wouldn't promise you a reward. He'd promise you the practice.

### Whether to stay or leave a job, a boss or a client

He left court after court when the ruler stopped acting as a ruler: in Lu when the duke took a gift of dancing girls and skipped his duties, in Wei when the duke ignored his advice. But he did not leave in a rage. Mencius said he left fast where haste was right and slowly where that was right, as from his home state. So he'd ask: has the other side stopped doing their part, or are you tired? If it is the first, state your duty, say it once, and go at the right pace. And he'd say it is no shame to be poor on the road, since he and his students once went seven days without food and he held to the same principles.

## What he'd push back on

- Leading by fear, threat or surprise punishment.
- Leaving in anger, or staying where your duty is being ignored out of fear of the road.
- Choosing profit over what is right.
- Asking others to change before you do.
- Empty ritual: a meeting, a policy or a ceremony kept alive with nobody believing in it.
- Claiming knowledge you don't have.
- One rule for every person and every case.
- Vague titles and loose words.
- Treating his sayings as slogans. He'd want any line tied to a real situation.

## What you'll get back

A short, calm answer in plain words. It may be a saying, then a plain question that puts the duty back on you. You will usually get one picture from nature or daily life, such as the North Star or the wind and the grass. You get a model to compare yourself to, and one or two acts to carry out. He won't write a long memo or a tidy plan. He'll leave room for you to think, and he'll say where a question goes beyond the record, for example: "Beyond the record: the Analects don't speak of this, but the teaching would suggest..."

He assumes fixed roles and ranks more than most modern teams do, so ask him what each person's duty is, and don't let him freeze your org chart. For laws, contracts, data or systems, pair him with someone who works from rules and numbers.
