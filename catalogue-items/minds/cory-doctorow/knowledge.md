# Cory Doctorow: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Doctorow's public essays examine how dependence on a platform can weaken a user's ability to reject worsening terms, and how interoperability can make alternatives more practical. [S2][S3] The everyday application is an account of what you depend on: files, contacts, features, support and permission to use the things you own. Test a small export, ask what still requires the original service and plan for the people affected by a change. These are original exercises. They do not establish that every platform is abusive, every workaround is safe or every modification is lawful. A real alternative must work for the people who would use it.

## Core ideas

### 1. Leaving has costs beyond cancelling an account

The 2024 essay emphasizes the relationships and audiences that make a service difficult to leave. [S2] An original application is a user considering a new community app. List the records, conversations, invitations and people they would depend on it to reach. Ask which parts can be moved and which require others to act too. A downloaded file may preserve a record without preserving participation. The output is a dependency map rather than a recommendation to leave immediately. A person may reasonably stay because the alternatives do not meet an access or community need. Naming that constraint is more useful than treating continued use as consent to every future change.

### 2. A data export and continued interaction do different jobs

The EFF policy essay distinguishes one-off data portability from ongoing interoperability. [S4] An original exercise is testing a notes export. Choose a small non-sensitive sample with a few real features: headings, dates, links and an attachment. Export it through supported means and inspect it in the intended destination. Record what transfers, what changes and what cannot be used. Keep the originals intact. The output is a migration test, not a claim that an export button guarantees a complete exit. A file can be readable while losing relationships or functions that matter to the user's actual work.

### 3. Inspect incentives alongside promises

Doctorow's 2024 argument asks what consequences would restrain a service owner from making a harmful change, rather than relying only on sincere intentions. [S2] An original application is comparing two tools for a small team. Ask what the team can preserve independently, how it can switch and what support commitments are documented. Distinguish a stated policy from an enforceable agreement and from an informal hope. The output is a question list and an exit test. Do not infer a hidden motive from a business model alone. The practical point is to examine the arrangement under change, including a change of management or priorities.

### 4. Individual workarounds do not supply a whole ecosystem

The October 2026 essay distinguishes personal technical fixes from the wider conditions needed for reliable alternatives and collective change. [S3] An original use is a community whose technically skilled member can move their own material but cannot support everyone else. Map the training, maintenance, access needs and ongoing moderation required for a group move. Assign only work someone has agreed to do. The output is a support plan or a decision that a proposed move is not yet viable. A clever personal solution can be valuable while leaving a shared service problem unresolved. Do not promise that publishing a script automatically creates dependable support.

### 5. Control and correctness remain separate questions

The 2026 essay acknowledges that alternative software can contain serious defects while defending users' interest in control over their devices. [S3] An original application is a household considering a community-maintained replacement for a discontinued tool. Ask what task must continue, what evidence exists about the replacement and who can maintain it. Keep any experiment limited to authorized, appropriate conditions with the original data preserved. The output is a verification brief, not installation advice from an old essay. A claim about who should control a device does not establish that a particular package is reliable, compatible or suitable for a consequential use.

### 6. Ask about a device's life beyond the sale

The public argument connects continuing use with the ability to repair, adapt and use alternatives. [S3][S4] An original purchasing exercise starts with the task a connected device must perform. Ask which functions depend on an account, network or remote service, what support is promised and what happens when that support ends. Include repair information and the user's practical ability to obtain help. The output is a dependency checklist for the actual product. Do not assume a simpler device is always better or that an advertised offline mode performs every needed function. Verify current specifications before making a real purchasing recommendation.

## How to make an exit plan practical

Begin with the work or relationship you want to preserve. A note archive, a family photo collection and a neighborhood group have different requirements. Write the essential functions and the people who rely on them. Separate a copy of information from the ability to continue using it. This is our original organizing exercise. It prevents a move from being judged only by whether a download completed successfully.

Choose a small test that can reveal a meaningful limitation without risking the originals. Use supported export and import routes where possible. Select examples that exercise the important features rather than a single empty file. Compare the source and destination, documenting missing dates, broken links or unavailable permissions. Mark an untested feature as untested. A successful sample is useful evidence, not proof that every item and edge case will transfer.

For a group, include the transition work. Who explains the change, assists people, handles questions and maintains the destination? Ask members what they need and whether they want to participate. Offer a reasonable overlap or fallback where possible. Do not scrape personal contact details or move private conversations without authority. A technically feasible migration can still fail if it ignores consent, accessibility or the social purpose of the group.

Finally distinguish what the user can do now from what requires wider support. A backup, a tested export and a clearer provider question may be available immediately. A market alternative, a repair network or a policy change requires others. State that boundary without turning it into helplessness. The output should identify a useful next action and the remaining dependency, rather than claim that either personal diligence or collective change alone solves everything.

## Where you can put the lens to work

### Test an app export before you need it

The user wants to rely on a tool for important notes. Choose a small sample representing ordinary use. Export it, inspect the actual format and open or import it in the intended destination. Compare the content and features the user values. Record the steps and the missing pieces, preserving the originals. The output is a migration test note and a question for the provider. This original exercise does not certify a whole product or promise future compatibility. Repeat a relevant check when the actual service or intended use changes, rather than treating a marketing claim as permanent evidence.

### Make a community backup contact plan

A group coordinates entirely through one platform. Ask members which fallback they are willing and able to use. Collect only the agreed information and explain who can access it. Write a short plan for announcing a disruption and reaching people without exposing their details publicly. Test the arrangement with a harmless agreed message if the group wants one. The output is a consent-based contact plan. Do not treat an address list as equivalent to a functioning community, or assume that everyone can access the same alternative tool.

### Compare connected-device dependencies

A household is considering a device whose ordinary task seems simple. Use the actual specifications to list account, network and remote-service requirements. Ask what continues offline, who supplies updates, which repairs are available and what is known about support ending. Keep unknowns visible. The output is a comparison brief rather than a product verdict from historical examples. A device can provide useful convenience and still create a dependency worth understanding. The user chooses the tradeoff with current evidence instead of being told that connected products are inherently good or bad.

### Review a platform change fairly

A service changes a feature or access condition the user relied on. Record what changed and the actual effect on their task. Separate that observation from a theory about the company's motives. List supported alternatives, what can be preserved and what costs a switch would create. The output is an options note and a clear provider question or feedback draft for the user to review. Do not invent a breach of law or a private promise. A critical account should remain accurate about the evidence and the practical choices still available.

### Turn a personal workaround into a shared question

One person can solve a problem with a technical modification, but others lack the skill or access. Ask what support would make the alternative useful to them and whether the proposed fix is appropriate for the task. Identify maintenance, documentation and authority needs. The output is a shared-service brief or a decision to keep the experiment personal. This original transfer respects the difference between a working demonstration and a dependable service. It does not certify software quality or make a blanket statement about permission to modify devices.

## Blind spots to watch

The framework can be criticised when a strong account of platform incentives becomes a blanket explanation of every product defect or business decision. A feature may fail for several reasons. Identify the change and its effects before assigning motive, and compare alternatives fairly. User control matters alongside reliability, privacy, support and access. A nominal exit that loses essential functions may not be a practical option.

Legal and technical questions also remain specific. An advocacy essay does not establish what a particular person may do in their jurisdiction, nor does it prove a replacement tool is safe. Avoid operational instructions based solely on historical examples or confident rhetoric. The useful everyday output is a dependency map, a tested supported route or a precise question, with no invented endorsement or promise that every digital problem can be solved by leaving a platform.
