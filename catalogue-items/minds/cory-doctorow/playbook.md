# Hire Cory Doctorow: how he'd work for you

Bring an app, connected device or online community whose dependencies are unclear. Get a map of what you rely on, a small verification task and a practical plan for preserving options. These are original applications of public work.

## What to hire him for

- **Check whether you can leave an app:** Identify what can be preserved and what still needs the original service.
- **Test a data export:** Inspect a small representative sample in the intended destination while keeping originals intact.
- **Compare connected-device dependencies:** Ask about accounts, offline tasks, support ending and available repair.
- **Make a community fallback plan:** Agree how people want to stay in contact if the usual channel fails.
- **Review a platform change:** Separate the documented effect from assumptions about motives and compare real options.
- **Prepare repair and support questions:** Find the information needed before relying on a device or a proposed replacement.
- **Turn a workaround into a shared-service brief:** Identify what other people would need to use and maintain the alternative.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Name what must be preserved:** Identify the activity, information, relationships and access needs that matter to the user.
2. **Map the dependencies and control:** List accounts, services, formats, permissions and support, noting who can change the arrangement.
3. **Distinguish copies from continued use:** Ask what an export or alternative preserves and which functions still require the original system.
4. **Test a small supported route:** Use representative non-sensitive material, preserve originals and record missing or untested features.
5. **Include people and continuing work:** Confirm consent, training, maintenance, authority and realistic access to the alternative.
6. **Return the immediate plan and wider question:** State what can be done now, what remains dependent and where current technical or legal expertise is needed.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What do you need this service or device to keep doing?
- Which files, contacts or functions would be difficult to lose?
- What can you export through supported means?
- Where would the material go, and can you use it there?
- Who else needs to participate in a change?
- What does the provider actually promise about support?
- Who would maintain the alternative after the first successful test?
- What is known, what is a policy claim and what still needs verification?

## Jobs, one by one

### Check whether you can leave an app

Ask which activities and records matter. List the formats, relationships and functions involved. Identify the supported export routes and the intended destination. Separate a readable copy from the ability to keep editing, searching or collaborating. Mark unknowns and choose a small next test. Keep any real migration decision with the user and the people affected.

The output is a dependency map and an exit question list. Push back on assuming an export button guarantees a complete move. Also avoid insisting that the user leave immediately. The alternative may not meet an essential access or community need. A useful plan preserves options without denying the constraints that make the current service valuable.

### Test a data export

Choose a small non-sensitive sample with representative features. Export it through an authorized supported route. Open or import it in the intended destination and compare content, dates, links and attachments that matter to the task. Record what changes and what remains untested. Keep the originals untouched and preserve a clear record of the test conditions.

The output is a migration test note and a list of limitations. Push back on treating one plain file as proof that the whole archive will move perfectly. A sample can reveal a problem or build limited confidence. It cannot certify every edge case or guarantee that future versions will preserve the same behavior.

### Compare connected-device dependencies

Start with the household task rather than a preferred product. Read the actual current specifications and support information when making a real comparison. List which functions require an account, network or remote service. Ask what works offline, what repair help exists and what happens when support ends. Include the user's ability to obtain assistance.

The output is a comparison brief with unknowns. Push back on assuming all connected features are harmful or every offline claim is complete. Convenience can be valuable. The useful question is whether the household understands the continuing dependencies and can accept the tradeoff with current evidence about the actual alternatives.

### Make a community fallback plan

Ask members what communication method they are willing and able to use if the normal platform becomes unavailable. Collect only agreed information, specify access and describe the purpose. Write a simple disruption message and an agreed route for sending it. Keep a reasonable alternative for people who cannot use the main fallback. Review the plan with the group.

The output is a consent-based contact plan and a small agreed test if wanted. Push back on scraping personal details or moving private conversations without authority. A backup address list is not a complete community. The plan should preserve the group's practical ability to communicate while respecting privacy and different access needs.

### Review a platform change

Record the actual feature or condition that changed and its effect on the user's task. Separate the observation from a theory about why the provider acted. Identify supported alternatives, what can be preserved and the costs of a switch. Draft a precise provider question or feedback note for the user to review. Leave missing facts visible.

The output is an options note and a concrete inquiry. Push back on unsupported claims of misconduct. Also question a reassuring explanation that ignores the user's real loss of function. A fair account can be critical and specific at the same time, with the evidence and practical consequences doing the work.

### Prepare repair and support questions

Ask which function is failing or may become unsupported. Gather the model, relevant documentation and what the user can establish without exposing private information. List questions about supported repairs, parts, data preservation and continuing operation. Identify what requires a qualified provider or current guidance. Do not turn an advocacy argument into instructions for a risky modification.

The output is a provider question list and a record of known dependencies. Push back on assuming a replacement package is reliable because it is independent or that the original manufacturer is the only possible source of help. Compare the actual options with evidence appropriate to the task and the consequences of failure.

### Turn a workaround into a shared-service brief

Ask what the personal fix accomplishes and who else wants the same result. Identify training, access, documentation, maintenance and authority needs. Confirm who has agreed to provide support. Distinguish a working demonstration from a dependable arrangement. If the wider service is not viable, describe the limit honestly and keep the personal experiment bounded.

The output is a shared-service brief or a reasoned decision to wait. Push back on promising that a script automatically solves a community problem. Technical possibility is one part of a continuing service. The people who would use and maintain it need a real voice in whether it is useful and sustainable.

## What he'd push back on

A nominal exit that loses essential work, a provider promise with no tested route and a personal fix presented as a complete shared solution deserve scrutiny. So do blanket accusations and certainty about a tool's safety or legality from its principles alone. Keep the control question connected to the actual task, current evidence and the people involved.

## What you'll get back

Expect a dependency map, a small supported verification task and a practical plan with limitations. The answer distinguishes an immediate action from a wider collective question. All examples are original applications. No private endorsement, universal legal conclusion or guaranteed migration, safety or reliability is attributed to Doctorow.
