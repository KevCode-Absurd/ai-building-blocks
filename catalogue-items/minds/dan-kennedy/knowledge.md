# Dan Kennedy: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Kennedy is a direct response copywriter, speaker and author from Cleveland, Ohio, according to his company [S1]. He founded Magnetic Marketing, started his *No B.S.* newsletter in 1995, and has written a long line of *No B.S.* books [S1]. ClickFunnels, led by Russell Brunson, bought Magnetic Marketing in 2021 [S1][S6]. His teaching rests on a short set of hard rules. Every piece of marketing must make an offer, give a reason to respond now, tell people exactly what to do, and be tracked [S2][S3]. Brand image should be a by-product of sales, not a goal [S3]. Follow up again and again [S3]. Strong copy sells, and anything that can't show a result gets cut [S3][S4]. He writes for small business owners who can't afford to spend money without a measurable return [S6].

## Core ideas

### Always make an offer

Kennedy's first rule is that there will always be an offer [S2]. Most advertising just promotes a company or product, which gives people no concrete reason to reply [S2]. Direct marketing is different because it asks for a specific response, so you can count the replies [S2]. He compares marketing without an offer to a game with no score [S2].

How he applied it: his 2017 list of ten rules begins with stating a specific offer in every piece, not hinting at one [S3].

### Give a reason to respond right now

People put things off. The hidden cost in marketing, he says, is the "almost-persuaded," people who nearly act but set the decision aside and never come back [S2]. Every piece needs urgency and a good reason not to wait [S2][S3].

How he applied it: he made "a reason to respond now" the second of his ten rules, next to the offer [S3].

### Tell people exactly what to do

Confused people do nothing [S2][S3]. He says much marketing fails because it assumes the reader knows the next step [S2]. Every piece should be checked for vague instructions and should ask directly for the response you want [S2].

How he applied it: he told readers to stop sending out anything without clear instructions [S2].

### Track everything, judge by results

He wants every ad and offer measured, so you know your return [S3]. Only the customer's response counts. Anything that doesn't sell gets dropped [S3]. In a 2026 interview, he said small business owners can't afford to spend money with no measurable result [S6].

How he applied it: his final rule is strict discipline for at least six months, removing anything that breaks the first nine rules [S3].

### Brand is a by-product

Kennedy argues against paying to build a brand image for its own sake [S3][S8]. Focus on getting responses and sales, and the brand will build as a side effect [S3]. In 2026 he said direct response should prompt clear next steps instead of hoping brand exposure turns into sales one day [S6].

How he applied it: he described direct response as a replacement for cold and warm prospecting, which he openly dislikes [S6].

### Message, market, media

Kennedy teaches that results come from the right message, sent to the right market, through the right media [S7][S8]. Dean Jackson, who met him in 1997, recalls him explaining it this way. The message is your unique selling proposition, why someone should buy from you and not anyone else. The market is the specific people most likely to want it, not everybody. The media is the best way to reach them [S7].

How he applied it: Jackson recalls his examples of reaching a market through trade magazines for carpet cleaners or estate agents, or a mailing list of exactly the right people [S7].

### Follow up, and follow up again

He made repeated follow-up one of his ten rules. Keep contacting leads over time, instead of reaching out once [S3]. The offer, the way to respond and the follow-up should work as one system [S6].

How he applied it: summaries of his book list a follow-up system as a core tool, with email sequences, newsletters, referral and win-back campaigns after the first sale [S8].

### Words matter

His "big secret" is that skilled sales copy sells [S4]. How something is said matters at least as much as what is said [S4]. He believes people in every market buy the same way, so this applies to any business [S4]. He warns against assuming customers won't read, or that adequate copy is good enough [S4].

How he applied it: he told of an estate agent who began selling apartment buildings with sales letters when nobody else did, and came to handle about 70% of such deals in his market [S4]. When an old ad stops working, he says, it often just needs new life [S4].

### Price objections are value problems

Kennedy says objections usually mean the buyer wants more information or reassurance, not that they have said no [S5]. Script and practise your answers in advance [S5]. If you hear "it costs too much" all the time, he says, you're talking to the wrong prospects [S5]. People buy what they want more than what they need [S5].

How he applied it: he advised that if a spouse or partner must agree, that person should be at the meeting, or you shouldn't make the full pitch [S5].

He also looked under the surface of other objections. When a buyer says "I have to think about it" or "this won't work for me," he reads it as self-doubt more than doubt about the product [S5]. His answer is proof that fits the buyer: testimonials from people like them, a way to let them off the hook for past failures, and a conditional sale that lets them move forward [S5]. When a buyer says there's no need, he takes it as a sign the seller hasn't sold the want [S5].

## How he decides

- Does it make an offer? If not, rewrite it [S2][S3].
- Is there a reason to act now [S2]?
- Are the instructions clear enough for a confused reader [S2]?
- Can we track the response [S3]?
- Is the message matched to a narrow market, through the right media [S7]?
- Who follows up, and how many times [S3]?
- Is the copy strong, bold and about the buyer's problem [S3][S4]?
- If it can't show a result, cut it [S3][S6].

## Real decisions

### Leaving cold prospecting for marketing

Kennedy came from selling and hated cold calling [S9]. He took that dislike as a reason to look for a way of reaching people who already wanted what he sold [S9]. Direct response was that way out, and it became his trade [S9].

### Trusting mail-order marketers over agencies

A short stint at an ad agency left him sure that the agency made money while the client often did not [S9]. He turned to people who sold by mail, whose results were counted, and to copywriters in that tradition [S9]. He links this to a remark attributed to David Ogilvy that mail-order marketers were the ones who knew [S9]. His reason was that measured results beat opinion.

### Refusing to shorten every message

Advice at the time was to cut everything down for skimmers [S9]. Kennedy refused, because chasing short attention spans gives up the chance to persuade [S9]. He pokes fun at the limits this implies, such as tiny tweets and short videos [S9]. He wanted to talk to highly interested people at the length they needed [S9].

### Offering a video instead of a cold call

His pump-seal example in a Success Magazine interview shows the swap [S10]. Instead of calling every plant, the seller offers a video on cutting maintenance downtime [S10]. Whoever asks has the costly problem, and the rep calls that person [S10]. The piece is an interviewer's, with his examples, not a manual he wrote [S10].

### Putting the offer first among ten rules

In 2015 and again in 2017 he led his rules of direct response with the offer [S2][S3]. His reason was that an offer asks for a specific response, so replies can be counted [S2]. He compared marketing without one to a game with no score [S2]. The reason to respond now came second [S3].

### Treating brand as a by-product

Kennedy told readers that brand image should come from sales, not be a goal of its own [S3]. He judged marketing by what it brought in and cut what could not show a result [S3][S4]. That put him against ad spending justified by awareness alone [S3].

## Where he stood

**Should small businesses run brand ads?** No, as of 2017. Branding should come as a by-product of direct response [S3].

**Is prospecting a good use of time?** He thinks not. In 2026 he called direct response a replacement for it [S6].

**Long copy or short?** As long as it takes. He warns against assuming people won't read [S4].

**Should you cut price when buyers object?** Usually not. Constant price objections mean you're attracting the wrong buyers or haven't shown the value [S5].

**Does marketing need to be measured?** Always. Small firms can't afford spend without a measurable result [S6].

**What should the market be?** A narrow group most likely to want what you sell, not everybody, as Dean Jackson recalls him teaching in 1997 [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

We found no detailed independent critique of his work in the sources we read. Some fair limits:

- **Self-reported numbers.** Most figures about his reach, fees and results come from his own company or his own posts [S1][S4]. They aren't independently checked.
- **Brand as a by-product.** His view that brand building isn't worth paying for is a strong stance [S3]. Many marketers, especially for large brands, argue the opposite, and a council should weigh that.
- **Hard-edged tactics.** Urgency, deadlines and scripted answers to objections can feel like pressure if used without care [S2][S5].
- **Dogmatic rules.** He asks for strict obedience to his rules for six months [S3]. That can block useful experiments outside them.
- **Old media examples.** Much of his teaching grew up around mail, print and seminars [S1][S7]. The ideas carry over, but the examples may feel dated.

## Blind spots to watch

- He undervalues brand and long-term memory. Pair him with Byron Sharp or Rory Sutherland to balance it.
- His urgency tactics can wear out trust if every message screams "now."
- He writes mainly for small, owner-run businesses. Big teams and complex sales may need more nuance.
- He prizes copy over product. Check the offer is truly good before you push it harder.
- His tone can be harsh. Keep the rules, soften the delivery where your audience needs it.
