## In one paragraph

Dan Wieden was a Portland ad man who built one of the world's largest independent agencies far from Madison Avenue [S2][S4]. After studying journalism at the University of Oregon, he wrote for Georgia-Pacific, then joined McCann-Erickson, where he was paired with art director David Kennedy [S1][S6]. On April 1, 1982, the two opened Wieden & Kennedy with one client, Nike [S1][S2]. He wrote "Just Do It" for Nike in the late 1980s [S1][S3]. The agency became known for work with attitude: "Bo Knows," the Spike Lee spots for Air Jordan, Honda's "Grrr," Old Spice and Chrysler's "Born of Fire" [S2]. Wieden built a culture around fun, outsiders, untested talent and the freedom to fail [S4][S6]. He stepped back from daily work in 2015 and died in Portland in September 2022, aged 77 [S1][S8].

## Core ideas

### Walk in stupid

Wieden treated not knowing as an advantage. He said being ignorant was the key to the agency's success [S4]. The Hall of Fame profile sums up the founders' approach as walking in stupid every day [S6]. An outsider sees what experts have stopped noticing.

How he applied it: he credited the agency's outsider position, away from New York, and a rebellious client with freeing it to rethink how advertising worked [S4]. He saw a lack of convention as a strength [S4].

How to use it: before you start, list what "everyone knows" about the problem. Then ask what you would do if none of it were true.

#### Chaos, not knowing, and the work first

In his speech to the agency he said W+K began as a ship of fools, and that not knowing is worth more than assuming you know [S12][S13]. He said chaos and discomfort drive growth better than order, and that authority should be questioned, since truth matters more than power [S12]. He put the work first and the client relationship second, and said that if the work falls short you go back to the drawing board until the idea is as good or better [S12]. Money, he said, is necessary like oxygen, but it is not the reason the agency exists, and recognition should go to people, not awards [S12]. He deliberately brought uncertainty even into departments that need precision, such as finance and traffic [S12].

### Fail better

Wieden coined sayings his staff still see on the agency's walls, known as Wiedenisms [S4]. One is "Fail better," borrowed from the writer Samuel Beckett [S4]. It came from his own life: he was fired early in his career [S4].

How to use it: treat a failed idea as a step. Ask what it taught you, then try a better one quickly.

### Don't marry the idea

He was willing to let go. Of one idea he said he wasn't married to it and they could drop it next round [S4]. Loving an idea is fine. Clinging to it is not.

How to use it: hold ideas lightly in early rounds. Keep what works and drop the rest without a fight.

### Make it fun

Wieden believed advertising should be fun, a lesson he said he learned from Kennedy [S6]. Fun became central to the agency's work [S6]. The Portland office was known for afternoon rock concerts, napping rooms and a yearly pie-baking contest [S7]. He also said he survived his early copywriting job by treating it as science fiction [S6].

How to use it: if the team isn't enjoying the work, the audience probably won't either.

### Give the brand attitude

The Hall of Fame credits Wieden with helping make advertising safe for entertainment, and with the rise of "attitude" advertising, where a brand speaks in its own distinct voice [S6]. He borrowed fragments of design, art and film, and invited audiences to question the claims being made [S6].

How he applied it: a 1987 Nike Air spot set to the Beatles' "Revolution" was praised for its bravery and for pushing advertising toward art [S9]. A 1985 Honda scooter spot with Lou Reed helped persuade Nike to move all its business to the agency [S7].

How to use it: write down how your brand would talk if it were a person at a party. Is it shy, cocky, funny, angry? Pick one. Then borrow something from outside advertising, a song, a film style, a kind of art, that carries that mood, and let it shape the work [S6].

### A short line anyone can say

"Just Do It" is three plain words people can say to themselves [S3]. Wieden said the phrase came from the last words of a condemned man, Gary Gilmore, "Let's do it." He changed it because he didn't like the "let's" [S3]. The line was first used in a campaign about marathon runners [S7].

How he applied it: from 1988 to 1998, Nike's share of the North American sport-shoe business rose from 18% to 43%, and its sales from $877 million to $9.2 billion [S3]. The line ran for decades, up to the 2018 "Dream Crazy" film [S3].

How to use it: write the line your customer would say to push themselves, not the line that praises your product.

#### Sell an idea and a sensibility

Asked about product ads, he said the brand was trying to peddle ideas, and that a product needs a sensibility, a spirit beyond taste or fit [S11]. A unifying line is needed when different teams make fragmented work [S11]. He described writing four or five options late at night and narrowing them, testing them on colleagues, and then, when a client pushed back, trusting his own call [S11]. He said he drew "Just Do It" from the last words of a convicted man [S11].

### Get out of talented people's way

Wieden's leadership favoured creative people [S4]. He liked outsiders, odd methods and talent nobody had tested yet [S6]. A former employee recalled his speech at the agency's tenth birthday in 1992, about people needing someone to get out of their way [S7]. His legacy lies as much in the culture and the people he mentored as in the ads [S4].

How to use it: hire people who see things differently, give them a real problem, and clear the path.

### Creativity can change young lives

In 1996 he and his family founded Caldera, an arts education group and camp for young people in Oregon [S5][S1]. Its guiding belief, in his words, is what happens when young people get access to the explosive power of creativity [S5]. They can then reshape their own surroundings [S5].

How to use it: when a business has money or skill to give, give people the tools to make things, not just a donation. Wieden's charity focused on young people's strengths rather than their problems [S5].

### Stay independent

The agency grew by persuading big companies to work with it in Portland rather than through mergers [S6]. It remains one of the largest independent agencies in the world [S2]. It describes its roots as an ad agency that didn't like advertising [S10].

How he applied it: the founders started with a borrowed typewriter and a cardboard box for a conference table, and billed under $1 million at first [S9]. They stayed in Portland and made clients come to them [S6].

How to use it: if you are small or far from the centre, don't apologise for it. Make it part of your story and your point of view [S4].

#### Hire for voice, and keep the agency unsold

He wanted people with distinctive perspectives, judged partly on instinct, and said good ideas should win whoever had them, junior or senior [S13]. He held that selling the agency would hurt the people there and destroy its culture, so its shares are held in a trust with that one duty [S11][S13]. He said failure is necessary to meaningful work, and rules and theories about creativity can kill it [S13]. He would turn down brands whose products he did not believe in [S11].

## Where he stood

**Is being an outsider a weakness?** No. He saw it as the agency's advantage [S4].

**Should advertising entertain?** Yes. He helped make it safe for entertainment [S6].

**What about failure?** Expect it, and fail better next time [S4].

**Who leads the work?** The creative people, with leaders who get out of the way [S4][S7].

**What is creativity for?** Beyond selling, it can change young people's lives [S5].

**Does success need a plan?** He said they never expected to be so successful, and called himself an old hippie [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Dark source.** "Just Do It" came from a condemned killer's last words, by his own account [S3]. Some may find that origin uncomfortable.
- **Backlash.** The 2018 "Dream Crazy" film with Colin Kaepernick drew boycotts and shoe burnings, though some analysts said it worked with its target audience [S3].
- **Culture.** A famously loose, fun culture can be hard on people who don't fit, and accounts of it come mostly from admirers [S4][S7].
- **Thin record.** He left little in writing, so many sayings come second-hand [S4][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- Attitude works for bold brands. A cautious business may need a gentler voice.
- "Walk in stupid" can skip useful facts. Pair him with a researcher.
- His model relied on a brave client like Nike. Without one, the brave idea may never run.
- Fun culture still needs fair pay, clear goals and care for people.
