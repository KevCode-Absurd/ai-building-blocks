# Daniel Ek: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Daniel Ek is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from three notes he wrote to Spotify staff, in January 2023, December 2023 and September 2025, from Spotify's account of its 2022 Investor Day, a 2026 Skavlan interview [S7], and public reference pages [S1][S2]. The notes to staff and the Investor Day account are held on disk only as short source records, so the detail on them rests on earlier reading of the pages. The interview transcript looks machine made, so its meaning is safer than its exact words, and it is a single interview. Treat each view as "as of" the piece it comes from. He became Spotify's executive chairman in January 2026 [S5]. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after these pieces, or about Spotify's future plans, and nothing here covers his private life.

## In one paragraph

Ek grew up in Stockholm, built websites as a teenager and sold an online advertising company before he was 25 [S1]. After the music file-sharing service Napster shut down, he concluded that the only real answer to piracy was a service that was better than piracy and also paid the music industry [S1]. He co-founded Spotify with Martin Lorentzon in 2006 and launched it in 2008 with a free tier funded by ads and a paid tier on top [S1][S2]. Spotify listed on the New York Stock Exchange in 2018 and reported its first full-year profit in 2024 [S2]. His notes to staff show a leader who values speed, admits when he over-invested, and treats each job as a mission [S3][S4][S5]. Critics, many of them musicians, have long argued that streaming pays most artists too little [S2].

## Core ideas

### Beat the bad option by being better

Ek's founding idea was that you can't stop people using a free but bad option by telling them not to. You have to offer something better [S1]. For music, that meant a legal service easier than piracy that also paid rights holders [S1].

How he applied it: Spotify launched in 2008 as a legal alternative to piracy, with a freemium model: free with ads, or paid without them [S2]. It pays about 70% of its revenue to rights holders [S2].

### Speed is the best defence, but it needs efficiency

In January 2023 he wrote that speed is the most defensible strategy a business can have [S3]. Speed alone isn't enough, though. It has to come with efficiency for a company to last [S3]. He thought Spotify had become fast but wasteful, with too much time spent aligning on slightly different plans [S3].

How he applied it: he merged engineering and product under one leader and business areas under another, and made them co-presidents to help run the company day to day [S3].

### Own the mistake in plain words

When he cut staff, he took the blame himself. In January 2023 he wrote that he had been too ambitious in investing ahead of revenue growth [S3]. Costs had grown twice as fast as revenue in 2022, which he called unsustainable [S3].

How he applied it: each note set out the support for people leaving: on average about five months of severance, unused holiday paid out, healthcare through the severance period, immigration help and two months of job support [S3][S4].

### Be relentlessly resourceful

By December 2023 he had decided that smaller cuts were not enough [S4]. He wrote that in 2020 and 2021 cheap money had funded hiring, content and new areas, and that these bets generally worked, but costs were now too high for a slower economy with more expensive capital [S4]. Spotify had become more productive but less efficient, with too many people supporting work rather than driving impact [S4].

How he applied it: he cut about 17% of staff in one move rather than spreading smaller cuts over two years [S4]. He said a leaner Spotify did not mean smaller goals, and called for the resourcefulness of the early days [S4].

### Build a platform for many creators

At Investor Day in June 2022 he described Spotify's goal as becoming the world's creator platform [S6]. He spoke of a platform with many sides, serving listeners and creators, and set long-range targets: a billion users, 50 million artists and creators, and about $100 billion in yearly revenue within a decade [S6]. He argued that tools for creators would draw talent and lift margins [S6].

How he applied it: Spotify bought podcast companies Gimlet and Parcast in 2019 and The Ringer in 2020 [S2]. In 2020 he argued that artists need to keep engaging fans rather than release albums only now and then [S1].

### Roles are missions

Ek described the jobs he has held at Spotify, from assembling furniture and negotiating early deals to running finance, product and sales, as a series of missions [S5]. In 2025 he called his move to executive chairman simply his next mission [S5]. He framed Spotify's purpose as turning ideas that seem impossible into reality [S5].

How he applied it: from January 2026 he became executive chairman, with Alex Norström and Gustav Söderström as co-CEOs reporting to him. He described it as a European-style chair role, more hands-on than many US chairs, focused on strategy, capital and regulation [S5].

### Make the place you build in better

In 2016 he and Lorentzon wrote an open letter warning that Spotify might leave Sweden unless rules on housing, taxes and education changed [S1]. He pointed to high taxes on staff stock options and to housing rules that made it hard to hire [S1].

How he applied it: by 2025 he said he wanted to put more energy into building very large companies that take on major challenges [S5]. His investment firm Prima Materia, set up in 2021, pledged $1 billion for European "moonshot" bets [S1].

### Profit and product together

In 2025 he said Spotify's focus remained a product people love and a healthy business side by side [S5]. He pointed to Spotify's first full year of profit as proof the company was in good shape [S5].

How he applied it: Spotify reported net income of €1.14 billion for 2024 and €2.21 billion for 2025 [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask what people use today instead, and make yours clearly better [S1].
- Move fast, then check that speed isn't wasting money [S3].
- Compare cost growth to revenue growth every year [S3].
- If a cut is needed, make one clear decision rather than many small ones [S4].
- Own the mistake in your own name [S3].
- Treat people who leave well, and spell out how [S3][S4].
- Ask what matters to you and what you enjoy, before what looks impressive [S7].
- Set long-range targets, then focus on the few moves that matter [S6].
- Hand over a role when the team is ready [S5].

## Real decisions

### Retire, then start again
After Advertigo was sold, he had enough money to stop and did, for a few months [S1]. He then wanted a new project, which became Spotify [S1]. In the interview he said money was not what drove him. The aim was to see if music could work better for listeners and artists [S7].

### Go back to the sceptical record labels
Napster, he argued, was a good service that failed artists, and weak legal choices fed piracy [S7]. He kept returning to doubtful music executives, argued with them, and let a working prototype win them over [S7]. Spotify launched legally in October 2008 [S1].

### Turn down the big buyers
He has said he refused to sell Spotify to large technology firms, and did not dwell on valuation figures [S7]. He later put another $50 million of his own money into Spotify shares in 2022 [S1]. His reason in the interview was about purpose, not price [S7].

### Move off peer-to-peer
Spotify first ran on a peer-to-peer model like the one he knew from uTorrent, then switched to a server-client model in 2014 [S1]. The record does not give his reasoning in his own words, so treat the cause as unstated.

### Warn the government, in public
In 2016 he and Martin Lorentzon wrote an open letter saying Spotify would have to leave Sweden unless rules on housing, stock option tax and schooling changed [S1]. His stated reason was that high tax on stock options made it hard to hire programmers [S1].

### Cut costs in one move
In January 2023 he cut staff and took the blame for investing ahead of revenue [S3]. By December 2023 he judged smaller cuts were not enough and cut about 17% in one step instead of two years of trims [S4]. He spelled out severance and support for those who left [S3][S4].

### Step back from CEO
In September 2025 he said he would move to executive chairman, with two co-CEOs reporting to him, calling it his next mission [S5][S1]. He kept control of capital, long-term strategy and regulation [S1].

## Where he stood

**How do you beat piracy?** By being better than it and paying the industry [S1].

**Is speed enough?** No. As of 2023, speed must be paired with efficiency [S3].

**Who was responsible for over-hiring?** As of January 2023, he said he was, because he invested too far ahead of revenue [S3].

**What is Spotify for?** As of 2022, a platform for creators as well as listeners [S6]. As of 2025, turning ideas that seem impossible into reality [S5].

**Should artists release less often?** As of 2020, he argued they should engage fans more continuously [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Artist pay.** Critics say streaming pays most artists very little. Per-play payouts are a fraction of a cent, and one estimate found that only about 0.19% of artists earned $50,000 or more on Spotify in 2020 [S2]. A 2024 rule requiring 1,000 plays a year before a track earns royalties drew mixed reactions [S2].
- **His comments on music.** After a 2020 interview, artists criticised his remarks on releasing music more often. In 2024 he posted that the cost of making content was near zero, faced a backlash, and said he had been too vague [S1].
- **Defence investment.** Several artists removed their music from Spotify over Prima Materia's investment in the defence technology firm Helsing, where he is chairman [S1].
- **Over-hiring.** By his own account, he invested too far ahead of revenue, which led to two rounds of layoffs in 2023 [S3][S4].
- **Unions.** Spotify ended joint talks with three Swedish unions in August 2023 [S2].

These are fair points from the record. His notes give his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- Platform thinking can treat creators as a supply side. Ask how the people who make the content are paid and treated [S2].
- His case for speed can lead to over-hiring, as he admitted [S3].
- Long-range targets can look bold on a slide and hard in practice. Check them against cash.
- His writing here is mostly staff notes about hard moments. It shows how he leads in a crisis more than how he builds products.
- Big bets outside the core, such as defence, can hurt trust with the people a platform depends on [S1].
