## In one paragraph

Ek grew up in Stockholm, built websites as a teenager and sold an online advertising company before he was 25 [S1]. After Napster closed, he decided the only real answer to piracy was a service better than piracy that also paid the music industry [S1]. He co-founded Spotify with Martin Lorentzon in 2006 and launched it in 2008 with a free tier and a paid tier [S1][S2]. Spotify listed in New York in 2018 and made its first full-year profit in 2024 [S2]. In his own words he is a problem-picker who thinks happiness follows impact, who scores a company by the problems it has solved, and who learned to coach people rather than play every position himself [S8]. Critics, many of them musicians, argue streaming pays most artists too little [S2].

## Core ideas

### Beat the bad option by being better

You can't stop people using a free but bad option by telling them not to. You have to offer something better [S1]. For music, that meant a legal service easier than piracy that also paid rights holders [S1]. Spotify launched in 2008 as a freemium service, free with ads or paid without them [S2].

### Pick problems, not solutions

In the Senra talk he said he is drawn to hard problems even when the odds of solving them look like 5 to 10 percent, if solving them would matter a lot. He credits his co-founder with the idea that a company's value is the sum of the problems it has solved [S8]. He thinks quality comes from focus and distillation, so less is more [S8].

### Happiness is a trailing indicator of impact

He argues that lasting happiness follows meaningful work, and that impact is personal and can't be defined by someone else [S8]. He calls himself lazy by nature and says hard problems are what pull him in [S8]. He manages energy rather than time, and says his best ideas often come after breaks [S8].

### Speed is the best defence, but it needs efficiency

In January 2023 he wrote that speed is the most defensible strategy a business can have, but Spotify had put less weight on efficiency, and teams were spending too much time aligning on slightly different plans [S3]. Operating costs had grown about twice as fast as revenue in 2022 [S3].

### Own the mistake in plain words

When he cut staff he took the blame. He wrote that he was too ambitious in investing ahead of revenue growth and took full accountability for the moves that led there [S3]. Each note listed the support for people leaving: about five months of severance on average, unused holiday paid out, healthcare through the severance period, immigration help and two months of job support [S3][S4].

### Productive is not the same as efficient

By December 2023 he judged that Spotify was more productive but less efficient, with too many people supporting work around the work rather than driving impact [S4]. He added that lean doesn't mean small ambitions, and called for a relentlessly resourceful company [S4].

### Judge a business on lifetime value and a few foundations

At Investor Day 2022 he named four foundations: being on every device, personalization, a free ad-supported tier feeding paid subscriptions, and a marketplace of creator tools [S9]. His main test for an investment was lifetime value per user. A bet is fine while lifetime value stays positive and gross profit grows [S9]. He also said investors had misread the margins because they judged Spotify as a music company and not as an audio platform [S9].

### Taste needs feedback

The first Spotify screens came largely from his own taste and a colleague's. As the customers changed, he saw his personal view get less reliable, so the company needed ways to collect outside input. He defines taste as judgment plus curiosity [S8].

### Roles are missions

He described his jobs at Spotify, from assembling furniture to deals to finance to product, as a series of missions, and called his move to executive chairman simply his next mission [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask what people use today instead, and make yours clearly better [S1].
- Choose the problem first, and be willing to take long odds on a big one [S8].
- Check speed against efficiency, and costs against revenue, every year [S3].
- If a cut is needed, make one clear decision, not many small ones [S4].
- Own the mistake in your own name [S3].
- Test a bet by lifetime value per user, not by short-run margin [S9].
- Sell only to someone who would carry the mission forward, not for price [S8].
- Hand a job over when someone is better at it, and test the handover for a fixed period [S8].
- Keep people near you who tell you the truth, and judge people by their best idea, not their worst [S8].
- Copy a practice only if it fits your own temperament [S8].

## Real decisions

### Say no to buyers
After selling his first company at 22, Ek received later offers for Spotify. His test was whether a buyer would advance the mission and care about it as much as he did, and he never found one. He said it was not about money, because he already had what he needed [S8].

### Give product reviews to someone better
Ek insisted on running product reviews himself, even though his product lead, Gustav Söderström, was better at it. Gustav told him his input was not adding much, and Ek's first reaction was defensive. He then agreed to a three-month trial of stepping back. It went well, he has not run product meetings since, and he moved his attention to creators and the space between creators and consumers [S8].

### Learn from Zuckerberg by following him around
To learn how to run large group meetings, Ek spent about a week sitting in on nearly all of Mark Zuckerberg's meetings and interviewing his executives. The big meeting format looked unworkable to him at first and worked in practice. He took on a few practices, and his lesson was that a method works only when it fits the leader [S8].

### Cut 6 percent and reorganize
In January 2023 he split the business under two leaders: Gustav for engineering and product, and Alex Norström for business areas, both as co-presidents. He cut about 6 percent of staff the same day. His reasons were that costs had grown about twice as fast as revenue in 2022 and that he had invested too far ahead of revenue [S3].

### Cut 17 percent in one step
In December 2023 he decided smaller cuts were not enough. The team had debated spreading smaller reductions over 2024 and 2025, and he chose one large reduction of about 17 percent. His stated reasons were slower growth, more expensive capital, and a cost base built in the cheap-money years of 2020 and 2021 [S4].

### Buy into podcasts and audiobooks
At Investor Day 2022 he defended podcasts as a deliberate, costly bet to widen the audience and deepen engagement. He conceded that they pulled down gross margin and weren't yet profitable, and he expected margins to rise later. He gave audiobooks the same reasoning, calling them a large, thinly served market [S9].

### Step back from CEO
In September 2025 he said he would become executive chairman on 1 January 2026, with Gustav and Alex as co-CEOs reporting to him. His reasons: they had shown they were ready, the plan was strong, and Spotify had its first full year of profit. He said he would keep a hand in strategy, capital and regulation, and spend time on building more large technology companies from Europe [S5].

## Where he stood

**How do you beat piracy?** By being better than it and paying the industry [S1].

**Is speed enough?** No. As of 2023, speed must be paired with efficiency [S3][S4].

**Who was responsible for over-hiring?** As of January 2023, he said he was [S3].

**What is Spotify for?** As of 2022, an audio platform for listeners and creators [S9]. As of 2025, turning ideas that seem impossible into reality [S5].

**What makes people happy?** As of 2026, doing work with impact [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Artist pay.** Critics say streaming pays most artists very little, and a 2024 rule requiring 1,000 plays before a track earns royalties drew mixed reactions [S2].
- **His comments on music.** After a 2020 interview, artists criticised his remarks on releasing music more often. In 2024 he posted that content costs were near zero, faced a backlash and said he had been too vague [S1].
- **Defence investment.** Several artists removed their music over Prima Materia's investment in Helsing, where he is chairman [S1].
- **Over-hiring.** By his own account he invested too far ahead of revenue, which led to two rounds of cuts in 2023 [S3][S4].
- **Unions.** Spotify ended joint talks with three Swedish unions in August 2023 [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- Platform thinking can treat creators as a supply side. Ask how the people who make the content are paid [S2].
- A case for speed can lead to over-hiring, as he admitted [S3].
- Long-range targets can look bold on a slide and hard in cash terms [S9].
- Much of his writing is about hard moments, so it shows crisis leadership more than product craft.
- Bets far from the core can cost trust with the people a platform depends on [S1].
