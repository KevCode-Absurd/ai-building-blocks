# Daniel Kahneman: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Kahneman's books are still in copyright, so this is our summary of his ideas, with a few short sourced quotes. He was a psychologist, and this mind offers general ideas about judgement. It gives no medical, mental health or therapy advice.

One honesty note up front. Some studies he cited in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, mainly in the chapter on priming, have not held up when others tried to repeat them. He said so himself in 2017 [S2][S6]. We flag those parts below and leave the shaky findings out of the advice.

## In one paragraph

Daniel Kahneman was an Israeli-American psychologist who showed, with his partner Amos Tversky, that people make the same kinds of errors in judgement again and again, and that the errors can be predicted [S1]. Their work on mental shortcuts and on how people weigh gains and losses changed economics, though Kahneman said he never took an economics course [S1]. He shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in economic sciences [S1]. His big popular book, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011), describes the mind as two systems: a fast, automatic one that does most of the work, and a slow, effortful one that we think of as ourselves [S2][S3]. His last book, *Noise* (2021), argued that random scatter in human judgement does as much harm as bias [S1][S8]. Through it all he stayed doubtful of confident answers, including his own [S4][S6].

## Core ideas

### Two systems: fast and slow

Kahneman described two families of mental work [S3]. System 1 is fast, automatic and effortless. It covers perception, intuition and practised skill. System 2 is slow, deliberate and follows rules [S3]. System 1 makes suggestions and System 2 mostly signs off on them, like an editor who only glances at the copy [S3]. He was clear that the two are characters in a story to help us think, not literal parts of the brain [S3].

How he applied it: the book's examples include finishing the phrase "war and..." (System 1) versus multiplying 17 by 24 in your head (System 2) [S2].

### What you see is all there is

System 1 builds the best story it can from what is in front of it and ignores what is missing [S3]. He called this WYSIATI. It explains why people jump to firm conclusions from small, weak samples, and why confidence tracks how neat the story feels rather than how good the evidence is [S3].

How he applied it: he warned that a coherent story with little evidence can feel more convincing than a messy story with a lot [S3].

### Heuristics and biases

With Tversky he showed that people swap a hard question for an easier one and answer that instead [S2]. Their 1974 paper "Judgment Under Uncertainty" set out several of these shortcuts, including anchoring [S1].

How he applied it:
- **Anchoring.** People asked whether Gandhi was older than 114 when he died guessed far higher ages than people asked about 35, though both numbers were clearly irrelevant [S2].
- **Availability.** Events that are easy to recall seem more likely than they are [S2].
- **The Linda problem.** Most people judged "feminist bank teller" more likely than "bank teller," which breaks the rules of probability. They had swapped in the question "does she sound like a feminist?" [S2]

### Prospect theory and loss aversion

Prospect theory, published with Tversky in 1979, challenged the standard economic model of choice [S1][S2]. People judge outcomes as gains or losses from a reference point, not as final states of wealth, and losses hurt more than equal gains please [S2]. People also feel a change from 0% to 10% chance much more than a change from 45% to 55% [S2]. The 1979 paper became the most cited in its journal [S1].

How he applied it: framing changes choices even when the facts are the same. Surgery described with a 90% survival rate is more acceptable than surgery with a 10% death rate [S2].

### The planning fallacy and the outside view

People plan as if their own project will go to plan, and are too hopeful about cost and time [S2]. The book links this optimism to an illusion of control [S2].

How he applied it: the book cites American kitchen remodels in 2002, expected on average to cost $18,658 but costing $38,769 [S2]. His fix, in the HBR checklist work and the book, is to look at how similar cases turned out before trusting your own estimate [S2][S7].

### When to trust intuition

Kahneman spent years working on this with Gary Klein, a defender of expert intuition [S3][S4]. They agreed that a person's confidence is not a guide. What matters is the setting [S3]. Intuition can be trusted when the setting is regular enough to learn and the person has had lots of practice with quick feedback [S3]. Where the setting is not regular, as with long-range political forecasts or stock picking, confident intuition is not worth much [S3].

How he applied it: he called the paper they wrote together "A Failure to Disagree," and said it was his most satisfying collaboration even though neither side changed its basic view [S4].

### Two selves: experience and memory

Kahneman separated the experiencing self, which lives moment to moment, from the remembering self, which judges an episode afterwards [S1][S2]. The remembering self mostly keeps the peak and the end, and largely ignores how long it lasted [S1][S2]. It is also the self that makes future choices [S1].

How he applied it: in a colonoscopy study, adding a milder ending to the procedure made patients remember it as less unpleasant, though it lasted longer [S1]. His "focusing illusion" work with David Schkade showed that students in the Midwest expected Californians to be happier, mainly because of the weather [S1].

### Noise

In *Noise*, written with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein, he defined noise as unwanted scatter in judgements that should be the same [S8]. Bias is a scale that always reads half a kilo heavy. Noise is a scale that reads differently each time [S8]. The authors found it in judges' sentences, insurance quotes, medical diagnoses, hiring and more [S8].

How he applied it: the book recommends a "noise audit" and "decision hygiene," such as breaking a judgement into separate parts and collecting views independently before a group talks [S8].

### Adversarial collaboration

Kahneman disliked what he called "angry science": critique, reply and rejoinder, where each side attacks the weakest form of the other's view and nobody concedes [S4]. In 2001 he proposed an alternative. Rivals agree on a fair test in advance, run it together, often with a neutral referee, and write up what they agree on first [S4]. He stopped replying to hostile papers [S4].

## How he decides

- Treat your own confidence as a feeling, not as evidence [S3].
- Ask what information is missing from the story you've built [S3].
- Trust expert gut calls only where the setting is regular and feedback is fast [S3].
- Look at how similar cases turned out before you trust your own forecast [S2][S7].
- Reframe the choice as a gain and as a loss, and see if your answer changes [S2].
- Break big judgements into parts and judge each one separately [S8].
- Get independent views before a group discussion [S8].
- Before you argue with a rival, agree on what result would settle it [S4].
- Assume you will explain away surprising results after the fact, and guard against it [S4].

## Where he stood

**Does knowing about biases cure them?** Not much on its own. His HBR article with Lovallo and Sibony starts from the point that awareness of biases has done little to improve business decisions, so leaders need a process that checks a team's thinking [S7]. He also said he and Tversky could still feel the pull of the errors they studied [S3].

**Can people change their minds?** Rarely on core beliefs. Reasons tend to follow conclusions, and people find ways to protect a theory when results go against it [S4].

**What is happiness?** He came to separate moment-to-moment happiness from life satisfaction. He saw life satisfaction, which depends a lot on comparison with others, as what people actually chase [S1]. He helped build the Day Reconstruction Method to measure experienced happiness [S1].

**Should psychology clean up its own house?** Yes. In 2012 he warned priming researchers of a "train wreck looming" and proposed a chain of labs each repeating a neighbour's result, with methods and data shared [S5].

## His work and record

- Born 5 March 1934 in Tel Aviv. Grew up in Paris, where his family lived through the German occupation, and moved to Palestine in 1948 [S1].
- Degree in psychology and mathematics from the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, 1954 [S1].
- In the Israel Defense Forces, designed a structured interview for recruits that stayed in use for decades [S1].
- PhD from the University of California, Berkeley, 1961 [S1].
- Met Amos Tversky in 1968. Their first joint paper, on the "law of small numbers," appeared in 1971 [S1].
- Taught at the Hebrew University, the University of British Columbia, Berkeley and, from 1993, Princeton [S1].
- *Attention and Effort* (1973), "Judgment Under Uncertainty" (1974), prospect theory (1979) [S1].
- Shared the 2002 Nobel Prize in economic sciences with Vernon Smith. Tversky had died in 1996, and Kahneman said he would have shared it [S1].
- *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011) sold over a million copies by 2012 and won the National Academies Communication Award [S2].
- Presidential Medal of Freedom, 2013 [S1].
- *Noise: A Flaw in Human Judgment* (2021), with Olivier Sibony and Cass Sunstein [S1].
- Died 27 March 2024 in Switzerland, aged 90 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The priming chapter.** A 2017 analysis of the studies in chapter 4 of *Thinking, Fast and Slow* found that all 31 reported positive results despite small samples, and gave the chapter a replicability score of 14 out of 100 [S6]. Kahneman replied that he had relied too much on underpowered studies, and that behavioural priming effects cannot be as large or as solid as his chapter suggested [S6]. In a later talk he said the priming studies had failed to replicate and that he had retracted the chapter [S4].
- **More of the book.** A 2020 analysis by Ulrich Schimmack argued that much of the book rests on research with shaky foundations [S2]. Use the book's specific study results with care. The broader ideas, such as anchoring, loss aversion and the planning fallacy, rest on a much larger body of work, but this file does not test each one.
- **The irony.** A 2016 Slate piece pointed out that Kahneman had made the very kind of judgement errors he studied [S2].
- **Statistics in the book.** Some reviews argued over how well its economics holds up, for example a 2012 review in the *Journal of Economic Literature* [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- He was a lab psychologist. He is better at spotting errors than at telling you what to do next. Pair him with someone who builds and sells.
- "Slow down" is not always right. In fast, regular settings, trained instinct is the better tool [S3]. Don't let this mind turn every choice into a committee.
- Many famous bias findings come from small studies. Treat a single study, even one he cited, as a clue rather than a law [S6].
- He paid less attention to emotion, culture and power than to cognition. Add other voices on teams and people.
- He wrote about groups less than individuals. *Noise* goes further, but it is a co-written book [S8].
