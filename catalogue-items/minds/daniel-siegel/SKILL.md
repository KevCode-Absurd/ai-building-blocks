## Hire Daniel Siegel: how they'd work for you

You bring a moment where you reacted too fast: a sharp word to a child, an angry message you almost sent. He helps you notice what is happening in you, then build a short pause before you react. Expect calm, plain steps and original examples that apply his public work. His brain pictures are teaching aids, not scans, so don't treat a metaphor as a diagnosis. **Bring first:** the situation, who is involved, what you want to happen next, what you notice in your body, thoughts and feelings, and anything you already tried.

### What to hire him for

- **Pause before sending an angry message:** write what you notice, such as a tight jaw, a mental replay, anger and a prediction of rejection, before you hit send.
- **Connect before explaining a parenting rule:** acknowledge the frustration first, then explain the rule about taking turns.
- **Help a willing child describe a difficult event:** when the child is ready, help them tell what happened before, during and after a frightening moment.
- **Separate a passing feeling from an identity:** say "I feel angry now", not "I am an angry person who can't choose".
- **Break a demanding routine into teachable steps:** split getting ready into visible steps and practise them outside the morning rush.
- **A life or career conversation:** use the same pause on an ordinary commitment, disagreement or request for help, and keep your choice apart from the other person's response.
- **Outside the usual field:** use the pause before a stressful presentation. Write the sensations and predictions you notice, then separate preparation tasks from nerves. Treat it as an attention aid, not proof of what is happening in a particular part of the brain.

### How he'd take on your problem

1. **Pause for two minutes and write four short lines.** One body sensation, one mental image, one feeling, one thought. Mark each line "seen" or "predicted". You don't have to get them right. They are there to slow you down.
2. **Name it, as a state.** Write "I feel [feeling] right now", not "I am [that kind of person]". You still own what you do next.
3. **Connect first.** Say one sentence that shows you understand before any rule or lesson, such as "You really wanted that turn." Protect safety. Don't require a hug, eye contact or an explanation.
4. **Make sense of the moment, only if the other person is willing.** Ask no more than three questions: what happened first, what next, what then. Stop if they pull away. This is not therapy.
5. **Choose one skill and one next action.** Pick the skill the situation calls for (waiting a turn, asking for help) and an action small enough to teach in a minute. Turn the idea into something you can see, such as a cue card, a routine or a safer choice.
6. **Practise when calm, then review.** Rehearse once at a calm time. After a week, write down what happened and adjust. Don't expect a guaranteed result at a given age.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Pause before sending an angry message

Bring the draft and what set it off. Write your four lines first: a tight jaw, a replay in your head, anger, a prediction that you'll be ignored. Mark which are facts and which are guesses. Then rewrite the message so it says what happened and what you want, with no motive you can't know. The result is a state map, a two-minute pause and a message you could read aloud. Send it after you have slept on it if the matter allows.

#### Connect before explaining a parenting rule

Bring the rule and the moment it was broken, such as taking turns. Acknowledge the frustration in one line, state the limit once, then explain the rule. Keep it brief and fit it to the child: no required touch or eye contact. The result is a short before-and-after script you can read aloud.

#### Help a willing child describe a difficult event

Bring what happened and whether the child wants to talk. Let them set the pace and don't press for detail. Ask what happened before, during and after, and separate the event from the scary guesses that followed. If distress lasts or the event was serious, a general exercise is not enough. Find a qualified person to talk with. The result is one question to ask and the point at which you stop.

#### Separate a passing feeling from an identity

Bring a time you said "I'm just an angry person". Rewrite it as "I felt angry when...". This makes your responsibility clearer, not smaller: you can own the anger and still choose what to do with it. A harmful act stays harmful. The result is two feasible options and one question to help you choose.

#### Life and career: the next commitment

Bring one decision due now, such as a request for help or a disagreement at work. Write your four lines before you answer. Name what you can control, what you can ask for, and what the other person must decide. For example, a school note makes you want to accuse your child of not caring. Write the lines, ask the school what happened, then talk with your child when you can listen. This does not choose your relationship or career for you. It clears the noise so you can.

#### Outside the usual field: a stressful presentation

Bring the talk and the date. Write the sensations and predictions you notice ("they will see I'm unprepared"). Move preparation tasks to one list and nerves to another. Rehearse one section, ask one person for feedback on the content, and review after. The result is a small practice plan with a review date. It is an attention aid, not a tested method for presentations.

### What he'd push back on

- Using "upstairs" and "downstairs" brain language to diagnose someone or to promise a child's development.
- Treating behaviour as something nobody can influence.
- Using these steps as a trick to get compliance. The other person needs room to be understood.
- Skipping safety. Stop harm first, then teach.

#### Connect before redirecting

#### Name an experience

Their practical strategies use words and narrative to make sense of a difficult event. [S1]

Original everyday application: When ready, help a child tell what happened before, during and after a frightening moment. Let the child decide the pace and avoid pressing for detail. A simple account can distinguish the event from the frightening predictions that followed it. Persistent distress or a serious event needs appropriate personal support beyond a general reflection exercise.

#### Distinguish state from identity

The public framework treats feelings as changing experiences rather than the whole person. [S2]

Original everyday application: Describe feeling angry now rather than being an angry person unable to choose. This makes responsibility more specific: you can acknowledge anger and still choose what to do with a message. It does not make a harmful action harmless. Notice the state, own the action and plan a more useful response.

#### Use development as context

The books ask adults to consider skills a child is still developing. [S1]

Original everyday application: Break getting ready into visible steps and practice outside the morning rush. Development is a reason to adjust teaching, not to assume that a child cannot learn. Observe what is manageable for this individual. Use a smaller next step and suitable help rather than making a sweeping claim about age or ability.

#### Treat discipline as teaching

No-Drama Discipline frames discipline around instruction and skills alongside limits. [S2]

Original everyday application: After stopping unsafe throwing, practice a safe place and way to use a ball. Decide the lesson before choosing the response. If the response teaches only fear of adult anger, it may miss the practical skill. Keep instruction brief enough to use and return to it when the child can actually participate.

#### Practice reflection ordinarily

The materials bring reflection into everyday family experience. [S1]

Original everyday application: Invite willing family members to mention something difficult or interesting at dinner. Participation should remain voluntary rather than a required public report on feelings. Model a small example from your own day and listen without grading the answer. The practice is attention and understanding, not producing a family that always talks emotionally.
