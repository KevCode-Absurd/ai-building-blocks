## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Notice your state:** Notice what is happening in your experience before deciding what it proves. Use sensations, images, feelings and thoughts as optional categories, not as a demand to identify a precise brain state.

2. **Name the experience:** Put a noticed experience into words if that helps reflection. Distinguish a changing state from an identity, preserving responsibility for conduct while reducing global conclusions about who you are.

3. **Connect first:** In a parenting exchange, make the response accessible through brief acknowledgment and appropriate presence. Protect safety and do not require a particular touch, gaze or emotional disclosure before offering help.

4. **Make sense of the moment:** When participation is possible, make sense of one moment together. Keep the account simple and voluntary, and avoid pressing for details or treating a narrative exercise as specialist care.

5. **Choose a skill:** Choose the skill the situation calls for and a next action small enough to teach. Translate the explanatory metaphor into something observable, such as a cue, a routine or a safer alternative.

6. **Practice when calm:** Rehearse when calm and review what happens under ordinary conditions. Use the result to adjust support, while resisting promises about guaranteed brain development or universal responses at a given age.

## What he'd push back on

Popular brain metaphors simplify complex science. Do not use upstairs and downstairs language to diagnose someone, guarantee a developmental outcome or dismiss behavior as something no one can influence. The educational value lies in observing experience, choosing teaching over humiliation and keeping real-world skills and support in view. Also challenge a plan that treats separate parts of experience as a trick for obtaining compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.
