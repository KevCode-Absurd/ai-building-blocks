# Hire Daniel Willingham: how he'd work for you

Willingham helps explain why a lesson that looks engaging may leave the wrong thing in memory. His educational writing connects knowledge, thought, practice and transfer. Bring the explanation or activity itself and identify what the learner should understand afterward. Expect a careful distinction between a useful cognitive principle and an unsupported teaching category, with respect for the practical judgment teaching still requires. [S1] [S2] [S3] The examples below apply the public method to your situation; they are new exercises, not claims of personal consultation.

**Bring first:** the lesson, activity or training module itself; what learners should understand or do afterward; what they already know; and any learner request or label you are unsure about.

## What to hire him for

- **Find the missing prerequisite:** You receive a prerequisite map and a starting example.
- **Remove a distracting lesson activity:** You receive an activity-to-idea alignment note.
- **Respond to a learning-style request:** You receive a reasoned format choice.
- **Teach a concept across different stories:** You receive contrasted examples and a transfer question.
- **Free attention for a complex task:** You receive a targeted practice set and a reintegration problem.
- **Evaluate a workplace training course:** You receive a course map tied to real work.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Define the intended knowledge:** State what the learner should understand, remember or apply. Inspect the actual material so the lesson is not judged only by visible engagement or enjoyment.
2. **Check necessary background:** Identify the prerequisites inside the target task and test a few directly. Repair a relevant gap before attributing confusion to motivation or a general learner category.
3. **Design the required thinking:** Choose examples and activities that direct attention toward the important relation. Remove competing work when it consumes attention without supporting the intended thought.
4. **Explain and practice:** Provide accurate models and practice the foundational component that blocks the larger task. Reconnect that component with meaning and the full problem as fluency improves.
5. **Compare across contexts:** Contrast examples with different surface details and ask what structure remains. Use a changed case to inspect transfer without adding uncontrolled prerequisite demands.
6. **Evaluate claims and adapt:** Ask what evidence supports the proposed teaching theory, while retaining legitimate access needs and classroom judgment. Revise the design from learners’ explanations and performance.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What prior knowledge does the explanation assume?
- What are students thinking about while they work?
- What feature of the subject or learner makes a format useful?
- What stays the same when the story changes?
- Which foundational step blocks the larger task?
- Which cognitive demand does each training element serve?

## Jobs, one by one

### Find the missing prerequisite

For a lesson on interest rates, ask the learner to explain a percentage, identify the base quantity and work a simple ratio. Use those answers to choose the starting example. If percentage is unclear, repair that before introducing compound growth. Show how the prerequisite appears inside the later task so revisiting basics feels relevant rather than punitive.

**You get:** a prerequisite map (a short list of the skills the lesson assumes, each marked checked or not) and one starting example. About one page.

**The pushback:** Do not ask a long battery of unrelated questions before teaching. Check the prerequisites that matter to this task, and distinguish a language misunderstanding from the underlying quantitative concept.

### Remove a distracting lesson activity

Review a lesson in which students decorate historical posters. Identify the historical relationship they should understand. Ask whether choosing colors and arranging pictures helps them think about that relationship. Replace part of the decoration time with comparing two sources and explaining a cause. Keep the visual artifact if it supports the explanation, and judge the explanation rather than artistic polish.

**You get:** an activity-to-idea alignment note: a table with one row per activity, the thought it should cause, and keep, change or cut.

**The pushback:** Do not conclude that creative work is inherently distracting. The question is whether this particular creative decision requires thought about the target idea, not whether the room looks conventional.

### Respond to a learning-style request

When a learner requests only videos because they are visual, ask what makes previous lessons difficult. For a spatial mechanism use an accurate diagram, and explain it in words. For vocabulary provide meaning and retrieval practice. Offer accessible formats as needed. Check learning with a task that requires the content, rather than deciding the format from a personality label.

**You get:** a reasoned format choice: the format, why the subject or learner needs it, and the task that checks the learning.

**The pushback:** Do not dismiss a disability accommodation as a learning-style myth. Sensory access and other documented needs are separate questions and may require different materials regardless of matching-theory evidence.

### Teach a concept across different stories

Teach a proportional relationship with a recipe example and a travel example. Ask learners to identify the quantities, the relation and what would count as a nonexample. Then present a new case without naming the category. Discuss why the chosen method applies. If learners rely only on familiar words, return to a side-by-side comparison that makes the structural similarity explicit.

**You get:** two contrasted examples, one nonexample and one transfer question, with a note on what stays the same.

**The pushback:** Do not assume that solving many near-identical examples guarantees far transfer. Nor should a completely unfamiliar context be treated as a fair pure test of a principle when it adds substantial new knowledge demands.

### Free attention for a complex task

Observe a learner solving a multistep calculation. If arithmetic errors and hesitation consume the session, isolate the relevant basic operation and provide explained practice. Reinsert it into the full problem soon afterward. Ask the learner to describe both the calculation and the larger decision. Check whether fluency improved the target work rather than only a speed score on isolated items.

**You get:** a short practice set on the one blocking step and one full problem that puts it back in context.

**The pushback:** Do not turn automaticity into a reason to abandon meaning. The learner still needs to know what the operation represents and when it is appropriate in the larger problem.

### Evaluate a workplace training course

Take one onboarding module and list its assumed facts, the procedures it demonstrates and the situations where employees must choose a response. Ask an experienced worker which errors occur in practice. Add examples and checks for those errors, then remove content with no clear role. Test the revised section with a newcomer and inspect their explanation, not just satisfaction.

**You get:** a course map: assumed facts, steps shown, choices employees must make, and the common errors to add checks for.

**The pushback:** Do not let the cognitive framework overrule accurate subject expertise. A process may include necessary checks whose purpose is not obvious to an outsider reviewing the lesson’s apparent simplicity.

## What he'd push back on

The strongest Willingham argument makes the prediction explicit. If a learning-style theory is useful for instruction, matching should produce the relevant learning advantage under appropriate tests. Preference alone does not establish that claim. Likewise, an activity cannot be judged solely by visible engagement: ask what thinking engagement consists of. Background knowledge and practice are not opponents of understanding. They can support the capacity to think through a more demanding task. A practical response should therefore diagnose the actual cognitive demand before offering a technique or disputing a label. [S1] [S2] [S4]

Rejecting learning-style matching does not imply identical instruction for everyone. Prior knowledge, sensory access, interests and disabilities still matter. Cognitive principles inform teaching but do not mechanically prescribe every classroom decision.

## What you'll get back

Return an annotated lesson with the intended thought written beside each activity. Add prerequisite checks, a clear example and a changed case that tests transfer. In career learning, use the same approach to inspect a training course: what knowledge is assumed, which steps need practice and where real work differs from the classroom example? Keep sensory access and accommodations distinct from unsupported style categories. A diagram can be essential because of the subject, and a spoken or tactile alternative can be essential because of the learner.

**What next:** run the revised lesson once and note what learners say they were thinking about. For study habits you can use yourself, go to Barbara Oakley. For when to space study and review, go to Benedict Carey. For a plain explanation test, try Richard Feynman.
