# David Allen: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that David Allen advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
David Allen approaches overload as a problem of undecided commitments. The GTD method asks people to get attention-demanding material into an external system, decide what each item means, and put reminders where they will be seen at the right moment. His system connects small physical actions to larger outcomes without making every action a calendar appointment. The useful distinction is between collecting a reminder and actually deciding what to do about it. Review keeps those decisions believable as circumstances change. [S1][S2]

## Core ideas
### Capture what has your attention
Capture gathers loose commitments before trying to rank or solve them. A collection point can hold an email, a paper, a thought or a reminder. It is an entry point rather than a final storage place. Allen makes capture the first stage because a reminder cannot be clarified reliably while it keeps vanishing back into memory. The next stages turn collected material into decisions; a growing inbox alone is incomplete GTD. [S1]

**An original application:** Suppose you have a tax document to find, a client question, a leaking tap and an idea for a course. Make one temporary inventory containing all four. Keep the client document in its secure location and capture a link or safe reminder. Do not spend the whole session repairing the tap. At the end of this pass, you should be able to look at the commitments together without rehearsing them mentally.

**Where this goes wrong:** Repeatedly moving the same unread email into new folders creates motion without a decision. A collection point needs a processing habit, not decorative categories.

### Clarify actionability
Clarifying asks whether an item requires action at all. If it does, identify a successful outcome and the next action. If it does not, choose among discarding it, keeping it as reference, or placing it on hold. This separates deciding from storing. A project title such as insurance is not itself an executable instruction, and an interesting article does not automatically become a commitment to read it. [S1]

**An original application:** A note saying renew insurance may conceal several steps. Define the outcome as coverage renewed before expiry. The first move might be locating the existing policy, rather than buying anything. A brochure attached to the note can remain reference. An optional comparison of other providers can be deferred if it is unnecessary for the current deadline. You have reduced one vague obligation into a specific action and supporting material.

**Where this goes wrong:** A to-do entry such as think about insurance can keep the decision ambiguous. Make thinking a bounded activity with a question and an expected result.

### Organize reminders for retrieval
Organizing puts reminders in categories useful for later action. GTD distinguishes projects, next actions, waiting-for items and reference. A calendar serves commitments tied to dates or times; action lists serve work that can be chosen when conditions fit. The software guide demonstrates these separations inside an ordinary task application. The logic is functional: find the reminder when you can act, instead of rereading every piece of background information. [S5]

**An original application:** For a small event, keep confirm venue as an action, event successfully held as the project, and supplier quote requested as a waiting-for item. The booked date belongs on the calendar. Store menu options as reference rather than mixing them with promises. When the supplier responds, update the waiting list and identify the next decision. A single huge checklist would obscure these different states.

**Where this goes wrong:** Invented due dates make the calendar noisy and weaken trust in genuine deadlines. Distinguish a preferred work session from a commitment that must happen then.

### Review to restore trust
The official Weekly Review has three broad phases: get clear, get current and get creative. It includes collecting loose material, processing inboxes, checking past and future calendars, updating action and waiting lists, and reviewing projects. Each active project needs a present next action. The review also revisits someday possibilities. This is maintenance of the decision system, not an instruction to complete all the work during the review itself. [S2]

**An original application:** On Friday, you notice that an old meeting note contains an unrecorded promise. Capture and clarify it. A project waiting for approval has actually received approval, so remove the waiting item and add its next action. An upcoming appointment needs a document, so record that preparation. Finally drop a course idea you no longer want. Your review has changed the system to match reality.

**Where this goes wrong:** Starting every tempting action during the review prevents the review from finishing. Capture the next move and return to examining the whole system.

### Choose action from a trusted system
Engage is the point at which the clarified and reviewed system supports a real choice of work. GTD does not end with an aesthetically tidy list. Its final stage is acting with confidence about what has been captured and decided. The five stages support one another: an action list becomes less useful when capture is incomplete, and a captured inventory becomes burdensome when no one chooses from it. [S1]

**An original application:** You have twenty minutes before leaving home. Your list shows one call requiring office hours, a document needing a laptop and an errand that fits the route. Choose with awareness of the available setting and the real commitments. If a new urgent issue arrives, capture it and decide its implications. You do not need to rebuild the entire system before taking the next useful action.

**Where this goes wrong:** The list is a support for judgment. Treating it as an automatic priority machine can make an obsolete commitment outrank a newly important responsibility.

### Keep possibilities separate from promises
The Weekly Review explicitly revisits someday or maybe material and permits moving items into active lists or deleting them. This gives creative ideas a home without silently converting them into obligations. Official workbook resources also distinguish project lists from larger focus horizons. The useful discipline is to represent a possibility honestly: an idea can be worth keeping while remaining outside the current set of promises. [S2]

**An original application:** You want a career change, a garden and a podcast, but have not agreed to launch all three this month. Put the uncommitted possibilities on a reviewable list. For the career question, choose a limited project such as comparing two roles and identify one next action. Keep the garden sketch as reference. This allows aspiration without filling every evening with imaginary deadlines.

**Where this goes wrong:** An unreviewed someday list becomes a second inbox. An overreviewed one becomes a recurring source of pressure to pursue every possible identity.

## How he decides
- **Capture what has your attention:** Gather the loose papers, messages and remembered obligations into a small number of collection points. Write the item in enough detail that tomorrow you can recognize what prompted it. Then reserve a separate pass to clarify it.
- **Clarify actionability:** For each captured item, decide whether action is required. Name the outcome if several actions are involved, then write the next visible action beginning with a concrete verb. Route nonactionable material to an appropriate destination.
- **Organize reminders for retrieval:** Put each reminder in the list that matches its role. Separate an action you can take from a result you are waiting for. Use dates for genuine dates and keep reference material available without presenting it as unfinished work.
- **Review to restore trust:** Schedule a review long enough to clear collection points and inspect calendars, actions, waiting items and projects. Confirm the next action for each live outcome. Reconsider deferred ideas after the current system is accurate.
- **Choose action from a trusted system:** Look at the real time commitments first, then select a feasible action from the relevant list. Let new information change the choice when necessary. Record any new promise or dependency created while doing the work.
- **Keep possibilities separate from promises:** Separate a current promise from an attractive possibility. Activate only the outcome you are ready to manage, with a next action. During review, retain, activate or remove deferred items according to their current relevance.

These working rules translate the ideas above into decisions a reader can inspect. Use the named sources to distinguish the author's published account from the practical examples we have written. The examples deliberately show ordinary constraints and incomplete information rather than promising that one framework resolves every situation.

## Where he stood
Allen treats personal and professional commitments as parts of one attention system. A broken household item can occupy the same mental space as an unfinished proposal. His official resources extend the basic workflow into project planning, focus horizons and recovery from backlogs. This supports using a modest set of trusted lists across life, while keeping confidential material in suitable places. It does not require putting every family detail into an employer-owned tool. [S1][S3]

## Their work and record
Getting Things Done first appeared in 2001, followed by Ready for Anything in 2003 and Making It All Work in 2008. A revised Getting Things Done appeared in 2015. The David Allen Company develops and teaches the method, and its official account describes Allen as an active authority behind its standards. The workbook resources and software setup guides show that GTD is intended to survive changes in tools. The Microsoft To Do guide illustrates the translation into an application without treating the application as the method itself. [S4][S5]

## Where he was wrong or criticised
The checked sources are largely official teaching materials and company descriptions. They explain the method but do not establish that every user will experience stress-free productivity. Our caution is practical: collecting an exhaustive inventory can become another avoidance project, and maintaining a system costs time. A trusted list also cannot resolve an employer assigning more work than a person can deliver. In that case, the inventory should support an explicit renegotiation rather than hide overload under better sorting. [S1][S2][S5]

## Blind spots to watch
GTD is strong at deciding what a commitment means and making the next move visible. It needs help when the dispute is over which commitments deserve to exist, who has power to refuse them, or what an acceptable workload is. Treat the complete list as evidence for those conversations. Avoid interpreting a long list as a moral obligation to finish everything. The weekly review includes reconsidering and deleting deferred possibilities, so retiring an obsolete project belongs within the practice. [S2]
