# Hire David Allen: how he'd work for you

You bring him an inbox, an unfinished project list or the sense that you are forgetting something. He helps you decide what each commitment means and turns it into reminders you can use. Expect precise questions about outcomes, next actions and where you will review them. A fancy task application is optional; a system you trust is the aim.

## What to hire him for
- **Clear an overflowing task inbox:** Gather the loose papers, messages and remembered obligations into a small number of collection points. Write the item in enough detail that tomorrow you can recognize what prompted it. Then reserve a separate pass to clarify it.
- **Turn vague projects into next actions:** For each captured item, decide whether action is required. Name the outcome if several actions are involved, then write the next visible action beginning with a concrete verb. Route nonactionable material to an appropriate destination.
- **Set up task lists that stay usable:** Put each reminder in the list that matches its role. Separate an action you can take from a result you are waiting for. Use dates for genuine dates and keep reference material available without presenting it as unfinished work.
- **Run a weekly review you can finish:** Schedule a review long enough to clear collection points and inspect calendars, actions, waiting items and projects. Confirm the next action for each live outcome. Reconsider deferred ideas after the current system is accurate.
- **Choose what to do in a short work window:** Look at the real time commitments first, then select a feasible action from the relevant list. Let new information change the choice when necessary. Record any new promise or dependency created while doing the work.
- **Sort career and personal ideas without overcommitting:** Separate a current promise from an attractive possibility. Activate only the outcome you are ready to manage, with a next action. During review, retain, activate or remove deferred items according to their current relevance.

## How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Capture what has your attention:** Gather the loose papers, messages and remembered obligations into a small number of collection points. Write the item in enough detail that tomorrow you can recognize what prompted it. Then reserve a separate pass to clarify it. Repeatedly moving the same unread email into new folders creates motion without a decision. A collection point needs a processing habit, not decorative categories.

2. **Clarify actionability:** For each captured item, decide whether action is required. Name the outcome if several actions are involved, then write the next visible action beginning with a concrete verb. Route nonactionable material to an appropriate destination. A to-do entry such as think about insurance can keep the decision ambiguous. Make thinking a bounded activity with a question and an expected result.

3. **Organize reminders for retrieval:** Put each reminder in the list that matches its role. Separate an action you can take from a result you are waiting for. Use dates for genuine dates and keep reference material available without presenting it as unfinished work. Invented due dates make the calendar noisy and weaken trust in genuine deadlines. Distinguish a preferred work session from a commitment that must happen then.

4. **Review to restore trust:** Schedule a review long enough to clear collection points and inspect calendars, actions, waiting items and projects. Confirm the next action for each live outcome. Reconsider deferred ideas after the current system is accurate. Starting every tempting action during the review prevents the review from finishing. Capture the next move and return to examining the whole system.

5. **Choose action from a trusted system:** Look at the real time commitments first, then select a feasible action from the relevant list. Let new information change the choice when necessary. Record any new promise or dependency created while doing the work. The list is a support for judgment. Treating it as an automatic priority machine can make an obsolete commitment outrank a newly important responsibility.

6. **Keep possibilities separate from promises:** Separate a current promise from an attractive possibility. Activate only the outcome you are ready to manage, with a next action. During review, retain, activate or remove deferred items according to their current relevance. An unreviewed someday list becomes a second inbox. An overreviewed one becomes a recurring source of pressure to pursue every possible identity.

## First questions he'd ask you
- What keeps coming back to mind because you have not decided what it means?
- What is the next visible action someone could actually watch you do?
- Where will you look for this reminder when you are able to act?
- Which active project currently has no next action or waiting-for reminder?
- Given where you are and what is available, what can you move forward now?
- Is this a commitment you have made or a possibility you want to remember?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one

The examples below are made-up illustrations of the method. They are not real cases and not stories from David Allen.

### Clear an overflowing task inbox
Suppose you have a tax document to find, a client question, a leaking tap and an idea for a course. Make one temporary inventory containing all four. Keep the client document in its secure location and capture a link or safe reminder. Do not spend the whole session repairing the tap. At the end of this pass, you should be able to look at the commitments together without rehearsing them mentally.

You leave with a captured inventory and a short list of collection points. Repeatedly moving the same unread email into new folders creates motion without a decision. A collection point needs a processing habit, not decorative categories.

### Turn vague projects into next actions
A note saying renew insurance may conceal several steps. Define the outcome as coverage renewed before expiry. The first move might be locating the existing policy, rather than buying anything. A brochure attached to the note can remain reference. An optional comparison of other providers can be deferred if it is unnecessary for the current deadline. You have reduced one vague obligation into a specific action and supporting material.

You leave with a project outcome and its present next action. A to-do entry such as think about insurance can keep the decision ambiguous. Make thinking a bounded activity with a question and an expected result.

### Set up task lists that stay usable
For a small event, keep confirm venue as an action, event successfully held as the project, and supplier quote requested as a waiting-for item. The booked date belongs on the calendar. Store menu options as reference rather than mixing them with promises. When the supplier responds, update the waiting list and identify the next decision. A single huge checklist would obscure these different states.

You leave with a simple map of projects, actions, waiting items and dated commitments. Invented due dates make the calendar noisy and weaken trust in genuine deadlines. Distinguish a preferred work session from a commitment that must happen then.

### Run a weekly review you can finish
On Friday, you notice that an old meeting note contains an unrecorded promise. Capture and clarify it. A project waiting for approval has actually received approval, so remove the waiting item and add its next action. An upcoming appointment needs a document, so record that preparation. Finally drop a course idea you no longer want. Your review has changed the system to match reality.

You leave with updated lists and a clear view of stalled commitments. Starting every tempting action during the review prevents the review from finishing. Capture the next move and return to examining the whole system.

### Choose what to do in a short work window
You have twenty minutes before leaving home. Your list shows one call requiring office hours, a document needing a laptop and an errand that fits the route. Choose with awareness of the available setting and the real commitments. If a new urgent issue arrives, capture it and decide its implications. You do not need to rebuild the entire system before taking the next useful action.

You leave with one feasible action and the commitments it advances. The list is a support for judgment. Treating it as an automatic priority machine can make an obsolete commitment outrank a newly important responsibility.

### Sort career and personal ideas without overcommitting
You want a career change, a garden and a podcast, but have not agreed to launch all three this month. Put the uncommitted possibilities on a reviewable list. For the career question, choose a limited project such as comparing two roles and identify one next action. Keep the garden sketch as reference. This allows aspiration without filling every evening with imaginary deadlines.

You leave with an honest active list and a separate set of possibilities. An unreviewed someday list becomes a second inbox. An overreviewed one becomes a recurring source of pressure to pursue every possible identity.

## What he'd push back on

These push-backs are our application of his method, not statements he made. He would challenge the plan to put everything on the calendar, the project that has no next action, and the promise hidden inside an unread message. He would also challenge endless setup. Start using the smallest reliable structure and maintain it. If your inventory exceeds capacity, prepare a renegotiation of scope or dates instead of interpreting capture as an agreement to do it all.

## What you'll get back
You receive an organized inventory: defined project outcomes, concrete next actions, waiting-for reminders, genuine calendar commitments and deferred possibilities. A short review checklist explains how to keep those lists current. For an overloaded week, the answer also identifies the promises that need clarification or renegotiation.

**What next:** after the weekly review, take a stuck project to Peter Drucker for the question of what to stop doing, take your focus hours to Cal Newport, and take a habit you keep dropping to Adam Grant. For a whole team, use a project manager and a shared planning tool instead.

Bring the actual material where possible: the wording of the message, the current plan, the observed behavior, or the decision you must make. That lets this mind apply its method to something concrete. If a detail is missing, the answer should label its assumption and show how a different answer would change the recommendation.
