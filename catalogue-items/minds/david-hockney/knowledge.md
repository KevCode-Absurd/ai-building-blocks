# David Hockney: what he knew and believed

Checked 2026-10-23. Current institutional biographies give Hockney's dates as 1937–2026. [S4][S5] Historical interviews below are read as records of his working ideas at their dates. The activities are our original adaptations, with paper, ordinary drawing materials or a device the user already owns.

## In one paragraph

David Hockney's useful practice joins sustained looking with experiments in how a picture holds space and time. The museum's transcript collection records his interest in multiple viewpoints and the different moments inside photographic joiners. [S1] His conversations with Gayford and Greig show attention to familiar surroundings and tools chosen for the marks or speed they permit. [S2][S3] The everyday invitation is to look again at a courtyard, a room, a tree or a person doing something ordinary. The subject does not have to become spectacular before it deserves a drawing. Change the act of looking, then choose whether one image, several pieces or a small sequence best shares what you noticed.

## Core ideas

### 1. A familiar place can keep revealing another picture

In the Gayford interview, Hockney follows changing blossom and light around his Normandy home. The museum transcript also records his account of understanding a local landscape through repeated springs. [S1][S2] Our adaptation chooses a nearby view that is easy to revisit: a courtyard corner, a window or a tree on an ordinary route.

Make a small drawing on several visits. Keep the viewpoint roughly stable at first so the change is easier to notice. Record a date, time and one observed difference. A shadow may shorten, a branch may become visible or a bright object may draw attention away from the wall. The note should concern what you actually saw, not a required mood of wonder.

Repetition can narrow the task productively. You no longer need to discover a location and solve its entire arrangement every session. A known place lets attention move toward a particular relation. If nothing seems different, choose a smaller question about edge, color or overlap rather than assuming the subject has been exhausted or inventing a change to fill the diary.

### 2. Looking unfolds over time

The archival South Bank Show excerpt describes Hockney's dissatisfaction with a single frozen photographic moment and his interest in pictures containing several moments. He discusses a portrait made while friends worked on a crossword. [S1] This is an artistic investigation, not a complete scientific account of human perception or photography.

An original portrait exercise asks a consenting friend to perform a simple action, such as turning a page. Make three quick drawings or photographs: attention before the turn, the hand moving and the new page settled. Arrange them as a sequence. Notice what the action makes available that a single posed view does not.

Decide whether time should be explicit or implicit. A strip shows an order directly. A collage can let several moments coexist. A single drawing can emphasize the trace of an action without claiming to be a record of every instant. Each form makes a different promise to a viewer. Label an assembled image as assembled when a viewer might otherwise mistake it for one moment.

### 3. Joining pieces is a composition decision

Hockney's museum transcripts discuss arranging camera views and deciding where one picture ends and another begins. The process is described as a kind of drawing. [S1] Our small kitchen collage begins with six photographs taken deliberately, each attending to a different relationship: the table edge, the window, the doorway or a person's place in the room.

Arrange rough copies before gluing. Ask where a viewer's eye moves and which joins create a useful relation. A slight mismatch can show the change of viewpoint. A repeated object may be intentional or may simply confuse the image. Decide which it is. Do not treat every accidental seam as an artistic discovery that must be defended.

The collage need not become a perfect panorama. Automatic stitching may solve a different task, while a carefully selected set of pieces can preserve choices about time and viewpoint. Conversely, a seamless photograph may be exactly right for showing a repair or documenting a room. Choose the form according to what you want the image to communicate, rather than treating one construction as universally superior.

### 4. Choose a tool for what it lets you do

In Greig's interview, Hockney discusses brushes and digital devices as tools, emphasizing the human making of an image. He identifies practical qualities of the tablet such as portability and the absence of drying time. [S3] Gayford's earlier conversation also connects the digital medium to working quickly with changing light. [S2]

Our tool comparison uses a subject and two methods already available. Make a small pencil drawing of a cup, then try a simple digital sketch if the user already has a suitable device. Ask what each method made easy or difficult. Pencil pressure, erasing, screen glare, undo and color access are concrete differences that can affect this task.

No purchase follows automatically. A pocket notebook may be the most available way to keep a visual diary. A phone can make a sequence without a printer. A tablet can be useful when its particular affordances suit the maker. The tool should support repeated looking and making; it cannot supply an observed experience on the user's behalf.

### 5. Keep the image connected to an actual encounter

Greig's interview records Hockney's concern with human looking and lived experience. [S3] His position about machines and images is an artistic judgment, not a rule everyone must adopt. Our practical transfer is a short observation note beside a drawing: what did you notice here, and which marks or choices in the image carry it?

If the answer is only that the picture resembles a fashionable style, return to the subject. Perhaps you noticed the chair appearing too large when close, the window cutting across a reflection or the sitter repeatedly adjusting a sleeve. Choose one relation and make it available in the image. You can work loosely or inventively while remaining clear about what prompted the choices.

This also supports an ordinary creative habit. A maker who feels out of subjects can keep a small record of encounters instead of collecting only finished images to imitate. A week of modest observations may supply useful work. It does not guarantee artistic importance, and it does not require every private experience to be turned into content for an audience.

## How to make an everyday image experiment

Choose one subject that can be revisited or one action that can be observed comfortably. Name the question: changing light, a moving hand, a room seen from several positions or the difference between two tools. Keep the first task small. One page or six images gives the work a useful edge without requiring a complete exhibition.

Make the first version and record the actual conditions. If you return later, note whether the light, position or subject changed. If you use photographs, decide whether they document one moment or assemble several. If you draw from a photograph, keep the distinction between the source and the new drawing clear. A study can be valuable without pretending to have been made directly from life.

Arrange or revise with a viewer in mind. Ask where attention enters, which relation becomes visible and where the image confuses the intended experience. Keep a seam or distortion when it serves the question. Change it when it does not. End with a small artifact and one observation worth taking into the next encounter.

## Where this lens helps

### The same courtyard after work

Make five small studies from a convenient window. Choose a different observed relation each day: a cast shadow, a reflected color, a branch against the wall, a doorway's dark shape or a change after rain. Keep the studies together. The output is a visual diary of one ordinary place, with no need to travel or purchase a new medium.

### A room as a personal experience

Photograph a few features of a familiar kitchen from slightly different positions. Use your own images and keep private information out of the frame. Arrange rough copies or digital tiles so a viewer can follow the room through the chosen relations. Label it as a composite. A useful result may remain visibly pieced together.

### A friend reading

Ask whether the friend is comfortable being drawn or photographed and how the result may be shared. Observe one small action over a short period. Choose a sequence or an assembled portrait depending on the intended experience. A more complex picture does not require a more intimate disclosure about the sitter.

### A creative practice with limited time

Use ten minutes and a subject already nearby. Keep a notebook or device ready enough that beginning does not consume the whole session. Write one sentence about what the image helped you notice. This is a small way to keep looking in your daily life, not a demand to leave work, acquire a studio or measure a hobby by public attention.

## Blind spots to watch

This approach can be criticised when a provocative claim about cameras is mistaken for settled vision science. A photograph, drawing, video or collage can each serve different purposes. Hockney's critique is part of his artistic investigation. [S1][S3] Use it to ask a productive question about an image rather than rank every medium on one scale.

Repeated looking also has practical limits. A person can work from a seated position, a single window or a photograph when that suits them. A subject may be unavailable, private or uncomfortable to observe. Choose another one. No ordinary creative task requires trespass, prolonged exposure to unpleasant conditions or another person's participation without agreement.
