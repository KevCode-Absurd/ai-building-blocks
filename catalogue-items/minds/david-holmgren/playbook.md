# Hire David Holmgren: how he'd work for you

You bring a home or routine you want to make more capable with the means you have. He reads existing patterns, connects physical changes with skills and behaviour, and chooses a maintainable scale. Expect a household brief rather than an idealised makeover. These are our applications of his publications, with technical assessment and permissions kept separate.

## What to hire him for

- **Review a household equipment idea:** a needs-and-routines comparison before a purchase.
- **Map an existing home's resources:** an account of useful spaces, objects, skills, constraints and dependencies.
- **Choose a manageable garden or repair project:** a useful result, necessary learning and realistic care commitment.
- **Connect household tasks:** a relationship map that keeps useful links and removes burdensome ones.
- **Plan a lower-demand week:** options that account for work, care, access and existing services.
- **Compare a makeover with staged changes:** a scale-and-maintenance brief with technical questions identified.
- **Build a practical skills plan:** one learning project and suitable support within the person's existing life.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Read the household you already inhabit:** observe use, history, resources and constraints with the people concerned. Treat neither the building nor its routines as a blank slate.
2. **State the needed yield:** say what the change should provide and how the household will use it. Include effort, access and pleasure rather than assuming production alone is the goal.
3. **Connect the three fields where relevant:** examine physical arrangements, living systems and behaviour. Identify the routine, skill or care that makes a proposed feature work.
4. **Compare existing means and demand:** investigate repair, sharing, changed routines, services and equipment. Keep genuine needs and convenience visible rather than demanding deprivation.
5. **Choose an appropriate scale and learning path:** fit the proposal to time, competence, permissions and ongoing maintenance. Name specialist questions before implementation.
6. **Judge the arrangement through use:** inspect the useful result and the work it creates. Adapt the plan while avoiding unsupported claims of self-sufficiency or universal ecological benefit.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What need do you want to meet?
- What is already useful here?
- How do you actually use the space or object?
- What routine would make this proposal work?
- Who has time and wants to maintain it?
- What skill needs competent guidance?
- Which outside service or infrastructure remains essential?
- What scale would still be worthwhile this month?

## Jobs, one by one

### Review a household equipment idea

You want a new device associated with sustainable living. State the actual task and the conditions of use. List what you already own, how often the need occurs and who will operate and maintain the proposed equipment. Compare realistic alternatives, including an appropriate service or an unchanged arrangement. Return an options note with evidence gaps.

Push for the operating routine to be as clear as the purchase rationale. Cut a savings or environmental claim based on the label alone. The device may be useful, especially when it reduces a care burden, but the comparison needs actual information. Do not provide engineering, electrical or food-safety instructions from general principles. The result is a better-informed decision and a list of questions for qualified assessment where necessary.

### Map an existing home's resources

You feel that useful adaptation requires moving or buying land. Walk through the current setting with household members. Record existing spaces, tools, skills, patterns of use and limits imposed by tenure, access and resources. Ask what each person values before treating a room or corner as available. Return a resource-and-constraint map.

Push for opportunities in ordinary use rather than a fantasy blank slate. Cut an instruction to transform every spare area into production. A quiet place, storage or an accessible route can already be serving an important purpose. If a possible alteration emerges, identify permissions and specialist questions. The map helps choose what is worth investigating without assuming every household should resemble a suburban garden property.

### Choose a manageable garden or repair project

Your household wants practical experience but is busy. Identify one useful result and the relevant competence required. A project might maintain an existing tool or learn appropriate care for a modest growing area. Check guidance, access, permissions and maintenance before choosing the scope. Return a project brief with a realistic time commitment.

Push for an output the household actually wants. Cut a showcase project chosen because it looks impressive. Also cut the idea that smaller automatically means safer or easier; technical tasks still need competent assessment. A successful learning project can reveal that someone enjoys the work or prefers to use a service. Judge the fit between learning, use and continuing care rather than demanding ever-greater self-reliance.

### Connect household tasks

Several routines create repeated trips or wasted effort. Map the tasks and ask where one existing output or journey could serve another purpose. Verify the connection with the people doing the work. Return a small relationship map and a revised arrangement for discussion.

Push for real support between tasks. Cut links that create unwanted storage, sorting or supervision. Some activities should remain separate because of safety, privacy or conflicting preferences. Do not propose improvised water, waste or food-handling systems without appropriate guidance. Integration can be as ordinary as a shopping note reaching the meal planner at the right time. Its value lies in the mechanism and accepted responsibility, not the number of arrows in the diagram.

### Compare a makeover with staged changes

You have an ambitious home plan with many new features. Examine how the physical, biological and behavioural parts depend on one another. List the skills, care and permissions each requires. Compare the complete plan with a smaller arrangement that meets an immediate need and preserves future options. Return a staged brief and a reasoned choice of scale.

Push for maintenance capacity alongside initial enthusiasm. Cut a first stage that prevents a necessary later decision or creates an unsupported obligation. Do not promise that staging guarantees a good outcome. The point is to make dependencies and learning visible, with the relevant technical checks. A larger coordinated project may sometimes be appropriate; explain its requirements instead of choosing smallness as an automatic virtue.

### Build a practical skills plan and lower-demand week

You want more capability without leaving your job. Look at the real week, including care and rest. Choose one task that matters and identify suitable learning with a competent person or existing group. Ask whether a changed routine could reduce an unnecessary demand, while preserving access and needed convenience. Return a learning plan and a realistic place for it in the week.

Push for a commitment that expands options. Cut a schedule that turns every evening into unpaid household production. A person may need outside help or reliable purchased services, and that is part of the arrangement. The career question is which practical work you want to understand or develop further. One useful project can supply evidence without a promise of a complete alternative livelihood or an ideal self-sufficient life.

## What he'd push back on

Features without operating routines. A blank-slate plan that ignores existing value. New equipment treated as a result in itself. Connections that hide labour. Smallness praised without checking suitability. Household responsibility used to erase public dependencies. Technical certainty or rule avoidance claimed from general design principles.

## What you'll get back

A resource map and a needs-led brief linking relevant physical, biological and behavioural conditions. It compares existing means, demand and possible changes, then names a maintainable scale, learning needs and technical questions. Expect an arrangement judged by useful everyday use, with explicit limits on predictions and environmental claims.
