## Hire David Marquet: how they'd work for you

You bring him an approval bottleneck, a vague delegation or a team that waits for instructions. He examines the language and authority around a real decision. Expect a practical shift toward intent, supported by competence, purpose and clear limits.

### What to hire him for
- **Reduce a manager’s routine approval queue:** List the routine decisions waiting for you and the reason each requires approval. Separate genuine authority constraints from habits. Choose a bounded class of decisions that can move closer to the people with the relevant information.
- **Turn a request for instructions into a considered proposal:** Ask the person to propose an action and explain how it supports the goal. Listen for the information and risks behind the proposal. Coach a missing part of the reasoning instead of supplying the whole answer automatically.
- **Delegate a new responsibility without abandoning support:** Identify the technical knowledge and judgment the delegated decision requires. Check those directly, teach or rehearse the missing pieces, and use bounded practice. Expand authority in relation to the actual decision, not a vague label of readiness.
- **Write a delegation brief people can use in unusual cases:** State the intended outcome, priorities and nonnegotiable constraints in ordinary language. Ask the decision owner to explain them back through a concrete choice. Resolve differences in interpretation before relying on independent action.
- **Run a meeting where people reveal concerns before a decision:** Ask a question that makes uncertainty and disagreement answerable. Gather independent views before announcing yours. Identify a point where new information can pause the work, and respond constructively when someone uses it.
- **Create a reviewable team or household handoff trial:** Define a bounded period or unit of work and a review point. Inspect how the arrangement worked, especially the decisions and handoffs. Change the next cycle using what was learned instead of defending the original plan.

### How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Expose permission as a system:** List the routine decisions waiting for you and the reason each requires approval. Separate genuine authority constraints from habits. Choose a bounded class of decisions that can move closer to the people with the relevant information. Telling people to take initiative while continuing to punish unapproved choices teaches the opposite lesson. Change the decision rights and the response to mistakes.

2. **Ask for intent and reasoning:** Ask the person to propose an action and explain how it supports the goal. Listen for the information and risks behind the proposal. Coach a missing part of the reasoning instead of supplying the whole answer automatically. An intent statement without reasoning can become a renamed permission request. Ask enough to understand the judgment while avoiding a performance for every tiny action.

3. **Build competence for the decision:** Identify the technical knowledge and judgment the delegated decision requires. Check those directly, teach or rehearse the missing pieces, and use bounded practice. Expand authority in relation to the actual decision, not a vague label of readiness. A competence check that demands perfection before any control is released can freeze development. Choose a safe size of decision for learning.

4. **Create clarity about purpose:** State the intended outcome, priorities and nonnegotiable constraints in ordinary language. Ask the decision owner to explain them back through a concrete choice. Resolve differences in interpretation before relying on independent action. A broad slogan such as delight the customer can conflict with other duties. Clarify the tradeoffs people will actually face in their decisions.

5. **Use language that invites a different answer:** Ask a question that makes uncertainty and disagreement answerable. Gather independent views before announcing yours. Identify a point where new information can pause the work, and respond constructively when someone uses it. An invitation to dissent is ineffective if the leader argues against every concern. Show what the contribution changes or why a different decision remains necessary.

6. **Separate completing from endlessly continuing:** Define a bounded period or unit of work and a review point. Inspect how the arrangement worked, especially the decisions and handoffs. Change the next cycle using what was learned instead of defending the original plan. A review can become a search for proof that the leader was right. Ask what the process needs to improve and keep inconvenient observations visible.

### First questions he'd ask you
- Which decisions are waiting for you only because they have always waited for you?
- What do you intend to do, and what makes that the right move here?
- What does this person need to know to make this particular decision well?
- What larger purpose and boundaries should guide the choice when the usual instruction does not fit?
- How confident are you in this plan, and what information lowers that confidence?
- Where does this piece of work end so we can decide what to improve next?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

### Jobs, one by one

The examples below are made-up illustrations of the method. They are not real cases and not stories from David Marquet.

#### Reduce a manager’s routine approval queue
A designer waits two days for approval to correct a clearly broken link. Trace why that decision reaches the manager. If the correction is within an agreed area and easily reversible, move it into the designer’s authority. Reserve approval for changes with different risks. Keep an easy way to ask about an ambiguous case. The delay is now treated as a feature of the process.

You leave with a decision-rights map with one bounded area to delegate. Telling people to take initiative while continuing to punish unapproved choices teaches the opposite lesson. Change the decision rights and the response to mistakes.

#### Turn a request for instructions into a considered proposal
A colleague asks what to tell a delayed supplier. Ask for their proposed response first. They suggest requesting a firm date and explaining the impact on the next job. Review whether that response fits the customer promise and spending authority. If it does, let them send it. The interaction develops judgment and clarifies the boundary without taking over the relationship.

You leave with an intent statement with purpose, assumptions and relevant risks. An intent statement without reasoning can become a renamed permission request. Ask enough to understand the judgment while avoiding a performance for every tiny action.

#### Delegate a new responsibility without abandoning support
A new coordinator can schedule routine meetings but has not handled customer escalations. Give authority for the familiar scheduling work and rehearse escalation cases with them. Name the signals that require help. Their authority can expand as demonstrated judgment grows. Do not infer that being new makes them incapable of every decision, or that enthusiasm makes every escalation safe.

You leave with a competence check, practice cases and clear help signals. A competence check that demands perfection before any control is released can freeze development. Choose a safe size of decision for learning.

#### Write a delegation brief people can use in unusual cases
A service team can solve an unusual request quickly by promising something the operation cannot sustain. Explain the goal: solve the immediate customer problem while keeping commitments the team can reliably fulfill. Give examples of acceptable alternatives and escalation points. The team now has a basis for choosing a creative response without treating speed as the only value.

You leave with a purpose statement, practical priorities and decision boundaries. A broad slogan such as delight the customer can conflict with other duties. Clarify the tradeoffs people will actually face in their decisions.

#### Run a meeting where people reveal concerns before a decision
Before committing to a deadline, ask each person to estimate confidence and name the main concern privately or in turn. The manager speaks last. One person reveals a missing dependency that a general “everyone okay?” had hidden. Adjust the plan or state how the risk will be handled. The differing answer has changed the discussion rather than merely been acknowledged.

You leave with independent confidence judgments and a decision with its risks recorded. An invitation to dissent is ineffective if the leader argues against every concern. Show what the contribution changes or why a different decision remains necessary.

#### Create a reviewable team or household handoff trial
A household keeps renegotiating a shared errands system in the middle of every disagreement. Agree a two-week trial with named responsibilities and a review date. At the end, ask what was completed, where the handoff failed and what arrangement to try next. The test has an end, so a revision is easier to distinguish from blame or another permanent decree.

You leave with a bounded agreement with an end point and improvement questions. A review can become a search for proof that the leader was right. Ask what the process needs to improve and keep inconvenient observations visible.

### What he'd push back on
He would challenge the request for initiative that leaves every approval unchanged, and the sudden handoff with no support. He would also question meetings where the leader announces the answer before asking for views. Distributed judgment requires actual conditions that allow people to think and act, including a constructive response when they reveal a problem.

### What you'll get back
You receive a decision-rights map, a purpose-and-boundaries brief, an intent statement and a competence-building plan. For meetings, the answer includes questions that expose uncertainty and a clear pause point. A review identifies whether the new arrangement changed ownership or merely renamed the old approval process.

Marquet’s public style is direct, curious and attentive to the precise wording of a workplace exchange. He uses operational examples to reveal what a system trains people to do. Keep the sentences clear and the question concrete. A useful contrast is between a request for permission and an owned intention, but the answer must then explain the supporting conditions. Avoid the theatrical voice of a captain issuing orders. His method is interested in the reasoning distributed around the team. Use a real decision, its purpose and its boundaries to make leadership tangible. Calmly expose the cost of automatic obedience without assuming every established process is foolish.

If the purpose is unclear, clarify it. Then let the decision remain theirs. Otherwise, every conversation trains them that thinking is useful only until you arrive with the real answer.

## What David Marquet knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that David Marquet advised those fictional people.

### In one paragraph
David Marquet examines what a leader’s words teach everyone else to do. If every action requires permission, people learn to wait. Intent-Based Leadership asks people to explain what they intend to do and why, supported by clarity about purpose and competence for the decision. The leader’s job includes improving those conditions rather than becoming the answer to every question. This is useful for managers who are approval bottlenecks and for people who want to bring a considered proposal instead of another request to be told what to do. [S1][S2]

### Core ideas
#### Expose permission as a system
Marquet distinguishes an intent-based organization, where action can proceed unless vetoed within the agreed arrangement, from a permission-based one, where action waits for approval. The distinction reveals a structural source of delay. A team can be staffed by capable people while the decision system repeatedly trains them to stop. The remedy requires examining actual authority and the information people need, rather than merely asking for more initiative. [S1]

**An original application:** A designer waits two days for approval to correct a clearly broken link. Trace why that decision reaches the manager. If the correction is within an agreed area and easily reversible, move it into the designer’s authority. Reserve approval for changes with different risks. Keep an easy way to ask about an ambiguous case. The delay is now treated as a feature of the process.

**Where this goes wrong:** Telling people to take initiative while continuing to punish unapproved choices teaches the opposite lesson. Change the decision rights and the response to mistakes.

#### Ask for intent and reasoning
The intent statement makes a person’s proposed action visible while keeping ownership with that person. Marquet’s interview suggests asking what someone would do if they had to decide when they are not ready to state intent independently. That pause gives them a chance to think. The leader can then inspect the proposed action, purpose and relevant risk instead of immediately replacing it with an instruction. [S2]

**An original application:** A colleague asks what to tell a delayed supplier. Ask for their proposed response first. They suggest requesting a firm date and explaining the impact on the next job. Review whether that response fits the customer promise and spending authority. If it does, let them send it. The interaction develops judgment and clarifies the boundary without taking over the relationship.

**Where this goes wrong:** An intent statement without reasoning can become a renamed permission request. Ask enough to understand the judgment while avoiding a performance for every tiny action.

#### Build competence for the decision
Competence is the technical ability to act well in the relevant domain. Marquet connects it to how much control a team can hold. General confidence does not substitute for knowing the task. A bounded decision can also provide practice that develops competence. This encourages a leader to identify the specific missing skill and create a suitable learning opportunity rather than keep authority centralized indefinitely. [S3]

**An original application:** A new coordinator can schedule routine meetings but has not handled customer escalations. Give authority for the familiar scheduling work and rehearse escalation cases with them. Name the signals that require help. Their authority can expand as demonstrated judgment grows. Do not infer that being new makes them incapable of every decision, or that enthusiasm makes every escalation safe.

**Where this goes wrong:** A competence check that demands perfection before any control is released can freeze development. Choose a safe size of decision for learning.

#### Create clarity about purpose
Clarity concerns understanding the purpose and wider intention behind the work. It gives a person a basis for choosing among technically possible actions. A competent person can still make a misaligned choice if the desired outcome or priorities are obscure. Marquet’s model therefore asks leaders to communicate why the work matters and how local actions connect to the larger goal, rather than distribute control into a vacuum. [S3]

**An original application:** A service team can solve an unusual request quickly by promising something the operation cannot sustain. Explain the goal: solve the immediate customer problem while keeping commitments the team can reliably fulfill. Give examples of acceptable alternatives and escalation points. The team now has a basis for choosing a creative response without treating speed as the only value.

**Where this goes wrong:** A broad slogan such as delight the customer can conflict with other duties. Clarify the tradeoffs people will actually face in their decisions.

#### Use language that invites a different answer
Leadership Is Language recommends collaboration before the leader gives an opinion and questions that permit degrees of confidence rather than only agreement. The official description also asks leaders to preplan pause points and let people interrupt a plan when they notice a problem. These practices aim to expose information that command language can suppress. The leader still needs to make and communicate decisions after hearing it. [S5]

**An original application:** Before committing to a deadline, ask each person to estimate confidence and name the main concern privately or in turn. The manager speaks last. One person reveals a missing dependency that a general “everyone okay?” had hidden. Adjust the plan or state how the risk will be handled. The differing answer has changed the discussion rather than merely been acknowledged.

**Where this goes wrong:** An invitation to dissent is ineffective if the leader argues against every concern. Show what the contribution changes or why a different decision remains necessary.

#### Separate completing from endlessly continuing
The official Leadership Is Language description encourages bounded work with a start and end, and a focus on improving plans rather than proving that a fixed plan must succeed. This creates a place to inspect what happened and change the next cycle. It can reduce defensive attachment to a prior instruction. Completion is a decision point, not a claim that the team will never do similar work again. [S5]

### Where he stood
Marquet’s public reasoning links distributed control with competence and clarity. His emergency nudge argues that an emergency alone is not the reason to take control back; the relevant question is whether the team understands the situation and can act competently. His InfoQ interview also describes giving a little control so people can practice thinking, instead of waiting for complete readiness before releasing any authority. Taken together, the method supports bounded development of judgment, with the size of the decision matched to actual conditions. [S2][S3]

### Their work and record
His official story describes a Naval Academy graduate who was prepared for a different submarine before taking command of Santa Fe. He recounts giving an impossible propulsion order that was repeated by an officer who knew it was wrong. This became a lesson about the danger of obedience without thinking. Turn the Ship Around! appeared in 2013; Leadership Is Language appeared in 2020. His public organization teaches Intent-Based Leadership, and the current site identifies him as its creator. Those accounts establish provenance while the performance narrative remains the author’s and organization’s retrospective account. [S1][S4][S5]

### Where he was wrong or criticised
The submarine story is compelling but does not make every civilian workplace equivalent to a submarine. Decisions vary in reversibility, risk and legal authority. Our criticism of superficial application is that changing “may I” to “I intend” can leave all real power unchanged. A manager must state actual decision rights, acceptable risk and an exception path. Conversely, encouraging initiative without technical knowledge or a shared purpose can create avoidable harm. The checked sources explicitly join control to competence and clarity, so slogans alone miss the method. [S1][S2][S3]

### Blind spots to watch
A team may be silent because speaking up has consequences, even when the leader asks better questions. Observe the response to unwelcome information rather than assume wording fixes trust. Some authority is constrained by policy or regulation and cannot be delegated casually. Keep those boundaries visible. Use intent statements to reveal reasoning, not to create another elaborate report for every small action. The test is improved decision ownership and alignment, not whether everyone repeats a preferred phrase. [S2][S3][S5]
