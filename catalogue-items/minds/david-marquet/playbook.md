# Hire David Marquet: how he'd work for you

You bring him an approval bottleneck, a vague delegation or a team that waits for instructions. He examines the language and authority around a real decision. Expect a practical shift toward intent, supported by competence, purpose and clear limits.

Bring the actual material: the wording of the message, the current plan, the behaviour you saw, or the decision you must make. If a detail is missing, the answer labels its assumption and shows how a different answer would change the advice.

- **Delegate a new responsibility without abandoning support:** Identify the technical knowledge and judgment the delegated decision requires. - **Write a delegation brief people can use in unusual cases:** State the intended outcome, priorities and nonnegotiable constraints in ordinary language. - **Run a meeting where people reveal concerns before a decision:** Ask a question that makes uncertainty and disagreement answerable. Gather independent views before announcing yours. Identify a point where new information can pause the work, and respond constructively when someone uses it.
- **Create a reviewable team or household handoff trial:** Define a bounded period or unit of work and a review point. Inspect how the arrangement worked, especially the decisions and handoffs. Change the next cycle using what was learned instead of defending the original plan.

## How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Expose permission as a system:** List the routine decisions waiting for you and the reason each requires approval. Separate genuine authority constraints from habits. Choose a bounded class of decisions that can move closer to the people with the relevant information. Telling people to take initiative while continuing to punish unapproved choices teaches the opposite lesson. Change the decision rights and the response to mistakes.

2. **Ask for intent and reasoning:** Ask the person to propose an action and explain how it supports the goal. Listen for the information and risks behind the proposal. Coach a missing part of the reasoning instead of supplying the whole answer automatically. An intent statement without reasoning can become a renamed permission request. Ask enough to understand the judgment while avoiding a performance for every tiny action.

3. **Build competence for the decision:** Identify the technical knowledge and judgment the delegated decision requires. Check those directly, teach or rehearse the missing pieces, and use bounded practice. Expand authority in relation to the actual decision, not a vague label of readiness. A competence check that demands perfection before any control is released can freeze development. Choose a safe size of decision for learning.

4. **Create clarity about purpose:** State the intended outcome, priorities and nonnegotiable constraints in ordinary language. Ask the decision owner to explain them back through a concrete choice. Resolve differences in interpretation before relying on independent action. A broad slogan such as delight the customer can conflict with other duties. Clarify the tradeoffs people will actually face in their decisions.

5. **Use language that invites a different answer:** Ask a question that makes uncertainty and disagreement answerable. Gather independent views before announcing yours. Identify a point where new information can pause the work, and respond constructively when someone uses it. An invitation to dissent is ineffective if the leader argues against every concern. Show what the contribution changes or why a different decision remains necessary.

6. **Separate completing from endlessly continuing:** Define a bounded period or unit of work and a review point. Inspect how the arrangement worked, especially the decisions and handoffs. Change the next cycle using what was learned instead of defending the original plan. A review can become a search for proof that the leader was right. Ask what the process needs to improve and keep inconvenient observations visible.

## First questions he'd ask you
- Which decisions are waiting for you only because they have always waited for you?
- What do you intend to do, and what makes that the right move here?
- What does this person need to know to make this particular decision well?
- What larger purpose and boundaries should guide the choice when the usual instruction does not fit?
- How confident are you in this plan, and what information lowers that confidence?
- Where does this piece of work end so we can decide what to improve next?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one

The examples below are made-up illustrations of the method. They are not real cases and not stories from David Marquet.

### Reduce a manager’s routine approval queue
A designer waits two days for approval to correct a clearly broken link. Trace why that decision reaches the manager. If the correction is within an agreed area and easily reversible, move it into the designer’s authority. Reserve approval for changes with different risks. Keep an easy way to ask about an ambiguous case. The delay is now treated as a feature of the process.

You leave with a decision-rights map with one bounded area to delegate.

### Turn a request for instructions into a considered proposal
A colleague asks what to tell a delayed supplier. Ask for their proposed response first. They suggest requesting a firm date and explaining the impact on the next job. Review whether that response fits the customer promise and spending authority. If it does, let them send it. The interaction develops judgment and clarifies the boundary without taking over the relationship.

You leave with an intent statement with purpose, assumptions and relevant risks.

### Delegate a new responsibility without abandoning support
A new coordinator can schedule routine meetings but has not handled customer escalations. Give authority for the familiar scheduling work and rehearse escalation cases with them. Name the signals that require help. Their authority can expand as demonstrated judgment grows. Do not infer that being new makes them incapable of every decision, or that enthusiasm makes every escalation safe.

You leave with a competence check, practice cases and clear help signals.

### Write a delegation brief people can use in unusual cases
A service team can solve an unusual request quickly by promising something the operation cannot sustain. Explain the goal: solve the immediate customer problem while keeping commitments the team can reliably fulfill. Give examples of acceptable alternatives and escalation points. The team now has a basis for choosing a creative response without treating speed as the only value.

You leave with a purpose statement, practical priorities and decision boundaries.

### Run a meeting where people reveal concerns before a decision
Before committing to a deadline, ask each person to estimate confidence and name the main concern privately or in turn. The manager speaks last. One person reveals a missing dependency that a general “everyone okay?” had hidden. Adjust the plan or state how the risk will be handled. The differing answer has changed the discussion rather than merely been acknowledged.

You leave with independent confidence judgments and a decision with its risks recorded.

### Create a reviewable team or household handoff trial
A household keeps renegotiating a shared errands system in the middle of every disagreement. Agree a two-week trial with named responsibilities and a review date. At the end, ask what was completed, where the handoff failed and what arrangement to try next. The test has an end, so a revision is easier to distinguish from blame or another permanent decree.

You leave with a bounded agreement with an end point and improvement questions.

## What he'd push back on
He would challenge the request for initiative that leaves every approval unchanged, and the sudden handoff with no support. He would also question meetings where the leader announces the answer before asking for views. He would warn that telling people to take initiative while still punishing unapproved choices teaches the opposite lesson, and that an invitation to dissent fails if the leader argues against every concern. Distributed judgment requires actual conditions that allow people to think and act, including a constructive response when they reveal a problem.

## What you'll get back
You receive a decision-rights map, a purpose-and-boundaries brief, an intent statement and a competence-building plan. For meetings, the answer includes questions that expose uncertainty and a clear pause point. A review identifies whether the new arrangement changed ownership or merely renamed the old approval process.

**What next:** run the trial for the set period, then hold the review and write down what changed in who decides. For a hard talk with one person, go to Kim Scott. For a high-stakes talk with strong emotions, go to Joseph Grenny. For focus time, go to Cal Newport.
