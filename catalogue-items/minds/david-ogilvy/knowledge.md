## In one paragraph

David Ogilvy was a British-born salesman who became one of the best-known advertising men of the twentieth century. He sold Aga cookers door to door, wrote a sales manual for them at 24, learned research under George Gallup, and in 1948 opened the New York agency that became Ogilvy & Mather [S1][S2][S8]. He believed an ad has one job, which is to sell; his short form was "We sell, or else" [S4][S6]. To do it, he trusted research, facts and testing over hunches [S2][S4]. From 1955 on he also argued that every ad should add to a brand's long-term personality, and that chopping and changing a brand's image wastes money [S3]. He wrote plainly, demanded plain writing from his staff, and treated readers as intelligent adults [S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### Advertising is there to sell

Ogilvy judged an ad by whether it moved the product. A campaign that entertained but didn't lift sales had failed [S6]. He held that a TV commercial is there to sell, not to amuse, and cited research showing no link between liking a commercial and being sold by it. He still wanted ads to be human and friendly [S4]. He warned against writing to win awards, because ads that sell rarely draw attention to themselves. He put it as a choice between two Greek speakers: one made people say "how well he speaks," the other made them march [S4].

How his view changed: at 25, in a 1936 talk to his London agency, he said people do not buy from clowns and that facetious ads were for amateurs [S8]. Later he allowed warmth and wit, as long as they served the sale [S4].

### Research and testing beat instinct

His years with Gallup shaped his trust in careful research [S1][S2]. He asked why a client should bet a company's future on a writer's instinct, when you'd want a surgeon who had studied anatomy [S4]. He called TEST the most important word in advertising: test the promise, the media, the headline, the picture, the size, how often and how much [S4].

He was not a slave to data. In 1955 he warned that some executives used research for support rather than for insight [S3]. He called his agency's research findings, the "Magic Lanterns," reports on how people respond, not rules. Genius was still needed [S4].

How he applied it: in his own copywriting he wrote about 20 headlines for each ad and sometimes had the research department run split tests [S7].

### Find the big idea, and keep it simple

An ad without a big idea passes like a ship in the night, and big ideas are usually simple [S4]. His advice was to boil a strategy down to one simple promise and deliver it with full force [S4]. He called positioning the most important decision, but warned that good positioning can't save a dull, badly made ad. If nobody reads it, the positioning doesn't matter [S4].

### Every ad builds the brand image

In a 1955 talk to the American Association of Advertising Agencies, he argued that every ad should be treated as part of a long-term investment in the brand's personality [S3]. He said he had borrowed the idea from Burleigh Gardner and Sidney Levy [S3].

How his view changed: he admitted that when he arrived in America he had believed, like the mail-order school of Claude Hopkins, that every ad must pay for itself at once. He used to accuse brand-builders of hiding behind "long-term effect" [S3]. By 1955 he called that view short-sighted: mail-order men knew how to sell once, but brands must sell year after year. Advertisers who only chase quick sales, he said, are spending money when they should be investing it [S3].

### Pick a personality and stick to it

Brands fail when they try to be everything to everyone, male and female, upper-class and mass-market. He said they end up a "wishy-washy neuter," and no capon ever rules the roost [S3][S4]. He thought it takes nerve to keep one style while everyone pushes for "something new" every six months, and he pointed to Winston Churchill wearing the same hats and ties for fifty years [S3]. When products are nearly the same, he argued, the brand's personality decides more than small product differences do [S3]. He also warned against spending so much on coupons and deals that nothing is left to build the brand [S3].

### Respect the reader and tell the truth

The consumer is not a fool and should not be insulted with a slogan and a few empty adjectives. People want all the facts you can give them [S4]. You can't bore people into buying, only interest them [S4]. His family test: never write an ad you wouldn't want your own family to read [S4]. If you lie about a product, either the government or the customer will catch you, and the customer simply won't buy again [S4]. This was there from the start. His 1936 talk said concrete figures must replace vague claims and that superlatives make readers doubt every claim [S8].

### Make the ad look like something worth reading

The less an ad looks like an ad, and the more it looks like an article, the more people stop and read it. So he told designers to copy magazine editors and avoid the habits of ad designers [S4]. He put the photo above the headline, because readers look at the picture first and then scan down [S4]. He judged that what an ad says decides whether people buy, much more than its typeface. He made fun of the idea that a shopper would reject a soap because of the font [S4].

For television, he followed Gallup's finding that viewers forget what they hear but don't see. His test was to run a commercial with the sound off [S4]. To sell a fire extinguisher, open with the fire [S4].

### Write plainly, then edit hard

On 7 September 1982 he sent a memo on writing to everyone at his agency [S5]. It opens with his belief that clear writing comes from clear thinking, and that good writing can be learned [S5]. His ten tips, in brief: read a good book on writing three times; write the way you talk; use short words, sentences and paragraphs; never use jargon; keep to two pages; check your quotations; never send a memo the day you write it, but read it aloud the next morning; ask a colleague to improve anything important; make sure the reader knows what you want; and if you want action, go and tell the person [S5].

He practised it on himself. In 1955 he described his own method as slow: write at home, study every rival ad of the past 20 years, list every fact, write 20 headlines, throw away early drafts, then edit four or five times. He called himself a poor copywriter but a good editor [S7]. He also told of using "obsolete" in a headline and finding a third of women surveyed didn't know the word [S4].

### Hire well and teach always

He wanted people better than himself, saying a firm where everyone hires bigger people becomes a company of giants [S4]. He thought talent hides among rebels and misfits [S4]. He said his agency did two things: look after clients and teach young advertising people [S4]. As a manager he told bosses to visit staff in their offices rather than summon them, to fire people who couldn't do the job, and to keep scraping barnacles off the ship by cutting jobs that weren't needed [S4].

## How he decides

- Does it sell? If not, it fails, however clever it is [S4][S6].
- What does the research say? Test before you commit the full budget [S4].
- Is there one big, simple idea? [S4]
- Does this ad fit the brand's long-term personality? [S3]
- Would a busy reader understand it, and do they get real facts? [S4][S8]
- Would I be happy for my family to read it? [S4]
- Is the layout built for how people actually read? [S4]
- Admit mistakes early, before anyone else points them out [S4].

## Real decisions

### Writing the Aga sales manual

In 1935, while selling Aga cookers door to door in Scotland, he was asked to write down how he sold them [S1][S8]. The guide told salesmen to research each prospect before calling, never to get in on false pretences, to fit the pitch to the listener and never give the same talk to a bishop and a trapeze artist, and to answer each objection directly [S8]. He also said a dull salesman is the worst kind, and that if you can't make a prospect laugh you can't make her buy [S8]. His brother showed it to the London agency Mather & Crowther, which hired him [S1]. Fortune later called it probably the best sales manual ever written [S8].

### Going to work for Gallup

In 1938 he persuaded his agency to send him to the United States for a year, and he worked at George Gallup's Audience Research Institute in New Jersey [S1]. He later credited Gallup as a major influence, above all for careful research and staying close to reality [S1]. The lessons show up all through his rules, from what viewers remember on TV to the photo above the headline [S4].

### Starting an agency with $6,000

In 1948 he opened his New York agency with about $6,000 of his own and backing from two London agencies, S. H. Benson and Mather & Crowther [S1][S2]. He struggled to win clients at first and built the business partly through direct mail [S1]. His later rule was to win new clients by doing great work for current ones, so his offices had no new-business departments [S2][S4]. He also warned against resigning accounts on impulse, or you'd empty your list every year [S4].

### Building Hathaway on a small budget

For Hathaway shirts he used a model with an aristocratic eye patch, the "man in the Hathaway shirt" [S1][S2]. In 1955 he said the agency had built a clear brand image for Hathaway on only $300,000 over four years [S3]. He added that the same money would not have done the job for an old brand with thirty years of ordinary ads behind it, since it's easier to shape a new image than fix an old one [S3].

### Choosing the brand over the offer at Thom McAn

In 1955 his agency had two campaigns ready for Thom McAn shoes: an "Amazing Offer" and a testimonial [S3]. They knew each offer ad would sell more shoes in the short run [S3]. They ran the testimonials anyway, because the offer would add nothing to the image they were building [S3]. He told the industry this was the kind of question the long view answers by itself [S3].

### Changing the image of a country

In 1955 he said the agency was trying to change how people saw Puerto Rico: a country in renaissance, proud of both its Spanish roots and its American citizenship [S3]. The campaign included "Pablo Casals is coming home, to Puerto Rico" [S2]. He said this was the work he was proudest of [S2].

### Hiring Eleanor Roosevelt, and regretting it

In 1959 he persuaded Eleanor Roosevelt to appear in a commercial for Good Luck margarine, paying her $35,000 [S1][S2]. Her mail split evenly between people sad that he had hurt her reputation and people glad of it [S2]. He later called it a mistake, not because it was undignified, but because celebrity endorsements in general were a bad idea [S1]. He called it not one of his proudest memories [S2].

### Putting the writing rules in a memo

By 1982 he had long since retired as chairman and moved to France, but he still cared how his people wrote [S1][S5]. On 7 September he sent the "How to Write" memo to the whole agency [S5]. It is short, numbered and ends with his advice to stop writing and go talk when you want action [S5].

## Where he stood

**Should ads entertain?** Only as far as it helps them sell. Liking an ad and buying the product are different things [S4].

**Short-term sales or long-term brand?** He moved from short-term to long-term. By 1955 he argued every ad is an investment in the brand [S3].

**Research or creativity?** Both, in that order. Research lights the way, but genius has to do the work [S3][S4].

**Long copy or short?** Readers want facts. An ad must tell the whole story, because people don't read ads in a series [S4][S8].

**Teamwork?** He was sceptical. He thought the best ads come from one person who studies the product and research, then shuts the door and writes [S4].

**Celebrities?** He used one in 1959 and then turned against the idea [S1][S2].

## His work and record

- Born 23 June 1911 in West Horsley, Surrey, England. Attended Fettes College and Christ Church, Oxford, and left Oxford after failing his exams [S1][S2].
- Worked in a Paris hotel kitchen in 1931, then sold Aga cookers door to door. Wrote the Aga manual in 1935 [S1][S8].
- Joined Mather & Crowther in London in 1935 [S1].
- Worked for Gallup's research institute in the United States from 1938 [S1][S2].
- Did intelligence work at the British embassy in Washington during the Second World War [S1].
- Farmed among the Amish in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, then moved to New York [S1][S2].
- Founded his New York agency in 1948 [S2].
- Campaigns included Hathaway, Commander Whitehead for Schweppes, the Rolls-Royce headline about the electric clock at 60 miles an hour (his favourite), Puerto Rico and Dove's "one-quarter cleansing cream" [S2].
- Books: *Confessions of an Advertising Man* (1963), *Blood, Brains and Beer* (1978) and *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) [S1][S2].
- Made a CBE in 1967 and named to the U.S. Advertising Hall of Fame in 1977 [S1][S2].
- Retired as chairman in 1973 and moved to Château de Touffou in France. In the 1980s he came back to chair the agency's India business and spent a year as acting head of its German office [S2].
- WPP bought his agency group in a hostile takeover in 1989 for $864 million, and he served as WPP's non-executive chairman for three years [S1].
- Died 21 July 1999 at Touffou, aged 88 [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Celebrity endorsement.** The Eleanor Roosevelt ad, which he later called a mistake [S1][S2].
- **Jingles.** He admitted he had no firm research to back his dislike of sung ads. It rested on his own experience as a salesman, since he never sang to a prospect [S4].
- **Temper and ego.** A review of Kenneth Roman's biography describes him as often harsh with staff, addicted to work, a snob and a name-dropper [S6].
- **The takeover.** He attacked WPP's Martin Sorrell in crude terms in 1989, then later said he liked him a great deal [S1].
- **Falling out of fashion.** The same review says changes in the industry left his methods looking dated [S6].
- **Language of his time.** His writing assumes male copywriters and female shoppers. His own agency's 2023 reissue notes this reflected his era [S4].
- **Hagiography.** The Wikipedia article on him carries a warning that it may read like a fan's account [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He distrusted design for its own sake. A council may need a designer to balance his view that only sales count [S4].
- His research comes from print and TV ads of the 1950s to 1980s. Test whether a finding still holds on a phone screen.
- He was sceptical of teamwork in creative work [S4]. Modern teams may need a different process.
- He wrote mostly for big brands with big budgets. Small businesses need to scale his testing advice down.
- He can sound certain where he admitted he had no data [S4]. Ask for the evidence.
