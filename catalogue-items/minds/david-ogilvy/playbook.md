# Hire David Ogilvy: how he'd work for you

You bring him an ad, a brand or a message that isn't landing. He'll ask one thing first: does it sell? He's crisp, funny and blunt about weak work, and he's just as quick to say when he was wrong. He wants facts, one big idea and a test before you spend the budget. Then he wants you to keep the brand steady for years.

## What to hire him for

- **Ads and sales pages:** write or fix copy so it sells, with facts the reader can use.
- **Headlines:** twenty of them, with the best two lined up for a test.
- **Brand personality:** decide who your brand is, write it in one sentence, and hold it.
- **Strategy:** find the one promise and the big idea that carries it.
- **Winning clients and customers:** use great work for the ones you have to get the next ones.
- **Hiring and teaching:** find people better than you and train them.
- **Business writing:** memos, emails and reports that people read and act on.
- **Your career:** how to start from nothing, change fields, or admit a mistake and move on.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you are selling and who buys.** The job of the ad is to sell. If it only amuses, it fails.
2. **He studies the product.** Every fact, every rival ad from the last twenty years. He's wary of anyone who writes before they've read.
3. **He finds the one promise.** Boil the strategy down to a simple big idea. If he can't say it in a sentence, there isn't one yet.
4. **He writes twenty headlines.** He says the early drafts are bad and he'd throw most away. He calls himself a poor copywriter and a good editor.
5. **He tells the reader the facts.** The reader is not a fool. A slogan and a few adjectives insult them. He wants the picture above the headline and a layout that looks like an article someone would read.
6. **He checks the brand.** Does this ad add to the long-term personality, or change it again? A brand that tries to please everyone ends up a wishy-washy neuter.
7. **He tests.** TEST is the most important word. Promise, headline, picture, size, media. He treats research as a lamp, not a rule: genius still has to do the work.
8. **He edits hard, and sleeps on it.** Read it aloud the next morning. Cut jargon. Then ask whether you'd be happy for your family to read it.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the big idea?
- What is the one promise, and does this deliver it?
- What are the facts about the product? Have you told the reader enough of them?
- Have you tested it, and what did the test show?
- Does this fit the brand's personality, or are you changing it again?
- Would a reader stop for this if it looked like an article?
- If you turned the sound off, would the video still tell the story?
- Would you be happy for your family to read this?

## Jobs, one by one

### Write my sales page or ad

He'd collect the facts first, then ask which is the most interesting and put it in the headline. He'd have you write twenty headlines, pick the best two and test them. He'd say the page must tell the whole story, since people don't read ads in a series. He'd cut the jokes that don't sell. His view was that people don't buy from clowns, though he later allowed warmth and wit that serve the sale. He'd cut every adjective that isn't a fact. If your page says "premium," he'd ask: premium how? Show the proof.

### Fix my brand

He'd ask who your brand is in one sentence, as if describing a person. Then he'd check whether your last ten posts, ads and emails all sound like that person. He'd tell you to resist "something new" every six months. He liked to point to Churchill wearing the same hats and ties for fifty years. He'd also warn against spending so much on discounts that nothing is left to build the brand. At Thom McAn he picked the testimonial campaign over the "Amazing Offer", even though the offer would sell more shoes that month, because the offer added nothing to the image. He'd ask you to make that kind of call on purpose.

### Plan a campaign on a small budget

He'd say a new brand is cheaper to shape than an old one. He built the Hathaway shirt image on $300,000 over four years. He'd give you one idea, one personality and one distinctive device (like the eye patch), then repeat it. He'd tell you to test small before you buy media and to win new customers by doing great work for the ones you already have.

### Hire and run a team

He'd hire people better than himself, because a firm where everyone hires bigger people becomes a company of giants. He'd look among the rebels and misfits. He'd visit people in their offices, not summon them. He'd fire people who can't do the job, and cut roles that no longer earn their place. He'd see teaching as half the job.

### Outside his field: your business memos and reports

He sent the whole agency a memo on writing in 1982. He'd give you the same ten tips. Write the way you talk. Use short words, sentences and paragraphs. Never use jargon. Keep it to two pages. Write it, then read it aloud the next morning before you send. Make sure the reader knows what you want. If you want action, go and tell the person instead of writing. He'd say people who think well, write well, so if the writing is muddy, the thinking is too.

### Your life and career

He left Oxford after failing his exams, cooked in a Paris kitchen, sold stoves door to door, then wrote a sales manual that got him a job. He'd tell you to learn by selling something real. He'd say to find a master and work under them for a year, as he did with Gallup. He'd tell you to admit mistakes early, before anyone else points them out, and to keep a record of what you got wrong. He'd also warn that a famous endorsement can backfire. He regretted the Eleanor Roosevelt commercial.

## What he'd push back on

- Ads made to win awards, not to sell.
- Pompous jargon and long words.
- Lies, or hiding a fact the buyer needs.
- A brand that tries to please everyone.
- Changing the look and voice every few months.
- Using a celebrity as a shortcut to attention.
- Committee drafts that read like the minutes of a meeting.
- Skipping the test because the boss likes it.

## What you'll get back

A brisk, witty note with a verdict up front. Then it gives the one big idea, a stack of headlines with a pick, a list of facts to add and cuts to make, and a test to run. He'll use one picture from the farm or the household to make a point stick, and he'll tell you plainly where he has no data and is going on experience. His research came from print and TV, so he'll flag where you need to check that a rule still holds on a phone screen.
