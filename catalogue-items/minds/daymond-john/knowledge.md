# Daymond John: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Daymond John is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from five pieces in his own words: a 2017 magazine column, a 2020 book excerpt [S1][S2], and three signed posts on his own website that carry no date and were read in 2026 [S3][S4][S5]. Facts about his life and FUBU come from reference pages [S6][S7]. He has written several books, including The Power of Broke (2016) and Rise and Grind (2018) [S6], which we did not use directly. Some of his website posts promote partner products; we use only the ideas. Treat each view as "as of" its source.

## In one paragraph

John grew up in Hollis, Queens, where his mother worked several jobs, and he is dyslexic [S3][S6]. In 1992 he started selling homemade hats for $10 each from his mother's house, and with friends built them into FUBU, short for "For Us, By Us" [S6][S7]. Banks turned them down again and again, so his mother placed a newspaper advert that led to a deal with Samsung, and the family took a second mortgage to fund the business [S6][S7]. FUBU's sales passed $350 million in 1998, and the brand has reported more than $6 billion in sales over its life [S6][S7]. He joined Shark Tank as an investor in 2009 [S6]. His core idea is what he calls the power of broke: having little money forces you to be resourceful, and resourcefulness wins [S1][S3]. In later writing he adds focus on a few priorities, a healthy money mindset, and selling to the audience you already have [S3][S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### The power of broke

John's best-known idea is that starting with no money can be an advantage. In his 2020 book excerpt he sums up his earlier work as the power of broke, which is about making things happen without money [S1]. On his website he says that resourcefulness beats resources, and that many strong businesses began with very little [S3]. Scarcity, he argues, can be a motivator [S3].

How he applied it: FUBU began in his mother's house, which the founders turned half into a factory [S1][S7]. In its early days the company traded and bartered for what it couldn't pay for [S3].

### Fix your money story

He argues that most people don't have a money problem, they have a money story problem [S3]. Beliefs picked up in childhood shape money decisions. He grew up treating money as survival, and even after FUBU made money he held it back out of fear and missed chances [S3]. He contrasts a scarcity mindset, which hoards and plays small, with an abundance mindset, which invests and takes calculated risks [S3].

How he applied it: he suggests writing down the belief, testing whether it's really true, studying people who built wealth from nothing, and making a small change such as raising prices by 10% [S3]. One of the beliefs he names is that charging more will drive customers away [S3].

### Focus beats hustle

In a later post, John says most entrepreneurs don't have a hustle problem, they have a focus problem [S4]. Long hours, constant email and meetings can feel productive while moving nothing forward [S4]. Top performers, he writes, don't work more hours. They protect fewer hours with more discipline [S4].

How he applied it: he describes an early stretch at FUBU when he felt busy and important, yet the business wasn't growing because his time went on urgent but unimportant tasks [S4]. His fix is a three-priority rule. Pick three priorities each week, and if they get done the week is a win [S4]. He also suggests blocks of two to three hours without interruptions, saying no more often, and checking messages at set times [S4].

### Shift the power

In Powershift (2020), John argues that his successes came from shifting power, from himself to others and from one strength to another [S1]. A powershift means taking control and creating real change, by building influence, negotiating for what you need and looking after relationships [S1]. He believes this skill is in everyone, but most people don't know how to use it [S1].

How he applied it: he opens with his three-year-old daughter, who reads each situation and picks a tactic until she gets her way, often from him [S1]. He credits his mother with teaching him to manage relationships with teachers, friends and bosses [S1].

### Turn attention into sales

John warns that followers and likes are not the same as sales [S5]. He describes three eras: retail, when FUBU needed buyers at stores like Macy's to grant shelf space; e-commerce, when brands could sell direct; and now social commerce, when people buy inside the apps they already use [S5]. It works because buyers already follow and trust the seller [S5].

How he applied it: he advises leading with value before asking for a sale, telling the story behind the product, going live at least weekly, cutting checkout friction and following up personally after a purchase [S5]. On Shark Tank, he says, he has seen founders who explain why they built something do better than those who only list features [S5].

### Tear down the walls

In 2017 John argued that private offices hurt communication. When everyone is behind closed doors, he wrote, things get lost in translation [S2]. He came to believe that a company is only as innovative as its workspace [S2]. Offices were designed for bulky machines, and most work is now digital [S2].

How he applied it: he moved his staff to an open floor with himself in the middle, then moved his team into a co-working space he opened, shared with staff from other brands [S2]. He said deals and decisions happened faster when people sat near each other [S2].

### Rise and grind

Alongside the power of broke, John describes a disciplined work ethic he calls rise and grind, which he built as his fashion career grew [S1]. His story is one of long effort with little money: years of rejection from lenders, family savings at stake, and a slow climb from a single shop in Queens to Macy's [S6][S7].

How he applied it: after a Las Vegas trade show brought in $300,000 in orders, FUBU needed money to fill them, which led to the Samsung deal in 1995 [S7].

## How he decides

- Ask what you can do without money first [S1][S3].
- Treat resourcefulness as your edge [S3].
- Check whether fear is making the decision [S3].
- Pick three priorities a week and protect time for them [S4].
- Say no more often than yes [S4].
- Lead with value and story before the sale [S5].
- Read who holds the power in a deal, and shift it [S1].
- Put people close together so they decide faster [S2].

## Where he stood

**Do you need money to make money?** No. He lists this as a limiting belief and points to FUBU's start [S3].

**Is working harder the answer?** Not by itself. Focus on fewer, better hours [S4].

**Should you charge more?** Often yes. Believe in your value and test a small rise [S3].

**Where should a small brand sell?** Where its audience already is, increasingly inside social apps, as of the mid-2020s [S5].

**Private offices or open space?** As of 2017, open space for nearly everyone [S2].

## His work and record

- Began selling homemade hats for $10 each in 1992 [S6].
- Co-founded FUBU, short for "For Us, By Us" [S7].
- Got funding from Samsung in 1995 after a newspaper advert placed by his mother [S6][S7].

- Published Display of Power (2007), The Brand Within (2010), The Power of Broke (2016) and Rise and Grind (2018) [S6].
- Joined Shark Tank as an investor in 2009; Bombas socks became his favourite investment [S6].
- Named a Presidential Ambassador for Global Entrepreneurship in 2015 [S6].
- Published Powershift in 2020 and launched Black Entrepreneurs Day the same year [S1][S6].
- Runs The Shark Group, a brand management firm [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **FUBU's decline.** Complex magazine blamed FUBU's fall on buying too much stock around 2001; the brand largely left the U.S. market in 2003 before a relaunch in 2010 [S7].
- **Holding cash out of fear.** By his own account, he held back money after early success and missed chances [S3].
- **A deleted post.** In 2021 he praised Louis Farrakhan on social media, then apologised and deleted the post [S6].
- **A dispute with founders.** In 2023 the LA Times reported complaints from the owners of a Shark Tank business, Bubba's-Q. A federal judge later issued a permanent restraining order against those owners for breaking a 2019 settlement [S6].
- **Promotion.** Some of his website posts promote partner products, so read the advice apart from the pitch [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- The power of broke assumes you can survive the lean years. Some people can't risk a family home [S7].
- Fast growth without stock control hurt FUBU. Pair him with someone strong on operations [S7].
- His later writing leans on social media selling. Not every product sells well in a live stream.
- His open-office view may not suit work that needs quiet focus, which his own focus advice values [S2][S4].
