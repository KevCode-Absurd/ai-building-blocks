# Dharmesh Shah: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Dharmesh Shah is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own blog, OnStartups, an edited interview transcript from 2020, a podcast transcript he posted, reports on his talks, and reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source, from 2013 to 2023. We could not find his birth year in a reliable source, so it is left blank. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after 2023.

## In one paragraph

Shah is a software engineer and founder who co-founded HubSpot with Brian Halligan in 2006 and serves as its chief technology officer [S1][S2]. The two met as graduate students at MIT. Their shared idea was that old marketing, such as cold calls, ads and email blasts, was losing power as people gained spam filters and caller ID, so companies should get found instead [S2]. They helped spread the name "inbound marketing" for that approach and wrote a book on it [S1][S3]. Shah's other big contribution is the HubSpot Culture Code, a slide deck he published in 2013 that treats culture like a product to be designed and updated [S1][S3]. On his blog he writes about startups in a plain, practical way, offering small frameworks for scoring ideas, signalling how strongly you feel and keeping customers [S5][S6][S7].

## Core ideas

### Get found instead of interrupting

Shah and Halligan believed traditional marketing was built around the marketer, not the person being marketed to [S2]. As buyers gained tools to block calls and filter email, interrupting them worked less well. The inbound approach draws people in with useful content, landing pages, search, blogs and social media, so the buyer finds the company when they are looking [S2]. In 2020 he added that buyers had grown more skeptical and now expect to serve themselves online, a shift the pandemic sped up [S3].

How he applied it: he ties HubSpot's first chapter to the inbound marketing movement the company helped promote. He says he and Halligan were drawn together by a shared love of small businesses [S3].

### Give away a tool that shows the problem

A free tool can reach far more people than a sales team. Shah built Website Grader, a free checker that scored a company's website, so that owners could see they had a problem HubSpot could solve [S7]. He says relative scores, such as a percentile against others, pull people in more than absolute ones, and that capturing email addresses should be built in from day one [S7].

How he applied it: he later described this as an accidental alternative to freemium that was not really freemium, and said it drove HubSpot's early reach [S12].

### You sell success, not software

Shah argues that in software sold by subscription, the vendor only wins if the customer gets value. In the old model, a buyer paid up front and whether they used the product was their problem. Under subscription, a customer who doesn't succeed cancels, and the vendor loses [S5]. So the job is to deliver the result the software promises [S5].

How he applied it: HubSpot invested in three things beyond the product. These were onboarding to get new customers to early success, HubSpot Academy to teach inbound marketing to anyone, and a community site for marketers [S5]. An early talk described a Customer Happiness Index the company used to track this [S8].

### Culture is a product

Shah says he was not the natural choice to lead on culture. Other chief executives told Halligan it mattered most, and Shah ended up writing a 16-slide deck when the company had fewer than 100 people [S3]. He treats it as an operating system of rules of thumb for decisions, and by 2020 it was on version 33. He calls it the most useful thing he has done at HubSpot [S3]. The company still says culture has to be revisited and revised, and it updated the code again in 2025 [S10].

How he applied it: the Culture Code was published openly in 2013 and has been viewed more than five million times [S1]. He warned that as a company grows, the urge to preserve culture is understandable but wrong, because culture has to adapt [S4].

### Hire adults, then use good judgment

His core assumption is that you have hired fully formed adults who will use good judgment and take responsibility. So the company writes few rules and fixes problems as they come up [S4]. Judgment has an order: put the team ahead of yourself, the company ahead of the team, and the customer ahead of the company. Getting that order wrong is what bad judgment means [S4].

How he applied it: by 2013 HubSpot let staff set their own hours and place of work, judged people on results, had no social media policy and offered untracked, unlimited vacation. He found the problem was that people took too little time off, not too much [S4].

### Transparency scales trust

Shah treats openness as a working tool. In 2013 he said HubSpot was highly transparent and that its young staff embraced it [S4]. Among his 15 lessons for HubSpot's 15th year, he listed that transparency feels uncomfortable but works, that a learning culture stops spreading by itself once a company grows, and that cynical people should be kept out [S8].

How he applied it: the same list warns that conflict between co-founders kills more companies than anything else, and that good intentions mean little without execution [S8]. He credits the long partnership with Halligan as part of HubSpot's story [S3].

### Score ideas on three numbers

When choosing what to build, Shah rates an idea from 0 to 10 on profit potential, passion and the probability it works, then multiplies the three [S7]. You can weight the factors to suit you. He warns against dropping an idea just because its odds are low, and prefers to think in expected value, the payoff times the chance [S7].

How he applied it: he says he and Halligan, at each fork, picked the path with the better chance of a spectacular result, even when it carried more risk [S7]. He also argues that the best ideas often appear only once you start building and talking to customers, so too much analysis on the sidelines can stall you [S7].

### Say how strongly you feel

In a growing company, a passing comment from a founder can be read as an order, and a strong view can get lost in a long thread [S6]. Shah's fix is a set of tags he calls FlashTags. They run from #fyi, which needs no reply, to #suggestion, to #recommendation, where the other person should take it seriously and explain if they say no, up to #plea, used rarely, for a specific ask based on trust [S6].

How he applied it: he wrote the idea on HubSpot's internal wiki in 2017 and published it in 2019 [S6].

### Focus on a few things you're good at

He limits himself to about three priorities at a time and changes them every few years. Past ones included brand, culture and boldness [S7]. He chose to have no direct reports, because he would rather build on his strengths than become a better manager [S7]. A 2015 profile describes him as the quiet tinkerer next to the outgoing Halligan [S2].

How he applied it: he puts a dollar value on his own hour and drops or hands off any task worth less than that [S7].

## How he decides

- Ask whether people would seek this out if you stopped pushing it [S2].
- Check whether the customer succeeds, because if they don't, they leave [S5].
- Write few rules, then fix wrongs as they happen [S4].
- Put customer over company, company over team, team over self [S4].
- Score ideas on profit, passion and probability, and think in expected value [S7].
- Start building and talking to customers before the idea feels finished [S7].
- Tag how strongly you hold a view before you share it [S6].
- Keep culture as a living document and update it [S3][S10].
- Value your time, and hand off what's worth less than it [S7].

## Where he stood

**Should marketing push or pull?** Pull. He saw old marketing as too focused on the seller, and built a company on helping buyers find you [S2].

**What does a software company really sell?** The customer's success. Software is the means [S5].

**Can culture be designed?** Yes. He treated it like a product, wrote it down and revised it dozens of times [S3].

**Rules or judgment?** Judgment, inside a clear order of priorities, with rules only where wrongs keep happening [S4].

**Should you drop long shots?** Not just for being long shots. A low chance can still be worth it if the payoff is big enough [S7].

**What about AI?** By late 2023 he was writing a free newsletter on AI agents at agent.ai, saying he had been digging into AI for a while [S11]. His later views are beyond this record.

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Culture claims under fire.** In 2016 a former employee, Dan Lyons, wrote a book and a *New York Times* essay that described HubSpot as having a "frat house" feel and called it a "digital sweatshop" [S9]. This was a direct challenge to the culture Shah had written about.
- **Layoffs.** HubSpot made its first layoffs in January 2023, cutting about 7% of staff [S9].
- **Staff ratings.** By July 2026, the company's overall Glassdoor rating had fallen to 3.4 out of 5 [S9].
- **Unlimited leave.** His own report from 2013 was that staff took too little vacation under the unlimited policy [S4].

These are criticisms of the company he co-founded, not of his personal conduct. His writing gives his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His frameworks come from a venture-backed software company. Inbound and free tools can take years to pay off, which a small shop with little cash may not be able to wait for.
- A written culture can drift from the lived one. Check what people actually experience, not just what the deck says [S9].
- "Use good judgment" works best with experienced, well-chosen staff. New or junior teams may need more structure.
- He thinks like an engineer and a builder. Pair him with someone strong on brand, storytelling or sales conversations.
