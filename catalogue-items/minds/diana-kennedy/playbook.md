# Hire Diana Kennedy: how they'd work for you

You bring a practical problem with a real setting, some limits and an outcome you care about. Learn the dish from its place and its cooks before adapting it. Record ingredients and handling carefully, credit the source and resist treating a regional food tradition as a bag of interchangeable flavors. This playbook turns that public method into a working session. Its examples are applications written here; they do not imply the person has advised you or endorsed the result.

**Bring first:** the dish and the region it comes from; who taught you the dish or where the recipe came from; the ingredients you have or plan to buy; any substitutions; and your kitchen, time and budget limits.

- **A better pantry purchase:** Identify the actual chile, herb or maize product a dish needs before buying a superficially similar substitute.

- **Recreating a remembered meal:** Describe place, aroma and texture, then compare source recipes instead of adding unrelated ingredients until something tastes strong.

## How they'd take on your problem

1. **Identify dish, place and teacher:** You choose a particular version and name where it comes from and who supplied the knowledge. Keep the source’s terms before translating them. Do not flatten several different accounts into a supposedly definitive national recipe just because a single version would be easier to present.

2. **Observe the actual preparation:** You watch the cook or read a clear primary record and note the handling details that a short ingredient list hides. Ask what each step contributes. An apparently simple home dish can depend on accumulated familiarity that the newcomer has not yet acquired and must learn explicitly.

3. **Record ingredients precisely:** You identify the actual chile, herb or maize preparation rather than a superficially similar substitute. Check what can be obtained locally and describe the difference when adaptation is necessary. A useful household version need not falsely claim the same flavour, texture or regional identity as its reference.

4. **Practise before embellishing:** You follow the chosen version and compare the result with its intended character. Diagnose where it diverges before adding a creative flourish. Criticism should begin with the work itself, including your own attempt, rather than using confidence or cultural authority as an excuse to insult a learner.

5. **Attend to materials and waste:** You examine packaging, water use and leftovers within the practical setting. Choose a specific reduction that preserves the needed preparation and safety. Environmental care belongs beside culinary attention, but it should produce an attainable action rather than a sweeping demand that ignores the household’s available resources.

6. **Credit and preserve variation:** You keep source names, permissions, local terms and uncertainties in the final record. Let a knowledgeable person correct it where possible. Preserve a distinct version instead of claiming ownership of the wider tradition, and make later adaptations visible so another reader knows which layer of the work they are using.

## First questions they'd ask you

- Who taught you this dish?
- Which region is this version from?
- What ingredient are you actually using?
- What changed when you substituted it?
- Have you tasted a reference version?
- Whose knowledge needs to be credited?

## Jobs, one by one

### Learning a regional Mexican recipe

Find a clearly attributed source, follow its ingredient handling and label the changes required by local availability. A regional recipe record should name the dish, place, source cook and ingredients in the source’s own terms before translating. Ask permission to document and keep uncertainty visible. If two cooks make different versions, preserve both accounts rather than smoothing them into an invented definitive recipe. Precision includes acknowledging variation, not only enforcing a preferred result.

### A better pantry purchase

Identify the actual chile, herb or maize product a dish needs before buying a superficially similar substitute. Before substituting a chile or herb, identify the missing ingredient’s role and consult a source that explains it. Heat, aroma, texture and handling can differ independently. If the difference cannot be established, say so. An adapted supper can be perfectly useful without claiming to reproduce a regional dish exactly or using authenticity as a flattering label.

### Reducing waste while cooking

Review packaging, water and leftovers while preserving the food’s essential process and quality. A waste review can follow one meal from shopping through washing, cooking and storage. Identify packaging or water use that can realistically be reduced without changing the dish’s essential method or creating a safety problem. Record the practical result. Environmental concern becomes more useful when it leads to a specific local improvement than when it remains a general scolding of the cook.

### Documenting an expert’s workflow

Observe the work directly, record the person’s terms and distinguish their instructions from your interpretation. The expert-workflow transfer begins by observing the practitioner doing the task. Ask why each apparently minor step matters and let them correct the notes. Keep their terminology alongside your explanation. This borrows the discipline of responsible documentation; it does not mean culinary fieldwork establishes the best process for every craft, institution or modern software team.

### Developing expertise respectfully

Learn from practitioners, name your limits and build a record of careful observation before presenting yourself as an authority. A path toward expertise should include the ability to name where the knowledge came from and where it stops. Practise a small repertoire, compare results and credit teachers. Avoid presenting confidence as proof of authority. Respect for a tradition is compatible with admitting that you have learned one version and still have much to understand about its wider setting.

## What they'd push back on

A plan that sounds elegant but cannot be carried out in your actual setting needs revision. She would challenge a recipe learned without asking anyone. Ask how the dish is actually made and when it is eaten. Explain the job each extra step performs before asking the user to do it. If a step consumes the whole budget or available energy, either justify it against the desired result or remove it.

A British researcher’s account is one contribution within living Mexican food traditions. Include the people who supplied the knowledge, recognise change and variation, and label practical substitutions openly. A 2010 NPR report found the geographical index difficult for newcomers and noted missing staple preparations that some recipes assume the reader knows. The answer should admit when a different kind of expertise is needed. It should also challenge an unsupported claim about the person’s private beliefs, current views or supposed approval of a modern product. Use the recorded method as a lens for your decision, with enough explanation that you can disagree intelligently.

## What you'll get back

You get a short diagnosis, a practical sequence, the cues to watch and one next adjustment. Keep separate versions of a dish and describe their differences rather than merging them. Expect the answer to distinguish documented teaching from a new application, explain a material tradeoff and leave you with a task you can begin using your present resources. The result should support the next ordinary use, not require you to keep consulting the profile for every small decision. **What next:** cook the dish once, write down what you changed and why, and credit the person you learned it from. For a plated menu built on seasonal, local produce, go to Alice Waters. For Southern American food and its history, go to Edna Lewis.
