# Dieter Rams: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Dieter Rams is alive, and his views can change. This data set rests on his own speech "Design by Vitsoe", given in New York in December 1976 [S1], an interview he gave to *Kinfolk* in 2017 as reported by Dezeen [S2], a 2000 interview with Designboom [S6], a page of his quoted words on the Italian site Design Culture [S7], an excerpt of a 1987 interview [S8], a short 2024 letter published by the Steve Jobs Archive [S9], a 2019 interview with the director of a film about him [S3], and two reference pages [S4][S5]. The 1987 piece is only an excerpt, so we use it lightly. Treat each view as "as of" its date. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about products or events since. His speech is still under copyright, so quotes stay short.

## In one paragraph

Rams is a German industrial designer who spent his working life with two companies: Braun, where he joined in 1955 and led design from the early 1960s until 1997, and Vitsoe, the furniture maker founded in 1959 around a shelving system he began in 1957 [S1][S4][S5]. He gave Braun's radios, record players, shavers and clocks a calm, coherent family look [S5]. He sees the designer as a rational "creative engineer" who shapes a product from its purpose, not an artist who dresses it up at the end [S1]. Good design, for him, means as little design as possible: remove what isn't needed so the product becomes quiet, clear and long-lasting [S1]. He wrote this up in ten principles of good design, summed up as "less, but better" [S4]. As early as 1976 he warned that thoughtless design for thoughtless consumption had to end, because the world was running short of resources [S1]. In later life he has said that durability is also the ecological choice [S2].

## Core ideas

### As little design as possible

In 1976 Rams said that using design to impress, polish or make things chic is not design at all. It is packaging [S1]. When you focus on the essential parts and leave out everything extra, he said, forms become quiet, comfortable, easy to understand and, above all, long-lasting [S1]. His ten principles end on the same idea: good design is as little design as possible, focused on what is essential [S4].

How he applied it: the SK 4 radio and record player of 1956, designed with Hans Gugelot, put the workings in a plain box under a clear lid [S4][S5]. Some critics expected buyers to dislike seeing the parts. Buyers found it stylish [S5].

### The ten principles

He wrote them as a test for his own work. In summary, good design is innovative and moves with technology; it is useful, in practical, psychological and visual ways; it is attractive, because beauty affects daily life; it is easy to understand; it is unobtrusive, a neutral tool rather than decoration; it is honest and doesn't overstate what a product can do; it is long-lasting and avoids fashion; it is thorough down to the last detail; it is kind to the environment; and it is as little design as possible [S4].

How to use it: the list works as a checklist for a design review. Ask each question of the thing in front of you, and note where it fails.

### The designer as a creative engineer

Rams rejects the idea of the designer as an artist adding a last-minute garment to a product [S1]. Design is the whole form, colour, material and build of a product, and the product must do its job well [S1]. The designer pulls the parts together, and aesthetic choices are justified by what the product is for [S1]. Functionality sits at the centre [S1].

How he applied it: he argued that real design can't be done by an outsider. It has to be part of the company, which is why he worked for only two firms for about 20 years by 1976 [S1].

### Design is for people, and for a system

You can't understand good design if you don't understand people, he said, because design is made for people [S1]. A product must fit the human body, senses and understanding [S1]. It must also work as part of a larger system, such as a home [S1].

How he applied it: the 606 Universal Shelving System, in production since 1959, was built to adapt to any room and to sit quietly next to furniture from any designer or era [S1][S5]. He said such furniture "does not shout" [S1]. He described Vitsoe's buyers as users, not consumers: people who choose things they will really use [S1].

### Order makes things useful

Every object people use needs a clear order, he argued, so that its form tells the user what it does [S1]. The arrangement, shape, size and colour of the parts come from a planned system, and that system becomes the product's language [S1]. For design to be understood by everyone, it should be as simple as possible [S1]. He contrasted this with products that scream for attention or try to impress, and so try to control our relationship with them [S1].

How he applied it: he gave Braun products a shared visual family, so that each one looked like it belonged with the others [S5].

### Honest and long-lasting

His principles say good design is honest. It doesn't try to make a product seem more powerful or valuable than it is [S4]. It also avoids fashion so it never looks dated [S4]. In 2017 he said beauty that is exemplary deepens and lengthens a person's relationship with an object, and that this also makes ecological sense [S2]. He added that practical value and beauty are not opposites [S2].

How he applied it: he admitted that restraint can limit novelty, but argued that restrained objects don't tire the eye over time [S2].

### Responsibility to the world

In 1976 he warned of a growing, possibly permanent shortage of raw materials, energy, food and land [S1]. He said the age of thoughtless design, thoughtless production and thoughtless consumption was over [S1]. He pictured future generations shuddering at how we filled homes, cities and landscapes with a chaos of junk [S1]. He saw a larger job for design in the built world, asking who studies the effect of pylons, highways and concrete blocks on people's minds [S1].

How he applied it: in 2017, aged 84, he questioned whether people really need so many new things, doubted talk of "sustainable growth", and called for fewer, better products [S2].

### Function first, with no "ism"

In the 2000 Designboom interview he said form has to come after function and he cannot imagine it any other way [S6]. He counts psychological needs as part of function, so the job is to weigh looks against use [S6]. He turned down labels: there is no functionalism, just functions [S6]. The Design Culture page records his lines that design is first and foremost a thinking process, that products should be like tools, and that innovation is never an end in itself [S7]. It also records that decoration for its own sake is packaging, and that cutting cost is fine only as part of making good design available to all [S7].

How he applied it: he mixed materials, such as wood with plastic or aluminium, when the job called for it, and named the Japanese idea of wabi sabi as a reference for calm and balance [S6]. The Design Museum adds that he urged designers to question everything generally thought to be obvious, and that he prefers sketching by hand to digital tools. His career began with architecture and a carpentry apprenticeship, and he credits his carpenter grandfather [S5].

### Back a design with a company that understands it

Rams said good design needs firms whose management truly understands and backs the design process, because that is the only way to make useful products and not ones driven by trends or profit [S7]. In 2000 he credited Erwin Braun and Nils Vitsoe, and called such owners rare in Germany [S6]. He pointed to Adriano Olivetti as the Italian example [S6].

How he applied it: he tied his working life to a few owners he trusted, and stayed with them for decades [S1][S6].

## How he decides

- Start from the purpose. If it doesn't serve the purpose, cut it [S1].
- Ask what can be left out [S1][S4].
- Make it easy to understand without a manual [S4].
- Don't let a product overstate itself [S4].
- Avoid fashion. Aim for a product that lasts [S4].
- Get every detail right, even the hidden ones [S4].
- Make it fit with the other things around it [S1].
- Ask whether it should exist at all, given what the world can afford [S1][S2].

## Real decisions

### A plastic lid on the SK 4 (1956)
In 1956 Rams worked with Hans Gugelot on the SK 4 record player. He suggested a plastic cover in place of the original metal one, which vibrated too much [S5]. The clear lid became the product's best-known feature, and in his 2000 interview the clear cover is named as the piece critics cite most and as a production standard [S5][S6]. A fix for a technical fault ended up as its look, which fits his view that form follows from solving the problem [S1].

### One system for the home, not one product (1957 to 1960)
In 1957 he began a storage system that became the 606 shelving, produced by Vitsoe since 1959 [S1][S5]. In 1960 he designed the 602 armchair for Vitsoe, the start of a long run of Vitsoe pieces [S7]. He said the shelving works because it fits any home and does not shout, living quietly beside furniture from any designer and era [S1]. He made it adaptable so it can change over time and not be thrown away [S1].

### Fixing a design language at Braun (1963)
As design director from 1962, he set Braun's system in 1963: uniform unit sizes, white or charcoal grey with aluminium covers, grey controls and a green on-off switch [S5]. His stated reason was that order makes a product useful by showing what it is for, and that products should be understood by everyone and fit together [S1][S5]. By 1968 he was director of design for almost all Braun products [S7].

### Adding black to the range (1965)
In 1965 he introduced black bases with the Studio 1000 hi-fi system, a shift that influenced consumer electronics for around thirty years [S5]. The choice shows his restraint: change one thing for a reason, not for fashion. In 1987 he was still defending the Braun look, pointing to a pocket calculator with coloured, convex buttons that looked fresh after a decade [S8].

### Building a team and staying inside one company
In the 1976 New York speech he said he had worked only for Braun and Vitsoe for about 20 years, and argued that a designer cannot do good design from outside, because the ideas must match the company's aims [S1]. In 1987 he credited his team of 16 for Braun's small square black alarm clocks that led the Japanese market, and said Braun's success came through design, not technology [S8]. In 2000 he said he was lucky to start and stop at the right moments, and named Erwin Braun and Nils Vitsoe as the rare charismatic owners who made it possible [S6]. He chose long relationships with a few firms over many clients.

### Refusing "isms"
In his 2000 interview he said form has to come after function, and that he cannot picture it any other way [S6]. He also said function includes psychological needs, so a designer has to weigh looks against use, and he rejected the label functionalism: there is no functionalism, just functions [S6]. His reason, as we read it, is that a label turns a way of thinking into a style, the thing he warned against in 1976 [S1][S6].

### Looking back with regret, and asking for a fresh look at his own rules
In a 2019 film, as reported by its director, Rams accepts that his work at Braun helped feed today's overconsumption and regrets not spreading his ideas on sustainability further [S3]. That account says that if he started again he would choose landscape architecture or urban planning [S3]. In a 2024 letter he said his ten principles are not set in stone, that each generation should review them, and that designers should see themselves as advocates for people's needs and for the planet [S9]. He repeated his old line that the days of thoughtless design for thoughtless consumption are over, and added that this has not yet come true [S9].

## Where he stood

**What is design for?** For people. It is a practical, rational job of making useful things clear and lasting, not decoration (as of 1976) [S1].

**Is beauty important?** Yes, but beauty grows from usefulness and good making, not from styling [S2][S4].

**Is more always better?** No. In 2017 he called the less-but-better way the only plausible way forward [S2].

**Should designers care about the environment?** Strongly. He said so in 1976, long before it was common [S1].

**Can an outside consultant do real design?** He didn't think so. He believed design must be built into the company [S1].

## His work and record

- Born 20 May 1932 in Wiesbaden, Germany [S4].
- Studied architecture and interior design in Wiesbaden from 1947, did a carpentry apprenticeship, and worked for architect Otto Apel in Frankfurt [S4][S5].
- Joined Braun in 1955 [S4].
- Designed the SK 4 radio and record player with Hans Gugelot in 1956 [S4].
- Began a storage system in 1957 that led to the founding of Vitsoe in 1959; this became the 606 Universal Shelving System [S1][S4].
- Led design at Braun from the early 1960s. Sources give 1961 or 1962 as the start, and he retired in 1997 [S4][S5].
- Designed the 620 chair programme for Vitsoe in the 1960s and the T2 lighter for Braun in 1968 [S4].
- Taught industrial design in Hamburg from 1981 [S5].
- The subject of the Design Museum exhibition *Less and More* (2009) and Gary Hustwit's film *Rams* (2018) [S3][S5].
- Jony Ive of Apple and Jasper Morrison have both acknowledged his influence [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **His own regrets.** Director Gary Hustwit said in 2019 that Rams regrets helping to drive overconsumption in his Braun years, and wishes his ideas on sustainability had spread further [S3]. Hustwit also said Rams has said he might not become a designer if he started again [S3]. This is reported by Hustwit, not said by Rams in the article.
- **A tension in the work.** He led design for a mass-market maker of consumer electronics while warning against thoughtless consumption [S1][S3]. He has been open about this.
- **Restraint has a cost.** He has accepted that his restrained style can hold back innovation [S2].
- **Copied as a look.** His influence on others, including Apple's designers, is well known [S5]. A clean look can be copied without his deeper aims of durability and less waste.
- **Tied to technology.** The Design Museum notes his direction was shaped by technology, and Braun later dropped its entertainment and film lines [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- His world is physical products made by large firms with time to perfect them. A small team shipping software weekly may need a lighter version of his rigour.
- Calm, neutral design can feel cold or anonymous for brands that need warmth, humour or loudness.
- "As little design as possible" can be taken as "remove everything". He meant remove what doesn't serve the purpose.
- He says little about marketing, price or what makes people choose one product over another in a shop.
- His taste is German modernist. Other cultures and users may read clarity differently.
