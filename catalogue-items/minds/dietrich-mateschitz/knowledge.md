## In one paragraph

Mateschitz was an Austrian marketing manager who sold toothpaste and shampoo for a German firm before he found, on trips to Asia, that local tonic drinks helped his jet lag [S3]. In 1984 he and Thai businessman Chaleo Yoovidhya set up Red Bull, adapting Chaleo's drink Krating Daeng, with each side owning 49% [S2][S8]. After about three years of work on the formula, the brand, the can and approval from regulators, Red Bull launched in Austria on 1 April 1987 [S3][S6]. He told Forbes that there was no market for it, so Red Bull would create one [S2]. He built the brand by owning a culture of extreme sports, events, teams and media rather than leaning on standard adverts [S2][S8]. By 2025 Red Bull sold almost 14 billion cans a year in 178 countries [S6]. He ran it as a private company and said he would neither list it on the stock market nor sell his stake [S1].

## Core ideas

### Create the category, don't join one

Mateschitz did not try to win a share of the soft drinks market. He set out to make a new one. He told Forbes that when Red Bull started, there was no market for it, and that Red Bull would create it [S2]. Red Bull's own profile calls the 1987 launch the birth of a new product category, energy drinks [S6].

How he applied it: at its peak Red Bull held about 75% of the energy drink market it had created [S3]. Even after rivals arrived, he told Forbes that Red Bull had made the market [S2].

### Keep going when the tests say no

Early research was brutal. Focus groups disliked the taste, the logo and the name, and he called it the worst disaster he'd seen [S2][S3]. Investors doubted the roughly half-million-dollar outlay, and it took about three years to win over regulators in Austria [S3]. He launched anyway.

How he applied it: he kept the product and the brand that testers rejected, and sold it first as a trendy, premium drink at Austrian ski resorts [S8]. In 1994 it sold out so fast in Germany that the factory ran out of aluminium for cans [S3].

### Become part of the culture, not a sticker on it

Red Bull built its name through sponsoring extreme sports and offbeat athletes rather than through standard advertising [S3][S8]. Forbes reported that in the early years he skipped TV adverts in favour of guerrilla marketing, such as backing athletes and events and paying students to throw parties [S2]. He said Red Bull's approach was to be inside the sport, not on the outside buying space on a car [S4].

How he applied it: Red Bull ran its own events, such as the Flugtag contest for home-made flying machines and an air race series [S3]. It bought the Jaguar Formula 1 team in 2004 and renamed it Red Bull Racing, which went on to win six constructors' titles [S7].

### Spend heavily on the brand

He treated Red Bull as a marketing company. In 2005 Forbes put Red Bull's marketing spend at about $600 million, roughly 30% of revenue, against about 9% at Coca-Cola [S2]. Bilanz estimated in 2017 that about a third of revenue still went to marketing, split between adverts and sponsorship [S1]. He told Forbes the company was always looking for a more creative, different point of view [S2].

How he applied it: the brand grew into media as well as sport, with a TV channel, ServusTV, and a media arm that in 2017 he said was still in its pioneering phase [S1][S7].

### Sell the original at a premium

Mateschitz defended Red Bull as the real thing. He told Forbes that people who appreciate the product want the original, and compared cheap copies to a fake luxury watch [S2]. The drink was priced and placed as a premium product, while the Thai original stayed cheap in its home market [S8].

How he applied it: when France and Denmark had not yet approved the drink, he told Forbes that each side could survive without the other [S2]. France later allowed it in 2008 [S8].

### Build from the bottom

He liked starting things more than running them. In 2017 he said he felt more at home in building phases [S1]. In his own account, reported after his death, entering football with a fifth-tier club near Leipzig was a gut-driven crazy idea, and so were going into Formula 1 and even founding Red Bull [S5]. He said he wanted a small club so he could build from the very bottom, and that buying an established one was out of the question [S5].

How he applied it: that small club became RB Leipzig, one of several football and ice hockey clubs Red Bull came to own [S5][S7].

### Trust people and keep control light

The 2008 profile says he organised the company around sports-team roles rather than standard corporate titles, with very few formal controls [S3]. It quotes him saying the company relied on self-motivation and responsibility instead [S3]. In 2017 he said that the core drinks business was mainly a matter of organising and executing, that trusted people filled the key posts, and that little would change without him [S1].

How he applied it: he worked mostly from an office at Hangar-7 at Salzburg Airport, away from the main headquarters [S1]. On sport, he said Red Bull wanted athletes with character as well as talent, and that trust in young people mattered [S5].

### Stay independent

Mateschitz kept Red Bull private. Asked in 2017 about a stock market listing or selling his stake, he rejected both [S1]. He said a sale to a giant like Coca-Cola was not on the cards, though he added that one should never say never [S1]. He said the partnership with the Thai Yoovidhya family had worked well for nearly 30 years, and that neither side planned to buy the other out [S1].

How he applied it: the Yoovidhya family held 51% of the company and Mateschitz ran it [S8]. He kept the tax seat in Austria and said he felt at home there [S1].

## Real decisions

### Adapting a Thai tonic instead of inventing a drink

On a work trip for Blendax in 1982 he tried Krating Daeng and found it cured his jet lag [S3][S8]. Rather than copy it, he struck a partnership with its maker Chaleo Yoovidhya and founded Red Bull GmbH in 1984, each side taking 49% [S2][S7][S8]. He then spent about three years on the formula, name, can and approvals before launch [S3][S6][S7].

### Launching against the focus groups

Test groups disliked the taste, the logo and the name, which he called the worst disaster he had seen [S2][S3]. He launched anyway on 1 April 1987 in Austria [S6]. His stated line was that there was no market, so Red Bull would create one [S2].

### Starting at ski resorts and in culture, not on television

He sold Red Bull first as a trendy premium drink in Austrian ski resorts [S8]. In the early years he skipped TV adverts and backed athletes, events and student parties [S2]. His reason, reported in 2015, was to be inside the sport, not outside it buying space on a car [S4].

### Buying the Jaguar F1 team

In 2004 Red Bull bought the Jaguar Formula 1 team and renamed it Red Bull Racing [S7]. Forbes estimated in 2005 that it cost about $100 million a year against about $70 million of revenue [S2]. Later it won six constructors' titles [S7]. He treated it as marketing spend, not a profit centre.

### Founding RB Leipzig from a fifth-tier club

His own account, reported after his death, was that going into football with a small club near Leipzig was a gut-driven crazy idea [S5]. He wanted to build from the very bottom and ruled out buying an established club [S5]. The club became RB Leipzig [S5][S7].

### Refusing to list or sell

Asked in 2017 about a stock market listing or selling his stake, he rejected both, and said a sale to a giant like Coca-Cola was not on the cards, though one should never say never [S1]. He said the partnership with the Yoovidhya family had worked for nearly 30 years [S1]. The family held 51% and he ran the company [S8].

## Where he stood

**New market or existing one?** New. He set out to create energy drinks as a category [S2][S6].

**Ads or culture?** Culture first: events, athletes, teams and media [S2][S8].

**Sell or list the company?** Neither, as of 2017 [S1].

**What drives him?** In 2017 he said his drive had not changed in decades: getting things moving, putting ideas into practice, and enjoying it [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Health and claims.** France, Denmark and Norway at first did not allow the drink, and the UK advertising regulator restricted its ads in 2001. In 2014 Red Bull paid $13 million to settle U.S. class actions over performance claims it couldn't back up [S8].
- **Losing share.** Its share of the U.S. energy market fell from 75% in 1998 to about 47% by 2005 as rivals arrived [S2]. By 2023 its global share was put at 13% [S8].
- **Brand bullying.** In 2013 Red Bull pressed a small English brewery called Redwell to change its name before the two settled [S8].
- **Media and politics.** ServusTV, which he owned, was criticised for allegedly downplaying COVID-19 [S7]. A 2017 interview in which he criticised Austria's refugee policy drew a strong backlash [S7].
- **Costly bets.** Forbes estimated in 2005 that his F1 team cost about $100 million a year against about $70 million in revenue [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- His method assumed huge, steady marketing budgets. A small firm can't spend a third of revenue on its brand [S1][S2].
- Health questions about the product came up again and again [S8]. Pair him with someone who checks the claims.
- "Create a category" works when the category is real. It can also become an excuse to ignore what buyers say.
- He shared very little of his reasoning in public, so much of this is read from results.
