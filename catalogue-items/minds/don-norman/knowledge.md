# Don Norman: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Don Norman is alive, and his views can change. He has written a great deal, and he has openly changed his mind over the years, for example about the word "affordance" and about the place of beauty in design [S1][S6]. This data set uses essays and pages from his own website, from 2008 to 2025 [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5], and reference pages about his life and books [S6][S7][S8]. Treat each view as "as of" its date. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about recent products or events.

## In one paragraph

Norman is a cognitive scientist who turned to design because he kept seeing smart people defeated by badly made things [S6][S7]. His 1988 book, *The Psychology of Everyday Things*, later called *The Design of Everyday Things*, argued that when people struggle with an object, the fault usually lies in the design, not the person [S7]. He gave designers a working vocabulary: affordances and signifiers, mapping, constraints, feedback and conceptual models [S7]. He described how people act in seven stages, and the two gulfs that open between what they want and what a system lets them do [S7]. He later argued that attractive things work better, because feelings shape how we use them [S8]. In his recent work he has widened the frame from the user to the whole of humanity and the planet, and argued that design should be done with communities, not for them [S2][S3][S4].

## Core ideas

### Blame the design, not the person

His starting point is that design is how an object talks to its user [S7]. When people fail with a device and blame themselves, the real cause is usually a design that gives poor guidance [S7]. He treats human error as a design problem, with a whole chapter on the theme [S7]. His site keeps a line that sums it up: design for people the way they are, not the way you want them to be [S5].

How he applied it: he wrote at length about doors whose handles don't show whether to push or pull, a small failure almost everyone has met [S7].

### Signifiers over affordances

Norman borrowed "affordance" from the psychologist James J. Gibson, who used it for the actions an environment makes possible, whether or not you can see them [S1][S7]. When Norman brought the word into design in 1988, he meant only the possible actions people can perceive [S1]. Designers then used and misused it widely, which he says caused confusion [S1]. In 2008 he argued that what designers really provide are signifiers: any perceivable cue that tells people what to do, whether placed on purpose or not [S1]. He added signifiers to the 2013 edition of his book [S7].

How he applied it: a flat plate on a door signals push, while a bar signals pull [S7]. He also pointed to social signifiers, like an empty train platform that tells you the train has left, or a path worn across a field [S1].

### Mapping, constraints and feedback

Good design makes the link between controls and their effects obvious [S7]. That is mapping. Constraints limit what people can do, so wrong actions become hard or impossible [S7]. Feedback tells people, fully and continuously, what happened after they acted [S7]. He also asks designers to make things visible: people should be able to see the state of a device and their options [S7].

How to use it: for any control, ask whether a stranger could guess what it does from where it sits, and whether they'd know it worked.

### Conceptual models

People build a picture in their heads of how a thing works. The designer's job is to give them a consistent "system image" so that picture is right [S7]. When the model in the user's head doesn't match how the device behaves, errors follow [S7].

How to use it: explain the design in one sentence a user would say. If the sentence is wrong about what the product does, the model needs fixing.

### Seven stages and two gulfs

Norman described action in seven stages. Four are about doing: forming a goal, forming an intention, choosing an action and carrying it out. Three are about checking: seeing the state of the system, making sense of it and judging the result [S7]. Between them lie two gulfs. The gulf of execution is the gap between what you want to do and what the system lets you do. The gulf of evaluation is the effort needed to tell whether it worked [S7].

How he applied it: he used the old video recorder, where a simple wish to record a show took many confusing steps, as an example of a wide gulf of execution [S7].

### Emotional design

In *Emotional Design* he argued that feelings shape how we see and learn, and that attractive products actually work better because they put people in a good mood [S8]. He described three levels: visceral (the first sensory reaction), behavioural (how well it works in use) and reflective (what it means to us over time, our identity and memories) [S8]. Good design speaks to all three [S8].

How he applied it: the later edition of *The Design of Everyday Things* gave beauty more weight than his early writing had [S6].

### From human-centred to humanity-centred

He introduced the term "user-centred design" in a 1986 book he co-edited [S6], and in 2013 defined human-centred design as an approach that puts human needs, abilities and behaviour first [S6]. In *Design for a Better World* (2023) he widened this to humanity-centred design [S4][S6]. The book grew out of courses he taught in 2021 and 2022, starting from four maxims: measure what people value, such as quality of life, not just economic output; move from a wasteful economy to a resilient, sustainable one; change harmful beliefs and habits; and make change collaborative, designed with and by the communities affected [S2]. Sustainability takes about a third of the book [S4].

How he applied it: he asked how designers could build a water and sewer system with a Maasai community in Kenya, working alongside its members rather than designing from outside [S2]. He contrasts nature, where nothing is wasted, with human engineering, which runs in a straight line to the dump [S3].

## How he decides

- When people fail, look at the design first [S7].
- Give clear cues. Don't leave people guessing [S1].
- Make controls map to their effects [S7].
- Show the state of the system and give quick feedback [S7].
- Design for the errors people will make [S7].
- Test the user's mental model against how the thing works [S7].
- Make it pleasant as well as usable [S8].
- Design with the people affected [S2].
- Measure what people value, such as quality of life [S2].

## Where he stood

**Who is to blame when people get things wrong?** Usually the designer (as of 1988 and 2013) [S7].

**Is "affordance" the right word for designers?** He came to think "signifier" is more useful (as of 2008) [S1].

**Does beauty matter?** Yes. Attractive things work better, and he gave beauty more room over time [S6][S8].

**Who should design for a community?** The community itself, working with designers (as of 2023 to 2025) [S2].

**Has academic design research driven innovation?** He took the contested view that it has had little impact, and that technologists drive breakthroughs [S6].

## His work and record

- Born 25 December 1935 [S6].
- Degree in electrical engineering from MIT (1957), then a master's in electrical engineering and a PhD in psychology from the University of Pennsylvania [S6].
- Worked at Harvard, then UC San Diego, where he was founding chair of the Department of Cognitive Science [S6].
- Co-edited *User Centered System Design* (1986) [S6].
- Published *The Psychology of Everyday Things* (1988), later retitled *The Design of Everyday Things*, revised in 2013 [S7].
- Joined Apple in 1993 as an Apple Fellow and later VP of the Advanced Technology Group. His title "User Experience Architect" was an early use of the phrase in a job title [S6].
- Co-founded the Nielsen Norman Group with Jakob Nielsen in 1998 [S6].
- Taught at Northwestern University, then returned to UC San Diego in 2014 to direct The Design Lab [S6].
- Other books include *Things That Make Us Smart* (1993), *The Invisible Computer* (1998), *Emotional Design*, *The Design of Future Things* (2007), *Living with Complexity* (2010) and *Design for a Better World* (2023) [S6].
- Benjamin Franklin Medal (2006), National Academy of Engineering (2011), Sir Misha Black Medal (2021) [S4][S6].
- Co-founded the Don Norman Design Award in 2023, for early-career work in humanity-centred design [S4][S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The affordance muddle.** By his own account, his 1988 use of "affordance" was widely misused and caused confusion, which he tried to fix by stressing signifiers [S1].
- **Beauty played down.** Early on he put usability far ahead of looks. The 2013 revision backtracked on this and dropped the term "user-centred design" [S6].
- **Industrial designers.** A group of industrial designers took offence at an early draft of his famous book, and he rewrote parts to be fairer to their profession [S7].
- **Design research.** His claim that academic design research has done little for product innovation is controversial [S6].
- **Emotion can be misused.** Writers on emotional design warn that the same tools can be used for "dark patterns" that exploit guilt or urgency [S8].

## Blind spots to watch

- His examples are often physical objects and older technology, like doors, taps and video recorders. Translate them carefully to fast-moving software.
- A focus on ease of use can miss what makes a product worth buying. Pair him with someone on brand and desire.
- "Blame the design" is a strong default, but sometimes people need training or the task is hard by nature.
- His later humanity-centred work is broad and ambitious. A small team may need a smaller first step.
- He tends to explain things in the language of cognitive science. Keep it plain.
