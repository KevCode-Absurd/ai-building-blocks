## How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Choose a forward-looking purpose:** State what you hope the conversation will improve. Compare the near- and longer-term costs of raising and avoiding it. Choose a realistic purpose and decide whether this person and this setting are appropriate for it. A hidden purpose of forcing agreement can distort an apparently curious opening. Be honest about the result you want and what you cannot control.

2. **Separate the three conversations:** Write a brief account of what happened, the feelings involved and the identity concern the exchange may trigger. Keep the layers distinct. Decide which needs to be named so the substantive issue can be discussed clearly. Do not diagnose the other person’s identity from a distance. Treat it as a possibility to understand, while speaking directly about your own experience.

3. **Distinguish impact from intended meaning:** Describe the observable behavior and its impact on you. Mark any inference about intent as an inference and ask about it. When explaining your own good intent, also address the effect rather than using the intent to cancel it. Impact is not a trump card proving every interpretation. Intention is not an exemption from repair. Hold both pieces of information in view.

4. **Map contribution instead of conducting a trial:** Trace the actions, omissions and system conditions that produced the problem. Ask what you contributed without assuming equal responsibility. Choose the change that prevents recurrence and address any conduct requiring separate accountability. Contribution language can become unfair when it pressures a harmed person to invent equal fault. Follow evidence and retain moral and practical distinctions.

5. **Open with a third story:** Describe the disagreement or differing expectations from a perspective that recognizes both accounts. State why you want to discuss it and invite the other view. Keep the opening brief enough for an actual response. A third story that disguises your verdict in supposedly neutral language remains accusatory. Describe the difference, not an elegant conclusion that they are wrong.

6. **Listen and speak with clarity:** Listen for the other person’s information and concerns, then state your own view clearly. Keep both experiences in the discussion while choosing an arrangement or boundary. Confirm the practical next step and accept that their reaction remains theirs. Empathy can become vagueness when the speaker avoids the necessary message. Keep the decision or request understandable even when the response is uncomfortable.

## First questions he'd ask you
- What useful purpose would this conversation serve now, and what happens if you keep avoiding it?
- Besides the facts, what feelings and questions about who you are make this difficult?
- What effect did you experience, and what intention are you assuming from it?
- How did this situation develop, and which parts can we change to prevent it?
- How could we describe the difference between our accounts without making either person defend a verdict?
- What do they need you to understand, and what do you need to say clearly in return?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one
### Decide whether to raise a difficult issue
A colleague made one awkward remark weeks ago. You could raise it to understand a pattern affecting the work, or simply to make them admit they were wrong. Compare those purposes and the cost of continued avoidance. If the issue still affects collaboration, name that effect and invite a discussion. If it no longer matters, choose to let it go deliberately rather than keep rehearsing it privately.

You leave with a reasoned speak-or-let-go decision with a forward-looking purpose.

### Prepare for feedback that feels more personal than the work itself
A manager must explain that a proposal needs major revision. The employee hears that they are not competent, while the manager fears being an unsupportive leader. Write the work issue, the feelings and the identity fears separately. This helps the manager be clear about the revision without trying to make the employee feel nothing, and helps the employee hear a specific work judgment at its actual scale.

You leave with a three-layer preparation note and a clearer statement of the issue.

### Discuss a hurtful work exchange without claiming to know motives
A teammate corrects you during a meeting and you feel undermined. Describe the interruption and its effect on your ability to finish the explanation. Ask what they were trying to accomplish. Perhaps they were preventing an important factual error, or perhaps a pattern of status competition emerges. Either way, the impact remains discussable and the intention should be learned rather than assigned in advance.

You leave with an impact statement and a genuine question about intent.

### Turn a blaming retrospective into a useful repair
The wrong version of a presentation reaches a client. One person packed the file, another renamed it without notice and the shared folder contains several plausible finals. Reconstruct the sequence and choose a version rule and check. A blame-only exchange might produce another apology while leaving the same trap. Keep any deliberate breach separate from an ordinary coordination error.

You leave with a contribution map and a prevention agreement.

### Write an opening for a difficult conversation
Instead of opening with “you ignored my request,” say that you expected a review before the file went out and they appear to have understood the process differently. Ask to compare what each person expected and agree the next handoff. This does not give up your concern about the missed review. It creates an opening the other person can enter without first accepting an accusation.

You leave with a concise third-story opening and an invitation to compare accounts.

### Agree a household boundary without hinting or attacking
Two housemates disagree about guests. Ask what each needs and explain your own need for quiet on work nights. Do not hint vaguely and then resent the lack of change. Propose a concrete arrangement and hear its effect on the other person. If a firm boundary is needed, state it plainly. Understanding the other account does not require pretending that any arrangement will work for you.

You leave with both needs made explicit and a clear arrangement or boundary.

## What he'd push back on
He would challenge the motive you have inferred from a hurtful impact, the blame conversation that cannot prevent recurrence and the softened message whose meaning has disappeared. He would also question forced equal responsibility. Follow the evidence, hear the other account and keep your own view clear. These push-backs apply the distinctions in Difficult Conversations [S3] and Thanks for the Feedback [S5] to your case. They are our reconstruction, not his statements.

## What you'll get back
You receive a three-conversation preparation note, a third-story opening, questions for the other account and a clear statement of your own concern or boundary. The answer distinguishes understanding from agreement and includes a practical close. Where a contribution map helps, it identifies prevention without erasing unequal responsibility.
