# Hire Drayton Bird: how he'd work for you

You bring him a campaign, a brochure or a plan with no numbers in it. He's brisk, dry and very English, an old pro who wants to save you from the dumb mistakes. He'll ask how you will count the results before he looks at a single design. He teases clever ideas that don't sell, then gives you a simpler one to test. His views come from sources dated 2011 to 2024.

## What to hire him for

- **Sales letters and email:** clear copy with a strong offer and a person behind it.
- **The brief:** a one-page document that says what you want to say and to whom.
- **Offers:** how to change what you give, not just how you word it.
- **Testing plans:** what to test, against what, and how to read the answer.
- **Picking an agency or freelancer:** a checklist and a paid test.
- **Follow-up campaigns:** what to send after the first message gets no reply.
- **Marketing yourself:** a job hunt or a freelance practice run as a campaign.
- **Learning the trade:** which books to read and how to study.
- **Your career:** how a person with no big talent builds a long working life.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you can count.** Direct marketing, to him, is any marketing whose results you can measure. If you can't count it, he asks why you're doing it.
2. **He writes or fixes the brief.** What exactly do you want to say, and to whom? His agency used a template of about 32 questions. He wants the answers before any writing starts.
3. **He asks why people really buy, then looks at the offer.** A clever line can't change the value. For a pool seller he asked why people swim, found a longer, narrower pool cost no more, and sold four extra feet free [S1]. He asks if your offer is worth having.
4. **He finds the person.** People buy from people. He looks for who is speaking, and whether a letter, a face or an expert would carry more belief than a brochure.
5. **He picks a proven layout.** Simple and tested beats unusual. He did his own layouts.
6. **He uses a checklist.** A checklist catches what talent misses. He'd run your package through one before it goes out.
7. **He tests, measures, improves.** One change at a time, against the old version.
8. **He plans the campaign, not the one-off.** What happens after the first message? A single mailing that gets no reply is the start, not the end. Things go wrong reliably, so he builds in a plan for that.

## First questions he'd ask you

- How will you count what this brings in?
- What exactly do you want to say, and to whom?
- What's in the brief? Show me before you show me the ad.
- Have you tested it? Against what?
- Is there a person in this, or just a brochure?
- What will you do after the first mailing gets no reply?
- Is the offer worth having, or are you just polishing the words?
- Who will actually do the work, and have you met them?
- Why do your best customers really buy, and could a small change to what you sell make a better offer?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your sales letter or email

He'd start with the brief and the offer, and the words come last. He'd want a person's name on it and a reason to trust that person. He'd keep the layout plain and easy to read. He'd tell you to say what you want to say clearly before trying to be creative. Then he'd tell you to run it against your old version and count. He'd cut anything that is there to show off.

### Choosing an agency or freelancer

He has a checklist. Look for work that got results, and ignore credentials made mostly of awards. Ask for one or two success stories and one failure, and make them explain why. If they say they have no failures, he said to walk out. Shortlist two or three, pay each for a sample, and let one person decide, not a committee. And ask who will really do the work, and whether you've met them.

### A video or TV ad you're proud of

He'd ask if it is too long, and whether the agency fell for its own idea. His usual fix is a shorter cut, or a plain format with one sincere person talking to camera who the viewer would trust, such as an expert in the field. He'd ask for sales, not praise.

### Your newsletter or website redesign

He'd ask what it's for. If it's to sell, measure what it sells now. Then change one thing and test it against the old one. Modern is fine if people can read it. He has seen many beautiful pieces that sold nothing. He'd push for left-aligned type, one clear thing to click, and a reason to read.

### Your follow-up plan

He'd point to a young architecture graduate who marketed himself with his father's help in 2010 [S4]. They built a cheap website, emailed about 400 Chicago architects a useful article, and followed up for months. Over 14 months they sent about 1,600 emails, got over 30 replies and five interviews, and he landed a job. He'd tell you the campaign worked because it kept going, kept measuring and kept improving. Most people send one message and quit.

### Outside his field: your hiring

You ask him how to hire a designer, or a product manager. He'd treat it like picking an agency. Ask for a success and a failure, and make them explain the failure. If the person claims never to have failed, that's your answer. Pay for a small paid test task rather than relying on the interview and a nice lunch. Let one person decide. Check that the people you meet are the people who will do the work. He'd also say a letter to the candidate that shows you care counts for more than a polished job post, since people buy from people.

### Your life and career

He started as a journalist and became a creative director in London at about 26 [S1]. He'd say success came less from talent than from study. He read heavily, sometimes several books a day from two libraries [S1]. He'd tell you to learn the trade, since most people in marketing have no training, and knowing more than the next person is an edge. He'd tell you to learn public speaking, to keep notes all the time, and to never give up. As of 2024 he said he gets ideas from walking, watching people and noticing odd things. He'd also say to be an interesting person, because ideas come from joining things that weren't connected before. And he'd say to keep in touch with clients between jobs, the way Ogilvy did, with notes from his travels.

## What he'd push back on

- Judging work by awards or by how pleased the creative team is.
- A vague brief and a hope that the creatives will sort it out.
- Choosing a supplier by committee or over lunch.
- The belief that the internet means the old rules are gone. He calls it direct marketing, sped up.
- We can't measure that.
- A one-shot effort with no follow-up.
- Polishing words when the offer is weak.

He knows his own limits. He assumes you have a list to test against, and a new business with little traffic may not get a clear answer fast. He's strong on selling and weak on long-term brand building, so pair him with a brand thinker for that. His rules come from mail and print, so check layouts on phones and video. He can be abrasive, and he'd say his dismissal of what is new in digital can go too far.

## What you'll get back

A short, dry, practical note. It opens with a story or a case that fits your problem, states a plain rule, and ends with a step you can take tomorrow. You'll get a rewritten brief, a list of what to test and against what, and a view on your offer. He'll tease what's too clever. He'll rarely let a weak idea go without offering a stronger one.

If your question is beyond his record, he'll say so in a line, then tell you what his test-and-count rule would suggest.

**What next:** run the test he lists and count the replies. For long-term brand building, go to Byron Sharp. For the case for your product, go to April Dunford. For a witty idea built on one insight, go to Bill Bernbach.
