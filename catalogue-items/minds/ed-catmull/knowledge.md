# Ed Catmull: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Catmull wanted to be an animator as a boy, studied physics and computer science instead, and became one of the founders of computer graphics [S1]. At the University of Utah he developed texture mapping and other basic methods for drawing curved surfaces by computer [S1]. He led computer graphics groups at the New York Institute of Technology and Lucasfilm, and in 1986 Steve Jobs bought the Lucasfilm group and founded Pixar [S1]. Pixar made Toy Story in 1995, the first of a long run of hit films [S1]. After Disney bought Pixar in 2006, Catmull and John Lasseter also ran Disney Animation [S1]. His leadership ideas centre on candour, backing people before ideas, protecting fragile early work and hunting for hidden problems [S2][S3][S4]. He shared the 2019 Turing Award, computing's top prize [S1].

## Core ideas

### People matter more than ideas

In his 2008 article, Catmull told of a studio head who said his central problem was finding good ideas, not good people [S3]. Catmull called this a misguided view of creativity [S3]. It overrates the first idea and misunderstands how to manage the big risks of making something new [S3]. In the book he argues that the right people, working well together, matter more than a great idea, because a strong team can fix or replace a weak one [S4].

How he applied it: Pixar bets on a director first. In 2026 he described how a director spends about a year with a small team, pitching three ideas, and the room asks which one the director truly wants to make [S2].

### Candour through the Braintrust

The Braintrust is a group of experienced filmmakers who watch a film in progress and give frank notes [S2][S4]. The director Andrew Stanton gave it its name [S2]. It works because the notes aim at the problem, not the person, and because the people in the room trust each other [S4]. The group can name a problem but cannot dictate the solution, and the director doesn't have to follow its suggestions [S2].

How he applied it: the group's rules were meant to keep power out of the room. Senior leaders such as Lasseter and Stanton stayed quiet for the first 10 to 15 minutes [S2]. Steve Jobs was kept out of Braintrust meetings because his presence would change how people spoke [S2]. Disney later built its own version, which it called the Storytrust [S2].

### Every film starts as an ugly baby

Catmull argues that early versions of every film are awkward and unformed, and that nobody can judge at the start whether something will be good [S2][S4]. The only fair questions early on are whether the director really wants to make it and whether the team believes it can solve the problems [S2]. The danger sign is when the team loses faith that the director can fix things [S2].

How he applied it: he said films often end very far from their pitch. Coco began as a full musical [S2]. Ratatouille needed a change of director along the way [S2].

### Protect the new from the Beast

Catmull named the constant demand for output "the Beast". When the need to ship drives creative choices, quality drops [S4]. New ideas need shielding until they are strong enough to face the world [S4]. He has said that at Disney, production needs were driving creative decisions, which he called backwards [S2].

How he applied it: the summary of his book links Disney's slump after The Lion King to this pressure, and its later success with films like Tangled and Frozen to a changed approach [S4].

### Mistakes are part of making things

Catmull treats mistakes as a natural part of creative work and a source of learning. Fear of failure stalls an organisation [S4]. In 2026 he added a twist: Pixar avoided the word "failure" in its reviews and said "this doesn't work" instead, which kept the focus on the idea rather than the person [S2]. He said his book's way of talking about failure may even have done people a disservice [S2]. He admits that about half his own ideas have been wrong, so the aim is to try things and change course fast [S2].

How he applied it: he said people feel exposed when they share unfinished work, so teams need to know their peers are there to help, not to judge them [S2].

### Look for the problems you can't see

Catmull believes many organisations fail because of problems their leaders can't see, not because of obvious bad choices [S4]. Success can hide trouble. Leaders should assume problems exist and go looking for them [S4]. In story work, he said, a problem that shows up in one scene often starts much earlier, so the fix means rethinking the setup [S2].

How he applied it: as Pixar grew, candour faded and costs rose. The response was Notes Day, when the company stopped work to gather honest feedback from staff [S4].

### Artists and engineers as equals

Catmull came from the technical side. He saw that film studios treated technical staff as second-class, and he set out to make artists and engineers equal partners [S2]. He sees storytelling and technical problem-solving as similar kinds of creativity [S2]. He credits his graduate years at the University of Utah with shaping his idea of a good place to work [S2].

How he applied it: he led the team that built RenderMan, Pixar's rendering software, which won Academy technical awards in 1993 and later years [S1].

### Guidelines, not rules

Catmull describes his goal as unlocking people's potential rather than enforcing rules [S2]. Pixar's processes were flexible guidelines that changed often [S2]. Change and chance are part of creative life, and the future is not set [S4].

How he applied it: after Disney bought Pixar in 2006, he promised Pixar staff their culture would be kept, and he kept the two studios separate for morale while letting them share ideas when one was stuck [S2][S4].

## How he decides

- Back the person, then let them find the idea [S2][S3].
- Ask whether the leader really wants to make it [S2].
- Name problems in notes. Leave the solution to the people doing the work [S2].
- Keep rank and power out of feedback sessions [S2].
- Don't judge early work as finished work [S2].
- Assume there are problems you can't see, and go find them [S4].
- Treat a failed attempt as information, and move on fast [S2].
- When a symptom shows up late, look earlier for the cause [S2].

## Real decisions

### Redoing Toy Story 2 from scratch

Disney first asked Pixar to make Toy Story 2 as a direct-to-video film, and Pixar agreed. Looking back, the account says this was a serious mistake, because the team did not want to make low-quality work and morale suffered [S4]. Pixar reversed course and aimed for theatres. When the team was still unhappy with the film, they chose to redo it from the start [S4]. The same period produced the Braintrust. Catmull's lesson in the summary: the right people and chemistry matter more than a great idea alone [S4].

### Betting on a director with three ideas

Rather than pick the best story, Pixar picks a person it believes can lead a story team [S2]. Each prospective director gets a small team and about a year to develop three ideas [S2]. Leadership then asks which idea the director truly wants to make [S2]. Catmull says three is a way to break a rut: when people stall on one, they can move to another, and the first often still wins [S2]. He also notes that directors know when they are claiming all three are equal [S2].

### Setting the Braintrust rules

The Braintrust is a group of peers who give candid notes on a film that is stuck [S2][S4]. Its rules: the group cannot tell the director what to do, the director owns the fix, powerful leaders stay quiet for the first 10 to 15 minutes so their views do not set the tone, and the aim is to find what does not work, not to prescribe a solution [S2]. Catmull's reason was that candour only works if power is kept out of the room and the focus is on the problem, not the person [S2][S4].

### Changing the director on Ratatouille

When Ratatouille got stuck, Pixar changed directors [S2]. Brad Bird and an outside writer restructured it, keeping the original director's premise [S2]. Catmull notes that credit for the finished film goes to the final director but belongs to many [S2]. His rule is to keep trying as long as the team believes it can solve the problems; the sign of failure is when the team loses faith [S2].

### Keeping Steve Jobs out of the room

Catmull kept Jobs out of Braintrust meetings because his presence changed the dynamic [S2]. Jobs instead acted as protector and gave notes after screenings, often telling people they could ignore him [S2]. Catmull says Jobs fired two board members who never disagreed with him [S2]. Jobs also argued that Pixar should go public so it would have a cushion if a film flopped [S4].

### Creating Notes Day

As Pixar grew, newer staff resisted the culture, and people began holding back suggestions, so candour was slipping [S4]. Rising production costs added pressure [S4]. Pixar answered with Notes Day, when the company stops work to draw out honest feedback from staff [S4]. The decision shows his habit of treating culture as something to renew, not to declare.

## Where he stood

**What is scarcer, good ideas or good people?** Good people. He called the opposite view a misunderstanding of creativity [S3].

**Can you tell early if a project will be good?** No. Early work is always rough [S2].

**Should the boss be in the feedback room?** Not if their presence will change what people say. That is why Jobs stayed out [S2].

**Is failure good?** He sees mistakes as part of the work, but as of 2026 he prefers to say "this doesn't work" over talking about failure [S2].

**Who should solve a problem the group spots?** The director and team, not the critics [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **No-poaching deals.** Pixar was named in a US government case over agreements among tech and film firms not to recruit each other's staff, covering 2005 to 2009 [S5]. Catmull was implicated in the later class action. In a deposition he defended his actions as protecting the long term as well as payroll [S1]. Pixar and Lucasfilm paid $9 million in one settlement [S5]. Wikipedia puts the total paid by Disney and its units at $100 million [S1].
- **Lasseter.** In 2017 his co-leader John Lasseter took leave after staff accused him of misconduct, and he left Disney at the end of 2018 [S6]. Reporting cited by Wikipedia said Pixar had people assigned to rein in his behaviour, which suggests the company knew of problems [S6]. Our sources don't say what Catmull knew. Treat this as an open question about the culture he led.
- **Rosy accounts.** His ideas on candour come mostly from his own telling. He himself said his framing of failure may have done people a disservice [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- His methods were built inside a well-funded studio that could spend years on one film. Small teams may not have that time.
- Candour depends on trust and fair power. Where staff fear the leader, as the Lasseter case shows, the best rules may not protect them [S6].
- He focuses on the creative process. Pair him with someone strong on money, deadlines and market fit.
- Backing people over ideas can turn into backing favourites. Check who gets chances.
