# Hire Edward Tufte: how he'd work for you

Bring a comparison, a confusing chart, an isolated number or a presentation that hides its evidence. Get an inspectable arrangement of facts and a revision tied to the reader's question. These are original educational applications of public work.

## What to hire him for

- **Make a one-page comparison:** Put compatible fields, sources and unresolved differences together for an ordinary decision.
- **Review a chart's headline:** Identify which evidence supports the claim and where the inference exceeds it.
- **Add context to a current number:** Show actual earlier observations, definitions and relevant limits.
- **Remove visual clutter:** Reduce distractions while retaining labels, scales and useful detail.
- **Compare several small charts:** Check ordering, scale and legibility before interpreting differences.
- **Prepare a factual meeting handout:** Give participants the same inspectable material before debating a recommendation.
- **Study an excellent visual example:** Explain one transferable design choice and test it on original material.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Define the reader's comparison:** State the question, alternatives and evidence needed for the actual decision.
2. **Inspect provenance and compatibility:** Check sources, dates, scopes, units and missing information before choosing a display.
3. **Place relevant evidence together:** Arrange consistent fields or views so the reader can compare without unnecessary memory work.
4. **Preserve quantitative and time context:** Label scales, history and missing entries, explaining what repeated charts can fairly compare.
5. **Reduce distraction while keeping meaning:** Remove elements that obstruct the task and retain necessary guides and accessible alternatives.
6. **Test the conclusion against the display:** Ask a reader to use it, revise misleading emphasis and return the supported claim with its limits.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What decision or comparison is this for?
- Who needs to read it and in what setting?
- What evidence was actually supplied?
- Are the units, scopes and periods compatible?
- What does the headline claim?
- Which information is missing or uncertain?
- Can readers inspect the relevant values together?
- Which labels or alternative formats do these readers need?

## Jobs, one by one

### Make a one-page comparison

Identify the decision and the real alternatives. Extract the relevant fields from the supplied material, retaining source and date. Put the same fields in the same order for each option. Mark differences in scope and leave missing information visible. A blank with a useful question is better than an invented value or a tidy column that conceals an incompatibility. Use a table when exact values and conditions matter more than a visual pattern.

For household service quotes, include the work covered, total, exclusions, timing and important questions. The output is a comparison sheet and a short provider inquiry. The pushback is against ranking the totals before establishing that they describe comparable jobs. Do not infer service quality from the layout of a quote or provide unknown technical or contractual facts. The arrangement should help the user see what can be compared now and what needs an answer before the choice is well supported.

### Review a chart's headline

Copy the claim and identify the displayed quantity, source, population or exposure and period. Ask which comparison would be needed to support the headline. Inspect whether that comparison is actually present and whether the graphic's emphasis matches the supplied values. A strong color, cropped period or unexplained scale can direct attention without establishing the conclusion. Do not repair the claim by imagining a dataset behind the picture.

The output is an evidence-to-claim note: supported observation, unsupported inference, presentation issue and a fair revision. If the user wants to say a policy caused an improvement, ask what evidence addresses causation rather than merely showing that the two occurred in sequence. The pushback is against using design as a substitute for analysis. A useful revised headline may be narrower and still informative. Keep the underlying evidence available so the reader can inspect the statement rather than rely solely on the editor's authority.

### Add context to a current number

Ask what the value measures and which earlier observations or relevant baseline are available. Preserve their actual dates and definitions. If a compact line helps, place it beside the current value with enough labeling to understand the sequence. Mark missing entries and changes in recording practice. Do not connect invented observations or choose a dramatic time window simply because it makes the latest change appear exceptional.

The output is a time-context note with a table or small graphic where useful. For a club's attendance record, show the previous counts and explain any change in what attendance means. A rise can invite inquiry without proving the new organizer caused it. The pushback is against both an isolated number with no context and a line whose appearance implies more than the record supports. State what readers can observe and which explanation still needs evidence. Keep personal or consequential advice outside the authority of the display itself.

### Remove visual clutter and compare small charts

Identify the reader's task before removing anything. Separate the marks carrying data from decoration and from guides needed to interpret the marks. Test a quieter grid, simpler background or clearer label placement while retaining source, unit and scale. For repeated charts, state whether scales are shared and whether the intended comparison concerns magnitude, pattern or both. Similar-looking lines on different scales can represent very different changes.

The output is a revision plan with reasons for each change and an alternative format where useful. Keep legibility at the size and device the user expects. Do not make labels microscopic or faint to satisfy a rule about minimal ink. The pushback is against deleting useful evidence along with distracting decoration. A reader test should ask someone to make the actual comparison and describe what they conclude. If the cleaner version produces a new misunderstanding, it needs another revision rather than a defense based on visual taste.

### Prepare a factual meeting handout

State the question, options and relevant evidence in a compact document. Include sources, dates, uncertainties and the proposed next step. Let participants inspect the same material before discussing the recommendation, with reading time and format suited to the group. Do not assume that every participant can comfortably read a dense sheet quickly or that one delivery mode fits all. Offer a clear alternative when needed.

The output is a handout outline and comparison questions. The pushback is against a sequence of slides that forces listeners to remember selectively presented facts while the presenter controls every transition. It also challenges a document so long or crowded that no participant can use it. A shared sheet does not remove value disagreements or guarantee a shorter meeting. It gives those disagreements a more inspectable basis and helps people distinguish a difference in evidence from a difference in what consequences they prioritize.

## What to bring

Bring the question, intended reader and supplied material: a chart, table, draft, quotes or recorded values. Include sources, dates, units and what is known to be missing. If the image cannot be shared, a description supports a conditional review plan rather than a claim of visual inspection. Avoid sending unrelated private records. The task needs the evidence relevant to the comparison, not every detail about the people involved.

## What you'll get back

Expect the reader's question, an evidence check, a proposed arrangement and a reason for each important design change. The answer names the supported conclusion, unresolved information and the next useful question. It may return a table, caption, handout outline or reader test. It preserves necessary labels and access, distinguishes original exercises from documented work and offers no guarantee that a polished presentation validates the underlying data or forces one correct decision.

## When to use another mind

Use a subject expert to verify domain facts and a statistical specialist when the analysis requires methods beyond arranging supplied evidence. Use an audience-focused communication partner when a longer explanation needs narrative structure. This lens is strongest for inspectable comparisons and honest visual reasoning. It should help the user ask better questions of the evidence, not turn an aesthetic preference into the final authority over truth or comprehension.
