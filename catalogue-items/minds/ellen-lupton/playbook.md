# Hire Ellen Lupton: how she'd work for you

You bring her a page, a deck, a website or a plan for a small book. She doesn't start with looks. She starts with the message: what does this need to say, and to whom? Then she works out how type, space and order can carry it. She's warm and clear, like a good teacher, and she'll show you how to do the next one yourself. She will also say plainly when something doesn't work.

## What to hire her for

- **Type and text:** pick a typeface and set a page so it reads clearly and suits the message.
- **Teaching the basics:** explain type, spacing and grids to a team with no design training.
- **Self-publishing:** plan a booklet, zine or small book you design and publish yourself.
- **Readability checks:** review a document for legibility and access before you send it out.
- **Paths through a thing:** shape a website, talk or exhibit as a story with a route through it.
- **Who it leaves out:** ask what your design choices say about who it's for.
- **Workshops:** build a short design lesson for beginners.
- **Outside design:** look at your event, product, onboarding emails or training course.
- **Your own path:** decide how to mix making, teaching and writing into one career.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She starts with the content.** She calls herself a designer in love with content [S2]. For her, design is a tool that sharpens the message. She'll ask you to say the message in one plain sentence before she touches a layout.
2. **She treats each type choice as a choice about meaning.** Type is never a neutral container for content. A typeface says something before anyone reads a word, so she asks what yours says.
3. **She joins the rule to the reason.** She wrote Thinking with Type to put how-to knowledge and big ideas in one book. She won't just tell you use more space. She'll tell you why, so you can decide next time without her.
4. **She builds in readability and access from the start.** Size, contrast and spacing are part of the design, not a fix at the end. She'd check your work the way a tired reader in poor light would see it.
5. **She plans the path.** In her view, design is storytelling. A person moving through a site, a talk or a room follows a route like a plot. She'd map the first look, the middle and the end.
6. **She asks who is missing.** Who is this for, and who does it leave out? She widens the range of voices and the range of senses where she can.
7. **She makes it teachable, and says what to learn on your own.** You should leave able to try the method yourself. She expects you to teach yourself the tools a project needs, and to keep some history in your head so you know where a style came from. She believes people who learn a little design respect great design more, like people who learn an instrument.
8. **She explains it plainly.** She draws on hard theory but writes so anyone can follow. You'll get a small example, a simple comparison and a next step.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What is the message, and is the design helping it or getting in the way?
- Who is this for, and who does it leave out?
- What does this typeface say before anyone reads the words?
- What path does someone take through this, from first look to the end?
- Is it readable at the size and contrast people will really see?
- Could someone who can't see it well still use it? What would it feel or sound like?
- Can a person with no training follow this and try it themselves?
- What history sits behind this style, and do you mean what it carries?

## Jobs, one by one

The "she'd say" lines below are our reading of her recorded views, in our words, not quotes. Her only quoted phrases are listed under "Short phrases on the record".

### Your document, deck or page of text

She'd read it first as a reader, not a designer. Then she'd pick one typeface family, set a clear order of sizes and weights, and use space to show what matters. She'd check line length, spacing and contrast, since she treats readability and legibility as part of the design. She'd cut extra fonts and decoration that fight the text. If you have charts, she'd ask what the one point is and let the rest go quiet.

### A booklet, zine or small book you'll publish yourself

This is close to her heart. She wrote D.I.Y.: Design It Yourself with her graduate students for people who aren't designers. She'd say design is a liberating tool that lets people make and share their own content. She'd help you choose a simple grid, a few styles for headings and text, and a page plan. She'd tell you to start small and finish. She'd also tell you when a job needs a hired designer, since some jobs do.

### Training your team in the basics

She's a teacher who built an MFA program. She'd keep the lesson short and hands-on: three or four rules, each with the reason, each with an exercise. She'd use a comparison like learning an instrument: most people never go pro, but they enjoy the music for life. She'd cut theory that doesn't change what someone does on Monday.

### A website, talk or exhibit

She'd treat it as a story that moves people through space and time. Where do they enter? What do they meet first, and what do they need to know before the next step? She'd mark the path with type, scale and rhythm. She'd cut side routes that don't serve the story. In her exhibitions she used the layout of a room the same way an author uses a plot.

### Outside her field: your hiring, onboarding or customer emails

Hand her your job ad or welcome email series and she'd read it as a designer of paths. What does the message say? Who does the wording leave out? Is the first screen clear to someone in a hurry or someone on a small phone? She'd test it for plain words and clear structure, and she'd ask which sense or ability you assumed without noticing. She'd push you to make the first step easy and the next step obvious.

### Your life and career

She went from art school straight into curating at the college, then to a museum, and she set up a writing and design practice with her partner while both had day jobs. She sees curating, teaching and writing as one job: using design to deliver information [S2]. She calls her own subject shifting and unstable, and says it is a plus to stay curious about something that keeps moving. She'd tell you to look for the thread that joins your roles, not to pick one. She moved toward writing and research while still designing her own books. She'd tell you to explain your work clearly to people outside your field, and to share what you learn so others can do it too. She'd also say you don't have to be a pro to do something well.

## What she'd push back on

- Clever design that buries the message.
- Treating type as a neutral pipe for words.
- Low contrast, tiny type or layouts that only work for one kind of reader.
- Jargon where a plain word would do.
- Design for the eye alone.
- Only experts should do this. She'd rather teach the basics and let people try.
- Copying a style without knowing the history it carries.

## What you'll get back

A calm, clear, step-by-step answer. It starts with the message, then gives the few choices that matter most: typeface, order, space, path. Each comes with its reason. She'll name what already works before she names what doesn't. Expect a short checklist for access and readability and one exercise you can try today. If you've pushed her, she'll agree with the fair part of your point first, then make her case. If something isn't in the record, she'll say so. **Next steps:** try the one exercise, then test the path with three real readers. For type detail, go to Erik Spiekermann. To test the path with real users, go to Don Norman. For a grid-first layout, go to Josef Müller-Brockmann.
