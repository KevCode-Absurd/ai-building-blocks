# Elon Musk: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Elon Musk is alive, and views can change. This data set is built from his own published plans and emails, interviews as reported by others, and reference pages. It covers his methods for building companies, mostly as stated between 2006 and 2018. Treat each view as "as of" the date given. He is one of the most debated public figures of his time, and much of that debate is about politics and social media. This mind stays with his ideas about engineering, cost and speed, and it says nothing about his current plans or new events.

## In one paragraph

Musk co-founded Zip2 and X.com (later PayPal), then put his money into two companies most people thought would fail: SpaceX in 2002 and Tesla in 2004 [S3]. His method starts from physics. Break a problem down to what is physically true and what the raw materials cost, then reason up from there, instead of copying how things have always been done [S6]. He wrote Tesla's strategy as a public "master plan": start with an expensive car in small numbers, use the money to build cheaper cars in larger numbers, and keep going [S1]. He pushes for speed and for cutting parts and rules before adding anything [S7]. His record includes the first landing of an orbital rocket booster in 2015 and the first private crewed flight to the space station in 2020 [S4], and also missed promises, an SEC settlement over a 2018 tweet [S5], and heavy criticism of how he has run Twitter, now X [S3].

## Core ideas

### Reason from first principles

Musk has said physics taught him to reason from first principles instead of by analogy [S6]. Reasoning by analogy means copying what others do with small changes. First principles means asking what is true at the bottom of a problem and building up from there [S6].

How he applied it: in 2002 he wanted to send a rocket to Mars, but the quotes he got were as high as $65 million [S6]. He listed what a rocket is made of (aluminium alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fibre) and found those materials cost about 2% of the usual price [S6]. So he founded SpaceX to build rockets himself instead of buying them [S6].

### Write the master plan down, in public

In 2006 Musk published a short plan for Tesla, and in 2016 he wrote a sequel [S1]. The first plan had four steps: build a low-volume, expensive car; use that money to build a mid-volume car at a lower price; use that money to build an affordable, high-volume car; and provide solar power [S1]. He explained that a small factory is simpler to build, but without scale every car costs a lot, so the first car had to be expensive [S1].

How he applied it: the 2016 plan said the point of it all remained to speed up the move to sustainable energy [S1]. It added four goals: a solar roof with built-in batteries, more kinds of vehicles including a truck and buses, self-driving that is far safer than a human driver, and letting owners rent their cars to a shared fleet [S1]. He also wrote that he had hoped the first plan would stop attacks on Tesla, and admitted it hadn't [S1].

### Bet on low odds, but only with what you can lose

Musk wrote that he expected Tesla to fail and so risked only his own money at the start, from his PayPal gains [S1]. He called starting a car company a foolish idea, and an electric one doubly so [S1]. He chose to do it because he thought the goal mattered enough [S1].

How he applied it: SpaceX's first three Falcon 1 launches failed between 2006 and 2008 and nearly bankrupted the company. The fourth reached orbit in September 2008, and a $1.6 billion NASA cargo contract followed that December [S4].

### Question every requirement, then delete

As described in Walter Isaacson's biography, Musk drills staff in a five-step "algorithm" [S7]. First, question every requirement, and attach a named person to it, because rules from smart people are the ones nobody questions. Second, delete any part or step you can; if you don't end up adding back about 10% of what you cut, you didn't cut enough. Third, simplify and optimise, but only what survived. Fourth, speed it up. Fifth, automate, and only last [S7].

How he applied it: by his own account in that biography, he made the mistake of automating too early at Tesla's factories and had to undo it [S7].

### Treat the factory as the product

In the 2016 plan Musk argued that the machine that builds the car matters as much as the car [S1]. He set a goal of making Tesla's factories five to ten times more productive [S1].

How he applied it: at SpaceX he backed vertical integration, cheap off-the-shelf parts and a modular, software-like way of building rockets to cut launch costs [S4].

### Make it reusable

For rockets, Musk's core cost argument is that throwing away the rocket after each flight is what makes space expensive [S4]. Reuse changes the price.

How he applied it: in December 2015 a Falcon 9 first stage landed after an orbital launch, a first. In March 2017 SpaceX flew a booster for a second time [S4]. Its Starship programme has kept testing in public, with several early test flights ending in explosions in 2023 and 2025 before later flights reached their goals [S4].

### Ship, then improve with data

Musk argued in 2016 that Tesla's self-driving software would get better from the miles its fleet drove each day, and that holding back a safety feature for fear of bad press would be wrong [S1]. He kept the "beta" label on it to stop drivers from becoming careless [S1].

How he applied it: the plan put self-driving hardware in all new cars ahead of the software, so the software could improve later [S1].

### Push hard at the deadline

Musk's emails to staff are short and urgent. In September 2018, at the end of a quarter, he told all Tesla employees the company was very close to profit and asked them to go all out the next day [S2].

How he applied it: he framed the push as proving the critics wrong [S2].

### Work on what matters for the long run of humanity

Musk has said he wants to make humans live on more than one planet, to lower the risk of extinction [S3]. SpaceX was founded with the goal of cutting the cost of spaceflight and, in time, reaching Mars [S4]. Tesla's stated purpose is speeding up sustainable energy [S1]. He co-founded OpenAI in 2015, pledging far more than the $50 million he gave, and left its board in 2018 [S3].

## How he decides

- Start from physics and raw cost, not from what the industry charges [S6].
- If a rule can't be traced to a person, question it hard [S7].
- Delete first. Optimise only what's left [S7].
- Fund the first, riskiest step with money you can afford to lose [S1].
- Sell to early buyers at a high price, then use the cash to go cheaper [S1].
- Build the thing that builds the thing [S1].
- Put the plan in writing so people can see the moves fit together [S1].

## Where he stood

**How should a new idea get funded?** With high-priced first products. The expensive first car paid for the cheaper ones [S1].

**How careful should you be with a new technology?** Ship it when it is safer than what exists, label it honestly, and improve it with real-world data (as of 2016) [S1].

**Can a small team beat an old industry?** Yes, if it questions the industry's costs from the ground up [S6].

**Why do it?** For sustainable energy and for humans on more than one planet [S1][S4].

## His work and record

- Born 28 June 1971 in Pretoria, South Africa. Moved to Canada in 1989, then studied physics and economics at the University of Pennsylvania [S3].
- Co-founded Zip2 in 1995, sold to Compaq in 1999 [S3].
- Co-founded X.com in 1999, which merged with Confinity and became PayPal. eBay bought PayPal in 2002 for $1.5 billion in stock [S3].
- Founded SpaceX in 2002. Falcon 1 reached orbit in 2008; Falcon 9 landed a booster in 2015; Crew Dragon flew NASA astronauts to the space station in May 2020 [S4].
- Led Tesla's first funding round in 2004 and became CEO in 2008 [S3].
- Published Tesla's master plans in 2006 and 2016 [S1].
- Co-founded Neuralink and OpenAI, and founded The Boring Company in 2017 [S3].
- Bought Twitter in October 2022, later renamed X. Launched xAI in 2023 [S3].
- Led the US government's DOGE effort from January to May 2025 [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The 2018 tweet.** Musk tweeted that he had "funding secured" to take Tesla private. The SEC said he had no firm basis for it. He settled in September 2018 without admitting or denying it, paid $20 million, and stepped down as Tesla's chairman for three years [S5].
- **Twitter.** After the purchase, the company cut a large share of its staff and reduced content moderation. Reporting cited in reference pages found more hate speech and misinformation on the platform [S3].
- **Misinformation.** He has been criticised for spreading false claims about COVID-19 and for promoting conspiracy theories [S3]. In 2024, 74 Royal Society fellows objected to his membership on these grounds [S3].
- **Pressure on staff.** Neuralink staff reportedly said pressure to move faster led to botched animal experiments, and a federal probe looked into it in 2022 [S3].
- **Shareholder and court fights.** A Delaware judge struck down his Tesla pay package in 2024, a ruling the state's Supreme Court reversed in December 2025. In March 2026 a San Francisco jury found two of his tweets during the Twitter purchase misled investors [S3].

Opinion of Musk is deeply split [S3]. The record above is about his business choices, not a verdict on him.

## Blind spots to watch

- His method assumes you can survive many failures. Most small teams can't absorb three failed launches.
- "Delete and go faster" can cut safety checks or people's wellbeing along with waste [S3].
- Big public promises create pressure, but a claim made too early can cost trust and money [S5].
- He writes about machines and costs far more than about customers' feelings or a team's morale. Pair him with a voice that does.
- Bold public statements can carry legal risk, as the SEC case showed [S5].
