# Eric Ries: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Eric Ries is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his blog Startup Lessons Learned (posts from 2008 and 2009), the principles page of The Lean Startup site, and two reference pages. Date each view to its source, mostly as of 2008 to 2011. A new book, Incorruptible, is listed for 2026, but its contents are not in our sources [S1]. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about later events.

## In one paragraph

Ries was born in 1978 and studied computer science at Yale, where he co-founded a student job site that ran out of money in the dot-com bust [S1]. He moved to Silicon Valley in 2001, worked on There.com, which soon failed, then co-founded IMVU in 2004 [S1]. Out of those wins and losses came the idea he named the lean startup in a September 2008 blog post [S1][S3]. It treats a new venture as a set of experiments: build the smallest product that teaches you something, measure how real customers respond, then pivot or keep going [S2][S7]. The method draws on Toyota's lean manufacturing, agile software and Steve Blank's customer development [S2][S3]. His 2011 book, The Lean Startup, made it famous, and in 2019 the SEC approved the Long-Term Stock Exchange he organized [S1].

## Core ideas

### A startup is an experiment

Ries argued that a startup exists to learn how to build a business that lasts [S7]. Progress isn't how much you build. It is validated learning: proof, from real tests with customers, about which parts of your vision are true [S7]. He also said a startup is an institution, not just a product, so it needs its own kind of management for extreme uncertainty [S7].

How he applied it: his principles page says entrepreneurs are everywhere, in big companies as well as garages. Anyone making something new under uncertainty counts [S7].

### Build, measure, learn

The core loop is to turn an idea into a small product, see how customers respond, and learn whether to pivot or persevere [S7]. The aim is to run the loop as fast as you can [S7]. A pivot is a planned change in the product, strategy or way of growing [S2].

How he applied it: the reference page gives Groupon as an example of a pivot. It began as an activism site called The Point [S2].

### The minimum viable product is about learning

In 2009 Ries defined the minimum viable product, or MVP, as the version of a new product that gives the most validated learning about customers for the least effort [S5]. He warned that, despite the name, it isn't about making minimal products [S5]. It takes judgment, and it adds work, because you have to talk to customers and read the data [S5]. If you just want to scratch an itch or sell something fast, you don't need one [S5].

How he applied it: IMVU's first version took about six months to launch, much faster than the nearly five years at his previous company [S5]. He also told of a team that spent two weeks building a feature nobody wanted. A simple ad test could have told them sooner [S5]. Zappos is a well-known example: its founder tested online shoe sales by photographing shoes in local shops before building a big system [S2].

### Beware vanity metrics

Vanity metrics make you look good but tell you nothing about cause or customers [S6]. His favourite example is "hits": a count of technical events, not people, totals rather than per-customer figures, and no clue what caused them [S6]. Actionable metrics break the number down so it shows cause and effect [S6]. Vanity numbers let each department credit itself when numbers rise and blame others when they fall, until everyone has a private version of reality [S6].

How he applied it: he described a company that had two bad quarters after raising money. Digging in showed one customer group leaving while a more promising group grew. The strategy was working, but the board couldn't tell insight from excuses because the company had tracked only gross revenue [S6]. He said rolling out changes to one group at a time, as controlled trials, would have given real evidence [S6].

### Ask why five times

After any problem, Ries ran a "five whys" review, an idea he took from Taiichi Ohno of Toyota [S4]. Ask why about five times to move from the symptom to the root cause, which is often a human or process problem, not a technical one [S4]. Then fix each level, with effort in proportion to the cost of the problem [S4]. Don't propose a full rewrite after a small issue [S4].

How he applied it: a site outage at IMVU traced back through a code loop and a skipped test to a new engineer who hadn't been trained. The fixes ran from restoring service to changing how new hires are taught [S4]. He said the write-ups should be in plain English and shared with everyone, and that the exact number five matters less than the habit [S4].

### Ship small, ship often

His 2008 post said lean startups run on free and open-source software, agile development and fast cycles of learning from customers [S3]. Those cut cost and waste, so a startup can try more and spend less [S3]. Some companies he worked with released new code dozens of times a day [S2].

How he applied it: IMVU built up layers of defence over time: test copies of the live site, automated tests, checks on every code change, gradual releases that reverse bad changes on their own, and alerts when numbers look wrong. That let it ship dozens of times a day, even with a lot of old untested code [S4].

### Measure progress with innovation accounting

Ries said startups need a new way to keep score [S7]. Innovation accounting means setting milestones, measuring results and choosing priorities in a way that holds founders to account, even before revenue is large [S7].

How he applied it: the same thinking runs through his vanity-metrics post. Ask how much real data was in the room for your last few big decisions. Without it, teams are rolling the dice [S6].

### Lean isn't cheap

He chose the word "lean" partly for low spending and partly for Toyota's lean thinking [S3]. The principles page stresses that lean isn't simply about spending less money [S7]. The reference page adds that it is about meeting demand with as little waste as possible, not about how much money you raise [S2].

How he applied it: he credited earlier work openly: lean manufacturing, lean software, Kent Beck's Extreme Programming and Steve Blank's customer development [S2][S3].

## How he decides

- Write down the guesses the plan depends on [S7].
- Test the riskiest guess with the smallest product that can teach you [S5].
- Measure with numbers that show cause and effect [S6].
- Change one group at a time and compare [S6].
- After a problem, ask why five times and fix each level [S4].
- Size the fix to the cost of the problem [S4].
- Then pivot or persevere [S7].

## Where he stood

**What counts as progress?** As of 2011, validated learning, not lines of code or features shipped [S7].

**Is an MVP a cheap, half-built product?** No. It is a tool for learning, and it takes judgment [S5].

**Are total sign-ups or page views good measures?** Rarely. They are often vanity metrics [S6].

**Is lean startup only for small tech firms?** No. He said entrepreneurs are everywhere, including in big companies [S7]. The method has been used at Intuit and in U.S. government programs [S2].

**When a problem happens, who's to blame?** Usually the process, not the person. Fix the system [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Running lean as a goal.** In 2010 Ben Horowitz argued the method overstresses running lean, when the real goal is to win the market. Lean supporters replied that lean isn't cost-cutting [S2].
- **Thin early evidence.** One critic said early support was mostly stories, though later studies of growth firms gave better support [S2].
- **Incremental results.** Academics including Felin, Gambardella, Stern and Zenger argued that lean manufacturing ideas fit startups poorly and lead to small, safe steps [S2].
- **Not new.** Wharton's Dan Levinthal argued many of its insights already existed in other research [S2].
- **Limits.** A 2019 article noted it may not suit products where prototypes are very costly, or teams without the skill to read experiments [S2].
- **Less success later.** The Startup Way sold less well than The Lean Startup [S1].
- **Early failures.** There.com and Catalyst Recruiting both failed. He built the method partly from those losses [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Testing works best when tests are cheap. Hardware, medicine or big buildings may not allow quick experiments [S2].
- A focus on measurable learning can miss bold, long-term bets that customers can't judge yet.
- Small tests can push teams toward small ideas [S2].
- Running experiments well needs skill with data. A team without it may fool itself [S2][S6].
- Pair him with someone who brings taste, vision or deep craft.
