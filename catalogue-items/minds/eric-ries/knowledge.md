## In one paragraph

Ries was born in 1978 and studied computer science at Yale, where he co-founded a student job site that ran out of money in the dot-com bust [S1]. He worked on There.com, which soon failed, then co-founded IMVU in 2004 [S1]. At IMVU he built a product on a brilliant-looking strategy, launched it in six months, and watched customers ignore it. Customers, not whiteboards, showed him the strategy was wrong [S8]. He named the lean startup approach in 2008 [S1][S3]. It treats a new venture as a set of experiments: build the smallest thing that teaches you something, measure how real customers behave, then pivot or keep going [S2][S7][S8]. The method borrows from Toyota's lean manufacturing, agile software and Steve Blank's customer development [S2][S3][S8]. His book made it famous in 2011, and the SEC approved the Long-Term Stock Exchange he organized in 2019 [S1].

## Core ideas

### A startup is an experiment, and progress is learning

He argued that a startup exists to learn how to build a business that lasts [S7]. In a startup the customer and what they value are unknown, so lean's usual test of waste has to change. Any effort that is not needed to learn what customers want is waste [S8]. He calls the proof validated learning: it shows up as better numbers in the core measures, backed by data from real customers [S7][S8]. He criticised the "just do it" school, where the plan is to ship and see what happens. A team on that plan is certain to succeed at seeing what happens and may learn nothing. If you cannot fail, you cannot learn [S8].

How he applied it: he treated Zappos as a model. Its founder first photographed shoes in local stores and bought them at full price if someone ordered. That tested whether people would buy shoes online, and taught him about payment, returns and service as well [S8]. He said the same thinking applies inside big companies and in government, since anyone making something new under extreme uncertainty is an entrepreneur [S7][S8].

### Find the leap-of-faith guesses

Every plan rests on assumptions. Most are ordinary. A handful, the leaps of faith, carry the whole plan [S8]. The two that matter most are the value guess (do customers get a benefit once they use it) and the growth guess (how will new customers find it) [S8]. He warned that comparisons to other successes hide the real guess. Rewrite "just as X did" as plain facts about your own customers, and the missing facts show [S8]. He also borrowed a useful pairing from Randy Komisar: look at one business that proved a question (an analog) and one that exposed a danger (an antilog), and the unanswered questions that remain are your leaps of faith [S8].

How he applied it: Facebook's early investors were impressed by two facts. More than half of users came back every day (value). Almost three-quarters of Harvard's undergraduates joined within a month with no marketing spend (growth). Ries read this as proof of two leaps tested, not luck [S8].

### Get out of the building

Facts about customers exist only outside the building, he said, echoing Steve Blank [S8]. He tied it to Toyota's rule of going to see for yourself, and told how a Toyota chief engineer drove tens of thousands of miles across North America to learn what minivan families wanted [S8]. Early talks are not meant to give final answers. They confirm that the customer has a real problem and help write a customer archetype, which stays a hypothesis until the numbers back it [S8]. He named two dangers: skipping this and building at once, or endlessly refining plans. His escape from both is the minimum viable product [S8].

### The minimum viable product is the fastest lap round the loop

An MVP is the version that gets you through one cycle of build, measure and learn with the least effort [S5][S8]. It differs from a prototype or a concept test because it tests business questions, not just design ones [S8]. It ranges from a simple advertisement to a rough working product. When in doubt, simplify, because people nearly always overrate how much a first version needs [S8]. His rule: remove any feature, process or effort that does not feed the learning you want [S8]. He gave several forms. A short demo video can test demand when the product is hard to show (Dropbox) [S8]. A concierge version gives one customer a hand-made service at a real price, so you learn what the work takes before you automate (Food on the Table) [S8]. In a human-behind-the-screen test, customers think they use the product while people do the work out of sight (Aardvark) [S8].

On quality, he said: if you do not know who the customer is, you do not know what quality is. A bad MVP teaches you what customers care about [S8]. He added that defects that slow learning are a different matter and are worth preventing [S8]. Early adopters tolerate rough products and even suspect polished ones, so extra polish for them is waste [S8].

### Innovation accounting

Normal accounting judges a new venture by plans it cannot predict. Ries proposed three steps. Use an MVP to measure where you really are, however bad. Tune the engine by making changes aimed at one rate at a time. Then reach a decision point: pivot or persevere [S8]. A good design is one that changes customer behaviour for the better. A good plan is a set of numbers you predicted in advance [S8]. He drew a sharp contrast between a team that sets a baseline, a hypothesis and an experiment, and a team that makes several changes together and celebrates if any number goes up [S8].

How he applied it: at IMVU he spent several months making the product better each week while the share of new customers who paid stayed near 1 percent [S8]. The plain table, not his effort, showed the problem [S8].

### Actionable metrics, not vanity metrics

Vanity metrics make you look good and tell you nothing about cause [S6]. Gross totals can hide that new groups of customers behave exactly like old ones [S8]. Actionable numbers pass three tests. They show cause and effect. They are accessible: simple, in units of people, and shared with everyone. They are auditable: you can check them by phoning the customers behind them [S8]. Cohort reports (each group that joined in a month gets its own row), plus split tests where half the customers get a change and half do not, are his tools [S8]. He also said a story is not done until it has been validated, and a team should stop building when too many are waiting for validation [S8].

How he applied it: he advised Grockit to test its lazy registration, a costly design feature. A group forced to register at once behaved the same as the lazy group, so the extra work was wasted and positioning probably mattered more than features [S8]. Earlier he described a company whose gross revenue hid one customer group leaving while a better group grew [S6].

### Pivot or persevere

A pivot is a structured course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis about product, strategy and engine of growth [S8]. It is not a formula: judgment and intuition stay in the loop [S8]. His warning is about the misguided choice to persevere, which can leave a company alive but not growing [S8]. Signs of the need: the product experiments keep losing force and the team feels it should be getting more done [S8]. Schedule a regular meeting a few weeks to a few months apart. Product and business leaders attend, with the full history of results and the customer conversations [S8]. He listed ten kinds of pivot: zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel and technology [S8]. Runway is not months of cash. It is the number of pivots you can still make, so the way to extend it is to get to each pivot faster [S8]. Pivots tend to come late because vanity metrics blur the failure, hypotheses are unclear, and founders fear it [S8].

### The three engines of growth

Growth runs on one of three engines, each with one governing rate [S8]. In the sticky engine, growth equals the rate of new customers minus the rate of customers lost [S8]. In the viral engine, the viral coefficient is how many new customers each new customer brings; above 1.0 growth feeds itself [S8]. In the paid engine, growth depends on the gap between what a customer is worth and what winning one costs [S8]. He advised focusing on one engine at a time, and warned that every engine runs out as its customer group is used up [S8]. He judged progress by direction: a rate that climbs steadily beats a higher rate that stays flat [S8].

### Small batches, quick releases and the five whys

Taken from Toyota, small batches find waste sooner. IMVU designed, built and released one feature at a time and made about fifty changes a day, kept safe by automated tests and a stop-the-line rule [S4][S8]. Large batches grow over time as everyone pads them to cut handoffs. He called that the large-batch death spiral [S8]. For problems, he adapted Taiichi Ohno's five whys. Ask why about five times, usually reaching a human or process cause [S4][S8]. Make a proportional investment at each level: small for minor symptoms, larger for painful ones [S8]. Without that structure the review turns into the Five Blames. Put everyone affected in the room, name a moderator with authority, begin with a narrow class of failure, and start with two rules: tolerate a mistake the first time, never allow the same one twice [S8].

## How he decides

- Write down the guesses the plan depends on, and say which is the leap of faith [S7][S8].
- Test the riskiest one first, with the smallest product that can teach you [S5][S8].
- Predict the numbers before the test [S8].
- Measure people in groups, not totals [S6][S8].
- Change one group at a time and compare [S6][S8].
- When the numbers fail, go and talk to customers, then form a new guess [S8].
- Pivot or persevere on a fixed date, with the evidence in the room [S8].
- After a problem, ask why five times and size each fix to the pain [S4][S8].

## Real decisions

### Launching a flawed product on a six-month clock

At IMVU in 2004, Ries and his co-founders set 180 days to launch an add-on that joined existing chat networks. The first version was poor, and they argued over bugs and cuts. They shipped anyway, because they feared building something nobody wanted more than they feared a bad product. Nobody tried it. They put dull money targets on early months: $300, then $350, then $400. These forced them to ask why people did not buy [S8][S5].

### Listening to customers only after the numbers failed

For weeks the team improved the product while behaviour stayed the same. Only then did Ries ask customers in. Earlier, he admitted, he had waved off bad feedback by asking for different test customers. Now he watched dozens of people refuse to invite friends, run many chat programs and want new friends, not old ones. The add-on strategy fell apart. He called himself the last to admit it. He threw away thousands of lines of code and moved to a stand-alone network. After the pivot, the same effort became more productive [S8].

### Five dollars a day of ads

To get a new report card each morning, IMVU bought about a hundred clicks a day with a five-dollar budget. Each day's visitors were a new group, so each product change got tested the next day on people who had not seen it. The funnel steps were sign-up, download, trial, repeat use and purchase [S8].

### Teleporting instead of walking

Customers asked avatars to move around. A realistic walk system would have cost months. Ries's team shipped a click-to-teleport hack instead and felt embarrassed. Customers named it among the top things they liked [S8]. His reason: customers do not care how long something took to build, only whether it meets their needs [S8].

### Missing a pivot while the numbers rose

IMVU passed $1 million a month in revenue, and Ries admits the team stopped using learning milestones. Tuning kept adding only a few points to activation, while the early-adopter market ran out and mainstream customers proved less forgiving. They fixed it late: build a customer archetype from conversations, then redesign in a sandbox with one cross-functional team, testing against the old design. The new one started worse and ended better. Revenue passed $25 million a year in 2009. He says they would have got there sooner had they pivoted sooner [S8].

### Testing a best practice at Grockit

When Grockit's founder ran his team on agile, Ries asked how they knew the priorities were right. The team said that was not their department. He introduced cohort reports and split tests. He urged them to test lazy registration, which was thought a best practice and cost a lot to maintain. The result showed no difference in registration, activation or retention, so the feature was waste [S8].

### Putting a safety net under fast releases

IMVU built test copies of the live site, automated tests, checks on every change, gradual rollouts that undo themselves and alerts on odd numbers. It chose to make failure cheap to undo, not to try to prevent every error. That let the team ship dozens of times a day [S4][S8].

### Tracing an outage to a training gap

In the case he uses to teach the method, a release failed because of a bad use of a subsystem by an engineer who was never trained, because his manager thought training took too long. The lesson was not to blame the engineer. He advised a proportional investment at each level, starting with the first hour of an eight-week training plan. At IMVU training grew this way into a program in which new hires changed production on day one. If one broke it, the line from senior people was shame on us for making it so easy [S4][S8].

## Where he stood

**What counts as progress?** Validated learning, not lines of code or features shipped [S7][S8].

**Is an MVP a cheap, half-built product?** No. It is a tool for learning, and it takes judgment [S5][S8].

**Are total sign-ups or page views good measures?** Rarely. They are usually vanity metrics [S6][S8].

**Is lean startup only for small tech firms?** No. Entrepreneurs are everywhere, including big companies and government. He added that tactics from his own stories may not transfer, and the principles must [S7][S8].

**When a problem happens, who's to blame?** Usually the process, not the person. Fix the system [S4][S8].

**Is lean the same as cheap?** No. It is about cutting waste and learning faster, not about how much money you raise [S2][S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Running lean as a goal.** In 2010 Ben Horowitz argued the method overstresses running lean, when the real goal is to win the market. Lean supporters replied that lean isn't cost-cutting [S2].
- **Thin early evidence.** One critic said early support was mostly stories, though later studies of growth firms gave better support [S2]. The book itself leans on stories, many told by him [S8].
- **Incremental results.** Academics including Felin, Gambardella, Stern and Zenger argued that lean manufacturing ideas fit startups poorly and lead to small, safe steps [S2].
- **Not new.** Wharton's Dan Levinthal argued many of its insights already existed in other research [S2].
- **Limits.** A 2019 article noted it may not suit products where prototypes are very costly, or teams without the skill to read experiments [S2].
- **His own errors.** He wrote that he was slow to see the IMVU strategy was wrong, and late to pivot after success [S8].
- **Less success later.** The Startup Way sold less well than The Lean Startup [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Testing works best when tests are cheap. Hardware, medicine or big buildings may not allow quick experiments [S2].
- A focus on measurable learning can miss bold, long-term bets that customers can't judge yet.
- Small tests can push teams toward small ideas [S2].
- Most of his cases are software or consumer web, told by him with hindsight and mostly success stories [S8].
- Running experiments well needs skill with data. A team without it may fool itself [S2][S6].
- Pair him with someone who brings taste, vision or deep craft.
