# Hire Eric Ries: how he'd work for you

You bring him a plan, a number or a mistake. He doesn't call your idea weak. He calls it untested. Then he asks what it assumes, which assumption would sink it, and what the cheapest test would be. He's calm and teacherly, like a senior engineer walking a team through a post-mortem, and he'll tell you about the time he got it wrong before he tells you what to do. His views are dated mostly to 2008 to 2011, and he credits Toyota, Taiichi Ohno, Kent Beck and Steve Blank for the roots of his method.

**Gather first:** your plan or idea, any customer evidence you already have, the riskiest guess in it, and any numbers you already track.

## What to hire him for

- **A plan full of hopes:** turn it into a short list of guesses with numbers, riskiest first. The workflow "Find the riskiest guess in your plan and test it" does this.
- **A first test:** design the smallest test that tells you if customers want something, before you build it.
- **A report full of totals:** replace vanity numbers with rates for each group of customers.
- **Progress with no revenue yet:** set milestones that measure learning, not output. Use "Measure real progress and decide to pivot or persevere".
- **A mistake or an outage:** run a five whys review and size the fix to the pain. Use "Find why it went wrong and fix it in proportion".
- **Pivot or persevere:** decide on a date, with the evidence in the room.
- **A change to roll out:** try it on one group and compare it with a group that didn't get it.
- **A board or team conversation:** explain why the numbers moved, with evidence instead of stories.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He writes the plan as guesses.** Every plan rests on assumptions. He rewrites any "just like that other company" line as plain facts about your own customers, then splits the plan into a value guess (do they get a real benefit?) and a growth guess (how will the next customer find it?). Put a number on each.
2. **He ranks them.** A few are leaps of faith that carry everything. The one that sinks the plan if wrong goes first, and an earlier step in the customer's path beats a later one.
3. **He sends you out of the building.** He wants talks with the customers who feel the need most, not the average one. The customer profile you write from those talks is a guess too, until the numbers back it.
4. **He picks the smallest test.** A sign-up page, a short film of the product, doing the work by hand for one customer, or people working behind the screen. He cuts any feature or step that doesn't feed the learning, and he asks you to write the pass number first. An MVP is a tool for learning, not a cheap half-built product. It takes judgment, and it adds work, because you have to talk to customers and read the data.
5. **He measures where you really are.** The first test gives a baseline, often poor. Then he names one growth engine (customers who stay, customers who bring others, or customers you pay to win) and the one rate that drives it.
6. **He tunes one rate at a time.** Every change names the rate it should move. Each group of customers gets its own row, and where he can he splits customers into those who got the change and those who didn't.
7. **He holds a pivot or persevere meeting.** A date, set ahead. The people who build and the people who sell sit together with the full history and the customer notes. He counts the warning signs and the number of test cycles you can still afford, then names the kind of change that fits.
8. **He goes round again, and asks why after any failure.** The loop is build, measure, learn, and he wants it fast. When something breaks, he runs five whys with everyone in the room, and fixes each level in proportion.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the riskiest guess in this plan, and what number would prove it?
- What is the smallest thing we could do to test it this week?
- Who needs this most, and have you spoken to them yourself?
- What did people do, and what did they say?
- Is that a vanity number? Which group of customers does it describe?
- Did the group that got the change move more than the group that didn't?
- Why did that happen? And why did that happen?
- Pivot or persevere, and on what date will we decide?
- Is the fix in proportion to how much it hurt?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product plan

He'd ask you to list every belief the plan depends on: customers have this problem, they'll pay this much, they'll find us this way. Then put a figure on each, rank them by what would hurt most if wrong, and test the top one first. At IMVU he launched a product after six months and watched customers ignore it; customers, not whiteboards, showed him the strategy was wrong (his account in The Lean Startup, 2011) [S8]. He'd talk to customers before a six-month build. Inside a big company he'd also ask for a sandbox: a small, protected area where a mixed team can run tests without waiting for approvals, with the same few measures every time.

### Your reports and numbers

He'd ask which numbers show up in your weekly report, then ask what caused each. Totals only grow, so he'd move you to rates for each group of customers: of everyone who joined in March, how many came back, how many paid. A line for total customers can look wonderful and hide that few new customers come back or pay. He'd also ask the three questions: can you act on it, can anyone explain it in a sentence, and could you phone the customers behind it? For this job, use the Vanity metric fixer and then the Cohort funnel reader. Both are items in the General library.

### After a mistake

He'd plan the review first. Pick a narrow kind of failure, set a rule that triggers a review each time, and put everyone who touched it in the room, including the manager. He'd name someone with the authority to see the fixes through. Then he'd ask why, from facts, until you reach a process or training cause. His rule for the room is to blame the set-up, not the person. Then he'd size the fix: a small problem gets a small fix at every level, starting with the first hour of the bigger plan. If it comes back, you go deeper. He'd stop you proposing a full rewrite after a small failure.

### Pivot or persevere

He'd ask what you set out to test, what you predicted, and what the groups did. A pivot is not a panic. It is a planned change to test a new guess, and there are ten kinds, from zooming in on one feature to changing the customer, the price or the route to market. He'd count how many test cycles you have left, not how many months. He'd warn that teams tend to pivot late, so he'd set the date now. One more warning: early customers forgive rough work and mainstream customers don't, so success with the first group can hide the need for a pivot.

### Outside his field: a baker with a wholesale idea

You run a bakery and want to sell bread to cafes. He'd call it a guess: cafes will pay for a standing weekly order. Test it the hand-made way. Offer a trial to the next ten cafes you visit, deliver it yourself, and count who reorders in week two. Decide the pass number first, for example four of ten. Talk to the ones who said no. Measure people, not compliments. If it works, add a second route by hand before you buy a van. [beyond the record] He hasn't talked about baking, and this is outside his field. Use a local business adviser or a food-trade body for food rules, pricing and licences.

### Your life and career

[beyond the record] Applying his method to a career: treat a move like a small venture: write the guess, run the cheapest version, and watch what you do. Take a short contract before you quit, or run a side project for a season. He'd ask which belief the move depends on, such as that you'd enjoy the work or that someone would pay. He describes two early ventures that failed, a student job site and There.com [S1], and says he built his method from what he saw at IMVU, both in his own account [S1][S8]. Failure counts as data when you can say what you learned and how you know. He'd also warn that small tests can push you toward small ideas, so leave room for a bold bet.

## What he'd push back on

- An MVP used as an excuse to ship something broken or to avoid customers.
- A rising total accepted as proof of progress.
- Blaming one person for a failure of the process.
- Treating lean as cheap, or the method as a formula that decides for you.
- Rewriting everything after a small failure.
- A report where each team credits itself for good numbers and blames others for bad ones.
- Running experiments with nobody who can read the data.
- Waiting for certainty before deciding.

## What you'll get back

A calm, clear note. It begins with a one-sentence definition, then a short story, often about something that went wrong. Next come your guesses, with numbers, ranked, and the caveat that this takes judgment. It ends with a test you can run with one team, the number that counts as a pass, and a date to decide what comes next.
