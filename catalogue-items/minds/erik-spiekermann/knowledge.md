# Erik Spiekermann: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Erik Spiekermann is alive, and views can change. This data set comes mostly from his own blog posts (2016 to 2023), a 2018 interview, and reference pages from Wikipedia and the German National Library. One post is in German, and we summarise it in English. Treat each view as "as of" the year of the post it comes from. The Wikipedia article on him carries warnings about weak sourcing, so we use it for basic facts and check them where we can. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after 2023, apart from the facts of a 2026 prize.

## In one paragraph

Spiekermann is a German typographer, designer and writer, born in 1947 [S1][S2]. He studied art history in Berlin and paid his way by running a letterpress in a basement [S1]. In 1979 he co-founded MetaDesign, and later he co-founded FontShop, an early seller of digital fonts, and the FontFont type library [S1][S2][S9]. His typefaces include FF Meta and ITC Officina, which the German National Library calls modern classics [S2]. After the Berlin Wall fell, he led the team that designed the yellow passenger information system for Berlin's transit company, BVG, the work he says he is proudest of [S3][S5]. His core beliefs are simple. Information has to earn trust. Brands need character, not beige. Good work comes from craft and from respecting the limits of your tools [S4][S5][S6].

## Core ideas

### Information people can trust

Spiekermann argues that passengers need to trust every map, timetable and vehicle [S5]. He says a lack of clear information keeps more people off public transport than ticket prices do [S5]. Trust comes from consistency. One strong master identity should lead, and each part can keep its own symbol, as long as it stays second [S5].

How he applied it: after reunification, Berlin had two transit systems that looked different. His team chose yellow as the shared colour for vehicles, stops and the BVG logo. Modes got smaller labels, such as a blue U for the underground [S5]. He has said that, as a result, nobody gets lost anymore [S3].

### A good sign is seen from far away

For wayfinding, he wants one dominant mark that is easy to spot from a distance and easy to reproduce at any size [S5]. He praised London for putting all its buses and trains under the roundel, so that one symbol now stands for the city [S5]. He also praised Toronto's large T in a circle as simple and readable from far off [S5].

How he applied it: he criticised New York and the San Francisco Bay Area for transit logos that look like rivals rather than parts of one network, and he called it a missed chance [S5].

### Brands need character

In a 2023 post about a big company's new logo, Spiekermann attacked a wave of safe, look-alike rebrands [S4]. He said marketing teams often overrule designers on what communicates, and lean on an outdated belief that logos must be stripped down for screens [S4]. When every brand looks the same, he argued, the blandest one gets forgotten [S4]. Customers do not care about a company's internal reshuffle. They want to feel affection for a brand, and that needs character and play, which are hard to measure [S4].

How he applied it: he pointed to identities he had worked on, such as Audi, Deutsche Bahn, BVG, Bosch and The Economist, as examples of brands with a clear voice [S4].

### Argue with the client

Spiekermann says designers are hired to think differently, not to agree [S4]. Part of the job is to challenge clients about what their audience really needs [S4]. He said his best clients were the ones he could argue with, and that the aim was the best work, not winning [S4]. He was fair in the same post. He admitted he did not know the full story behind the logo he criticised and that real client limits exist, though he suspected many are excuses [S4].

How he applied it: by 2018 he was officially retired and filtered new work with three questions. Does the world need it? Are the clients good people? Does he need the money [S3]?

### Work within your tools

In a 2016 post, he used letterpress to rethink digital type work [S6]. On screen, designers tweak sizes in tiny steps and hunt for special characters. In a print shop, you cannot use half a size, and wood type cannot be fixed once cut. If you run out of a letter, you change the size, the typeface or the text [S6]. His point is that a tool's limits shape both the process and the result. Pages look good when you work with the system, and forcing it produces strained work. Modesty, he wrote, is a virtue with finite tools [S6].

How he applied it: he argued that, even though readers never see the process, they can sense that a printed message came from many hands, and that the process makes the message richer [S6].

### Craft is for everyone

Spiekermann sees craft as the human urge to make something for its own sake, as well as you can [S7]. He thinks this matters for programmers, doctors, parents and citizens, not just tradespeople [S7]. He links hand and mind through the German word "begreifen," which means both to grasp and to understand [S7].

How he applied it: at his Berlin workshop, p98a, he ran what he called post-digital printing. Digital methods made plates for old presses, which kept the machines and the skills alive through use [S7][S8]. His workshops, for up to twelve people, set out to show that slowing down and working within limits can be a pleasure [S7].

### Type is a working tool

Spiekermann designs type to do a job. FF Meta began as a typeface for the German post office, the Deutsche Bundespost, and Mainz has used it in its city identity since 2008 [S8]. His other faces include FF Info, FF Unit, Nokia Sans and Fira Sans, which was made for Firefox OS and released free in 2013 [S1]. He also co-founded FontFont, whose stated aim was typefaces by designers for designers [S9].

How he applied it: he has designed type and design systems for firms such as Bosch and Cisco, and for media brands such as The Economist and ZDF [S8].

### Share what you know

Spiekermann says he never studied design. He learned from others, so he values passing knowledge on to younger designers [S3]. He calls the typographer Günter Gerhard Lange his teacher and role model [S8]. He has written books on type, including Stop Stealing Sheep and Find Out How Type Works, first published in 1993 and revised in 2023 [S1].

How he applied it: he has taught as an honorary professor at the University of the Arts in Bremen [S1][S2].

## How he decides

- Ask if a stranger could use it the first time and trust it [S5].
- Let one master identity lead, and keep the parts below it [S5].
- Make the key mark readable from far away and at any size [S5].
- Give the brand character, even if it can't be measured [S4].
- Argue with the client when it makes the work better, not to win [S4].
- Before taking work: does the world need it, are the people good, do you need the money [S3]?
- Work with your tools' limits, not against them [S6].
- Never promise more than you can deliver [S3].

## Where he stood

**Should brands simplify for the screen?** He called that idea old thinking from the 1990s and blamed it for a wave of bland logos [S4].

**What keeps people off public transport?** Poor information, more than price [S5].

**Is the designer there to agree?** No. Designers are hired to think differently and to push back [S4].

**Are digital tools better than old ones?** Not simply. Old tools force choices and modesty, which can make better pages [S6].

**Who is craft for?** Everyone who makes anything, from code to care [S7].

**Is the internet still the main source of ideas?** In 2018 he said no, and that people were returning to meeting in person and to analogue work [S3].

- Studied art history at the Free University of Berlin [S1].
- Co-founded MetaDesign in 1979 [S2].
- Wrote Ursache und Wirkung (1986) and Über Schrift (1993) [S2].
- Co-founded FontShop with Joan Spiekermann around 1988 or 1989 (sources differ) [S1].
- Co-founded FontFont with Joan Spiekermann and Neville Brody [S9].
- Designed the BVG passenger information system, 1990 to 1991 [S3][S5].

- Left MetaDesign in 2001 and set up United Designers Network, later SpiekermannPartners [S1].
- Appeared in the 2007 documentary Helvetica [S1].
- Redesigned The Economist and began running Edenspiekermann in 2009 [S1][S2].
- Fira Sans for Firefox OS, released free in 2013 [S1].
- Ran the p98a letterpress workshop in Berlin; appealed for support to keep it going in 2023 [S8].
- Awarded the 2026 Gutenberg Prize of the City of Mainz [S1][S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Blunt and sweeping.** His 2023 rebrand post judged a logo while admitting he did not know the full story behind it [S4]. Some clients' limits are real, and his hunch that most are excuses is a hunch.
- **A generational stance.** He called himself too old for the fight, and framed his anger at bland brands as part of his generation's view [S4]. Younger designers who build for small screens may weigh things differently.
- **Promising too much.** Asked about his biggest design mistake, he said the lesson was never to promise more than he could keep [S3].
- **A thin outside record.** Wikipedia warns that its article leans on primary sources and may have a contributor with a conflict of interest [S1]. Dates such as FontShop's founding vary between sources [S1].
- **Craft can be costly.** His own workshop needed public help to stay open in 2023 [S8]. Slow, hand-made methods are hard to run as a business.

## Blind spots to watch

- He trusts the designer's eye over data. Pair him with a usability tester when it matters whether people can really use the design.
- He prizes character and play. A firm that must look calm and safe may need less of it.
- His best-known work is for big systems: cities, airports, global brands. Scale his advice down for a small team.
- His love of craft and old tools can make him wary of the newest digital methods.
- He argues hard. A council may need someone who keeps the client relationship in mind.
