## In one paragraph

Hemingway was an American writer who learned his trade as a newspaper reporter and turned its plain, fast style into fiction [S4]. As a cub reporter at *The Kansas City Star* he was told to use short sentences, and he never stopped [S4]. He was badly wounded as a Red Cross ambulance driver in Italy in 1918, and moved to Paris in 1921, where Gertrude Stein, Ezra Pound and James Joyce were part of his world [S4]. His novels and stories, from *The Sun Also Rises* (1926) to *The Old Man and the Sea* (1952), changed the sound of American prose [S4]. His central idea was the iceberg: if a writer truly knows something, he can leave it out, and the reader will still feel it [S4][S5]. He won the Pulitzer Prize in 1953 and the Nobel Prize in 1954 [S4]. Critics have also attacked his portrayal of women and of Jewish characters, and the macho world of his books [S4].

## Core ideas

### The iceberg: leave the big thing out

In *Death in the Afternoon* (1932) he compared good writing to an iceberg, whose dignity of movement comes from having only one-eighth above water [S5]. A writer who knows enough can leave things out, and the reader will feel them as strongly as if they were stated [S4]. But leaving things out because you don't know them only makes hollow places in the writing [S4].

How he applied it: he said that in "Big Two-Hearted River", a story about Nick Adams fishing, all mention of the war was left out, though the story is about a soldier coming home [S5]. In *A Moveable Feast* he wrote that he cut the ending of "Out of Season", where the old man hangs himself, because leaving it out would strengthen the story [S5].

### Short, plain sentences

*The Kansas City Star*'s style guide told reporters to use short sentences, and that rule became the base of his prose [S4]. About 70% of his sentences are simple, with no clauses hanging off them [S4]. He often links clauses with "and" instead of commas, which gives a sense of things happening right now [S4].

How he applied it: the 1924 *in our time* vignette of six cabinet ministers being shot uses short, flat sentences about rain, wet leaves and nailed-shut shutters. One minister is too sick to stand and sits down in a puddle. There is no comment on the horror. The facts carry it [S1].

### Show emotion through things, not statements

Many followers mistook his plain style for coldness. His aim was to show emotion realistically, through images rather than by naming it [S4]. Critics have called his prose photographic, built from snapshots [S4].

How he applied it: *A Farewell to Arms* opens with a house in a village, a river with white pebbles, troops marching and dust powdering the leaves [S3]. The sadness of war is in the dust and the falling leaves, not in any statement about it [S3].

### Rhythm and repetition

He uses repetition and long chains of "and" to build a rhythm, so that plain words take on weight [S4]. In the *Farewell* opening, words like road, leaves, dust and troops come back again and again, and the sentence about them runs on like the marching it describes [S3].

How to use it: read the passage aloud. If the rhythm is right, plain words can carry deep feeling.

### Dialogue that talks around the point

His characters often avoid saying what matters. In *The Sun Also Rises*, the narrator Jake Barnes tells Robert Cohn that you can't get away from yourself by moving from one place to another [S2]. The novel ends with Jake's flat, ironic reply to Brett's dream of what might have been: "Isn't it pretty to think so?" [S2]. The pain lies under the words.

### Write what you know, exactly

His years as a reporter in Kansas City and Toronto taught him to report events right in front of him with little context, and he brought that habit into his fiction [S5]. He admired Rudyard Kipling's compression and cut his own prose as far as it would go [S5]. His biographer Carlos Baker said he learned to get the most from the least and to suggest more than the literal truth [S5].

How he applied it: the opening of *The Sun Also Rises* introduces Robert Cohn through a few hard facts: a boxing title at Princeton, a broken nose, a reason he learned to box [S2].

### The world breaks people

His books return to love, war, travel, loss, wilderness and death [S4]. Facing death with courage and dignity marks an authentic life in many readings of his work [S4]. Nature is often a place where men find renewal [S4]. In *A Farewell to Arms*, the narrator Frederic Henry reflects that the world breaks every one, and that afterward many are strong at the broken places [S3].

### Stop while you know what comes next

In the Paris Review interview, Hemingway described writing early, while the house is quiet, starting each day by rereading what he had written and stopping at a point where he knew what would happen next [S7]. He said the hard part was waiting for the next session [S7]. He guarded the hours: he called visitors and the phone the destroyers of work and said worry destroys the ability to write [S7].

### Revise at every stage

He said he rewrote each day's pages up to where he stopped, then went over the whole draft again, then used the typed copy and the proofs as more chances to fix it [S7][S8]. The last chance, he said, is in the proofs [S8].

## How he decides

- Know far more than you put on the page [S4][S5].
- Cut what you know, never what you don't know [S4].
- Use short sentences and plain words [S4].
- Name things. Let the reader see them [S3][S4].
- Let feeling come from images, not adjectives [S4].
- Let characters talk around the real subject [S2].
- Build rhythm with repetition and "and" [S3][S4].
- Report the facts and let them work [S1][S5].
- Stop each day where you know the next step, reread, and revise at every stage [S7][S8].
- Keep sentences short, openings short, and say things in the positive [S10].

## Real decisions

These are choices visible on the page, or recorded by him in the Paris Review excerpts, or documented by the secondary pages. Where a reason is ours, it is marked as our reading.

### Reporting an execution with no comment
In the 1924 vignette, six cabinet ministers are shot at half-past six against a hospital wall. The text gives the rain, the wet leaves, the nailed shutters and the sick man who sits down in a puddle. It never calls the scene cruel (our reading: the facts carry the feeling) [S1].

### Taking the newspaper's rules as his own
At The Kansas City Star in 1917 and 1918 he worked under a style sheet that asked for short sentences, short first paragraphs, plain forceful English and positive statements [S10]. In 1940 he called these the best rules he had learned for writing, according to a 1954 Atlantic article [S10]. He also later praised the paper's habit of saying whether someone was slightly or dangerously hurt, since every injury is serious [S10]. He kept the habits when he moved to fiction [S4].

### Introducing Robert Cohn by facts
The Sun Also Rises opens its portrait of Cohn with a boxing title he did not care about, a flattened nose and the shyness that drove him to learn. The narrator adds that nobody at Princeton remembered the title. The reader sees the man through hard facts and a dry aside [S2]. The published opening is itself a cut: he removed about a chapter and a half that began with Lady Brett Ashley and a passage on the narrator's use of "I", after F. Scott Fitzgerald urged him to [S11]. The page gives no dates or reasons from Hemingway.

### Ending the novel on a ride and a short line
The book ends with Jake and Brett in a taxi, a mounted policeman raising his baton, and the car slowing so that Brett is pressed against him. Jake answers her wish with "Isn't it pretty to think so?" The feeling stays under the line [S2].

### Cutting what the reader should feel but not read
He later wrote that he cut the ending of "Out of Season", where an old man hangs himself, because leaving it out made the story stronger. He also said all mention of the war was left out of "Big Two-Hearted River", though the story is about a soldier home from it [S5].

### Stopping each day in mid-flow
He told the Paris Review that he wrote early in the morning, reread the earlier pages, and quit at a moment when he knew what came next, so the next day began from a known point [S7]. This gave him a clear place to start the next day, though he found the wait until then the hardest part [S7]. He named the phone and visitors as the destroyers of work [S7].

### Rewriting the last page of A Farewell to Arms
Describing his rewriting, he said he rewrote the last page of A Farewell to Arms 39 times and that the trouble was getting the words right [S7][S8]. He used each later stage, the typed copy and the proofs, as one more chance to mend the text [S8]. (Press reports of surviving drafts give other counts; we rely on his own figure.)

### Dropping the epilogue of For Whom the Bell Tolls
In summer 1940 he wrote an epilogue and sent it to his editor Max Perkins, then asked on 26 August whether the book should end without it [S9]. He said it really stops where Jordan feels his heart against the forest floor, that he had a strong tendency to tie everything up, and that an epilogue seemed to date the book [S9]. Earlier, on 21 April 1940, he had wired the title after weighing about twenty-five [S9]. At the galley stage he made Jordan work under communist discipline instead of carrying a party card, after the publisher objected [S9].

## Where he stood (themes and views from the record)

**What makes writing strong?** What is left out, if the writer knows it (as he wrote in 1932) [S4][S5].

**Should a writer explain feelings?** No. Show the things that cause them [S4].

**Can you escape your problems by travelling?** His narrator in *The Sun Also Rises* says no [S2]. That is a character's view.

**What does suffering do to people?** In *A Farewell to Arms*, it breaks them, and some grow strong where they broke [S3].

- Six months as a cub reporter at *The Kansas City Star*, 1917 [S4].
- Wounded by mortar fire on 8 July 1918 while serving as a Red Cross ambulance driver in Italy [S4].
- Moved to Paris in 1921 as a correspondent for the *Toronto Star* [S4].
- Published *in our time* in Paris in 1924, edited by Ezra Pound [S1].
- *In Our Time* (1925), *The Sun Also Rises* (1926), *A Farewell to Arms* (1929), *For Whom the Bell Tolls* (1940), *The Old Man and the Sea* (1952) [S4].
- Set out the iceberg idea in *Death in the Afternoon* (1932) [S5].
- Pulitzer Prize for Fiction (1953) and Nobel Prize in Literature (1954) [S4].
- Badly hurt in two plane crashes in Africa in 1954 and suffered poor health after [S4].
- *A Moveable Feast*, his Paris memoir, was published after his death [S4][S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Women.** Feminist critics have attacked his female characters, and Nina Baym called Brett Ashley and Margot Macomber outstanding examples of his "bitch women" [S4]. More recent scholars have found more complexity in them [S4].
- **Antisemitism.** One critic argued *The Sun Also Rises* makes Robert Cohn unattractive because he is Jewish. That portrayal has been widely criticised as antisemitic [S4]. The book's opening dwells on Cohn being treated as a Jew at Princeton [S2].
- **Misogyny and homophobia.** In recent decades critics have described parts of his work in those terms [S4].
- **Later work.** *Across the River and into the Trees* (1950) got bad reviews [S4]. Critic Jackson Benson argued the distance that made the early books work was missing from it [S5].
- **Hard to read.** Some readers find the omissions so deep that the stories are hard to follow without outside context [S5].
- **False attributions.** The famous six-word story "For sale: baby shoes, never worn" is popularly credited to him, but versions existed when he was a child and the link to him first appeared in 1989. He very likely didn't write it [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- Spare style can turn flat or monotonous if you cut the wrong things. Leave out only what you truly know.
- His world is mostly men at war, in sport and in the wild. Women and outsiders are often seen from outside.
- His views of Jewish and female characters reflect his time and his prejudices. Don't copy them.
- Short sentences are not always clearer. Some ideas need a longer, careful sentence.
- He is a stylist, not a planner. Pair him with someone on structure and plot.
