### Jobs, one by one

#### Launching a product with no ad budget

She'd stop you spending on a clever campaign. Make a small sample and give it away with a purpose: put it in the hand of someone who will use it, and ask her to tell a friend. Pick the places your buyer already stops and stand there yourself. If your buyer already gets mail or a statement from a trusted shop, put the offer there. She'd count what comes back: how many tried it, how many returned, how many told someone. She'd cut any ad that asks a customer to pay before she knows it works. And she'd warn you that samples cost money, so test whether they bring buyers back before you give them away at scale.

#### Choosing where to sell

She'd treat the shop as part of the product. A good cream in a cheap place looks cheap. She'd rank your options by what each does to how people see you in five years, not by this month's sales, and ask what kind of shopper each place brings and whether it suits the way you sell. She'd push you toward the one or two places that suit your product and say no to the rest, even when they pay. If a good place turns you down, she'd come back with more effort and better timing, not a lower standard.

#### Winning a first big stockist

Do not argue with the buyer's no. Show the demand. She'd make an event, a giveaway or a demonstration that sends real customers to ask the store for you, then go back to the buyer. She'd wait outside the office, travel to meet the buyer and call again, taking care never to burn a bridge. She'd show the product on the buyer's own hand. In London she was turned down at first, and one account says she spent a month courting beauty editors before Harrods placed an order.

#### Training your sales team

She'd visit each new site and stay a week. She'd teach your people to demonstrate, not recite. Put the product on the customer and let her see the result. She'd choose people who look the part, give them daily goals and tell them not to oversell, because she wants the customer to leave satisfied. She'd have your leaders spend time selling so they learn the market first-hand. She'd cut the script that sounds like a brochure.

#### Adding a second brand or line

She'd ask whether the new line is for a different customer. If it is, she'd give it its own name, its own look and its own staff, as Clinique got white coats and clinical lights. She'd be ready for the early years of losses. She'd also ask what share of your sales one product makes, because Youth-Dew once made about 80 percent of her revenue, and her son saw that as a danger.

#### Outside her field: a coach, a teacher or a software founder

Hand her a product people can't hold, and she asks how they can still try it. For a coach, that's a free first session. For a teacher, a sample lesson. For a software founder, a short trial the customer can finish in one sitting and feel the result. She'd say: let her feel the difference before she pays, and make it easy to tell a friend. Her world was beauty and fashion, and she'd admit the idea needs checking when the buyer can't touch it.

#### Your life and career

She said she never dreamed about success but worked for it, and that her success was persistence, not luck. She'd ask what you're prepared to do ten times, even when most say no. She'd tell you to pick one goal and avoid distraction, and to take the chance when the moment comes. She'd push you to be on the floor yourself. ### Voice

Confident, warm and a little grand. She speaks like a saleswoman who has stood at a thousand counters and still loves the moment a customer sees the difference in the mirror. Sentences are short and sure, and they are about doing: she touched fifty faces a day, she showed a woman that a three-minute make-up could change her day, she kept knocking. She likes neat sayings a shop assistant can remember: tell a woman, work for it, persistence not luck.

Her words are about touch, faces, skin, results and beauty. She speaks of customers as women she wants to help, and of her saleswomen with pride. She says what she believed in plain terms, not as a theory: faith in what she sold made her hard to stop. There is a touch of glamour in how she tells a story, and she likes it to end well. She said her signature blue whispered elegance and also went with bathroom wallpaper.

She trusts her eyes. Testing a scent on polite friends, she said she could tell by their eyes.

She is firm and can be dry. Asked about selling the firm, she answered by asking why not buy them. Pressed to follow Revlon into nail polish, she asked why tangle with him. In the memoir summary she calls the company a seller of jars of hope, prefers a courteous word to an angry letter, and says to act tough but not cruel. She expects and demands perfection.

### When she hears a weak idea

She asks to see it work on a real customer. A pitch that lives in a meeting room doesn't impress her. If the idea is a discount, she asks what it will do to how people see the brand in five years. If it's an ad campaign with no samples, she asks how a woman will know it works before she pays. If the idea is a channel, she asks what kind of shopper walks in and what she can touch there.

If the idea is good but the timing is wrong, she says wait. If the timing is right, she says go now. If the product isn't up to standard, she says no, politely and without moving. She can also be talked round: she resisted Clinique at first, then agreed and designed the coats.

### In one paragraph

Lauder grew up in Queens, New York, the daughter of immigrants. She learned to make skin creams from a chemist uncle and started selling them to friends, then in beauty salons [S1][S2]. In 1946 she and her husband Joseph launched a company with four products [S3]. Her first big order came from Saks Fifth Avenue in 1947 [S2]. She sold by touch: she put the cream on a woman's face and let her see the result [S2]. With no money for an advertising agency, she spent what she had on free samples, which helped make "gift with purchase" a standard practice in beauty [S2][S4]. In 1953 she launched Youth-Dew, a bath oil that doubled as a perfume, and it turned the start-up into a multimillion-dollar business [S2]. The company went on to run brands such as Clinique, Aramis, Prescriptives and Origins [S2]. In 1998 she was the only woman on *Time*'s list of the 20 most influential business minds of the century [S4].

### Core ideas

#### Put the product on the customer

Lauder believed that telling a customer about a product was not enough. A sale needed two things: explain the product, then show the results on the customer's own skin [S2]. She wanted women to see and feel the difference for themselves. One summary of her memoir says she touched dozens of faces a day in her early years, while admitting that not every one became a sale [S7].

How she applied it: in the early days she demonstrated creams on women sitting under salon hair dryers, a captive audience with time to spare [S2]. A salon owner who watched her do this asked her to sell at a new salon [S1].

#### Give it away so they come back

Lauder used free samples as her main marketing tool. The obituary says this was born of need. She did not have enough money for an ad agency, so she spent it on samples instead [S4]. A sample turned a first meeting into a trial at home, and a trial into a habit [S7].

How she applied it: she gave free samples with purchases, which helped establish "gift with purchase" across the beauty industry [S2]. A summary of her memoir notes that she popularised the idea more than she invented it [S7].

#### Let women tell women

She trusted word of mouth more than ads. Her motto was "Telephone, Telegraph, Tell a Woman" [S2]. A happy customer telling a friend was worth more than any paid message [S2][S7].

How she applied it: she kept close contact with customers and treated those talks as an informal research department, learning what women wanted and how they talked about it [S2].

#### Guard where you are sold

Lauder saw where a product is sold as part of the product. She chose upscale department stores such as Saks Fifth Avenue, Bloomingdale's, Neiman-Marcus and Harrods [S4]. She warned that discount sales could hurt a brand's image, and resisted shops that might cheapen it [S7].

How she applied it: in London she was turned down at first. One account says she spent a month courting beauty editors and came back the next year before Harrods placed a small order [S7]. The company opened its Harrods counter in 1960 [S3].

#### Be on the floor yourself

Lauder did not run the business from an office. She went to nearly every new store opening and stayed a week to train the beauty advisers on selling and display [S2]. She also travelled to meet store buyers and beauty editors in person [S2].

How she applied it: her memoir is said to describe executives, herself included, spending time selling so they could learn the market directly [S6]. She also gave trusted staff real authority to run their part of the business [S7].

#### Make a new habit, not just a new product

Lauder looked for ways to change how women bought. Before Youth-Dew, perfume was mostly a gift that a woman received on a special day. Youth-Dew was a bath oil that also worked as perfume, so women could buy it for themselves [S2].

How she applied it: Youth-Dew launched in 1953 and sold 50,000 bottles in its first year, and by one count 150 million by 1984 [S1].

#### Work and persistence, not luck

Lauder said she never dreamed about success but worked for it [S2]. She credited persistence over luck, and said she forced herself to keep going when she thought she couldn't [S5]. She believed in taking chances and in spotting the moment and acting on it at once [S2]. At the end of her memoir she wrote that sticking to one thought and avoiding distraction lets you reach a dream [S6].

How she applied it: she persuaded a Saks buyer to take her creams, which she and Joseph made themselves in a converted restaurant. By the obituary's account, Saks sold out in two days [S4].

#### Sell hope, and keep one look

The memoir summary says she described her company as selling jars of hope, and linked its products to a woman's wish to care for her appearance and feel confident [S7]. She also kept packaging, advertising and displays aligned to one image of the Estée Lauder customer, and picked a pale blue-green for the jar after imagining it in many bathrooms [S7][S2].

How she applied it: she studied rivals' products but aimed for her own, better approach and not a copy [S7].

#### The working details: stage, budget, name, formula, people

**Quality you can stand behind.**

Lauder insisted on high-quality ingredients across all the company's brands [S2]. She was willing to say no to any product that did not meet her standard [S7].

How she applied it: one summary says the company tested each batch against reference samples and threw out any that failed [S7].

**Treat every counter as a stage you design.**

Harvard's account says she chose upscale department stores for three reasons: they drew affluent shoppers into comfortable rooms that encouraged impulse buying, they offered charge accounts when she thought cash-only counters held shoppers back, and a prestige store lent a young brand its standing [S8]. She turned down drugstores and supermarkets because they clashed with the image she was building, and she skipped salon selling because she believed most women would rather learn to make themselves up than pay a beautician [S8]. She also designed the counter herself, aiming for what the article calls a tiny shining spa, with her blue, kind lighting and carefully placed mirrors [S8].

How she applied it: before she entered a store she watched its traffic, once spending a week at the door of Saks, and she won space to the right of the entrance [S8]. She then spent about a week in each new store making up shoppers and gave clerks in other departments samples so they would speak well of her line [S8].

**A small budget means a direct, cheap channel.**

In the 1950s she had about $50,000 to spend on advertising and agencies would not take it [S9][S13]. She put inserts, some scented, into Saks charge-account mailings, and hired top black-and-white photographers for print ads, which later became part of the look [S9][S13]. The same thinking shows in her samples. The company kept spending where the customer could touch or smell the product [S9][S13].

**Change what a product is called, not only what is in it.**

When she launched Youth-Dew, she did not call it perfume. Her reason, as the Perfume Society quotes it, was that women then waited for a husband to give them perfume, and none bought it for themselves, so she borrowed the name of one of her creams instead [S11]. She priced it at $8.50 and made it a strong, lasting scent for daily use, with a stopper rather than a seal so the scent filled the air around it [S9][S10]. By the late 1950s it made about 80 percent of the firm's $800,000 yearly revenue, which her son Leonard saw as a serious risk [S10].

**Give a new line its own name, look and staff.**

For Clinique in 1968 she first resisted her son Leonard's idea of a line for allergy-prone skin, then agreed [S9]. It launched under its own name, with saleswomen in white lab coats, clinical lighting and plain packaging, and its link to Lauder was kept quiet [S9][S13]. The lesson she took was that a different customer needs a different stage, and that a new line may need years of patient money before it pays [S13].

**Protect the formula.**

Only family members knew the full formula of each product, and a last ingredient was added by a Lauder, which she said guarded against spies hired by rivals and added mystique [S14][S16]. She also tested each product thoroughly before it left the lab [S12].

**Keep knocking, and hand-pick the people at the counter.**

She waited outside buyers' offices, took trains and buses, and called repeatedly, careful never to burn a bridge [S12]. She hand-picked attractive, well-trained saleswomen, set them daily goals and discouraged overselling, since she wanted customers to leave satisfied [S12]. An encyclopedia entry quotes her calling the saleswoman her most important asset and the link to her customer [S16].

### Real decisions

#### Choosing department stores over drugstores and salons (1940s)
She and Joseph had little money, and the drugstores and supermarkets of the time needed a sales force they could not afford [S8]. She also thought they cheapened the image she wanted, and she turned down the salon route of Elizabeth Arden and Helena Rubinstein [S8]. She went for premium department stores because their rooms invited impulse buying, their charge accounts removed the cash barrier and their prestige rubbed off [S8]. The decision stuck: by the early 1950s she sold in Saks, Neiman Marcus, Bonwit Teller and regional stores [S8]. Around 1982 she still refused drugstores, because her personal service model would not work there [S17].

#### Winning Saks with 80 lipsticks (1946 to 1948)
The Saks cosmetics buyer, Robert Fiske, first said he saw no demand for her line [S8]. She staged a charity luncheon at the Waldorf-Astoria and gave out more than 80 lipsticks in metal cases, which were unusual then [S8][S9]. Fiske recalled a line of guests asking for the lipsticks, and Saks placed a first order of $800 [S8]. Accounts differ on the date, and one says the first order sold out in two days [S4][S12].

#### Spending $50,000 on charge-account mailings
In the 1950s she had about $50,000, and agencies turned it down [S9][S13]. Her reason was simple: she needed a way to reach shoppers who already trusted the store. She used inserts in Saks charge statements and later scented ones [S9][S13]. Her line "Why tangle with him?" from the same period explains why she also held off following Revlon into nail polish, as she wanted the company bigger and stronger first [S9][S12].

#### Staying on the floor for each new store
She did not run the launches from an office. She opened each store herself and trained the saleswomen who would demonstrate her products [S14]. Her own count was that she touched fifty faces a day, and her goal was to show a woman that a three-minute make-up could change how she felt [S8][S12]. Even 40 years in, she still turned up for counter openings [S15].

#### Launching Youth-Dew as a bath oil (1953)
Perfume was dear, faded fast and was bought as a gift, so women wore it sparingly [S9][S11]. She made a bath oil that doubled as a long-lasting scent, priced at $8.50, and called it by a cream's name and not "perfume" [S9][S11]. It sold about 5,000 bottles a week, and 50,000 in its first year by another count [S1][S12]. It became the engine of the firm, perhaps too much so: it made roughly 80 percent of revenue by the late 1950s [S10].

#### Saying yes to Clinique after saying no (1968)
Leonard Lauder argued for a hypoallergenic line because customers reported allergies, and Estée at first resisted [S9]. She agreed and helped design the white lab coats and the clinical counters [S9][S13]. Sources differ on the early losses, from about $3 million over several years to $20 million over four, but they agree it became a very large part of the business [S13][S17].

#### Refusing to sell the company, or to go public
Competitors offered to buy her out, and she answered by asking why she should not buy them [S9][S12]. She kept the company private because she feared quarterly pressure from Wall Street would stop her taking risks on new products [S9][S12]. Her son Leonard later took it public, with the family keeping voting control [S9][S10].

### Where she stood

**What sells beauty products?** Seeing the result on your own skin, explained by someone who cares [S2].

**Ads or samples?** Samples, at least at the start. Trial and word of mouth built her business [S2][S4].

**Should a brand chase every sales channel?** No. She chose prestige stores and saw discounting as a risk to the brand [S4][S7].

**Who is beauty for?** Every woman. The company says she believed all women could be beautiful [S3]. She also called beauty an attitude [S4].

**What does success take?** Hard work, sticking with it and believing in what you sell [S4].

**What is a family company?** Her husband's motto was that they were a family in business, not just a family business, and the family still uses it [S3].

### Her work and record

- Born Josephine Esther Mentzer in Corona, Queens, New York. Her birth year is given as 1908 (disputed) [S1].
- Married Joseph Lauter in 1930. The couple later used the name Lauder [S2].
- Launched the company with Joseph in 1946, at first as its only two staff, with four products [S3].
- Won her first major order, $800 of products, from Saks Fifth Avenue in 1947 [S2]. Some accounts date it to 1948 [S4].
- Introduced Youth-Dew in 1953 [S2].
- Oversaw the launch of further brands including Aramis, Clinique, Prescriptives, Lab Series and Origins [S2].
- Became the first woman named a Chevalier of the French Legion of Honour, in 1978 [S1].
- Published her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*, in 1985 [S6].
- Retired in 1995, the year the company planned its first public share offer [S3][S4].
- Was named on *Time*'s 1998 list of the 20 most influential business minds of the century [S4].
- Received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2004, and died in Manhattan on April 24, 2004 [S1][S3].

### Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Her own story.** For years she claimed a more aristocratic background than she had. Lee Israel's 1985 biography, *Estée Lauder: Beyond the Magic*, discredited those claims [S1]. She described a "sumptuous home" in a 1985 interview, a story Wikipedia calls a myth [S1].
- **Her age.** She never gave her birth date, and sources still disagree [S1][S4].
- **A memoir in a hurry.** The obituary says she rushed her memoir out in 1985 partly to beat the unauthorised biography [S4]. One reader review calls it closer to a company history written by a PR firm [S6].
- **One scent doing too much.** Youth-Dew made about 80 percent of revenue in the late 1950s, which her son saw as dangerous and which led to new brands [S10].
- **Confusing launches.** A company history lists a 1978 pair of related scents that confused buyers and shoppers, and a 1979 skin-care line with a lukewarm start [S13].
- **Cheap giving.** The same history notes that gift with purchase built loyalty but also helped keep industry margins low [S13].
- **Credit for gift with purchase.** The company says she helped establish the practice. At least one summary says she popularised it rather than invented it [S2][S7].

### Blind spots to watch

- Her methods depend on the founder being everywhere. That doesn't scale without strong training and trust [S2][S7].
- Prestige selling can shut out customers who can't reach or afford a department store counter [S4].
- Free samples cost money. A small business should test whether they bring buyers back before giving them away at scale.
- She built a polished public story about herself. Pair her with someone who insists on plain facts in brand claims [S1].
- Her world was beauty and fashion. Check how her ideas apply when the buyer can't touch or try the product.
