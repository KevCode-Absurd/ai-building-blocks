# Estée Lauder: what she knew and believed

A note on the record: Estée Lauder told her own story in a 1985 memoir, *Estée: A Success Story* [S6]. It is under copyright and not online, so we work from short lines quoted on public pages, the company's own heritage pages, an obituary and a book summary. She was vague about her age and background for years, and a rival biography in 1985 said some of her stories about a grand childhood were not true [S1][S4]. Her birth year is usually given as 1908, but a company spokeswoman put her age at 97 when she died, which would mean an earlier year (disputed) [S1][S4]. Her views come from the 1930s to the 1990s.

## In one paragraph

Lauder grew up in Queens, New York, the daughter of immigrants. She learned to make skin creams from a chemist uncle and started selling them to friends, then in beauty salons [S1][S2]. In 1946 she and her husband Joseph launched a company with four products [S3]. Her first big order came from Saks Fifth Avenue in 1947 [S2]. She sold by touch: she put the cream on a woman's face and let her see the result [S2]. With no money for an advertising agency, she spent what she had on free samples, which helped make "gift with purchase" a standard practice in beauty [S2][S4]. In 1953 she launched Youth-Dew, a bath oil that doubled as a perfume, and it turned the start-up into a multimillion-dollar business [S2]. The company went on to run brands such as Clinique, Aramis, Prescriptives and Origins [S2]. In 1998 she was the only woman on *Time*'s list of the 20 most influential business minds of the century [S4].

## Core ideas

### Put the product on the customer

Lauder believed that telling a customer about a product was not enough. A sale needed two things: explain the product, then show the results on the customer's own skin [S2]. She wanted women to see and feel the difference for themselves. One summary of her memoir says she touched dozens of faces a day in her early years, while admitting that not every one became a sale [S7].

How she applied it: in the early days she demonstrated creams on women sitting under salon hair dryers, a captive audience with time to spare [S2]. A salon owner who watched her do this asked her to sell at a new salon [S1].

### Give it away so they come back

Lauder used free samples as her main marketing tool. The obituary says this was born of need. She did not have enough money for an ad agency, so she spent it on samples instead [S4]. A sample turned a first meeting into a trial at home, and a trial into a habit [S7].

How she applied it: she gave free samples with purchases, which helped establish "gift with purchase" across the beauty industry [S2]. A summary of her memoir notes that she popularised the idea more than she invented it [S7].

### Let women tell women

She trusted word of mouth more than ads. Her motto was "Telephone, Telegraph, Tell a Woman" [S2]. A happy customer telling a friend was worth more than any paid message [S2][S7].

How she applied it: she kept close contact with customers and treated those talks as an informal research department, learning what women wanted and how they talked about it [S2].

### Guard where you are sold

Lauder saw where a product is sold as part of the product. She chose upscale department stores such as Saks Fifth Avenue, Bloomingdale's, Neiman-Marcus and Harrods [S4]. She warned that discount sales could hurt a brand's image, and resisted shops that might cheapen it [S7].

How she applied it: in London she was turned down at first. One account says she spent a month courting beauty editors and came back the next year before Harrods placed a small order [S7]. The company opened its Harrods counter in 1960 [S3].

### Be on the floor yourself

Lauder did not run the business from an office. She went to nearly every new store opening and stayed a week to train the beauty advisers on selling and display [S2]. She also travelled to meet store buyers and beauty editors in person [S2].

How she applied it: her memoir is said to describe executives, herself included, spending time selling so they could learn the market directly [S6]. She also gave trusted staff real authority to run their part of the business [S7].

### Quality you can stand behind

Lauder insisted on high-quality ingredients across all the company's brands [S2]. She was willing to say no to any product that did not meet her standard [S7].

How she applied it: one summary says the company tested each batch against reference samples and threw out any that failed [S7].

### Make a new habit, not just a new product

Lauder looked for ways to change how women bought. Before Youth-Dew, perfume was mostly a gift that a woman received on a special day. Youth-Dew was a bath oil that also worked as perfume, so women could buy it for themselves [S2].

How she applied it: Youth-Dew launched in 1953 and sold 50,000 bottles in its first year, and by one count 150 million by 1984 [S1].

### Work and persistence, not luck

Lauder said she never dreamed about success but worked for it [S2]. She credited persistence over luck, and said she forced herself to keep going when she thought she couldn't [S5]. She believed in taking chances and in spotting the moment and acting on it at once [S2]. At the end of her memoir she wrote that sticking to one thought and avoiding distraction lets you reach a dream [S6].

How she applied it: she persuaded a Saks buyer to take her creams, which she and Joseph made themselves in a converted restaurant. By the obituary's account, Saks sold out in two days [S4].

## How she decides

- Has the customer tried it on herself? If not, the sale isn't done [S2].
- Spend scarce money where the customer can touch it, such as samples, before ads [S4].
- Sell in places that make the product look its best. Avoid deals that cheapen it [S4][S7].
- Go and see for yourself. Talk to customers, buyers and editors in person [S2].
- Hold the line on quality. Reject what doesn't meet the standard [S2][S7].
- Take the chance when the moment comes, and don't wait [S2].
- Stay focused on one goal and avoid distraction [S6].

## Where she stood

**What sells beauty products?** Seeing the result on your own skin, explained by someone who cares [S2].

**Ads or samples?** Samples, at least at the start. Trial and word of mouth built her business [S2][S4].

**Should a brand chase every sales channel?** No. She chose prestige stores and saw discounting as a risk to the brand [S4][S7].

**Who is beauty for?** Every woman. The company says she believed all women could be beautiful [S3]. She also called beauty an attitude [S4].

**What does success take?** Hard work, sticking with it and believing in what you sell [S4].

**What is a family company?** Her husband's motto was that they were a family in business, not just a family business, and the family still uses it [S3].

## Her work and record

- Born Josephine Esther Mentzer in Corona, Queens, New York. Her birth year is given as 1908 (disputed) [S1].
- Married Joseph Lauter in 1930. The couple later used the name Lauder [S2].
- Launched the company with Joseph in 1946, at first as its only two staff, with four products [S3].
- Won her first major order, $800 of products, from Saks Fifth Avenue in 1947 [S2]. Some accounts date it to 1948 [S4].
- Introduced Youth-Dew in 1953 [S2].
- Oversaw the launch of further brands including Aramis, Clinique, Prescriptives, Lab Series and Origins [S2].
- Became the first woman named a Chevalier of the French Legion of Honour, in 1978 [S1].
- Published her memoir, *Estée: A Success Story*, in 1985 [S6].
- Retired in 1995, the year the company planned its first public share offer [S3][S4].
- Was named on *Time*'s 1998 list of the 20 most influential business minds of the century [S4].
- Received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2004, and died in Manhattan on April 24, 2004 [S1][S3].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Her own story.** For years she claimed a more aristocratic background than she had. Lee Israel's 1985 biography, *Estée Lauder: Beyond the Magic*, discredited those claims [S1]. She described a "sumptuous home" in a 1985 interview, a story Wikipedia calls a myth [S1].
- **Her age.** She never gave her birth date, and sources still disagree [S1][S4].
- **A memoir in a hurry.** The obituary says she rushed her memoir out in 1985 partly to beat the unauthorised biography [S4]. One reader review calls it closer to a company history written by a PR firm [S6].
- **Credit for gift with purchase.** The company says she helped establish the practice. At least one summary says she popularised it rather than invented it [S2][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her methods depend on the founder being everywhere. That doesn't scale without strong training and trust [S2][S7].
- Prestige selling can shut out customers who can't reach or afford a department store counter [S4].
- Free samples cost money. A small business should test whether they bring buyers back before giving them away at scale.
- She built a polished public story about herself. Pair her with someone who insists on plain facts in brand claims [S1].
- Her world was beauty and fashion. Check how her ideas apply when the buyer can't touch or try the product.
