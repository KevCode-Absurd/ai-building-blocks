### How she'd take on your problem

1. **Describe the story:** Ask each person what story they currently tell about the relationship. Notice where a complex person has become only a household role or a prediction about familiar failures.

2. **Find the paradox:** Name the tension that cannot simply be argued away, such as closeness and autonomy. Ask what each side protects before declaring either wish immature, selfish or unquestionably correct.

3. **Explore context:** Explore the family and cultural expectations entering the discussion. Separate an inherited assumption from a present commitment, and ask which expectations were actually discussed and mutually chosen.

4. **Restore curiosity:** Invite a story or a view of the person that the familiar routine leaves out. Make curiosity voluntary and reciprocal rather than another demand to disclose feelings or perform novelty.

5. **Clarify expectations:** State what each person wants the relationship to mean now. Clarify promises and endings where avoidable ambiguity is causing harm, without treating one person's wish as a shared agreement.

6. **Try an experience:** Choose an inexpensive, consensual change in conversation or activity. Review whether interest and agency increased while practical fairness remained intact, rather than measuring the experiment by prescribed romantic intensity.

### Jobs, one by one

The examples below are made-up illustrations of the method, not real cases.

#### Negotiate time together and time apart

Bring what each of you wants more or less of, and what you assumed without saying. Protect one personal interest for each partner, then ask "What was absorbing about that?" instead of counting hours. Time apart needs both people's agreement and a fair split of the chores. The aim is to meet a fuller person, not to make anyone jealous. Ask yourself whether the time apart nourishes you or lets you dodge a talk you still owe. You get a reframed dilemma, three new questions and one experiment you both agree to.

#### See your partner beyond a household role

Bring the last three things you said to each other. If they were all logistics, that is the problem to work on. Watch your partner teach, make music or solve a problem, then ask about that part of their life. Frustration over chores can shrink a person to a pile of unfinished tasks. Widen the picture without excusing neglect: the capable person and the undone task are both real. You get a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

#### Start a more curious conversation

Bring the story you tell about the friend or partner ("they never want to do anything"). Replace it with an open question, such as "What would interest you this month?" People change their interests and what they can do. Let the answer surprise you before you file it under who they have always been. You get one question to ask and when you will ask it.

#### Sort mixed feelings before a relationship decision

Bring the decision actually due and the date it is due. Make two lists: what you value and what repeatedly hurts. Mixed feelings are information to explore, not a reason to wait forever. Tell a tolerable tension from a harmful situation. If it is harmful, stop and seek support. You can decide without resolving every feeling first. You get two workable options and one question to help you choose.

#### End a dating ambiguity kindly

Bring the message thread and what you want. If you don't want another date, send a short, kind, clear message instead of reaching out only when you feel lonely. Say it once and don't ask the other person to feel a certain way. You get a message of three or four sentences to adapt.

#### Life and career: the next commitment

Bring a commitment or request you have been putting off, and what you are afraid will happen if you ask. For example, a couple says they have nothing left to talk about. Their evenings are full of accurate reports about shopping and bills, with no stories. Ask each person: "What have I not seen you enjoy lately?" Choose one separate interest to protect and one new shared conversation to try, such as a walk with one unfamiliar question and real listening. Review after a week: did curiosity show up, and was the housework still shared fairly? This conversation can clarify a choice. It does not make your relationship or career decision for you.

#### Outside the usual field: a long-standing collaboration

Bring the people involved and how long you have worked together. Choose only questions a colleague can decline without giving a reason, such as "What part of this project do you most enjoy?" Never ask about their feelings, home life or past. Offer one small change to how you work together and let each person say no. The boundary: this is about the work, and nobody has to share anything personal. This is an analogy, not a tested method for work. You get one small practice with a purpose and a review date.

#### See beyond the household role

Her otherness essay describes seeing a familiar partner anew in their own element. [S1]

Original everyday application: Watch a partner teach, make music or solve a problem, then ask about that part of their life. Domestic frustration can narrow attention until the person becomes a collection of unfinished chores. Broaden the frame without excusing neglected responsibilities. Both the competent person and the unfinished task can be real.

#### Curiosity exceeds certainty

Her writing questions assuming that knowing someone well means knowing everything about them. [S1]

Original everyday application: Ask what would interest your friend this month instead of predicting rejection of every plan. Replace an identity statement with an open question. A friend can change interests, capacities and priorities. Let their answer surprise you before folding it into the familiar story about who they have always been.

#### Hold ambivalence carefully

Her writing names mixed feelings in important relationships without supplying an instant verdict. [S5]

Original everyday application: List what you value and what repeatedly hurts, then identify the decision actually due. Ambivalence is information to explore rather than a reason to delay forever. Distinguish a tolerable tension from a harmful situation. You can seek support and make a decision without resolving every contradictory feeling first.

#### End avoidable ambiguity

Her accountability essay criticizes keeping people uncertain about a relationship's ending. [S2]

Original everyday application: If you do not want another date, send a kind clear message instead of contacting someone only when lonely. Clarity need not include a cruel review of the person's faults. State the decision and do not imply an invitation you do not intend. Safety and unwanted contact can change whether a final conversation is appropriate.

#### Examine inherited expectations

Her autobiographical account describes cultural transition and mixed-culture families. [S3]

Original everyday application: Ask which traditions each partner learned and which obligations you consciously want to keep. An unspoken expectation may feel like morality to one person and intrusion to another. Name the cultural and family story before judging the disagreement. Then choose a present arrangement you can both own.

#### Change the familiar prompt

Her public work invites new conversation rather than rehearsing only logistics. [S4]

Original everyday application: On a walk, ask about an untold aspiration instead of repeating the household status meeting. Choose a question that invites a story, and share one yourself. Curiosity needs permission rather than interrogation. A novel prompt helps only when there is actual attention to the answer and room for the person to decline.
