## In one paragraph

Frida Kahlo was born in Coyoacán, Mexico City, on 6 July 1907, though she later gave 1910 as her birth year to present herself as a daughter of the Mexican Revolution [S1]. Polio at six left her right leg weaker, and in 1925 a bus crash pierced her pelvis and broke many bones [S1]. While recovering in bed she began to paint, with a mirror fixed above her easel [S1]. She married the muralist Diego Rivera in 1929, divorced him in 1939 and remarried him in 1940 [S1]. Her small, vivid paintings, many of them self-portraits, drew on Mexican folk art, religious ex-voto paintings and pre-Columbian symbols to explore pain, the body, gender and identity [S1]. She had her first solo show in New York in 1938, and in 1939 the Louvre bought one of her works [S1]. She died in Coyoacán on 13 July 1954, aged 47 [S1]. Largely forgotten for decades, she became a world icon from the late 1970s [S1].

## Core ideas

### Paint the subject you know best

Kahlo said in a 1945 interview that she painted self-portraits because she was so often alone, and because she was the person she knew best [S2]. Her art begins at home: her face, her body, her clothes, her pets, her house in Coyoacán.

The lesson for anyone making something personal: start with what you know from the inside. It is the one subject no one else can do.

### Paint your reality, not dreams

The Surrealist leader André Breton arranged her 1938 New York show and described her work as a ribbon around a bomb [S1]. But she rejected the Surrealist label [S1]. In 1953 *Time* quoted her saying that people thought she was a Surrealist, but she never painted dreams; she painted her own reality [S2]. Critics still argue over whether her work is Surrealist, symbolist or magical realist [S1].

The lesson: strange images can be true. Don't let others rename what you're doing.

### Show the pain honestly

Her paintings face suffering directly. *Henry Ford Hospital* (1932), painted in Detroit after she lost a pregnancy, shows her weeping in a floating hospital bed, tied by red threads to objects including a snail, an orchid and a pelvis bone [S4]. It is described as opening a defining era of her career [S4]. *The Broken Column* (1944), painted soon after spinal surgery, shows her torso split open around a crumbling stone column, her skin pierced by nails, a steel corset holding her together [S5]. She looks forward, through tears [S5].

Yet she refused self-pity. In 1953 she told *Time* that she was not sick but broken, and happy to be alive as long as she could paint [S2].

### Make one image carry the feeling

Kahlo packs meaning into objects. In *The Two Fridas* (1939), two versions of herself sit hand in hand: one in a white European dress, one in a traditional Tehuana dress, their exposed hearts joined by a single blood vessel [S3]. The Mexican Frida holds a tiny portrait of Rivera; the European Frida holds surgical forceps, and blood drips onto her white skirt [S3]. Critics read it as her dual heritage, a German father and a Mexican mother, and as the pain of her divorce that year [S3].

The lesson: one well-chosen object can say what a paragraph cannot.

### Draw on popular and sacred traditions

She owned about 2,000 retablos, small Mexican devotional paintings [S1]. Her work drew on these and on Mexican folk art and Catholic and pre-Columbian symbols [S1]. *Henry Ford Hospital* borrows the retablo format, painted small, on metal [S1][S4]. *The Broken Column* echoes Christian images of martyrdom, such as Saint Sebastian [S5].

### Wear your identity

Her embrace of Mexicanidad, a pride in Mexican and Indigenous culture, showed in her dress as well as her painting, most famously the Tehuana clothes she wore and painted [S1][S3]. Identity, for her, was something you could show and build. A former MoMA chief curator described her work as a theatre of the self [S1].

### Teach a new generation

From 1943 she taught at La Esmeralda, an art school in Mexico City, and her students became known as "Los Fridos" [S1].

### Art and politics

Kahlo joined the Mexican Communist Party in 1927, left in 1929 alongside Rivera, and rejoined in 1948, campaigning for peace [S1]. Her politics moved from support for Trotsky to support for Stalin, and late in life she wrote in her diary that she had never been a Trotskyist [S1][S2].

**Keep painting to the end.** In April 1953 she had her first solo exhibition in Mexico, arriving by ambulance and receiving guests from her four-poster bed [S1]. In her diary that year, before a leg amputation, she drew feet and wrote that she did not need them if she had wings to fly [S2].

### The self becomes the image

Her work was little known until the late 1970s [S1]. By the early 1990s she had become an icon for feminist, Chicano and LGBTQ movements, and her face is now reproduced as widely as Che Guevara's [S1]. Baddeley notes that people think of Van Gogh through his paintings, but of Kahlo through her own image [S1]. In 2025 *The Dream (The Bed)* sold for $54.7 million, a record at auction for a work by a woman artist [S1].

The lesson: a strong self-image can travel further than any single work. It can also swallow the work.

## Where she stood

**Was she a Surrealist?** No, she said. She painted her reality [S2].

**Why self-portraits?** Because she was often alone, and knew herself best [S2].

**What did illness mean for her art?** It was a subject, never the end of painting [S2][S5].

**What is Mexican identity worth?** Everything. She made it visible in her dress and art [S1][S3].

**What did politics mean to her?** She was a lifelong communist, though her loyalties shifted [S1][S2].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Life over art.** Art historian Oriana Baddeley argues that her dramatic life story has overshadowed her paintings, which are too often read as literal diaries [S1].
- **Fridamania.** Journalist Stephanie Mencimer argues that she has been turned into a poster child for many causes, with her biography cleaned up and distorted [S1].
- **Politics.** She moved from Trotsky's side to admiration for Stalin, which caused a rift [S1].
- **Her birth year.** She changed it to 1910 for symbolic reasons [S1].
- **The label.** Critics still dispute how to classify her, against her own rejection of Surrealism [S1][S2].

## How she wrote to friends

Her 1939 letters to Nickolas Muray are warm, playful and physical. She opens with a pet name and goes straight to the point, and her sentences run on with commas, dashes and exclamations [S6][S7]. She teases him, mentions pain only in passing, and says she should not complain while he loves her [S6]. In a June 1939 letter she reports Diego Rivera's praise of a portrait Muray made, comparing it to a Piero della Francesca, and describes Muray as "inside the magenta rebozo" [S7]. The transcriptions differ slightly between sources, so check the book before quoting them [S6].

## Real decisions

### Painting while she recovered

After the bus accident of 17 September 1925 she began painting during her recovery [S1]. In 1945 she said she painted self-portraits because she was so often alone and knew herself best [S2].

### Marrying Diego Rivera

She married Rivera on 21 August 1929 [S1]. The 1939 painting *The Two Fridas* is read by critics in part as the pain of their divorce that year [S3].

### Painting loss in a folk format

After losing a pregnancy in Detroit in 1932, she painted *Henry Ford Hospital* in a small retablo format, on metal, with red threads tying her to objects [S1][S4]. She chose the plain devotional form over a grand one.

### Refusing the Surrealist label

André Breton arranged her 1938 New York show and praised her work [S1]. She still told *Time* in 1953 that she never painted dreams, only her own reality [S2].

### Teaching and showing up

From 1943 she taught at La Esmeralda, and her students became known as "Los Fridos" [S1]. In April 1953 she attended her first Mexican solo show by ambulance and received guests from a four-poster bed [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her method centres on the self. Some projects need to look outward.
- Pain-centred work can overwhelm. Match the weight to the job.
- Her symbols come from her culture. Don't borrow another culture's sacred images lightly.
- Her fame has made her face a brand. Don't reduce her to an image on a tote bag.
- Many "Frida quotes" are fake. Use only the sourced lines.
