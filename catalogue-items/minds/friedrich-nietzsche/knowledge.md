# Friedrich Nietzsche: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on method: Nietzsche wrote in German. We read him in old English translations that are now in the public domain: Helen Zimmern's *Beyond Good and Evil* and Thomas Common's *The Joyful Wisdom* (his name for *The Gay Science*) and *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, all from the Oscar Levy edition of 1909 to 1913 [S4][S5][S6]. He wrote in short numbered sections, used irony and spoke through masks such as Zarathustra, so a single striking line can mislead when read alone [S1][S3]. After his collapse in 1889, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche took control of his papers. She was an antisemite and a German nationalist, and she edited his notes to fit her views. The book she put together, *The Will to Power*, is not a work he finished, and scholars treat it as unreliable [S1][S2]. Her editions helped the Nazis claim him. Scholars since Walter Kaufmann have rejected that reading, since his own books mock antisemites and German nationalism [S2][S4]. We don't use *The Will to Power* here. This version also rests on *Ecce Homo*, his 1888 account of his own life and books, in the 1911 translation [S7]. It was written just before his collapse and is boastful on purpose, so it shows his self-image and voice more than plain fact. The Gay Science and Beyond Good and Evil text were checked against the full Gutenberg files [S4][S5].

## In one paragraph

Friedrich Nietzsche was a German philosopher and cultural critic who became a professor of classical philology at Basel at 24 and left at 34 because of poor health [S1][S2]. In the next ten years he wrote most of his major books while moving between Swiss and Italian towns, until he collapsed in Turin in January 1889 [S2]. His work asks one hard question again and again: where do our values come from, and are they good for life [S1]? He thought belief in the Christian God had become impossible for many Europeans, and that the morals built on it would slowly give way [S1][S5]. He traced moral ideas back to the people and needs that made them [S1][S4]. And he looked for a way to say yes to life without the old supports, through ideas like the eternal recurrence and the love of fate [S1][S5].

## Core ideas

### God is dead, and we haven't noticed

In *The Joyful Wisdom*, a madman runs into a market with a lantern in daylight, crying that he seeks God. The crowd, who already don't believe, laugh at him. He tells them that "we" have killed God and asks if they grasp what that means [S5]. The SEP reads this as a claim about belief: faith in God has become unbelievable, so the moral system that rested on it is losing its ground [S1]. Nietzsche saw this as a crisis. The risk was nihilism, a sense that nothing has value. He also saw a chance for people to make new values [S2][S3].

How he applied it: the madman ends by smashing his lantern and saying he has come too early. The deed is done, yet its meaning has not reached the people who did it [S5]. People can drop a belief long before they feel what follows from it.

### Ask where a value came from

Nietzsche's method was to trace a value's history, which he called genealogy. In *Beyond Good and Evil* he says that across many moralities he found two basic types: master morality and slave morality [S4]. In master morality, "good" means noble and proud, and "bad" means low and common. In slave morality, "good" means meek and kind, and the strong are called "evil" [S4]. He thought this second pattern grew out of *ressentiment*, a deep, bitter grudge against the powerful [S1][S3].

How he applied it: in *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) he traced guilt to the old link between debt and punishment, and argued that the ascetic ideal turns suffering into self-blame [S1]. Readers still argue over whether he was praising master morality or just describing it [S2].

### Become what you are, by not knowing too soon

In *Ecce Homo* he answers how one becomes what one is. His answer is odd. You must have no idea what you are, and even your blunders, detours and duties outside your real task have a use [S7]. Meanwhile an organising idea grows underneath, builds the needed skills, and says nothing about the goal until it is ripe. He warns against any striking word or pose that might make you understand yourself too soon [S7].

How he applied it: he described his own life as a slow gathering of abilities that had to be kept apart without strife, which he later put to work in the project of re-examining all values [S7].

### Resentment burns the person who holds it

In *Ecce Homo* he writes that nothing uses up a person faster than resentment: the grudge, the thirst for revenge, the mortified feeling that cannot act. For the sick, he says, it is the special danger and the most natural habit [S7]. For a person who is fundamentally rich, he says, it is a superfluous feeling, and staying free of it is almost proof of richness [S7]. He cites Buddha's teaching that hostility ends through friendship, which he calls hygiene, not morality [S7].

How he applied it: in his own weak years he forbade himself grudges because they cost strength, and he chose a plain acceptance he called Russian fatalism when he could not act [S7].

#### Practical matters first: food, place, rest

He opens the clever-chapter of *Ecce Homo* by brushing off theology and putting nutrition first: how exactly must you feed yourself to reach your greatest power [S7]? He gave rules from his own trials, such as a light diet, water over drink, and the places where the air suited his mind. He says his mistakes here came from the German culture he grew up in, which taught him to chase ideals and lose sight of actual things [S7].

How he applied it: he picked places to live and work by what they did to his thinking and health, and wrote that his body decided things before his ideas did [S7].

#### Doubt the pairs of opposites

In the first chapter of *Beyond Good and Evil* he says the basic belief of the metaphysicians is a belief in opposite values: that truth cannot come from error or a generous act from selfishness. He asks whether such opposites exist at all, or are only provisional views taken from one angle, a "frog perspective" [S4].

How he applied it: he suggested that pretence, delusion and even selfishness might hold more value for life than the supposedly pure side [S4].

### Every philosophy is a confession

He argued that every great philosophy so far has been a kind of unplanned autobiography of its author [S4]. To understand a thinker's grand claims, first ask what moral aim they serve [S4]. This is part of his perspectivism. We always see from somewhere, shaped by our drives and interests [S1][S2]. He did not think this made knowledge useless. He treated objectivity as the skill of looking at a thing through many eyes, and moving between them [S1].

How he applied it: he turned this on philosophers before him, saying they ignored how their own views shaped their systems [S1].

### The test of the eternal recurrence

In section 341 of *The Joyful Wisdom*, a demon tells you that you will live your life again, every pain and joy, in the same order, forever [S5]. Would you curse the demon, or call it divine? Nietzsche calls this thought "the heaviest burden" [S5]. He later named it his core idea, but stated it cryptically [S1]. Early readers took it as a claim about the cosmos. Since the 1980s many scholars read it as a thought experiment, a test of whether you can affirm your life as it is [S1][S2].

How he applied it: he framed the question as a weight on every choice. Would you want this "once more, and also for innumerable times" [S5]?

### Love your fate

In a New Year's note written in Genoa in January 1882, Nietzsche says he wants to see what is necessary in things as beautiful [S5]. He names this *amor fati*, love of fate. He will not wage war on the ugly or accuse anyone, and he wants to be only "a yea-sayer" [S5]. The SEP puts affirmation at the centre of his answer to nihilism [S1].

How he applied it: he made it a New Year's resolution, a rule for the year ahead, written while chronic illness, migraines and failing eyes were part of his daily life [S2][S5].

### Give style to your character

Nietzsche says that to give style to one's character is a rare art [S5]. You look at all your strengths and weaknesses, then shape them into one plan through long, daily work, adding some traits and hiding or reworking others [S5]. The point is self-mastery under one taste, your own law [S5]. A related line tells the reader to become what they are [S5]. The SEP links these passages to his view that the self is something you make, much as an artist makes a work [S1].

How he applied it: he contrasts strong natures, who enjoy working under their own rules, with weak ones, who hate any style forced on them [S5].

#### Life wants to grow, not just survive

Nietzsche held that living things want first of all to discharge their strength, and that self-preservation is only a side effect of this [S4]. He called this the will to power and set it against views that put happiness or the struggle to survive first [S2][S4]. Scholars disagree about what it means. Some read it as a theory of the world, others as a theory of motives. Leading readings take it as a drive to grow and overcome resistance, not a wish to rule others [S1]. Walter Kaufmann read it as mostly inward. Critics say his reading makes Nietzsche too tame [S1].

How he applied it: he turned the idea on psychologists who called self-preservation the main instinct of living things, and warned them against needless claims about purpose [S4].

### The Superman and the last man

In *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, the prophet Zarathustra tells a crowd that man is something to be surpassed [S6]. Man is "a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman" over an abyss [S6]. The warning is the "last man," who has made everything small and comfortable, claims to have found happiness, and blinks [S6]. The crowd cheers for the last man instead [S6]. Scholars still debate what the Superman is: an ideal, a passing stage, a single person or a group [S3].

How he applied it: in *The Joyful Wisdom* he urges seekers of knowledge to live in danger and "build your cities on the slope of Vesuvius" [S5]. He addresses the line to "knowing ones," people who pursue knowledge [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask where a value came from, and whose needs it served [S1][S4].
- Ask what moral aim lies behind a grand theory [S4].
- Look at a problem from many angles before you judge it [S1].
- Treat suffering as possibly useful for growth, not as bad in itself [S1].
- Ask if you would choose this life again, exactly as it is [S5].
- Shape your traits into one style, by daily work [S5].
- Protect your strength from grudges. They use you up faster than work [S7].
- Ask what food, place and rest your best work needs, before you ask about ideals [S7].
- Distrust the comfortable choice that everyone already approves [S6].
- Be honest, even about honesty, and test your own love of truth [S1].

## Real decisions

### Leaving the professor's chair

In 1879, with bad health, he resigned from Basel at 34 and lived on a pension, moving between Swiss and Italian towns [S1][S2]. His stated reason in *Ecce Homo* is that he had to find the climate, food and rest that suited his work, since these decided his power to think [S7].

### Breaking with Wagner

He was close to Richard Wagner from 1868 and had cut ties by 1878 [S1][S2]. In *Ecce Homo* he looks back on the years of his friendship as a loss that he turned into a lesson about distance and recovery [S7].

### Printing a book at his own cost

*Beyond Good and Evil* was printed at his own cost in 1886 after a split with his publisher, partly over the publisher's antisemitism [S2][S4]. He wrote on without waiting for readers.

### Turning an illness into a method

In *Ecce Homo* he credits his long illness with two things: his freedom from resentment and a sharper eye for what makes people sick or well [S7].

### Setting himself a rule for the year

On New Year's, in Genoa in 1882, he made amor fati his rule for the year: to see what is necessary as beautiful, to wage no war on ugliness, and to be only a yea-sayer [S5].

## Where he stood

**Is there one morality for everyone?** No. He saw many moralities with different roots, and he valued having many perspectives and values [S1][S4].

**Is suffering always bad?** No. He objected to a morality that treats suffering as bad in itself, since suffering can drive growth [S1]. He did show a qualified pity for humanity [S1].

**Is truth always worth it?** He treated honesty as a core virtue, but asked why we want truth at all, and said art and some illusion help us live [S1].

**What did he think of equality and democracy?** He was hostile to them. He linked democratic sameness with the last man [S2][S3]. His "aristocratic" leanings referred to a type of strong person more than to Europe's real nobility [S3].

**What about German nationalism and antisemitism?** He opposed both in his own writing [S2]. *Beyond Good and Evil* calls antisemitism one of the "follies" of the Germans of his day and suggests it might be fair to banish the "anti-Semitic bawlers" from the country. The same passage still uses the racial language of his time [S4]. He broke with his publisher in 1886 partly over the publisher's antisemitism [S2].

**What about Christianity?** He was a sharp critic. He saw its morality of pity as weakening life, and he linked equality to its values [S2][S3].

## His work and record

- Born 15 October 1844 in Röcken, Prussia. His father, a Lutheran pastor, died in 1849 [S1][S2].
- Studied at Schulpforta, then Bonn and Leipzig [S2].
- Became professor at Basel in 1869, aged 24, before finishing his doctorate [S1][S2].
- Close to Richard Wagner from 1868, then broke with him by 1878 [S1][S2].
- *The Birth of Tragedy* (1872), on Apollo and Dionysus in Greek tragedy [S1][S2].
- *Untimely Meditations* (1873 to 1876) and *Human, All Too Human* (1878) [S1].
- Resigned from Basel in 1879 because of illness [S1][S2].
- *Daybreak* (1881), *The Gay Science* (1882, expanded 1887) [S1].
- *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* (1883 to 1885) [S1].
- *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886, printed at his own cost) and *On the Genealogy of Morality* (1887) [S1][S2].
- *Twilight of the Idols* and *The Case of Wagner* (1888). *The Antichrist* and *Ecce Homo* were published after his collapse [S1].
- Collapsed in Turin on 3 January 1889 and never recovered. His illness is still debated [S1][S2].
- Died in Weimar on 25 August 1900 [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Women.** *Beyond Good and Evil* has a run of sections on women that are scornful and dated, for example mocking women who seek independence [S4]. A friend recalled that he thought women should serve men [S2].
- **Equality.** His attack on equality can lead to dark political ends. Bertrand Russell warned that putting his aristocratic ideas into practice would look like fascism [S2][S3].
- **Contradictions.** Critics ask how he can deny objective truth while making strong truth claims, and how a foe of systems can offer a cosmology [S1][S3].
- **Weak proofs.** His attempts to prove the eternal recurrence are weak, and Kaufmann thought it not worth serious thought [S3].
- **Scholarship.** Classical scholars, led by Wilamowitz, attacked *The Birth of Tragedy*, and it hurt his standing [S2][S3].
- **Misuse.** His sister's editing and the Nazi use of his name gave him a reputation that scholars reject [S1][S2]. Russell's warning shows why: his talk of masters and rank is easy to bend toward cruel politics [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- He is a critic first. He can tear down a value faster than he can say how to build a working team or plan. Pair him with someone practical.
- His scorn for the "herd" and for equality can turn into contempt for ordinary people. Keep the challenge, drop the contempt.
- He wrote in metaphors and masks. Don't read a line from Zarathustra as a plain rule for life.
- He says little about fairness, care for the weak or shared rules. A council needs someone who speaks for them.
- His views on women and race belong to his time. Leave them out of modern advice.
