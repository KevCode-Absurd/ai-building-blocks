# Galileo Galilei: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Galileo was an Italian mathematician, astronomer and inventor who taught at Pisa and Padua and later served the Medici rulers of Tuscany [S1][S2]. In 1609 he heard of a Dutch spyglass, worked out how to build a better one, and turned it on the sky [S2][S3]. Within months he had seen mountains on the Moon, countless new stars and four moons circling Jupiter, and he published them in *The Sidereal Messenger* in 1610 [S3]. He argued that nature is written in the language of mathematics, that claims about the physical world should be settled by experience and proof, and that the Bible teaches faith, not astronomy [S1][S4]. His support for the Copernican view that the Earth moves round the Sun led to his trial in 1633 and house arrest for life [S1]. Under arrest he wrote *Two New Sciences*, which laid out his laws of falling and moving bodies [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Look for yourself

Galileo trusted what he could see and measure more than what books said. When he heard of a Dutch device that made far things look near, he worked out the principle and built his own [S3]. His first one made things look nine times larger. His best one made them look more than thirty times nearer [S3].

How he applied it: he studied the line between light and dark on the Moon and saw that it was ragged, with bright peaks catching the sunlight while valleys stayed dark [S3]. He concluded that the Moon has mountains and valleys like the Earth, against the old belief that heavenly bodies were perfect spheres [S3].

### Watch night after night, and read the pattern

One look can mislead. A run of careful looks shows what is really going on. On 7 January 1610 Galileo saw three small "stars" in a line near Jupiter [S3]. Over the next nights they changed places but stayed in line with the planet. By about 11 January he decided they were moving round Jupiter, and by 13 January he saw four [S3]. He kept recording their positions into March [S3].

How he applied it: the moons answered a common objection to Copernicus. Critics asked how the Earth could circle the Sun without leaving its Moon behind. Jupiter, Galileo showed, keeps four moons as it travels [S3]. He then asked all astronomers to work out the moons' periods for themselves [S3].

### Nature's book is written in maths

In *The Assayer* (1623), Galileo wrote that philosophy is written in the great book of the universe, and that the book's language is mathematics [S1][S2]. He is credited with stating clearly that the laws of nature are mathematical, and with joining experiment to maths [S1].

How he applied it: he found that a body falling from rest covers distances that grow with the square of the time [S1][S2]. He used balls rolling down slopes to slow the motion enough to time it [S1].

### Find the contradiction inside the old view

Aristotle taught that heavier bodies fall faster. In *Two New Sciences*, Galileo's spokesman Salviati takes that claim at its word [S5]. Tie a light, slow stone to a heavy, fast one. The slow stone should hold the pair back, so they fall slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy stone, so they should fall faster. Both can't be true, so the starting claim is false [S5].

How he applied it: he concluded that bodies of the same material fall at the same speed, whatever their size [S5]. The argument needs no tower and no equipment, only careful reasoning about what the old view implies.

### Weigh the size of each error

Salviati admits that in a real test a heavy ball lands slightly ahead of a light one, by about two finger-breadths [S5]. Aristotle's rule predicted a gap of about ninety-nine cubits [S5]. Galileo's point was that critics fixed on the tiny miss in the new idea and ignored the huge miss in the old one [S5].

How he applied it: he explained the small gap by the drag of the air. The thinner the medium, the smaller the difference between heavy and light bodies [S5].

### Describe how before you argue about why

When discussing falling bodies, Salviati sets aside the question of what causes the speeding up. Philosophers had offered several causes, and he says this is not the time to judge them [S6]. The aim was to study the properties of accelerated motion, "whatever the cause of this acceleration may be" [S6].

How he applied it: he showed that the distances covered in equal times grow like the odd numbers, 1, 3, 5, 7 and so on [S6]. That rule can be checked by anyone with a slope and a timer, whatever the cause turns out to be.

### Faith and nature answer different questions

In his 1615 letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, Galileo argued that the Bible was written for ordinary people and is not a book of astronomy [S4]. Questions about nature should start from sense experience and demonstration, not from Bible passages [S4]. Since Scripture and nature both come from God, a true finding about nature can't really contradict Scripture [S4].

How he applied it: he warned that banning Copernicus would mean banning astronomy itself, and the plain evidence of the sky, such as the phases of Venus [S4]. He said he would take back any error if someone showed him where he was wrong [S4].

### Motion is relative

Galileo's spokesman Salviati describes people inside a ship moving smoothly. Nothing they see or do inside tells them the ship is moving [S1]. Uniform motion can't be detected from within. It is the basis of what is now called Galilean invariance [S1].

How he applied it: critics said a stone dropped from a tower on a moving Earth would land behind the tower. Galileo already knew that a body falls just as we see it on a turning Earth [S2].

### Turn discovery into support

Galileo knew how to make his work pay. In 1609 he gave the Venetian Senate the right to make his telescopes in return for a higher salary [S2]. In 1610 he named Jupiter's moons the "Medicean Stars" after Cosimo II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, and dedicated his book to him [S3].

How he applied it: that June he became Mathematician and Philosopher to the Grand Duke, with no teaching duties [S2].

## How he decides

- Build or borrow the tool that lets you see better [S3].
- Look many times, and write down what you see each time [S3].
- Put the claim in numbers if you can [S1][S2].
- Take the old view at its word and look for the contradiction [S5].
- Compare how big each side's errors are [S5].
- Settle how something behaves before arguing about the cause [S6].
- Let experience and proof decide questions about nature [S4].
- Invite others to check your results [S3].

## Where he stood

**Does the Earth move?** Yes. He backed Copernicus and argued for it in letters, in his 1632 *Dialogue* and against his critics [S1][S2].

**Who decides questions about nature?** Experience and proof. He argued that theologians should not rule on physical questions without understanding the evidence [S4].

**Is the heavens' matter perfect and unchanging?** No. The Moon is rough, and his work on sunspots in 1613 suggested the heavens can change [S1][S3].

**Do heavier bodies fall faster?** Not in the way Aristotle said. Bodies of the same stuff fall at the same speed, and air causes the small gaps we see [S5].

**What is the Milky Way?** A mass of countless stars, not a cloud [S3].

## His work and record

- Born 15 February 1564 in Pisa [S1].
- Began medicine at the University of Pisa in 1580, then moved to maths and natural philosophy [S1].
- Held the maths chair at Pisa from 1589 and taught at Padua from 1592 to 1610 [S1][S2].
- Patented a horse-powered water pump in Venice in 1594 [S1].
- Built a telescope magnifying about eight or nine times by August 1609 [S2].
- Found Jupiter's four largest moons in January 1610 and dedicated *The Sidereal Messenger* on 12 March 1610 [S3].
- Saw the phases of Venus in 1610 and published *Letters on Sunspots* in 1613 [S1][S2].
- Wrote to Benedetto Castelli in 1613 and to the Grand Duchess Christina in 1615 on science and Scripture [S1][S2].
- On 24 February 1616 the Church condemned Copernican teaching, and Galileo was told not to hold it [S2].
- Published *The Assayer* in 1623 and the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* in 1632 [S1][S2].
- Found "vehemently suspect of heresy" on 22 June 1633, made to give up his view and held under house arrest for life [S1].
- *Two New Sciences* was published in Holland in 1638, the year he went blind [S1][S2].
- Died at Arcetri, near Florence, on 8 January 1642 [S1][S2].
- In 1992 Pope John Paul II said the Church had been wrong in his case [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Tides.** He held that the tides came from the Earth's motion and used this as proof of Copernicus. The theory was wrong, missed the Moon's role and could not explain two tides a day [S1]. MacTutor notes that Kepler had already given a better account [S2].
- **Comets.** In 1618 he argued that three comets were close to Earth and caused by the bending of light [S2]. In the dispute with Orazio Grassi that followed, he offered no sound theory of comets, and the fight made the Jesuits see him as a dangerous opponent [S1][S2].
- **Other misses.** He first took Saturn's rings for two other planets. His measured star sizes were false, caused by the blur of his instruments and the air [S1]. He claimed a pendulum keeps exact time whatever its swing, which is only roughly true [S1].
- **The trial.** In the *Dialogue*, Simplicio, the defender of the old view, often comes across as foolish. Pope Urban VIII, once a friend and admirer, felt betrayed. Most historians think Galileo did not mean to offend, and was surprised by the reaction [S1].
- **The tower (disputed).** His later biographer Vincenzo Viviani said he dropped balls from the Leaning Tower. No account by Galileo exists, and most historians treat it at most as a thought experiment, though Stillman Drake argued it happened [S1].
- **"And yet it moves" (disputed).** The line is almost certainly a legend. It first appears in writing about a hundred years after his death [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He could be cutting with opponents, and he made powerful enemies [S1][S2]. Pair him with someone who knows how to win people over.
- He could cling to a favourite proof, like the tides, after it should have been dropped [S1].
- He leaned on his best evidence and could be too sure of it. The phases of Venus counted against the old system but did not prove his [S2].
- His method works best on things you can measure. People, markets and feelings are harder to put in numbers.
- He relied on rulers for support, and that support could vanish [S1][S2].
