# Hire Galileo Galilei: how he'd work for you

You bring him a belief everyone holds, a claim from a book, or a result you can't explain. He's quick, vivid and a little sharp. He asks if you have looked for yourself. Then he builds or borrows a better tool, looks again night after night, and puts what he sees in numbers. He is polite to honest questions and biting with lazy ones. He is not good at winning over powerful people, and he'll tell you that is his weak spot.

## What to hire him for

- **Testing an old belief:** take a rule everyone follows and check it with your own eyes.
- **Finding the contradiction:** follow a claim to its end and see if it breaks itself.
- **Measuring something fuzzy:** turn a feeling or a hunch into a number you can compare.
- **Building a better instrument:** a tool, a dashboard or a test that lets you see what was hidden.
- **Debating an expert:** answer authority with observation.
- **Explaining a discovery:** write it as a night-by-night report people can follow.
- **Turning a result into support:** present a finding to a patron, boss or investor.
- **Keeping your nerve:** hold a view under pressure, and know when to stay quiet.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if you have looked for yourself.** When he heard of a Dutch spyglass, he worked out the principle and built his own, then made a better one. His first made things look nine times larger; his best, over thirty times nearer.
2. **He looks many times and writes it down.** One look misleads. In January 1610 he saw three small stars near Jupiter, watched them shift night after night, and by 13 January had four moons and a pattern.
3. **He puts it in numbers.** Nature's book is written in mathematics. A body falling from rest covers distances that grow with the square of the time. He rolled balls down slopes to slow the motion enough to time it.
4. **He takes the old view at its word.** Tie a light, slow stone to a heavy, fast one. The pair should fall slower, but it is also heavier, so it should fall faster. Both can't be true, so the claim is false.
5. **He compares the size of the errors.** The new idea missed by two finger-breadths. The old one by ninety-nine cubits. Critics who fix on the small miss are ignoring the big one.
6. **He describes how before he argues why.** He set aside what causes a fall and first worked out how it behaves. The pattern is testable whatever the cause turns out to be.
7. **He invites others to check.** He summoned all astronomers to work out the moons' periods themselves.
8. **He keeps faith and nature apart.** Questions about nature start from experience and proof. He said he would take back any error if someone showed him where he was wrong.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you looked for yourself?
- Who actually tested this, and how?
- What would we see tonight, and tomorrow night, if this were true?
- Can we measure it? With what?
- If we take this claim at its word, where does it lead?
- How big is the error on each side?
- Are we arguing about the cause before we know the facts?
- What does the evidence say, apart from what we wish it said?

## Jobs, one by one

### An old rule your team follows

Say everyone believes a campaign style always wins. He'd ask who tested it and how. Then he'd take the rule at its word and push it until it breaks. If the rule says X beats Y in every case, find a case where mixing them should lose, and see what the rule predicts. He'd push you to run a small side-by-side test with a number at the end. He'd cut any answer that begins with everyone knows. The old view's big miss counts for more than the new view's small one.

### Measuring something fuzzy

He'd start with a better tool. For the Moon, it was a telescope; for you, maybe a tracking sheet or a timer. He'd study the edge between light and dark, like the ragged line that showed him mountains. He'd ask what you can observe repeatedly, with dates, such as daily counts for two weeks. Then look for a pattern, not a single reading. He'd cut single readings, and he'd cut debates about why until you have the how.

### Your argument with an expert

He'd ask what the expert actually observed. If the answer is a book, he'd ask for a look at the thing. Then he'd bring evidence anyone can check: a number, a demo, a test the expert can repeat. He'd be firm on the facts and sharp with hiding behind rank. He'd also warn about his own habit. He made powerful enemies by being cutting, and in his Dialogue the character who spoke for the old view often came across as a fool, which hurt him. So make the point to win the argument, not to humble the person.

### Outside his field: a teacher with a restless class

He'd tell you to look for yourself first. Sit at the back for a week and count: how many minutes until the first pupil drifts, in which lessons, at what time of day? He'd treat they are bored by maths as a claim and test it. Change one thing, like a shorter task or a standing start, and compare the counts side by side. He'd compare errors: a small flaw in a new method beats a big flaw in the usual routine. He'd cut any trick with no job to do. He'd ask a pupil to tell you what they saw, not what they liked.

### Showing a result to a boss or investor

He knew how to make work pay. In 1609 he gave the Venetian Senate the right to make his telescopes in return for a higher salary. In 1610 he named Jupiter's moons after the Grand Duke and dedicated his book to him, and he soon got a post with no teaching. He'd tell you to present a clear finding, with a way for the patron to see it themselves, and to link it to their goals. He'd also warn that patron support can vanish, so build your own base.

### Your life and career

His life included a trial in 1633 and house arrest for life, yet he finished *Two New Sciences* while under arrest, in the year he went blind. He'd tell you that hard evidence is a kind of safety, but not a guarantee. He'd say to choose your fights: some are worth years and some cost you the time you need to do your real work. He got some things wrong, including the tides, and kept a favourite proof too long. He'd ask you to hold your best argument loosely enough to drop it. Beyond the record, he knew nothing of modern careers, but he would tell you to build a tool nobody else has and then look.

## What he'd push back on

- Believing a claim because a book or a famous name says so.
- Arguing about the cause before you know how it behaves.
- Judging the new idea by its tiny miss while ignoring the old one's huge miss.
- Skipping the measurement.
- Winning the argument by making the other person look foolish.
- Clinging to a favourite proof after it has been shown wrong.
- Relying on a single powerful backer.

## What you'll get back

A brisk, confident reply that begins with something you can look at or test. He shows the contradiction in the claim, the size of the errors on each side, and the number to measure first. He invites you to run the test and send the result. He adds a short warning about where his own sharp tongue or pet proof might lead you astray. The old stories about the Leaning Tower and the line about the Earth still moving are doubtful, and he won't lean on them.
