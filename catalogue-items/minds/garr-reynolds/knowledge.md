# Garr Reynolds: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Reynolds describes Presentation Zen as an approach to communicating through restraint in preparation, simplicity in design and naturalness in delivery. [S4] In an interview, he explicitly resists treating it as one rigid method for every presentation. [S2] Its everyday value is a disciplined set of choices: decide what this audience needs, organize the explanation before decorating slides, give visuals a real purpose and rehearse the spoken delivery. A useful talk can include a separate document for detail, room for questions or no slides at all. Visual elegance does not replace evidence, accessibility or the audience's actual decision.

## Core ideas

### 1. Work out the message before working on the template

His visual-thinking essay describes externalizing ideas on large sheets of paper and still using notebooks and whiteboards to prepare. [S5] An original application is a short project update. Write the audience's question at the top of a page. Put the main answer and possible supporting points on separate cards. Move them until the relation is clear. Only then decide which cards need a visual. This prevents an existing template from deciding the argument by accident. The point is not to prove that paper is superior for everyone. Use an accessible arrangement that lets you see and revise the structure without committing to polished layouts too soon.

### 2. Separate the spoken explanation from the detailed record

His site criticizes the combination of dense reading material and presentation slides. [S4] In the charting interview, he recommends tables, handouts or advance material when the audience needs substantial data. [S3] An original exercise is to divide a crowded deck into three parts: the spoken sequence, the visuals that help it and the detail people need to consult afterward. A chart may belong on screen while the full table belongs in a document. Keep the two consistent. This is an information-design choice, not permission to omit evidence from a consequential decision or make the record impossible to access.

### 3. Simplify toward a visible purpose

Reynolds's interview answers emphasize visuals that support the spoken message and reject blaming software alone for poor presentations. [S2] An original slide review asks what the audience should notice first and what relation the slide makes easier to understand. Remove decoration that competes with that purpose. Retain the units, date, source, necessary qualification and labels that support interpretation. A process diagram may need more than a single image, and a comparison may need an honest baseline. Simplicity is the result of selecting and organizing what matters; it is not the rule that fewer visible marks always make a better explanation.

### 4. Give data time to become a discussion

In the charting interview, Reynolds warns about showing information too quickly and asks what the data means. [S3] An original use is a meeting with one important table. State what is measured and which comparison matters. Pause before explaining the interpretation. Ask what the table leaves unresolved, then connect it to the proposed next step. Provide the detailed values separately if people need to examine them. The output is a short chart explanation and a real question period. A fluent narrative should not carry the audience past a weak inference or make a correlation sound like an established cause.

### 5. Use design relations deliberately and credit their source

His design-fundamentals essay discusses contrast, repetition, alignment and proximity, explicitly crediting Robin Williams's *The Non-Designer's Design Book*. [S6] An original application is a slide with three stages of a volunteer task. Align the stage headings, place each instruction near its stage and use a consistent visual treatment for repeated elements. Reserve stronger contrast for the current decision or exception. Test whether the groupings communicate the correct relationships. Do not attribute these broad principles solely to Reynolds, or use color as the only way to distinguish essential categories. The purpose is a readable relation, not a display of fashionable minimalism.

### 6. Rehearse for an audience rather than a perfect script

His interview recommends complete rehearsals and practice with people who can respond to the actual presentation. [S2] An original application is a community talk the user knows well but keeps overloading. Rehearse the opening, transitions and ending aloud. Mark where explanation takes longer than expected and where a visual fails to do its job. Ask a listener what they understood, then cut or clarify the material. Keep notes for precise figures and required wording. Natural delivery does not mean careless improvisation, memorizing every sentence or pretending to be someone without nerves. It means preparing well enough to attend to the people in front of you.

## How to apply the approach to a short talk

Start with a one-page audience brief. Name who is attending, what they already know, how long you have and what they should understand or be able to decide afterward. A presentation for an unfamiliar group needs different context from an update to teammates. A request for a decision needs a different ending from a short lesson. Write those constraints before choosing a visual style.

Next make a sequence in ordinary words. A practical original structure might show the current situation, the important observation, the available response and the question the group must answer. That is an example, not a universal Reynolds formula. Test whether each part earns its place. Move interesting background into a separate note when it does not change the audience's understanding of the main point. Keep a necessary caveat with the claim it qualifies.

Choose visuals only after the explanation exists. A diagram can show a handoff; a chart can show a comparison; a photograph can establish a relevant physical detail. Some sentences need no visual. For each selected aid, say what the listener should look at and why. Check that it is legible at the expected viewing distance and that the spoken explanation does not depend solely on sight. Make the supporting document useful on its own.

Finally rehearse with the actual time limit. Include pauses, a question and the ending rather than timing only the uninterrupted script. If the talk is too long, remove or relocate content before accelerating delivery. Record unresolved factual questions as follow-up work. The output is a message, a compact sequence, purposeful visual notes and a detail sheet that agree with one another.

## Where you can put the lens to work

### Rescue a crowded five-minute update

You have twenty slides because every stage of a project felt worth recording. Ask what the meeting needs to decide now. Select the current constraint, the evidence that matters and the request. Put the historical detail in a readable appendix or separate note. Sketch the spoken sequence before changing the slide design. This original application produces a timed outline and a detail map. It does not promise an arbitrary ideal slide count. Some updates need only a document and a question; others benefit from a small diagram or demonstration.

### Explain a shared cost without hiding the table

A group needs to compare the costs of two event arrangements. Keep the actual values in a table people can inspect. On screen, show the comparison that matters, with units and the relevant assumptions. Explain any costs excluded from the estimate and invite a question before asking for a choice. The output is a chart or table explanation and the complete supporting record. This is an original communication exercise, not a current price estimate. A visually clean choice should not conceal the information that would make the less attractive option reasonable.

### Prepare a talk when the display may fail

A community meeting room may not support the planned equipment. Rehearse the explanation using a simple printed diagram or spoken description. Mark which points require an alternative format and what materials the audience needs. Keep the opening and the final request understandable without a decorative screen. The output is a practical delivery plan with a backup. Do not assume every audience member can use the same printed format; ask about the actual access needs and available arrangements instead of treating a single backup as sufficient for everyone.

### Replace a script with a usable rehearsal map

You lose your place whenever you forget one sentence. Write the main point for each section and the transition that connects it to the next. Keep exact figures and necessary wording in notes. Rehearse from the map, then ask a listener whether the argument remains understandable. The output is a speaking outline and a short list of places needing clarification. It does not require removing all notes or performing a style of confidence. The goal is a reliable explanation that leaves enough attention for the audience's response.

## Blind spots to watch

The approach can be criticised when visual simplicity becomes an aesthetic demand that removes necessary context. A technical review, an accessible teaching resource and a persuasive keynote have different requirements. A document may need dense information because readers must inspect it. A spoken talk may still need captions, written terms, an equation or a reference. Test the format against its actual purpose and audience rather than treating text as a defect in itself.

Examples of admired presenters are also not proof that their style suits every person or situation. Keep useful principles while allowing the user a comfortable voice and the audience a fair chance to question the content. Do not make broad promises about memory or persuasion from the appearance of a slide.
