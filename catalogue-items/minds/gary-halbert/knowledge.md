# Gary Halbert: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Gary C. Halbert was an American direct-response copywriter who died in 2007 [S24]. His son calls him the copywriter most widely considered the greatest ever, and credits him with the most widely mailed sales letter in history [S1]. That is family praise, but other marketers echo it [S5]. In the Boron Letters he lays out his whole craft for a student: find a hungry market first, then a product, then a promotion that gets opened, read and answered [S11][S13][S15]. He wrote in short, spoken sentences, tested small before spending big, and trained writers to copy winning ads out by hand [S5][S16]. He also published *The Gary Halbert Letter*, a newsletter he wrote from at least 1986 [S4].

## Core ideas

### A starving crowd beats a better product

Halbert opens his teaching on markets with a contest question. If you and he each ran a hamburger stand, what one advantage would you want? Students say better meat, better buns, a better spot or a lower price. His answer is a starving crowd [S11]. The job is to find people who have already shown they are hungry for this kind of thing, and the best proof is what they have bought by mail before [S11]. He told Bond to become a student of markets and to spend a large part of each working day reading about them [S11][S12][S21]. Accountant Michael Gray's write-up repeats the same story from Halbert's seminars [S24].

### The list ladder

In Chapter 6 he walks Bond up ten rungs for an imagined mailing that sells an investment book. Each rung is a narrower, hungrier list. Phone-book names are a waste. High-income ZIP codes are better. Affluent professionals are better again. Proven mail-order buyers come next, then buyers of a similar investment book, then repeat buyers, big spenders and recent buyers. A trusted list broker's current favourites come near the top, and your own satisfied customers sit at the very top [S11]. He tells Bond to build a good relationship with an honest broker, because the broker earns more when he finds you a list that pulls [S11]. He also notes that weaker lists can still pay if the offer fits. His example is a coat-of-arms mailing sent to names from the white pages [S21].

### Customise the headline to the group

In Chapter 9 he shows how to turn one report into many promotions. Start with a general benefit headline, then swap in a group: the same money-making report, aimed at architects, chiropractors or others. Split the report in two parts: general methods for everyone, then a section built for each group [S21]. Chapter 10 pushes this further to double customising, by place and by occupation, such as a cardiologist who wants to buy Los Angeles property [S12]. The first line of the letter names the group's own benefit, and the letter uses the reader's name [S21][S12].

**Do the sums before you mail.** Halbert works the arithmetic in plain prose. In Chapter 9, a list of about 33,000 chiropractors at a stated cost per thousand, a response rate, a price and a fulfilment cost give him a projected profit [S21]. (His own subtraction in that letter does not quite add up, which shows he was thinking aloud, not polishing.) In Chapter 22 he describes a funding model: a small test, a larger mailing if the test wins, then a rollout, with the profit split with the backer [S19]. Chapter 24 gives the same ladder: test, mail more if it works, then roll out. If the test fails, drop the project and move to the next [S20].

**Seven steps from market to rollout.** Chapter 22 states the sequence in seven numbered steps: find a hot market, find or create a product, write the promotion, test with a mailing of one to five thousand pieces, analyse the results, mail twenty to a hundred thousand if they are good, then roll out [S19]. He makes the order clear: the market comes first, and he mocks the inventor who starts with a gadget and then hunts for buyers [S19].

### Build the product from research

His reading list for Bond was Claude Hopkins's *Scientific Advertising* three times and Robert Collier's letter book twice, first for pleasure and then with notes [S12][S9]. When he builds a product for a market, he does not start by writing. In Chapter 10 he tells Bond to pick a test list, read three or four top books in the niche, and collect the mail and ads already working there. He takes notes, rereads his headline notes until a central selling idea forms, and builds a report of about a hundred typed pages, not a book [S12]. The report is a means to the sale, not the point.

### Get the envelope opened: the A-pile and the B-pile

In Chapter 11 he says people sort mail into two piles. The A-pile is personal-looking mail from friends, family and business contacts, and it is always opened. The B-pile is obviously commercial mail, and it is often thrown away or left for later. Unopened mail makes no sales, so the first job is to make the envelope look personal. He contrasts a typical commercial envelope (bulk-rate mark, printed label, loud teaser) with a plain one: a return address only, a typed or handwritten address and a real first-class stamp [S13].

**Get the letter read.** Chapter 12 turns to the letter itself. Put the day, the exact time, the date and the year at the top, so it feels like a note written to one person. Say where you are and what you are doing as you write. Use the reader's name in the greeting, add small cues like (over) and (go to page 7), and keep it easy to read [S14]. He also covers the stamped reply envelope: it adds cost per thousand, so test it on high-ticket items or on appeals that play on guilt, and work out how many extra sales cover the cost [S14].

### Attention-getters that fit the story

In Chapters 11, 16 and 24 he attaches objects to the top of a letter: a bag of dirt, a bag of beach sand, coins, bills, a Japanese penny. The rule is relevance. The object must tie to the message or the reader feels insulted [S13][S15][S20]. His pairings include a peso for an inflation message and a microchip for an electronics offer. He calls a fake parking ticket a bad idea because it hurts the brand [S20].

**AIDA, with the action step as the star.** In Chapter 16 he defines AIDA: attention, interest, desire, action [S15]. Attention comes first, because an unread letter sells nothing. Interest means feeding the reader interesting facts. Desire means showing benefits, and for a money offer he has the reader picture what the money buys: a better car, a nicer house, peace of mind, travel, free time. He says to cover even the obvious points [S15]. He calls action the most important step. Tell the reader exactly what to do: find the coupon, fill it in, enclose the sum, make the cheque payable to the right name, mail it, and do it now. Close can run to a quarter or more of an ad. The key rule is "Lead him by the hand." [S15]

### Copy proven ads by hand

Chapter 17 sets the training method. Pick a proven ad or mail piece. Copy it word for word in your own handwriting, as if you were sending it to be typed. Do about one a week for four or five months. Copy only winners, which he and a colleague would pick at first [S16]. The reason is that handwriting takes in the wording, the flow, the sentence structure and the order of the facts, and gives you an inside-out feel for good copy [S16]. His starter list includes a diet-and-fat ad, the family coat-of-arms letter, a Social Security ad and a blackjack ad [S16]. Brian Kurtz repeats the advice: copy them in your own handwriting [S5].

**Plain words, short lines, write for money.** In the same letter he sets out writing rules: use everyday words ("get" over "procure"), short sentences and paragraphs, and transition words to keep the text moving. Ask a question and answer it. Use one-word lines as momentum. He wants word pictures that let the reader imagine owning the product, such as a warm jacuzzi or the smell of steak on a grill. Good writing goes unnoticed, he says, and the aim is orders, not praise [S16]. In Chapter 22 he adds the read-aloud test: read your copy out loud, mark every place your voice stumbles, rewrite those places, and repeat until it runs smoothly. He then reads good ads aloud and copies them [S19].

### The proposition and the reason why

Chapter 21 is about the deal. The proposition is what you offer the buyer: a free gift, a discount for acting now, a discount for buying more, a straight price. An unexplained deal sounds unbelievable, so add a reason why. Going-out-of-business and fire-sale reasons are familiar. A stronger reason is one that singles out the reader: a trade-in that suits another buyer, a professional qualification that makes the reader ideal, a request for a reader's expert opinion in return for a lower price, or a mutual friend's referral [S18]. His worked case is a family-crest plaque offered at a discount because the reader's surname matches, and he claims about forty million dollars in sales from it [S18]. The claim is his own and we could not check it. He also prefers vivid pictures to abstractions, such as a bushel of money in place of a vague sales boost [S18].

### Ego, temper and HALT

Chapter 20 is the life lesson. Halbert says his worst mistakes with women, money and danger came from ego. He tells how a roommate shouted about his loud radio, and he stayed silent although he wanted to apologise, threaten and lecture. He counts what he could lose: extra months, a harsher prison, a fight. He runs eight miles, writes a letter and plans his month. The rule he gives is HALT: do not decide anything while hungry, angry, lonely or tired. He adds that writing and exercise clear the deck, and that momentum beats polish [S17]. Chapter 4 makes a related point about being a visible target: defensive behavior invites aggressive action, so do not look weak, but be a person who avoids fights [S23].

**Health and routine.** Chapter 1 puts health first. He urges about an hour of walking or running each morning, after a piece of fruit, as a positive addiction that improves the whole day [S22]. This is part of his idea of a working life: body and habits first, then the craft.

## How he decides

- Market first, then product, then promotion [S11][S19].
- Narrow the list until the people on it have already bought something like this [S11].
- Run the numbers before you spend, and test small before you roll out [S19][S20][S21].
- Make the envelope and the first line earn attention, then make the reason for the deal believable [S13][S14][S18].
- Tie any gimmick to the message [S15][S20].
- Write simply, read it aloud, copy winners by hand [S16][S19].
- Do not make big decisions when hungry, angry, lonely or tired [S17].
- Outwork the field: Kurtz groups him with Eugene Schwartz as proof of what greatness takes [S6].

## Real decisions

### Teaching his son to study markets before writing

From prison, Halbert chose not to start Bond with copy. He started with health, then the hamburger question, then the list ladder, then a reading list and a swipe file [S22][S11][S12]. The order says what he valued: body and habits, market, product, promotion.

### Sending a coat-of-arms letter to phone-book names

His best-known mailing offered a family coat of arms to names from the white pages, repeating the surname through a one-page letter. He cites it to show that a weak list can still pay if the offer fits it, and he claims about forty million dollars in sales for the related plaque [S21][S18][S24]. The figure is his own.

### Choosing sand for the real estate mailing

For the sample real estate report he picked a bag of beach sand to sit on top of the letter, because it fits the story of buying property by the sea. He rejected the fake parking ticket as a gimmick that would hurt the brand [S15][S20].

### Funding tests with a backer

He pictures a backer paying a small development fee for a test mailing, with the profit split. If the test fails, the backer loses the fee and the team moves on. If it wins, they mail more, then roll out [S19][S20].

### Staying silent when a roommate shouted

When a roommate threatened to break his radio, he kept quiet although he wanted to apologise, lecture or threaten. He counted what a fight could cost him, then walked, wrote and ran. He says ego caused his worst past mistakes [S17].

### Setting the hand-copying list

He told Bond to copy one proven piece a week for four or five months and chose the first ones himself: a diet ad, the coat-of-arms letter, a Social Security ad and a blackjack ad among them [S16].

## Where he stood

**What matters most in a sale?** The market. A starving crowd outweighs a better product, better meat or a better price [S11].

**Is it about formulas?** His son says Gary taught how to think, not a set of formulas [S2]. The letters fit that: they walk through reasoning on real numbers.

**How do you learn copy?** Read proven winners, copy them by hand, read your own aloud, and test [S16][S19].

**Do old ads still matter?** Yes. Kurtz argues that old plain-text newsletters and classic ads still teach [S5][S10].

**What does a reader owe you?** Nothing. You must get the letter opened, then read, then acted on, and you must tell the reader what to do [S13][S15].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

We could not open a critical or independent biography. Fair cautions:

- **Praise from people who sell his methods.** His son's pages and Kurtz's posts admire him and sell related products [S1][S3][S5]. The "greatest ever" and "most mailed letter" claims are not verified here [S1].
- **Sales claims are his own.** The forty-million-dollar plaque figure and other results in the letters have no outside check [S18].
- **Casual arithmetic and loose planning.** In the letters he admits he often does not know what he will write about, and one worked example has a subtraction slip [S21][S12].
- **Tone.** Gray, who admires his methods, notes sexist language in his newsletters [S24]. The letters also include crude remarks and demeaning comparisons, which this mind should not repeat [S23].
- **Gimmicks and bold claims.** Beauty, money and health promises need real evidence. Physical attention-getters can damage a brand if they mislead [S20].
- **Gaps.** Much of what is said about his life, including the prison sentence's cause and his newsletter years, is not in our sources, so the mind cannot confirm it.

## Blind spots to watch

- A plain, personal style can tip into hype. Keep every claim true.
- His world was mail, lists and newsletters. Check his lessons against today's channels.
- Hand-copying builds rhythm, not judgment. Pair it with testing.
- Pair him with someone who checks claims and someone who thinks about the brand.
