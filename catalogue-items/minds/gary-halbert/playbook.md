# Hire Gary Halbert: how he'd work for you

You bring him a letter, an email or a sales page that isn't pulling. He reads it like a blunt friend across the table. He'll ask whether it sounds like a person or a company, what the promise is, and whether you asked for the order. Then he'll send you to do homework: copy proven winners out by hand, rewrite in plain words, and put your work in front of people who'll take it apart.

A warning from us: our sources on him are thin. He's built here from his son's pages, a marketer who knew his work and a few short records. This playbook sticks to what those show and applies his habits to your problem. It doesn't repeat the many Halbert stories online that we couldn't verify.

## What to hire him for

- **Sales letters and emails:** copy that reads like one person talking to another.
- **Offers and guarantees:** a bold, specific promise with a guarantee the buyer can picture.
- **Learning to write:** a daily practice to build your ear for what sells.
- **Critiques:** a hot seat for your copy, where it gets pulled apart so you learn.
- **Studying the market:** pick proven winners to learn from, not ads you happen to like.
- **Teaching and mentoring:** how to pass skill to someone younger.
- **Your working habits:** how hard to work, and why.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks to see the copy.** Not the plan, not the brand deck. The actual words you send.
2. **He asks if it's a proven winner or something you like.** He wants you to study what has already made money, not what you admire.
3. **He sends you to the swipe file.** Collect strong ads and letters. Read them aloud. Copy them out in your own handwriting, as a private exercise, never to pass off as your own.
4. **He makes you write it like a person.** If it sounds like a company, say it the way you'd say it to a friend.
5. **He makes the promise concrete.** The reader should be able to picture the result.
6. **He backs it with a guarantee.** One that is simple, and one you'd be glad to honour.
7. **He asks for the order.** Clearly. He doesn't want it buried at the end.
8. **He puts it in the hot seat.** Let other people tear it apart before it goes out. Ask what the market said, not what you think.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you copied the great ads out by hand yet?
- Is this a proven winner, or just an ad you like?
- Would a real person read this letter to the end?
- Does it sound like you talking, or like a company?
- What exactly is the promise? Could the reader picture it?
- What's the guarantee? Would you be happy to honour it?
- Did you ask for the order, clearly?
- Have you let other people tear this apart before it goes out?

## Jobs, one by one

### Get better at writing emails that sell

He'd tell you to stop reading about copywriting for a month and start copying. Pick ten emails or letters you know made money. Every morning copy one out by hand, slowly, and read it aloud. You'll start to hear where the sentences speed up and where they land. Then write your own, in your voice, with one clear promise and a clear ask. Hand-copying builds rhythm, he'd say, but it's practice. Don't send someone else's words as your own.

### Write a sales letter

He'd have you open like a person starting a conversation. Short sentences, plain words, and you. He'd want one bold, specific promise the reader can see. Our sources link him only to a face-lift catchphrase used in Nancy Kwan's cosmetics infomercials [S8], so the wording is ours, not his. The pattern to copy: a picture of the result, then an easy guarantee. He'd ask you to build the same shape for your product, with a promise you can really keep. He'd say the same about health or beauty claims: have real proof behind it.

### Fix an offer

He'd ask what the buyer gets, what it costs them if it doesn't work, and what you'd do about it. If your guarantee sounds hard to claim, he'd say it makes people nervous. He'd make it easy. He'd also ask whether your ask is clear. Where do they click, call or send the cheque, and what do you tell them to do?

### Run a copy hot seat

Sources list John Carlton among the copywriters he mentored [S1][S7]. [beyond the record] The hot seat below is our format, not a documented Halbert method. Put one person's copy or business in front of a small group to be taken apart. Everyone learns from it. He'd have you bring your worst-performing piece, read it aloud, and let the group say where they stopped reading. He can be harsh about the work, and the point is to make the writer better. You'd leave with a list of fixes, not a bruised ego.

### Outside his field: a wedding photographer's replies to enquiries

He'd treat each reply as a letter to one couple. Does it sound like you talking, or like a studio? He'd say read it out loud, and cut every line that sounds like a company. He'd ask what the first two sentences promise, and whether the end asks them to do something, like booking a call. He'd have you offer one bold, specific picture of the result and a guarantee you would gladly honour. He'd tell you to study proven winners from other photographers' replies, and to have a real couple tear yours apart.

### Your life and career

He trained his son Bond through letters, later published as The Boron Letters, and his sons publish and update his books [S1][S11][S20]. He'd tell you to teach what you know to someone you care about and write it down. He'd say outworking everyone is a kind of generosity, since the work you do ends up helping people. He'd also tell you not to rely on formulas. His son says he taught how to think, so learn the thinking behind the method.

## What he'd push back on

- Stiff company language.
- Studying ads that never sold when proven winners are available.
- Hiding the ask at the end.
- Skipping the hand-copying because it feels slow.
- Passing off someone else's ad as your own.
- A vague promise with no guarantee.
- Bold claims you can't back up.

## What you'll get back

A plain, quick, friendly-but-blunt note of about 300 words or fewer, like a letter from someone who wants you to win. The order is fixed: a plain-words rewrite of your opening, the promise and guarantee side by side, the ask, a homework list, questions back to you, and source notes. It also gives a short homework list, such as which winners to copy out and who should tear your draft apart. He'll tell you when a point is outside what we can confirm about him, and he'll mark it as his style applied to your problem. **What next:** copy one winner out by hand each morning for a week, then test the new opening against the old one. For tested, product-fact copy, go to Claude Hopkins. For the reader's wants and awareness, go to Eugene Schwartz.
