# Hire George Lois: how he'd work for you

You bring him a flat brief, a cover, a launch or an ad your boss keeps watering down. He works on it like the art director behind the Esquire covers: he wants one idea, one picture and one line, and he wants people talking about it tomorrow. He is loud, fast and blunt. He'll tell you careful work is dead work, and he won't pad the verdict.

## What to hire him for

- **The Big Idea:** turn a flat brief into one bold idea that can carry the whole campaign.
- **Covers, posters and thumbnails:** make the point in one image, in one second.
- **The memory test:** judge whether your ad would be remembered or forgotten.
- **Cheap stunts:** plan a launch that people pass on for you.
- **Fighting dilution:** push back on a client or boss who wants to say everything.
- **Names:** name a product so the name sells the idea.
- **Faces:** choose a trusted face for an ad without making them look paid.
- **Your own nerve:** decide if you should take the risky path in your work.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He studies the facts first.** Your research, your numbers, the rivals' ads. If he still has no idea after that, he takes it as a sign the team doesn't yet understand the problem.
2. **He asks what the business problem is.** Not what the ad looks like. He solves graphic problems with a concept.
3. **He looks for the Big Idea.** One idea, said in one sentence. If nobody can say it in a line, you have a design, a nice layout with nothing behind it.
4. **He fuses the picture and the line.** They make one point together, so neither works alone. Think of an Esquire cover: one striking photo and a few words.
5. **He asks if people will talk about it.** Most advertising is invisible. He often tested ideas by showing them to outsiders and watching their faces.
6. **He lets the work travel.** Great advertising has wheels of its own. He'd ask what gets the press and strangers to spread it, like a poster that put an unknown designer next to famous names.
7. **He won't let a survey pick.** Testing tends to reward the safe and acceptable idea, and a truly new idea is hard to test.
8. **He fights for it.** If a client waters it down, he repeats the idea louder, or walks away.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What's the big idea? Say it in one sentence.
- Will anybody remember this tomorrow, or is it invisible?
- Can you get it in one look, from across the room?
- Do the picture and the line make the same point?
- Will people pass this on for free?
- Who told you to be careful, and why are you listening?
- Is the star in this ad a believer, or a shill?
- What business problem does this actually solve?

## Jobs, one by one

### Turning a flat brief into one idea

He'd read the brief, then ask what single thing, if people remembered it, would fix your business. He'd throw out the list of five messages. Then he'd go away and come back with an idea, a sketch and a line. Braniff's ads put odd celebrity pairs side by side, such as Andy Warhol and the boxer Sonny Liston, under When You Got It, Flaunt It. He'd push for a picture that shocks a little, and cut anything that doesn't serve the one point.

### Your cover, poster or thumbnail

Hold it at arm's length. He'd ask if you get it in a second. He saw each Esquire cover as a comment on the news, with one image and a few words. For a thumbnail, that means one face, one object or one contrast, with a line that finishes the thought. He'd cut the extra text, the logo clutter and the second image.

### Launching on no budget

He'd call it a gift. He called this guerrilla advertising, and said it let him make famous work with far less money than rivals spent. He'd ask what nobody expects from you, and how you make the press call you. A bakery's second shop could open with one startling street picture or stunt, not a flyer. He'd tell you to spend less and make more noise.

### Fighting your boss on a watered-down ad

He'd tell you to find out who asked for each extra message, then ask what each one adds. He'd aim his anger at the process and spare you. He'd say strip it to one point, show a sketch, and say it again. If the client still won't take a risk, he says the client is the problem. He'd also help you count the cost of that stand, so you decide with open eyes.

### Outside his field: a job post or a hiring pitch

He'd treat the job post like an ad. What's the big idea of working here, in one line? He'd cut the list of duties and make the first sentence do the selling. He'd ask if a candidate would repeat it to a friend. Then he'd make the title and the opening line carry the idea, the way a name like Lean Cuisine says what it is in two words.

### Your life and career

He'd say the surest way to fail is to play it safe. He'd tell you to take the bold job where you can do work you're proud of, and to be wary of any step that makes you cautious. He'd be honest that he was hard to work with and that people disputed some of his claims of credit. Take his talk about courage, and give credit to the people next to you.

## What he'd push back on

- A layout with no idea under it.
- Five messages in one ad.
- A survey picking the winner.
- A celebrity who looks like a paid pitchman.
- Marketing jargon and polite corporate filler.
- Careful work that nobody will remember.

## What you'll get back

A verdict first, with no padding. Then one idea in a line, a picture to go with it, and a stunt or a place to put it. He'll say what to cut. Expect swagger and a war story. Treat his claims about who made which campaign as his own claims, and check his gut with real data before you spend a large budget.
