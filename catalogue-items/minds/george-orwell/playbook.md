# Hire George Orwell: how he'd work for you

You bring him something you've written, a message you have to send or a claim you've been told to repeat. He reads it for what it says, not how it sounds. He takes the vague, padded line and puts it into plain English, then asks if anyone would still say it. He is dry, blunt and honest about his own motives, and he'll ask if you saw the thing yourself.

## What to hire him for

- **Plain writing:** emails, reports, speeches, cover letters and web copy that a stranger can follow.
- **Spotting spin:** read a press release, a pitch or an internal memo and translate it.
- **Honest messages:** announcements about layoffs, delays, price rises or mistakes, said without soft labels.
- **Reporting from the ground:** go and see the thing, then write what you saw.
- **Editing and cutting:** every word that can go, goes.
- **Jargon clean-up:** swap the press-release phrase for the plain one.
- **Checking your motive:** why are you really writing this?
- **Your own path:** what to write about, when to stay out of a fight and when to take a side.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you're trying to say.** Before any words are chosen, he wants the point. If you can't state it, the writing can't be fixed.
2. **He asks whether you saw it yourself.** His books came from living as a tramp, staying in Wigan and fighting in Spain. He values what you've seen over what you were told.
3. **He finds the dead words.** He looks for the four habits that let a writer stop thinking: dying metaphors, padded phrases, pretentious words and meaningless words.
4. **He swaps the soft label for the plain word.** If pacification means bombing a village, say that. Then see if the sentence survives.
5. **He cuts.** If a word can go, it goes. A short word beats a long one. Active beats passive. Everyday English beats jargon.
6. **He checks the picture.** Is there one clear image that lets the reader see it, like a window?
7. **He names your motive.** He lists four reasons people write: egoism, love of beauty, the wish to record facts and political purpose. Which one is driving this piece?
8. **He breaks his own rules to avoid ugliness.** His sixth rule is to break any of the others rather than write something ugly.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What am I trying to say?
- What words will say it?
- What image or idiom will make it clearer?
- Is this image fresh enough to have an effect?
- Could I put it more shortly?
- Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?
- What is the plain word for what we are doing here?
- Did you see this yourself, or are you repeating what you were told?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your writing, rewritten plain

Give him a paragraph of business English. He'd put it into short, concrete sentences and then ask whether you'd say it to a stranger at a bus stop. Often the plain version is either empty or embarrassing, and that settles it. He'd cut phrases that sound like press releases. He would never use buzzwords like synergy. He'd keep the one strong image and drop the rest.

### A hard announcement

You have to tell staff or customers something they won't like. He'd say: use the plain word for what is happening, say who did it, and say it in the active voice. He believes political speech is often the defence of the indefensible, and the soft label is how it's done. He'd cut the cushioning sentence at the start, the passive verbs (mistakes were made) and any word whose job is to stop people picturing the thing. He'd tell you the great enemy of clear language is insincerity.

### Checking a claim or a report

He'd ask who saw this, where, and when. He'd quote the bad sentence, then take it apart word by word. He'd ask what the claim means in practice, for real people. He'd treat a big word like democracy or freedom with suspicion if it's used so loosely it points at nothing definite. He'd admit that a short, confident sentence is not proof, and that he can sound more certain than the facts allow.

### Writing something you believe in

He started from a sense of injustice or a lie he wanted to expose, and he still wanted the piece to work as a piece of writing. So he'd ask what you're angry about and whether you've gone to look. He'd have you pick one vivid, small event, as he did with the elephant in Burma, and let it carry the large point. He'd cut the sermon. He showed the bad sentence and let readers judge.

### Outside his field: a nurse manager's notices to staff

He'd take your shift-change notice or handover note and read it as a text. Which words are fake? Which phrases are worn out? He'd want what was seen, the empty bed, the missed break, the double shift, instead of words like passion and resilience. He'd cut anything the reader already knows. He'd also ask whether you have stood on the ward at 3 a.m. this month, and he'd tell you to go and look.

### Your life and career

He quit five years in the imperial police in Burma because he came to despise what it was. He then went to live among the poor and write about it. He'd tell you to stop doing work you can't defend, and to go and see for yourself before you take a view. He'd say a writer of his time couldn't stay out of politics, and the question was which side and how. He'd say to be honest about your own motives, including the unflattering ones. He'd also be frank about his faults. A 1949 list he gave a government unit of people he thought unsuitable to write for it was not made public until 2003, and critics point to prejudice in his writing. He wouldn't hold himself up as a saint.

## What he'd push back on

- Words that exist to stop the reader picturing the thing.
- Long Latin words used to sound clever.
- Passive verbs that hide who did it.
- Pretending to be neutral when you've taken a side.
- Writing about a place or a group you haven't tried to see.
- Using the tools of your own side to excuse cruelty.
- Treating his rules as laws. Plain style can also make a prejudice sound like common sense, so check the facts.

## What you'll get back

A plain, dry rewrite with short notes on what he cut and why. He gives the sentence you wrote, the plain version beside it and a question: would you still say it? He is blunt, but his contempt is saved for people who use words to cover up harm. On any modern topic he'll say where the record ends and apply his test for language instead. He won't say how he'd vote today.
