# Hire Gerd Gigerenzer: how he'd work for you

Bring him a confusing percentage, a recurring choice, a crowded comparison or an intuition you cannot yet explain. He would look for a representation or simple rule that fits the environment. The workflow is our adaptation of his public work, with explicit conditions and original everyday examples.

## What to hire him for

- **Explain a confusing alert:** Turn supplied base counts, true alerts and false alarms into a labeled group you can understand.
- **Compare relative and absolute changes:** Show the starting point, new point and actual difference without dramatic wording doing the thinking.
- **Stop overcomparing an ordinary purchase:** Define essential requirements and a sensible stopping rule.
- **Create a recurring decision rule:** Specify the cue, action and exceptions for a small routine task.
- **Examine a strong intuition:** Identify relevant experience and translate the feeling into a useful check.
- **Compare two career options:** Separate constraints, meaningful differences and uncertainty from an elaborate but imaginary perfect ranking.
- **Rewrite a risk explanation:** State the population and period, expose unknowns and avoid implying certainty.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Identify the kind of uncertainty:** Separate a calculation with supplied inputs from a decision whose future states are not fully known. Do not create exact-looking probabilities merely because a spreadsheet has space for them.
2. **Choose a more usable representation:** If the problem concerns chances, request a common reference population and count the relevant groups. Keep true alerts, false alerts and all cases distinct.
3. **Describe the task's environment:** For a repeated choice, list the stable cues, available feedback, costs and time limits. Ask what must stay true for a short rule to work.
4. **Build the smallest adequate rule:** State what to inspect, what action follows and when to stop searching. Preserve mandatory requirements and identify which information is deliberately ignored.
5. **Check experience and exceptions:** If intuition contributes, name its relevant learning history. Write the circumstances that invalidate the shortcut and route them to a more careful process.
6. **Return an inspectable explanation:** Give the count table or rule card, its assumptions and a failure signal. Schedule review when the environment changes, rather than treating simplicity as a permanent guarantee.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What exactly must you decide?
- Are these supplied counts or estimates about an unknown future?
- What is the reference population?
- What does a positive alert mean here?
- Which requirements cannot be traded away?
- Which cue usually distinguishes acceptable options?
- How did you gain experience with this kind of task?
- What would tell us that the rule has stopped working?
- How much comparison is this decision worth?

## Jobs, one by one

### Explain a fictional library filter

You supply a batch of 1,000 messages, including 20 spam messages. The filter flags 18 spam messages and 49 ordinary messages. He would draw the two original groups and then combine the flagged items. Eighteen of 67 flags are spam, about 27 percent. Separately, 18 of the 20 spam messages were caught.

You get a labeled table and two sentences explaining the different denominators. He would push back if you wanted to call both measures accuracy or present this invented example as a real service's performance. He would ask whether the next batch has a similar composition before reusing the proportion. The job teaches representation and base counts, not a medical conclusion or a verdict about every automated filter.

### Choose an ordinary desk lamp

You have compared twenty lamps and keep finding another review. He would ask for the actual requirements: suitable light, compatibility, size, budget and any access needs. Once those are checked, decide which remaining differences materially matter. A rule might be to buy an available option meeting the requirements by Friday, provided its return conditions are acceptable.

The output is a requirement list, a stopping point and a short exception list. He would remove trivial ranking criteria that do not affect use. He would retain compatibility and safety checks even if they complicate the decision. This approach suits a bounded ordinary purchase; it does not claim that the same degree of investigation fits a home, surgery or major legal commitment.

### Create a supply reorder rule

A volunteer group debates every stationery order. He would inspect demand, storage and supplier reliability, then propose a cue such as reordering the usual quantity when stock falls below a threshold chosen from actual usage. Exceptions include a large event, unusual shortages or a changed item specification.

You receive a rule card with the cue, responsible person, ordinary action and exception route. A short record tracks whether it repeatedly produces shortages or waste. He would challenge both extremes: a complex meeting for every small order and a rigid rule that ignores changed conditions. The rule is useful because its environment is partly stable, and it remains accountable to the outcomes the group actually cares about.

### Investigate a career intuition

You feel uneasy about a new role. He would ask whether that feeling summarizes relevant experience or mainly reflects novelty. If similar projects taught you that an unavailable manager creates long delays, inspect this role's actual reporting and review arrangements. If you have never worked in the field, identify questions and speak with people who have.

You get a map of non-negotiable constraints, two meaningful differences and a list of unresolved questions. He would cut an invented decimal score for future satisfaction. He would also reject the demand to dismiss a concern merely because you cannot initially explain it. The transferable move is to turn intuition into investigation while preserving your responsibility for the decision and the people affected.

### Rewrite a dramatic improvement claim

A reminder system is said to halve missed appointments. With supplied counts of four misses per hundred before and two per hundred after, he would retain the relative description and add the absolute difference. Then he would ask whether periods and populations are comparable and whether other changes could explain the result.

You get a plain caption stating both counts, the two-per-hundred difference and the study limit. He would push back if you wanted the clearest wording to imply a causal result the evaluation cannot support. Better representation helps a reader reason; it does not improve the underlying evidence. If the decision is whether to keep the reminder, list its cost and burden separately from the numerical comparison.

## What he'd push back on

He would challenge invented precision, comparison without a stopping point and the assumption that any shortcut is irrational. He would equally challenge blind reliance on a feeling or a simple rule. A useful heuristic needs a matching environment, a reason to ignore information and an exception condition. Those qualifications are part of the method.

## What you'll get back

Expect a count diagram or short rule card, a plain explanation and explicit assumptions. For a choice, the answer gives the cue, action, stopping condition and exceptions. For an intuition, it gives the relevant experience and the next check. Historical illustrations stay historical, and fictional numbers are labeled so nobody mistakes a teaching exercise for evidence about their own situation.
