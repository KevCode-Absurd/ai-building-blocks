# Guy Kawasaki: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Guy Kawasaki is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from versions of his talk "The Art of Innovation", given in 2007, 2009 and 2014, a 2016 podcast transcript and a reference page. He gave the talk many times and the details shift between versions, so we note where they differ. Date each view to its source. His own website blocked our reader, so his books and blog posts are summarised here only through these talks [S1].

## In one paragraph

Kawasaki was born in Honolulu in 1954, studied psychology at Stanford and later earned an MBA at UCLA [S1]. He joined Apple in 1983 and helped market the first Macintosh, working as its chief evangelist for about four years [S1]. Since then he has written books including The Macintosh Way (1990), The Art of the Start (2004) and Enchantment (2011), co-founded the venture firm Garage Technology Ventures in 1998, and became chief evangelist of the design site Canva in 2014 [S1]. His best-known talk sets out about ten rules for innovators: make meaning, make a mantra, jump to the next curve, roll the DICEE, don't worry about being rough, polarize people, let a hundred flowers bloom, keep improving, find a niche, and pitch with the 10/20/30 rule [S2][S3][S4]. He ends with a bonus: don't let the bozos grind you down [S4].

## Core ideas

### Make meaning, not money

Kawasaki says to start with the wish to change the world, not to make money [S3][S4]. Companies built mainly for money tend to fail and draw the wrong founders and staff [S3]. Money usually follows meaning [S4].

How he applied it: he points to Apple, Google, eBay and YouTube, each of which made something available to everyone that only a few had before: computing, information, trade and video [S4]. In 2016 he said Canva's aim was to make design open to everyone [S5].

### Make a mantra, not a mission statement

Mission statements are long and vague and try to please too many groups [S5]. A mantra is two to four words that say why the company exists [S4][S5]. He mocked a fast-food chain's long mission statement and suggested a short mantra in its place [S4].

How he applied it: he gives Nike's as authentic athletic performance and FedEx's as peace of mind [S3][S4].

### Jump to the next curve

Making the current product a bit better isn't innovation [S4]. He uses the history of ice. First, workers cut ice from frozen lakes. Then factories made ice anywhere, in any season. Then came the home refrigerator [S4]. Ice cutters never became ice factories, and ice factories never made fridges, because each defined itself by what it did, not the benefit it gave [S2][S4]. In 2007 he framed the target as ten times better, not 10% better [S2].

How he applied it: the Macintosh team aimed for a new kind of personal computer, not a slightly better Apple II [S4]. In 2016 he linked this to Clayton Christensen: challengers attack leaders from below with something simpler and cheaper, then move up [S5].

### Roll the DICEE

Great products are DICEE [S2][S3][S4]. The letters shift between versions. In 2014 they were deep, intelligent, complete, empowering and elegant [S4]. In 2007 and 2009 the last E was emotive [S2][S3]. Deep means rich features, such as a sandal with a bottle opener built into the sole. Intelligent means it understands your problem, such as a car key that lets parents cap the top speed. Complete means the whole experience, including support. Elegant means someone cared about the design [S3][S4].

How he applied it: in 2014 he said a Mac becomes one with you, while Windows has to be wrestled to the ground [S4]. Expect that kind of loyalty to Apple in his examples.

### Don't worry, be crappy

Waiting for perfection means you never ship [S4]. A revolutionary first version will have flaws. That's fine, as long as it is a real leap [S4][S5]. He says this doesn't mean shipping junk [S4][S5].

How he applied it: the first Macintosh had little software, no hard disk, little memory and a slow chip, and still pointed to the future of computing [S3][S4].

### Polarize people

A product built for everyone tends to be mediocre [S2][S3]. Great products delight some people and annoy others [S4][S5]. He doesn't say to annoy people on purpose, only not to fear it [S4].

How he applied it: he loved his TiVos, which many advertising people hated because they skip ads. Harley-Davidson, the Mac and the iPhone split opinion the same way [S4]. In 2007 and 2009 he cited the boxy Scion xB car [S2][S3].

### Let a hundred flowers bloom

Customers often find uses you never planned [S3][S4]. Take their money, learn why they bought, and give them more reasons to buy [S2][S3]. Let customers shape how you position the product [S4].

How he applied it: the Mac was meant for spreadsheets, databases and word processing, and failed at all three. Desktop publishing with Aldus PageMaker saved it [S4]. Avon's Skin So Soft lotion found buyers as a mosquito repellent [S3].

### Churn, baby, churn

Innovators need to be a little in denial while building, to ignore naysayers [S4]. Once you ship, start listening to users and keep improving: version 1.1, 1.2 and so on [S3][S4]. You can't stay rough forever [S2].

How he applied it: in 2016 he said Apple itself should improve its products faster [S5].

### Niche yourself, then perfect your pitch

He draws a grid of value and uniqueness [S4]. Low on both is the dot-com dog food trap. Valuable but not unique means competing on price. Unique but not valuable is pointless. Aim for the top right: valuable and unique [S4][S5]. For pitches, his 10/20/30 rule is ten slides, twenty minutes and at least 30-point type [S3][S4][S5]. One version of the font rule is the age of the oldest person in the room divided by two [S4]. The goal of a pitch to investors is to avoid being ruled out, not to leave with a cheque [S3]. He also urges a custom opening for each audience [S4].

How he applied it: his niche examples include Fandango, which spares you the ticket line, a Breitling watch with an emergency beacon, and Smart cars that park nose-in to the kerb [S2][S4]. He admits he breaks his own slide rule [S4].

## How he decides

- Ask what meaning the product makes before what money it makes [S3][S4].
- Sum up the why in two to four words [S4].
- Ask if this is a new curve or a better old one [S4].
- Check the product is deep, intelligent, complete, elegant and empowering [S4].
- Ship once it's a real leap, then improve fast [S4][S5].
- Follow the buyers you didn't expect [S3][S4].
- Aim for valuable and unique [S4].
- Keep the pitch to ten slides and twenty minutes [S3][S4].
- Ignore critics who lack vision, however famous [S4][S5].

## Where he stood

**Should you wait until it's perfect?** No, as of 2014. Ship a real leap with rough edges, then fix it [S4].

**Should you please everyone?** No. Great products polarize [S4][S5].

**Do mission statements help?** No. Use a short mantra [S4][S5].

**Can big companies jump curves?** Rarely. They are tied to their current business, so young founders often lead [S5].

**Should rich, famous critics worry you?** No, as of 2014. Wealth and fame don't guarantee good judgment [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The Yahoo call.** By his own account, in the mid-1990s he turned down a chance to interview for Yahoo's CEO job, a choice he estimated cost him $2 billion [S3]. He also said early doubters, himself included, dismissed Yahoo as a business [S2][S3].
- **Breaking his own rules.** He admits he doesn't follow his own slide rule when he speaks [S4].
- **Apple bias.** His examples lean heavily on Apple and knock Windows [S4]. In 2016 he also said some of Steve Jobs's methods were questionable [S5].
- **Shifting lists.** His rules change between versions of the talk, including what DICEE stands for and whether there are ten or eleven steps [S2][S3][S4].
- **"Be crappy" can be misread.** He has to keep explaining that it doesn't mean shipping junk [S4][S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- His rules come from tech and consumer products. A service business or a public body may need different tests.
- "Ignore the bozos" can turn into ignoring fair warnings. Some critics are right.
- Some famous wrong predictions he quotes are often said to be misquoted or never said (disputed). Check before repeating them.
- Polarizing works for fans of a brand. It can hurt when you need broad trust, as in health or finance.
- Pair him with someone who tests ideas with data, or who thinks about operations and costs.
