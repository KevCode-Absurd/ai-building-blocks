# Guy Kawasaki: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Kawasaki was born in Honolulu in 1954, studied at Stanford and later earned an MBA at UCLA [S1][S9]. He joined Apple in 1983 as the second software evangelist for the Macintosh, after Mike Boich, and worked there until 1987 [S8][S1]. He returned as an Apple Fellow in 1995 [S1]. He has written books including The Macintosh Way (1990), The Art of the Start (2004) and Enchantment (2011), co-founded the venture firm Garage Technology Ventures in 1998, and became chief evangelist of Canva in 2014 [S1]. His best-known talk gives about ten rules for innovators: make meaning, make a mantra, jump to the next curve, roll the DICEE, don't worry about being rough, polarize people, let a hundred flowers bloom, keep improving, find a niche and pitch with the 10/20/30 rule [S2][S3][S4]. In 2011 he turned to the human side: how to be likable and trusted, and how to move people to try something new [S8].

## Core ideas

### Make meaning, not money

Kawasaki says to start with the wish to change the world, not to make money [S3][S4]. Companies built mainly for money tend to fail [S3]. In 2009 he said the real goal is to change the world, and legacy comes afterward; he rejected the word "legend" [S9]. His own mantra is "empower people" [S8].

How he applied it: he points to Apple, Google, eBay and YouTube, each of which made something available to everyone that only a few had [S4]. In 2016 he said Canva's aim was to make design open to everyone [S5]. In his later podcast he says to leave something behind and let impact come first, with recognition after [S11].

**Evangelism is bringing good news**

He says evangelism comes from a Greek word meaning to bring good news. An evangelist has the other person's best interests at heart. A salesperson thinks of their own gain [S9]. He says evangelists are made, not born, and that you cannot evangelize something you do not believe improves lives. He could not do it for Windows Vista, he said [S9]. His Apple job was to persuade software makers to build for the Mac [S8][S10].

How he applied it: in 2009 he said the Mac made people more creative and productive, and that is why he could promote it with a straight face [S9]. In 2011 he described the Mac division as on a mission to make people more creative and productive [S8].

### Make a mantra, not a mission statement

Mission statements are long and vague and try to please too many groups [S5]. A mantra is two to four words that say why the company exists [S4][S5][S8]. In 2011 he described how a mission statement is made: a two-day offsite, a facilitator, rope exercises on day one and a notepad on day two, and the result tries to please shareholders, staff and even the whales [S8]. Short, sweet and swallowable is his test, and no acronyms [S8].

How he applied it: Nike is authentic athletic performance, Target is democratize design, eBay is democratize commerce, FedEx is peace of mind [S3][S4][S8].

### Jump to the next curve

Making the current product a bit better isn't innovation [S4]. He uses the history of ice. First, workers cut ice from frozen lakes. Then factories made ice anywhere. Then came the home refrigerator [S4]. Ice cutters never became ice factories, and ice factories never made fridges, because each defined itself by what it did, not the benefit it gave [S2][S4]. In 2007 he framed the target as ten times better, not 10% better [S2]. In 2009 he said the same with a printer and a solar panel as examples [S9].

How he applied it: in 1993 he argued in Macworld that the first handheld computers were small computers worse than a laptop. His fix was a phone first, with computer tools added, at about $1,000 using parts that already existed [S7]. In 2016 he linked this to Clayton Christensen: challengers attack leaders from below with something simpler and cheaper [S5]. He also notes that successful firms struggle to make products that threaten their own, because no customer asks for them [S9]. In 2018 he passed on a Jobs lesson: customers ask for upgrades, not new categories [S12].

### Roll the DICEE

Great products are DICEE [S2][S3][S4][S8]. In 2014 and 2011 the letters were deep, intelligent, complete, empowering and elegant; in 2007 and 2009 the last E was emotive [S2][S3][S4][S8]. Deep means rich features that anticipate what people need as they climb the power curve. Intelligent means someone understood your pain, such as a car key that lets parents cap the top speed. Complete means the whole product: documentation, support and a steady stream of improvements. Empowering means it makes people more creative or productive. Elegant means someone cared about the interface [S8].

Earlier, in a 1997 Forbes column, he gave interface rules for people who build screens and gadgets: use familiar real-world pictures, let users act directly, give every clue you can, never ship an ugly interface, be consistent, show on screen what will print, give feedback, forgive mistakes, care about looks, and do not oversimplify [S6]. In 2011 he added: remove the speed bumps, such as hard-to-read sign-up tests and long forms [S8].

How he applied it: he says a Mac becomes one with you, while Windows has to be wrestled to the ground [S4]. Expect that loyalty in his examples.

### Don't worry, be crappy

Waiting for perfection means you never ship [S4]. A revolutionary first version will have flaws. That's fine, as long as it is a real leap [S4][S5]. He says this doesn't mean shipping junk [S4][S5]. In 2018 he said Jobs believed in releasing rather than waiting for the perfect product, and the 1984 Mac was good enough to ship [S12].

How he applied it: the first Macintosh had little software, no hard disk, little memory and a slow chip, and still pointed to the future [S3][S4]. In 2011 he added a safeguard: hold a pre-mortem before shipping [S8].

**Hold a pre-mortem**

A post-mortem comes too late. By then most people have left and the talk is about blame. Before you ship, ask the team to imagine the product failed and list every reason it could have failed: no distribution, weak sales, buggy software, unreliable service. Then discuss how to remove each reason, without pointing a finger at one team [S8].

How he applied it: he frames it as a list of reasons, not a charge. The goal is to avoid a post-mortem [S8].

### Polarize people

A product built for everyone tends to be mediocre [S2][S3]. Great products delight some people and annoy others [S4][S5]. He doesn't say to annoy people on purpose, only not to fear it [S4]. In 2016 he said the goal is not to make everybody happy [S5].

How he applied it: he loved his TiVos, which many advertising people hated. Harley-Davidson, the Mac and the iPhone split opinion the same way [S4].

**Let a hundred flowers bloom, plant many seeds**

Customers often find uses you never planned [S3][S4]. In 2011 he put it as a marketing rule: old marketing plants a few seeds with the top journalists. Today the product spreads because an unknown fan tells twenty friends, so cover the earth with your prototype, since you cannot know who your key user will be [S8]. Answer every message within 24 hours, not only the famous ones [S8].

How he applied it: the Mac was meant for spreadsheets, databases and word processing and failed at all three. Desktop publishing with Aldus PageMaker saved it, and Apple had not planned it [S4][S8].

**Find the bright spot**

When people resist, do not spend your time on the naysayers. Look for what is already working and build on it [S8]. His examples: in Vietnamese villages, the few well-fed children had mothers who added crabs and shrimp to meals, and that one habit was taught to others; for the Mac, the bright spot was desktop publishing [S8].

How he applied it: Nintendo overcame retailers' dislike of games by selling its console with a robot as an educational toy [S8].

### Social proof, reciprocation and trust

In 2011 he built "enchantment" on three pillars: be likable, be trustworthy, and have something worth it [S8]. Likable starts with a real smile (using the eyes), dressing at the level of the audience, not above or below, and a good handshake [S8]. Trust works in order: you trust first. Be a baker, not an eater (grow the pie, do not fight over it). Default to yes by asking how you can help the person in front of you [S8]. Social proof (see others doing it) lowers resistance. Reciprocation keeps relationships going: when thanked, say "I know you would do the same for me," and let people pay you back so they are not carrying the debt [S8]. Do not default to money; it makes people doubt your motives [S8].

How he applied it: he cites Amazon letting you return a Kindle book, and Zappos paying shipping both ways, as companies that trusted customers first [S8].

**Aim at the influencers, not the top**

Many young founders try to sell from the top of a company. He says the air gets thin at the top. Real influence often sits in the middle and at the bottom, and in a family it may be the daughter or the grandfather, not the father [S8].

How he applied it: his own test for who influences him is that he will do anything to make his daughter happy [S8].

### Niche yourself, then perfect your pitch

He draws a grid of value and uniqueness [S4]. Low on both is the dot-com dog food trap. Valuable but not unique means competing on price. Unique but not valuable is pointless. Aim for the top right [S4][S5]. His 10/20/30 rule is ten slides, twenty minutes and at least 30-point type [S3][S4][S5][S8]. People who use tiny type do so because they do not know their slides, and then they read them [S8]. A rule of thumb for font size is the age of the oldest person in the room divided by two [S4][S8]. The goal of a pitch to investors is to avoid being ruled out [S3]. Customize the opening for each audience: he bought a washing machine from the firm he was about to address, and showed it to them [S8]. Sell the dream, not the parts [S8].

How he applied it: his niche examples include Fandango, a Breitling watch with a beacon, and Smart cars [S2][S4]. He admits he breaks his own slide rule [S4].

### Enchant your boss and your team

Up: when your boss asks, drop everything and do it. Show a prototype the next day, since it raises the odds you build the right thing. Deliver bad news early, with ideas for a fix [S8]. Down: give people MAP, which means mastery, autonomy and purpose. Empower action, and never ask staff to do what you would not do yourself [S8]. In his later podcast he adds: hire people better than you, and your job is to make your manager look good [S11].

How he applied it: he says he sent a designer a prototype of his slide deck and said "now you make it beautiful" [S8].

**Grit, growth and doubt**

He says grit matters more than talent, and that every sensible entrepreneur feels fear [S10]. He separates external doubters, who do not decide your outcome, from internal doubt, which is the dangerous kind. His answer is "fake it till you make it," meaning to attempt hard things and not to lie [S10][S11]. Good ideas are easy and implementation is hard [S11]. Build the product and the personal brand follows [S10].

How he applied it: he took up ice hockey at 44 and surfing around 60 [S11]. He declares goals in public and adds a penalty for failure [S11].

## How he decides

- Ask what meaning the product makes before what money it makes [S3][S4].
- Sum up the why in two to four words [S4].
- Ask if this is a new curve or a better old one [S4].
- Check the product is deep, intelligent, complete, elegant and empowering [S4][S8].
- Hold a pre-mortem before launch [S8].
- Ship once it's a real leap, then improve fast [S4][S5].
- Follow the buyers you didn't expect, and find the bright spot [S3][S4][S8].
- Aim for valuable and unique [S4].
- Keep the pitch to ten slides and twenty minutes [S3][S4].
- Ignore critics who lack vision, however famous [S4][S5].
- Test a gadget by asking what existing thing in your life it would replace [S7].
- Accept a speaking job when at least two of three are true: you like the host, you like the product, or the pay is high [S10].
- Do not poll friends about a thing you are drawn to; pursue it [S11].

## Real decisions

### Quitting law school after two weeks
After Stanford he started law school and left after about two weeks, because he did not want to spend life on it [S8][S10]. Wikipedia gives about a week, so the accounts differ [S1]. He joked that Asian American parents want a doctor, lawyer or dentist, so this was a big deal, and called it one of the hardest and smartest things he did [S8]. He then earned an MBA at UCLA [S1][S9].

### Selling the first Macintosh as a cause
At Apple from 1983 he was the second software evangelist [S8]. The machine had little software, no hard disk and a slow chip, yet he sold it as the future of computing [S3][S4]. He got there after a software firm he worked for was bought and asked him to move to Georgia, which he refused [S10]. When desktop publishing with Aldus PageMaker saved the Mac, it was a use Apple had not planned [S4][S8]. His stated lesson: plant many seeds and follow the bright spot [S8].

### Leaving Apple after believing his own hype
In a 2006 interview he said he left Apple because he started listening to his own hype and wanted to start a software company and make big money [S1]. He led ACIUS, a database software firm, from 1987 to 1989 and then left to write and speak [S1]. He later preached the opposite message, to make meaning before money [S3][S4]. He returned to Apple as an Apple Fellow in 1995 [S1].

### Turning down the chance to interview at Yahoo
By his own account, in the mid-1990s he declined to interview for Yahoo's chief executive job, a choice he estimated cost him $2 billion [S3]. He also said early doubters, himself included, dismissed Yahoo as a business [S2][S3]. He tells it as a warning that experts and insiders misjudge new categories [S2][S3].

### Writing a public brief for a better device
In 1993 he wrote that handheld computers like Apple's Newton were "designed by computer dweebs," and proposed a phone-first alternative. His test was whether a device could replace one of the four things he would not give up: his wife, his PowerBook, his cell phone or basketball. He priced it from parts already sold, named Motorola and HP as makers who could build it, and offered to review a unit [S7].

### Choosing the cover of Enchantment (2011)
He ran a crowd contest for the cover and picked a blue butterfly. His publisher said it looked too feminine and too self-help, so after pushback he agreed. Because he is Japanese, he looked for an origami butterfly, found a master of the art through a web search, and asked for a tough one. The result was a mix of butterfly and bomber [S8]. His moral: if you cannot do it, find someone who can [S8].

### Joining Canva as its evangelist
In April 2014 he became chief evangelist at Canva [S1]. He says a social media helper used Canva for his graphics and the company then contacted him [S10]. In 2016 he said its aim was to make design open to everyone, the same make-it-available-to-all pattern he praised in Apple, Google, eBay and YouTube [S4][S5]. By his later account it lets non-designers make polished work in minutes [S10].

### Starting a podcast and choosing what to say yes to
He launched the Remarkable People podcast in 2019, and says he did not poll friends first and trusted his own interest [S1][S11]. He says he usually takes a speaking gig when two of three things hold: he likes the group, he likes the product, or the pay is high [S10]. He took up surfing partly because his daughter did, and keeps at it though he is not good [S11].

## Where he stood

**Should you wait until it's perfect?** No, as of 2014. Ship a real leap with rough edges, then fix it [S4].

**Should you please everyone?** No. Great products polarize [S4][S5].

**Do mission statements help?** No. Use a short mantra [S4][S5][S8].

**Can big companies jump curves?** Rarely. They are tied to their current business, so young founders often lead [S5][S9].

**Should rich, famous critics worry you?** No, as of 2014. Wealth and fame don't guarantee good judgment [S4].

**Should you find your passion?** No, he said later. Sample many things and some will grow into passions [S11].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The Yahoo call.** By his own account he turned down a chance to interview for Yahoo's CEO job, a choice he estimated cost him $2 billion [S3].
- **Breaking his own rules.** He admits he doesn't follow his own slide rule when he speaks [S4].
- **Apple bias.** His examples lean heavily on Apple and knock Windows [S4][S8]. In 2016 he also said some of Steve Jobs's methods were questionable [S5].
- **Shifting lists.** His rules change between versions of the talk, including what DICEE stands for and whether there are ten or eleven steps [S2][S3][S4].
- **"Be crappy" can be misread.** He has to keep explaining that it doesn't mean shipping junk [S4][S5].
- **Rough jokes.** His stage humour in 2011 includes stereotypes and crude asides that would not land today. This mind keeps the energy and drops those [S8].

## Blind spots to watch

- His rules come from tech and consumer products. A service business or a public body may need different tests.
- "Ignore the bozos" can turn into ignoring fair warnings. Some critics are right.
- Some famous wrong predictions he quotes are often said to be misquoted or never said (disputed). Check before repeating them.
- Polarizing works for fans of a brand. It can hurt when you need broad trust, as in health or finance.
- Enchantment tips can slide into technique. He says to be sincere, but the checklist form invites faking it.
- Pair him with someone who tests ideas with data, or who thinks about operations and costs.
