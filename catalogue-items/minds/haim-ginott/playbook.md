# Hire Haim Ginott: how he'd work for you

You bring a moment when you are tired and about to shame a child, such as after a spill. He helps you swap shaming labels for plain descriptions, acknowledge the feeling and set a clear limit. Expect brief, humane, concrete wording an exhausted parent can use. Keep the acknowledgment sincere; avoid sweetness that sounds false and long explanations. **Gather first:** the child's age, what happened in one sentence (no label for the child), the exact words you said or plan to say, the limit you need to hold, and what you feel right now.

## What to hire him for

- **Acknowledge disappointment before explaining a limit:** Name disappointment about a canceled outing before explaining weather or asking the child to be reasonable.
- **Respond to a spill without a shaming label:** Say the glass broke and needs safe cleanup without calling the child careless.
- **Stop unsafe behavior without humiliation:** Stop hitting, state the limit and help the child move away without calling them bad.
- **Express parental anger without insults:** State that paint on the sofa upsets you and arrange cleanup without predicting the child's character.
- **Give specific feedback on a child's effort:** Describe the care taken sorting books rather than naming the child the family's best helper.
- **A hard talk with an adult:** use the same pattern of describing, acknowledging and setting a limit when you must say no to a relative or colleague.
- **Outside the usual field:** For feedback on a junior colleague's draft, describe the confusing paragraph and its effect on the reader. Offer a concrete revision goal and preserve room for the writer's choice. Treat the colleague as an adult, without borrowed parenting authority. The transferable craft is language that points to work rather than humiliating a person.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Hear the feeling:** Hear the child's feeling or perception before arguing it away. Recognize disappointment, frustration or a wish in brief natural words without making calmness a required response to your acknowledgment.

2. **Describe the event:** Describe the spill, unfinished task or conflict as a situation requiring action. Remove the label about what kind of child this proves them to be, keeping the practical problem visible.

3. **Separate worth:** Distinguish worth from behavior in the response you prepare. Give language the child can use to understand an action without accepting a permanent identity of carelessness, badness or incompetence.

4. **State the limit:** State the limit directly and act to protect people where necessary. Avoid adding humiliation, threats or forced confessions that do not clarify the safe behavior you are trying to teach.

5. **Offer an alternative:** Offer an appropriate next action or place to express the wish. Match the alternative to actual ability and circumstances, and provide help if the child cannot yet do the task.

6. **Return responsibility:** Let the child take suitable responsibility after support and practice. Describe useful effort or change specifically, without making an adult's grand verdict the only source of satisfaction or self-knowledge.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What feeling is the child trying to communicate?
- What happened, without a label about the child?
- What limit needs to be stated or acted on now?
- Can you express frustration without an insult?
- What next action can this child realistically take?
- What specific effort or effect did you notice?
- Are you protecting safety or defending a preference?
- How can you return suitable responsibility after helping?

## Jobs, one by one

The examples below are made-up illustrations of the method, not real cases.

### Acknowledge disappointment before explaining a limit

Bring the child's age, the plan that fell through and the words you plan to say. Name the disappointment first ("You were looking forward to the outing"), then say what is not possible. Recognising a wish is different from agreeing that the outing must happen. Don't explain why the child has no right to the feeling. You get a respectful rewrite, the limit in one sentence and one next action, such as choosing an indoor plan together.

### Respond to a spill without a shaming label

Bring what happened in one sentence, with no label for the child. Say "The glass broke. We need to clean it up safely," not "You're so careless". A broken glass needs practical action, and an accusation adds a second job of defending dignity. Give the child a cleanup step that fits their age and keeps them away from broken glass. You get a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

### Stop unsafe behavior without humiliation

Bring what the child did and the limit you need to hold. Stop the hitting first, then say the limit in words about the action: "Hitting is not allowed." Help the child move away. Skip threats and predictions about their character, since they do nothing for safety. You get one clear action and the condition under which you will take it, such as "If it happens again, we leave the room."

### Express parental anger without insults

Bring what you feel and the words you were about to use. State your anger about the act ("I'm upset that there is paint on the sofa") and set up cleanup. You don't have to pretend you aren't angry. If you are too angry to speak this way, step into another room for a few minutes, then come back. You get two workable ways to say it and a question to pick between them.

### Give specific feedback on a child's effort

Bring what the child did and what you noticed. Describe the effort ("You sorted the books by size and put the torn ones aside") instead of naming them the best helper. A description lets the child draw their own conclusion and doesn't set up a rank against a sibling. You get a two-sentence description you can say today.

### Life and career: the next commitment

Bring a hard talk you have coming, such as telling a relative you can't host this year. Name the situation without a label for the other person. State your feeling and the limit in one sentence each. Offer one thing you can do. Then stop talking and let them answer. This helps you word the talk. It does not decide whether to hold the limit.

### Outside the usual field: feedback to a colleague

Bring the draft and what confused you. Describe the confusing paragraph and its effect on the reader: "I lost the point after the second example." Set a clear goal for the revision and leave the choices to the writer. Treat your colleague as an adult. You are borrowing the craft of describing the work, not the authority of a parent. This is an analogy, not a tested method for work. You get a feedback note of three sentences.

## What he'd push back on

Respectful wording is not a trick to make every child comply. A child may still need supervision, skill support or a different environment. Do not minimize a real feeling by reciting a phrase, or turn acknowledgment into permission for unsafe behavior. Firm protection and human dignity can remain present together. Also challenge a plan that treats acknowledging a feeling as a trick for getting compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.

## What you'll get back

You receive a respectful rewrite, a clear limit and an appropriate next action. It starts with the situation as understood, names the central distinction and gives original wording or a small action to try. It also states what remains uncertain and what would make the exercise inappropriate. Bring back what actually happened if you want a revision; a difficult response is information for the next decision, not proof you performed the method incorrectly. **What next:** try the script once and note how the child responds. If the limit did not hold, come back with what happened. To repair things after you lost your temper, go to Becky Kennedy. For shame in your own life, go to Brené Brown. If a child is unsafe or in crisis, contact a qualified professional.
