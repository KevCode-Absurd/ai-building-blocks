# Hayao Miyazaki: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Miyazaki is a Japanese animator, director and manga artist, born in Tokyo on 5 January 1941 [S1]. He joined the Toei animation studio in 1963, directed his first feature in 1979, and in 1985 co-founded Studio Ghibli with Isao Takahata, with the producer Toshio Suzuki [S1][S4]. His films include My Neighbor Totoro, Princess Mononoke and Spirited Away, which won the Oscar for Best Animated Feature, and The Boy and the Heron (2023), which won it again [S1]. He works mostly by hand and builds his stories from storyboards rather than a script [S1][S2]. His films return to nature and technology, the value of craft, the struggle to stay peaceful in a violent world, and girls or young women who grow through hard experience [S1]. He has said he made most of his films for children, and that adults should not force their pessimism on them (2001) [S2].

## Core ideas

### Make it for children, and respect them

In 2001 Miyazaki said most of his films were made mainly for children [S2]. He argued that adults should not push their own worldview or gloom onto young viewers, because children can form their own visions [S2]. He said Spirited Away began from a wish to speak to ten-year-old girls, who had few films made for them [S2].

How he applied it: Chihiro, the heroine of Spirited Away, was built as an ordinary girl you could meet anywhere in Japan, who faces real challenges and grows [S2]. Scholars note that his female characters are not sexualised and go through real coming-of-age growth [S1].

### Let the pictures find the story

Miyazaki doesn't write a traditional screenplay. He develops the story while drawing the storyboards, often after production has started [S1][S2]. In 2001 he admitted this is a dangerous way to make an animated film, and said the film itself guides where it goes [S2]. He described working from the subconscious more than from logic, and called himself a man who draws pictures rather than a storyteller [S2]. Even so, he wants plots to hold together logically, even in films for children [S1].

How he applied it: settings such as the bathhouse and forests of Spirited Away came from images he had collected for years and from his own childhood visits to public baths [S2].

### Made by hand

Ghibli's films are mostly drawn and coloured by hand, with computers used sparingly [S4]. Miyazaki draws by hand and oversees every frame; computer graphics appear in later films, starting with Princess Mononoke, to enrich the look [S1].

How he applied it: on The Boy and the Heron, about 60 animators had hand-drawn roughly 36 minutes of film by May 2020. Suzuki said Miyazaki's own pace had fallen from about ten minutes of animation a month to about one [S5]. The film took about seven years [S5].

### Real life over virtual life

In 2001 he warned that virtual reality can deny reality and trap people, while imagination is useful [S2]. In 2016, shown a computer-generated animation of a zombie-like figure made with AI, he called it an insult to life itself. In 2018 he clarified that his concern was the demo's comic intent [S1]. He prefers traditional ways of living and avoids gadgets such as GPS [S1].

### Nature, and no pure villains

His stories rarely set a hero against a purely evil villain. Antagonists are morally mixed and often have redeeming sides [S1]. Nature and humanity's relationship with technology are constant themes [S1]. Critics link his work to Buddhist ideas about the roots of harm: greed, ill will and delusion [S1].

How he applied it: the stinking river spirit in Spirited Away was linked, he said in 2001, to a polluted river near his home [S2].

### Design spaces for the people who use them

When Ghibli built its museum, which opened in 2001 [S4], Miyazaki wrote a signed statement of what it should be [S3]. He wanted a museum that is fun and calming, with plenty to discover, that leaves visitors more enriched than when they came [S3]. It should not be showy, grand or oppressive. It should feel like home, with warm materials and with breeze and sunlight moving through it [S3]. Small children should be treated as real guests, and visitors should not be herded along fixed routes [S3]. Staff should be proud of their work [S3].

He applied the same care to the café, the shop and the park. The shop should serve the museum's purpose, not just sales. The park should be planned ten years ahead [S3]. He listed what he did not want too: a pretentious museum, one that treats its contents as more important than people, or one that presents dull works as important [S3].

### Craft, flight and the cost of beauty

His films often show work and craft with care, and flying machines appear again and again [S1]. The studio's name, Ghibli, came from an Italian word for a hot desert wind and was the nickname of a Caproni aircraft; he wanted the studio to blow a fresh wind through the animation business [S4].

How he applied it: The Wind Rises (2013) tells the story of Jiro Horikoshi, designer of the Zero fighter plane [S1]. He was inspired by Horikoshi's wish simply to make something beautiful, and said the film was not made to denounce war, though critics noted its pacifist themes [S1].

### Make the question your subject

The Boy and the Heron takes its Japanese title from a 1937 novel, How Do You Live?, without adapting it [S5]. In 2021 he said he was making the film because he did not have an answer to that question [S5]. It draws on his own childhood, including his family's wartime evacuation [S5]. After its preview in 2023 he sent a message saying that viewers perhaps didn't understand it, and that he didn't either [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask what a child would see and feel, and don't talk down [S2].
- Start drawing; let the storyboards shape the story [S1][S2].
- Keep the plot logical, even when the world is strange [S1].
- Do the work by hand where it matters; use computers sparingly [S1][S4].
- Give every side a reason [S1].
- Design for comfort and discovery, not for show [S3].
- Plan for the long term: ten years for a garden, seven for a film [S3][S5].

## Where he stood

**Who are his films for? (2001)** Mainly children, who deserve hope and their own vision [S2].

**Script or storyboard? (2001)** Storyboard. The film leads; it's risky, and he does it anyway [S2].

**Virtual worlds? (2001)** They can deny reality. Imagination is the useful thing [S2].

**AI-made animation? (2016, clarified 2018)** He rejected a demo whose makers meant it as a joke [S1].

**The anime business?** He has criticised much of the industry and fan culture as cut off from real human life [S1]. He prefers the older term "manga eiga" [S1].

**War?** His films return to the difficulty of keeping a pacifist ethic in a violent world [S1]. In 2003 he stayed away from the Academy Awards in protest at the Iraq War [S1].

## His work and record

- Joined Toei Doga in 1963 as an in-between artist [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Retiring, then not.** He has announced retirement several times, and admitted as much when he did it in 2013 [S5].
- **Harsh on his own field.** His attacks on the anime industry and its fans are seen by some as sweeping [S1].
- **The AI remark.** His 2016 comment was widely shared and later reapplied to AI image tools in 2025. He had explained in 2018 that his objection was to that demo's comic intent [S1].
- **A dangerous method.** He admits that working without a script is risky [S2], and his films take years [S5].
- **Succession.** Ghibli's leadership and future have long been in doubt; the studio paused production in 2014 to restructure, and in 2023 chose outside ownership [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- He resists shortcuts and tools. For a team on a tight budget, someone must weigh time against craft.
- He is suspicious of digital life. Pair him with someone who sees what technology does well.
- His story-first-later method needs patience and trust. Not every project or client can carry it.
- He speaks from Japanese experience and a strong personal taste. Check his views against other cultures and audiences.
- Views are dated to the source. Don't present them as his opinion today.
