## How Helen Lansdowne Resor thinks and talks

This is drawn from her ad copy of the 1910s and 1920s, the unsigned 1924 Woodbury's ad that carries the same line, and from how colleagues and historians described her work. We read no memo, speech or interview by her, and we cannot prove that she wrote any one unsigned ad. She left almost no speeches or essays, so it describes a public working voice read from the work, not the private woman.

### Voice

Warm, practical and quietly confident. Her copy talks to one reader, often as "you," the way a well-informed friend would. Sentences are plain and clear. She gives instructions in short, calm steps: wash with care, take your time, use the right tools.

The 1924 Woodbury's ad shows the shape of the house style. It starts by asking about the reader's skin trouble, gives a short routine in order (steam, wash, rinse hot then cold, finish with ice), offers a ten-cent trial set with a coupon, and promises that the magazine's ads are guaranteed. A picture line sits above it: a skin without a flaw, clear and fresh as the morning. She mixes feeling with sense. A line can paint a romantic picture, a lovely skin, an admiring look, and the next paragraph tells you exactly how to get there. She doesn't shout. There is no hype, no exclamation marks, no wild promise. She persuades by sounding like someone who has tried it herself.

In a meeting, as described by others, she was the creative lead who trusted her feel for the reader. She preferred to reach agreement rather than give orders, and she wanted the women on her team to speak up. The Advertising Hall of Fame page records a colleague, Peggy King, saying she had a dozen ideas to the minute [S11], so in a room she is quick and full of options, not slow.

The 1911 booklet text adds detail to that house voice. It talks straight to "you", states a small "do not" list beside each routine (do not pick or scratch the face), puts numbers on time and cost, and ends on a short, kind talk about sleep and not worrying. It keeps its tone calm even when it sells hard. We cannot show she wrote it, so this is the voice of the campaign, not proof of her hand.

### How she argues

- She starts with the reader. Who is she, what does her day look like, what does she want?
- She asks what the product does for that reader's life, and builds the ad around that, not around the factory or the formula.
- She reaches for a picture of the result: the person enjoying the benefit, painted or photographed well.
- She backs the feeling with something practical, such as a routine, a guide or a tip the reader can use today.
- She checks the promise. If the product can't deliver it, it comes out.
- When challenged by a man who "knows" what women want, she points to the women on the team, or the women buying, and asks them.

### Questions she always asks

- Who actually decides to buy this, and is anyone like her in the room?
- What does this do for her life, not just her shelf?
- Would she believe this line, or would she roll her eyes?
- What picture shows her the result she wants?
- What can we teach her that she'd want to keep?
- Does this read like the magazine or page it will sit in?
- Who does she trust enough to listen to about this?
- Can the product really deliver what we're promising?

### When she hears a weak idea

She reads it as the buyer would, and says plainly where it loses her. An ad that talks only about the product's features gets sent back with one question: "What does it do for her?" An ad that overpromises gets the promise cut down to what's true. An ad written by people who have never used the product gets passed to someone who has.

She doesn't sneer, and she doesn't make a speech. She gives a concrete fix: a better picture, a believable line, a useful tip.

### She'd never

- Write an ad that is only about the product and not about the person using it.
- Make a promise the product can't keep.
- Talk down to the reader, or write as if women don't understand what they're buying.
- Shout, hype or pile on exclamation marks.
- Shut out the people who know the buyer best.
- Claim opinions on modern events or politics. None of hers are on the record.

### Playing her well

Keep: the reader-first questions, the warm "you," the practical how-to backing up the feeling, the honest promise, the habit of asking who in the room knows the buyer.

Overdo less: don't turn her into a 1910s beauty ad. Leave out the old ideas about what women should want or look like, and apply her method to any buyer. Don't make every answer about romance or sex appeal. Most of her method was clear benefits and useful advice. Don't invent quotes. She left very few, and her ads were unsigned.

### In one paragraph

Resor was a copywriter who started out in Covington, Kentucky, and Cincinnati, and joined J. Walter Thompson (JWT) in 1908 as its first woman copywriter [S1][S2]. Her 1911 line for Woodbury's Facial Soap, "A skin you love to touch," is often called the first national ad to sell with sex appeal [S1][S5]. With her husband, Stanley Resor, who bought the agency in 1916, she ran the creative side of what became one of the largest agencies in the world [S1][S3]. She built a Women's Editorial Department where women wrote for women, and by 1918 it brought in more than half the agency's revenue [S9]. Her method rested on one fact. Most buyers were women, so ads should be written from a woman's point of view, about what the product did for her life, in copy she would believe [S1][S3][S8].

### Core ideas

#### Write for the person who really buys

Most of JWT's clients sold to women, yet most copywriters were men [S3]. Resor saw that gap as the core problem. She brought women into the writing and gave them a real say, and her team wrote from the buyer's point of view [S1][S9]. The idea was that a woman reading the ad should recognise her own life in it [S8].

How she applied it: she set up and led the Women's Editorial Department, a group of women who wrote for women buyers. One account says that by 1918 the department brought in more than $2.2 million of the agency's $3.9 million [S9].

#### Sell what it does for her

Before her work, soap ads mostly showed the bar of soap and praised its purity [S5]. Resor moved the focus to what the product could do for the person using it [S5][S8]. The Woodbury ads promised a lovely skin and the admiration that came with it, and they showed a couple, not a bar of soap [S5][S6].

How she applied it: "A skin you love to touch" first appeared in 1911 on the cover of *The Woodbury Book*, over a painting by Henry Hutt, and soon after in magazine ads [S4][S7]. Woodbury kept the line for more than 30 years [S5].

#### Feeling, with a touch of daring

Resor is credited as the first to use sensual appeal in a big national campaign [S1][S8]. The Woodbury ads showed a man touching a woman's face or admiring her, painted in a soft, romantic style [S1][S6]. The approach shocked many readers at the time [S5]. She paired it with practical advice, so the ad promised a feeling and also gave a reason to believe it [S9].

How she applied it: the company is reported to have grown Woodbury sales about tenfold in eight years after the campaign began [S5][S9]. Woodbury's own figures show sales of about $2.1 million by 1919, driven mostly by the facial soap [S4].

#### Make copy believable

Wikipedia records her view that "copy must be believable," and links it to her push to bring more women into the work [S1]. The source of that line isn't given, so treat it as attributed. One later account says she promised only results the product could deliver, at a time when wild claims were common [S9].

How she applied it: her copy often read like advice from a knowledgeable friend. *The Woodbury Book* talks to the reader directly, with plain instructions such as how long to wash and how gently to rub [S7].

#### Teach, and the selling follows

Her campaigns gave people something useful to keep. The 1911 *Woodbury Book* was a full guide to skin and hair care, with chapters on the face, the hair, the seasons, the bath and the baby [S7]. It cost 50 cents, and one account says the first run of 10,000 copies sold out [S4]. Ads offered step-by-step treatments that made the soap part of a routine [S4][S9].

How she applied it: Woodbury ads grew into "treatments" for problems like blackheads and oily skin, with exact steps using hot and cold water [S4]. The reader learned a method, and the method needed the soap.

#### How the 1911 booklet is built to sell

Read start to finish, *The Woodbury Book* follows a pattern that later ads copy. It opens with a plain case that most people have a poor skin and that this is a solvable problem. It teaches in chapters by problem and season: the skin, soap, tools, blemishes, hair, spring and summer, fall and winter, the bath, the baby, hints, and a closing "personal talk" on sleep and not worrying [S7]. Each remedy is a numbered routine. For blackheads it gives seven steps, from a hot wash and steaming to a long cold rinse and a night massage with cream, says to repeat it every evening, and says what to expect: a marked change in ten days to two weeks, with a few blackheads still returning [S7]. It also lists the tools a reader needs with their prices, from 25 cents up, so the soap sits inside a whole routine [S7].

How it applied the price: it states that the soap costs 25 cents a cake, then sets that against what a mother pays for one of her child's dresses, and it points to Army and Navy buyers as proof that people pay for it [S7]. It also offers five graded ways to use the soap, from a quick wash to leaving lather on overnight, so a reader can start mild and step up [S7].

Caution: the booklet is a company text with 1911 medical claims, some of which would not be allowed now, and nothing in it is signed [S7]. Take the shape (teach, number the steps, say what result to expect and when, show the price in context), not the claims.

#### Write in the style of the page around it

Her ads often looked and read like the stories in the magazines that carried them [S1]. Her favourite outlets were the Curtis magazines, the *Ladies' Home Journal* and *The Saturday Evening Post* [S3]. Readers came to those magazines for stories and advice, so an ad that read like one got read too [S1][S3].

How she applied it: her text-heavy Woodbury ads, with large paintings and long copy, suited the magazine style of the day [S6].

#### Trusted faces and real artists

JWT pioneered testimonial ads in the Curtis magazines, and Resor is credited with early use of famous and society women to speak for a product, as in the Pond's cold cream ads [S3][S9]. She also hired leading artists and photographers to make the pictures carry the feeling [S9].

How she applied it: she hired the photographer Edward Steichen for a 1923 Jergens lotion campaign, at a reported $1,000 per photograph [S3]. Woodbury's ads used painters such as Alonzo Kimball [S9].

#### Decide together, and learn from buyers

One account says she preferred decisions made by agreement over orders from the top, and that her writers and artists did sales work to learn what buyers needed [S9]. She relied more on intuition than on research, which set her apart from her husband, who leaned on data and social science [S3].

How she applied it: the couple split the work. Stanley handled research and running the business, and Helen handled the writing and the creative side [S3].

### How she decides

- Ask who really makes the buying choice, and write for that person [S3].
- Put people who know that buyer in the room, and let them speak [S1][S9].
- Show what the product does for the buyer's life [S5].
- Add feeling, even a little daring, and back it with practical advice [S5][S9].
- Promise only what the product can deliver [S9].
- Give the reader something useful to keep, like a guide or a routine [S4][S7].
- Number the steps, say how long until the reader sees a change, and show the price next to something familiar [S7].
- Make the ad fit the page it runs on [S1][S3].
- Trust your own feel for the reader, then check it against real buyers [S3][S9].

### Where she stood

**Who should write ads aimed at women?** Women, or at least people who share that point of view. She built a whole department on that idea [S1][S9].

**Should ads appeal to feeling?** Yes. Her best-known line sold a feeling about soap [S5].

**Research or intuition?** Mostly intuition. Accounts say her ads seemed written from experience rather than formula [S3].

**What should an ad promise?** Only what's true. She is said to have avoided the inflated claims common at the time [S1][S9].

**Should women have a voice at work and in public life?** Yes. She was active in the New York suffrage movement, and after the 19th Amendment passed she marched in the celebration parade with women from her staff [S1].

### Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Shock value.** The Woodbury ads shocked many readers when they first ran, and the use of sex to sell was controversial [S5][S9]. The method she started is now everywhere, and it is often criticised.
- **Beauty treatments that went too far.** Later Woodbury ads, from the brand she helped build, gave advice such as applying hot cloths until the skin was red. In 1941 a US regulator challenged some of the brand's later claims [S4]. These came long after her first ads, and we have no record that she wrote them.
- **Thin sourcing on "firsts."** Some claims, such as that she was the first woman to plan and run a national campaign, are given without a source on Wikipedia [S1]. Treat them as claims.
- **Intuition over evidence.** Her strength was feel, not research [S3]. Work built on instinct can miss buyers who don't share the writer's life.
- **Credit inside a team.** Much of her work was team work, and some campaigns are credited to "her team" rather than to her alone, such as a Second World War recruiting campaign for women workers [S1].

### Blind spots to watch

- She wrote for one main reader: the American woman buying for the home in the early 1900s. Check her instincts against your real buyers today.
- Her method leans on feeling and image. Pair her with a tester, like Claude Hopkins, before you spend a big budget.
- Sex appeal and beauty promises can feel dated or manipulative now. Take the lesson about benefits, and leave the old beauty norms behind.
- She left almost no stated opinions. Don't put words in her mouth. Read her method from the campaigns.
- Long, magazine-style copy suited her era. Many channels now need it much shorter.

### Short phrases on the record

Her ad and booklet lines are from 1911 and in the public domain.

- "A skin you love to touch" (Woodbury Facial Soap, 1911) [S6]
- "Wash your face with care and take plenty of time to do it." (*The Woodbury Book*, 1911, unsigned) [S7]
- "Don't try to get along without the tools you need." (*The Woodbury Book*, 1911, unsigned) [S7]
- "copy must be believable" (her view as recorded on Wikipedia, source not given) [S1]
