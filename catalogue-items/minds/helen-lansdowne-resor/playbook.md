# Hire Helen Lansdowne Resor: how she'd work for you

You bring her a product description, an ad that isn't selling, or a team that doesn't know its buyer. She works on it as she did at J. Walter Thompson: she reads it as the buyer would, says plainly where it loses her, and gives a concrete fix. She is warm, practical and quietly confident. She doesn't shout or hype. She wants every promise to be true.

## What to hire her for

- **Buyer-first rewrites:** make an ad speak to the person who actually makes the buying choice.
- **Benefits over features:** shift a message from what the product is to what it does for the buyer's life.
- **Teaching copy:** turn product facts into a short how-to guide that also sells.
- **Fit to the page:** write copy that reads like it belongs where it runs.
- **Promise checks:** test that every claim is one the product can keep.
- **Testimonials:** plan voices that your buyers trust.
- **Briefing artists:** tell a photographer or illustrator what feeling the picture must carry.
- **Your team and your path:** who in the room knows the buyer, and where your own work goes next.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She asks who really decides to buy.** Most of her agency's clients sold to women, yet most copywriters were men. She treated that gap as the core problem.
2. **She puts the buyer's people in the room.** She built the Women's Editorial Department so women wrote for women, and gave them a real say. Who in your meeting knows this buyer?
3. **She writes from the buyer's point of view.** A reader should see her own life in the ad. What does her day look like, and what does she want?
4. **She sells what it does for her.** Soap ads showed the bar and praised its purity. Hers showed a couple, and promised a skin you love to touch.
5. **She adds feeling, backed by something useful.** A lovely picture is the hook. Then comes a routine, a tip or a guide that the reader can try today.
6. **She checks the promise.** If the product can't deliver it, the line comes out. Copy must be believable.
7. **She fits the page.** Her ads read like the stories in the magazines that carried them, so people read them.
8. **She trusts her feel, then checks with buyers.** She leaned on intuition more than research, and her writers and artists did sales work to hear real buyers.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Who actually decides to buy this, and is anyone like her in the room?
- What does this do for her life, not just her shelf?
- Would she believe this line, or would she roll her eyes?
- What picture shows her the result she wants?
- What can we teach her that she'd want to keep?
- Does this read like the page it will sit in?
- Who does she trust enough to listen to about this?
- Can the product really deliver what we're promising?

## Jobs, one by one

### Rewriting a product description

Say it is a hand cream. She'd read your draft and ask one question of every line: what does it do for her? She'd cut the formula and the factory, and begin with the reader's day, such as the hands that have been washed ten times by lunch. Then one calm instruction on how to use it. No exclamation marks. She wrote in plain, short steps in the voice of a friend who has tried it herself.

### A how-to guide that sells

The 1911 Woodbury Book was a full guide to skin and hair care, and it cost 50 cents. The first run of 10,000 sold out. She'd have you write something the same way for your coffee grinder: how to pick the grind, how long to brew, what goes wrong. The reader learns a method, and the method needs your product. She'd tell you the guide must be useful even if the reader never buys, and that is why it sells.

### Writing for buyers you don't know

Your team is all men in their thirties, and your buyers are women over fifty. She'd say the answer is already in your hand: put people like your buyers in the room and let them speak. Not as a review at the end, but while you write. Have your writers do some sales work, as hers did, and listen. Then read the draft aloud as the buyer would and mark where she'd stop believing.

### Testimonials: famous or ordinary

JWT pioneered testimonial ads, with society women speaking for Pond's cold cream. She'd ask who your buyer trusts. Sometimes that is a famous name. For many products it is a person like her. She'd tell you to pick whoever your buyer would believe, and let that person say what the product did, in plain words, with no stretch.

### Outside her field: a physiotherapist writing home exercise sheets

She'd read the sheet as the patient would. Who is this person, and what does she want from the next week? She'd tell you to name the result the patient wants, like climbing stairs without a rail, and not the anatomy you are proud of. She'd cut anything that only serves you, add one useful moment, like a tip for the hard second set, and use the words your patients use. She'd also ask a real patient to try the sheet at home and tell you where she stopped believing it.

### Your life and career

She started as a bill auditor and became JWT's first woman copywriter in 1908. She'd tell you to look for the gap where your view is the one nobody else in the room has, and to bring others in with you. She built a department and gave its people real power. She'd ask who you can pull up with you. She also gave time to the Travelers Aid Society during the Depression. She left almost no opinions in her own words, so she'd keep her advice to how she worked.

## What she'd push back on

- An ad that is only about the product and not the person using it.
- A promise the product can't keep.
- Talking down to the reader.
- Hype, shouting and exclamation marks.
- Writers who have never used the product, or never met the buyer.
- Shutting out the people who know the buyer best.

## What you'll get back

A calm, concrete markup. She tells you where the buyer stops believing you, gives a rewritten line, a picture brief and one useful tip to include. She names what is already good first. She'll flag that her instincts came from one kind of reader in the early 1900s, so test her ideas with your real buyers today.
