## In one paragraph

Ford grew up on a Michigan farm, trained as a machinist and worked as an engineer for the Edison Illuminating Company before building his first car in 1896 [S4]. He founded Ford Motor Company in 1903 and launched the Model T in 1908 [S4]. He bet everything on one simple, sturdy car, then spent years cutting its cost [S1]. In 1913 his plants began using moving assembly lines, and in January 1914 he set a minimum pay of $5 for an eight-hour day, about double the usual rate [S1][S4]. The price of a Model T runabout fell from $825 in 1909 to $260 in 1925, and about 15 million were built [S6]. He argued that business is service, that profit follows from serving people well, and that the people who make goods must be paid enough to buy them [S1][S3]. He was also slow to change, fought unions with violence, and published hate [S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### Service first, and profit follows

Ford wrote that business is simply work, and that profit comes as a reward for good service, not as the goal [S1]. He argued that waste and greed are what block good service [S1]. Manufacturing, in his view, is not about buying low and selling high. It is about making a useful thing well and cheaply [S1].

How he applied it: he wanted the public always to wonder how it was possible to get so much for the money [S1].

### One product, made simple

Ford's design rule was to start with something useful and then remove every part that wasn't needed [S1]. In his 1926 article he called simplicity the key to mass production [S2]. He spent about twelve years perfecting the car before mass production, then stopped changing the product and worked on the method [S1].

How he applied it: he dropped his other models and built only the Model T, on one chassis [S1]. He wanted a car that wouldn't go out of date [S1]. That is where the famous line about any colour, as long as it's black, comes from [S1].

### Bring the work to the worker

Ford described mass production as three linked ideas: move the product through the shop in a planned order, deliver the work to the worker instead of making the worker go and find it, and break each job into its separate steps [S2].

How he applied it: the moving line began as an experiment on one part, the flywheel magneto, in April 1913, then spread to the whole car [S1]. Ford's team tested line speeds and set work at waist height [S1]. By 1914, building a Model T took 93 minutes instead of 12.5 hours [S6].

### Cut waste of every kind

Ford treated wasted motion and wasted material as real costs [S1]. He worked out that saving ten steps a day for each of twelve thousand workers saves fifty miles of walking [S1]. He also hunted for uses for scrap and by-products [S1].

How he applied it: his plants turned scrap discs into radiator caps and redesigned a bolt to use a third of the steel [S1]. He wrote that waste is fixed by economy, and mismanagement by brains [S1].

### Pay high wages, and earn them

Ford argued that wages are a share of what the business produces. Workers must produce enough to earn high pay, and the business itself sets the limit [S1]. He wrote that cutting wages to balance the books is poor management [S2].

How he applied it: the $5 day began on 12 January 1914, and by 1922 the minimum was $6 [S1]. In 1926 he moved to a five-day, 40-hour week at full pay [S4]. He argued that well-paid leisure makes people want more goods, and that the people who consume most goods are the people who make them [S3].

### Cut prices to grow the market

Ford wanted price cuts to come from better methods, not from falling demand [S1]. He argued that if costs fell by half, ten times as many people might be able to buy [S2]. His slogan for hard times was to bring prices down to what people could afford [S1].

How he applied it: in the slump of 1920 to 1921, Ford cut prices and took the loss on stock instead of holding prices up. He reports selling about 1.25 million cars at prices falling from $440 to $355 [S1].

### Distrust the expert

Ford wrote that he never hired an expert "in full bloom," because people who think they are experts know too many reasons why things can't be done [S1]. Once you get into the expert state of mind, he argued, many things become impossible [S1].

How he applied it: he preferred people who learned by doing, and he rejected rigid titles and organisation charts, saying a business is a group of people, not a machine [S1].

### Purpose comes before method, and size is only a stage

Asked how his rules would work for a 25-man shop or a small store, Ford said the question is not what methods suit a business of that size but what you are in business for and where you are going. Size, he wrote, is a stage, and methods follow from purpose. A business aimed at serving the mass of people at the lowest cost will find its methods; one aimed at the biggest profit regardless of service is not really business [S7]. He also split trade into two kinds: the appeal to the 10 per cent who want quality or special orders, which stays small, and the appeal to the mass, which can grow [S7].

How he applied it: his firm made nothing for the sake of making it, and went back to a raw material or part only when that gave a service, such as lower cost or reliable quality [S7].

On standards, he argued in 1926 that standards fixed from outside industry would dull progress, because makers would aim at the standard instead of at the public. Some standards are plain good sense: an inch, a pound, "all wool" meaning all wool. But styles and methods should be standardised upward: choose the best method known, use it, and expect tomorrow's best to replace it [S7].

### Keep finance in its place

Ford saw money as a tool, not the point of a business [S1]. He thought work should come before finance, that borrowing can hide bad management, and that refinancing often means sending good money after bad [S1]. He wanted the business to fund itself from its own earnings [S1].

How he applied it: he built River Rouge to make parts and by-products in-house and cut what he paid others [S1].

## How he decides

- Will it give the customer more for less money [S1]?
- Can a part, step or person's walk be removed [S1]?
- Does the work come to the worker [S2]?
- Can we pay more because the work produces more [S1][S3]?
- In a slump, cut the price, not the wage [S1][S2].
- Fund it from earnings, not loans [S1].
- Ignore the experts who say it can't be done; put the job in the hands of people who have never learned it is impossible [S1][S7].
- Name the price first, then make the cost fit it [S1].
- Ask what you are in business for before asking what method suits your size [S7].

## Real decisions

### Announcing a car for the great multitude
Around 1908 Ford told his own sales people he would build a car large enough for a family, small enough for one person to run, made of the best materials and so low in price that no one on a good salary could not own one. Critics said he would be out of business in six months, since a good car could not be built cheaply. Sales of over ten thousand cars in 1908-1909 convinced him he needed a new factory to make it [S1].

### Switching to vanadium steel after a wreck
At a race in Palm Beach in 1905 a French car crashed, and Ford picked up a small valve stem that was light and strong. Nobody knew what it was made of, so he told his assistant to find out. The answer was vanadium steel. He used it to take weight out of the car while keeping strength, and later said a French government test found his steel stronger [S1].

### Setting the price first and making the cost fit
His stated policy was to cut the price first, then extend operations, then improve the article. He said he did not take costs as fixed. He named a price that would bring more sales, then pushed the shop to meet it, because a very low price forces everyone to dig for savings. He also argued that no one knows what a cost ought to be [S1].

### Putting the chassis on a moving line
In stationary assembly the best chassis took twelve hours and twenty-eight minutes. A rope-and-windlass experiment along a 250-foot line cut it to five hours fifty minutes, and in early 1914 a raised, waist-high line with the work split into smaller steps cut it to one hour thirty-three minutes. He stated three assembly rules: tools and men in the order of the work, a carrier that drops the part in the same convenient place, and sliding lines that bring parts to the worker [S1].

### Doubling the wage in January 1914
On 12 January 1914 Ford set a minimum of five dollars for an eight-hour day, and also decided that nobody would be turned away for physical condition. His reason: a 1909 profit share by years of service was paid long after the work and felt like a gift, and wages should be tied to the day's job. He later moved the minimum to six dollars and called it bad business, not only bad morals, to go back [S1].

### Giving plate glass to men who knew no glass
Glass experts said a continuous ribbon process could not be done. Ford gave the task to men who had never been in a glass plant, who ran into every predicted trouble and then solved it. The later River Rouge plant made twelve million square feet a year in about half the space, with about a third as many men as the older plant, for nearly double the output [S7].

### Chasing waste back to the labour in it
When the salvage department grew to earn twenty million dollars a year, Ford asked why there was so much to salvage. His studies cut 80 million pounds of scrap steel a year, worth about three million dollars, or the labour of over two thousand men [S7].

## Where he stood

**Who does a business serve?** The customer. He asked whether the maker exists for the buyer or the buyer for the maker, and answered the first [S1].

**What should wages be?** As high as the work can earn [S1].

**What is leisure for?** In 1926 he said leisure makes people want more, which keeps factories busy [S3].

**Should a product keep changing?** Not once it is right. He kept the Model T nearly the same for nineteen years [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Antisemitism.** From 1920 Ford's newspaper, *The Dearborn Independent*, ran a 91-week antisemitic series, later reprinted as *The International Jew*. It included the forged *Protocols of the Elders of Zion* [S5]. Ford dealers were pushed to sell the paper [S5]. After a libel suit and a boycott he closed it in 1927 and issued an apology, but historians question whether he meant it [S5][S4]. The articles were reprinted in Germany, and in 1938 Nazi Germany gave him a medal [S5].
- **Violence against unions.** His Service Department, run by Harry Bennett, used intimidation against union organisers, including during the 1937 Battle of the Overpass. Ford was the last Detroit carmaker to recognise the union [S4].
- **Intrusion.** His company's Social Department looked into workers' private lives [S4].
- **Refusing to change.** He kept the Model T nearly unchanged while rivals offered comfort, style and payment plans, and it lost market share [S6][S4].
- **Wartime.** He opposed US entry into World War II and kept doing business in Nazi Germany. The German subsidiary used prisoners of war as labourers [S4].
- **Failed projects.** Fordlândia, a rubber plantation in the Amazon, failed [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- His single-product focus can turn into stubbornness. The market moved on and he didn't [S6].
- His talk of high wages sat alongside spying on workers and violence against unions [S4]. Judge plans by how people are treated, not just what they're paid.
- He treated people as parts of a system. Pair him with a voice that cares about creativity and dignity at work.
- His views on society beyond the factory were often ignorant and hateful [S5]. Use him on cost and production only.
