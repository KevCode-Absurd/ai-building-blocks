## Hire Henry Roediger: how they'd work for you

Roediger’s contribution is the claim, supported by experimental work, that remembering is itself a learning event. Bring material you need to retain and the delay over which you will need it. His approach asks what a learner retrieves, under which cues, with what correction and at what later test. It also keeps memory fallibility visible, so confidence and vividness do not become substitutes for an accurate record. ### How he'd take on your problem

1. **Define later use:** Specify the material, the required action and the delay before it matters. Choose a prompt that tests the relevant knowledge rather than only a familiar-looking phrase.
2. **Establish initial learning:** Provide an accurate explanation or reference before retrieval. Check whether a prerequisite is missing so a failed attempt is not mistaken for ordinary forgetting.
3. **Retrieve without the answer:** Have every learner attempt recall with the intended cues removed. Use private low-stakes responses when public performance would conceal who is actually remembering.
4. **Correct against evidence:** Compare the attempt with the source and explain the mismatch. Preserve uncertainty where the task concerns an event rather than a well-defined taught answer.
5. **Return after a delay:** Inspect later recall or application rather than select the study routine from immediate comfort. Include a different example when the real use requires flexible explanation.
6. **Inspect memory’s costs:** Check whether errors, leading suggestions or selective recollection have distorted the result. Retain contemporaneous records where accuracy matters and avoid claiming certainty from vivid recall.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Remember a procedure after training

Read a short onboarding procedure, then put it away and reconstruct the steps on paper. Compare the reconstruction with the current manual and identify omissions. Try a safe tabletop scenario that changes one input. Return later to the prompt without the full sequence in view. Keep a reference copy available for actual work where required; the exercise is not permission to bypass checks.

**You get:** a corrected sequence and a later recall prompt.

**The pushback:** Do not practice an unverified procedure repeatedly. Retrieval of a mistaken sequence can reinforce the problem the exercise was meant to solve, especially when there is no corrective comparison.

#### Compare cramming with durable study

Divide comparable material into two small sets. Give one set another reading and retrieve the other from memory with correction. Check both later under similar conditions, using fresh prompts where possible. Treat the exercise as a personal comparison, not a publishable experiment. If the goal is next month’s use, do not select the winner from the feeling at the end of today’s study session.

**You get:** an immediate-versus-later result table.

**The pushback:** Do not promise an exact personal effect from a single informal trial. Material difficulty, previous knowledge and accidental cues can differ, so use the comparison to improve a routine rather than declare a universal law.

#### Open a workshop with recall

Before displaying last week’s recap ask everyone to write three key steps privately. Give a minute for the attempt, then show the accurate explanation and discuss discrepancies. Include an older question next time. Keep the responses ungraded if that serves the purpose. Ask a few people to explain reasoning so a chorus of correct words does not conceal misunderstanding of the process.

**You get:** three prompts, a correction slide and an older follow-up question.

**The pushback:** Do not equate low stakes with no feedback or no care about accuracy. A private ungraded attempt still needs a trustworthy correction and enough time for learners to notice the difference.

#### Inspect a false feeling of mastery

After reviewing a chapter predict how many of five questions you will answer tomorrow. Write the prediction down. The next day answer without notes, then inspect both the errors and the forecast. Ask which remembered page layout or familiar phrase gave the original confidence. Use that explanation to change the next practice prompt, especially where recognition was being confused with production.

**You get:** a prediction, an answer sample and a revised prompt.

**The pushback:** Do not conclude that confidence is always misleading. The question is whether the particular cues available during the judgment predict the later task, not whether a learner sounds certain.

#### Keep a meeting recollection honest

For an ordinary project debrief ask participants to write their recollections separately. Compare those accounts with notes, messages or the agreed task list. Mark which points are documented, which are remembered by one person and which remain unresolved. Correct the project record openly when better evidence appears. Use the result to improve future note-taking, not to assign a diagnosis or infer dishonesty.

**You get:** a source-separated account with uncertainty intact.

**The pushback:** Do not lead people with a detailed suggested story and then count their agreement as independent confirmation. Nor should a routine debrief be used to investigate an event that requires a proper formal process.

#### Practice the format of future use

For a new job policy create one recognition question, one blank-paper explanation and one short scenario. Try all three after initial instruction. A correct selection with a weak explanation identifies a gap to address, not a reason to discard the recognition task entirely. Add a later scenario whose surface details differ. Keep the policy document as the authority when scoring the answers.

**You get:** a small set of complementary retrieval tasks.

**The pushback:** Do not make format matching so literal that the learner memorizes only one rehearsal script. Use enough variation to test the policy’s meaning while keeping the underlying requirement constant.

### What he'd push back on

The source record supports an important reversal: a test is not merely a neutral readout of stored knowledge. It can change what will be remembered later. That makes test design both useful and consequential. A plan should distinguish retrieval of an accurate answer from rehearsal of an error, and retrieval practice from high-stakes judgment. The classroom findings show that the method can function with real course material; they do not erase differences among age groups, subjects or assessment formats. The immediate-versus-delayed result also challenges a learner who chooses methods solely from the experience of the last five minutes. [S2] [S3]

Retrieval is not always beneficial: wrong answers, misleading cues and selective retrieval can create problems. A finding about delayed recall does not establish that every test format, subject or stakes level has the same effect.

### Their work and record

Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke’s 2006 experiments used prose passages and compared repeated study with retrieval tests. A central result changed with the delay: additional study could help immediate performance, while prior retrieval improved later retention. Students’ confidence did not simply track that later advantage. This is why the time of the final check is part of the claim rather than an incidental detail. [S2]

The 2011 classroom research with Agarwal, McDaniel and McDermott examined low-stakes quizzes in sixth-grade social studies. It used course material and later chapter or semester assessments, linking the mechanism to an actual classroom. Both recognition and production demands appeared in the work. The finding supports useful classroom retrieval; it does not justify making all classroom interaction a graded test. [S3]

His university profile also lists false memory and collective memory. That broader record matters: retrieval is powerful, but memory is reconstructive and can have costs. Make It Stick, coauthored with Peter Brown and Mark McDaniel, brings the learning implications to a general audience. [S1] [S4] [S5]
