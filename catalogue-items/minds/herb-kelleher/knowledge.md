## In one paragraph

Kelleher was a New Jersey lawyer who moved to Texas and, with his client Rollin King, set out to start a cheap airline linking Dallas, Houston and San Antonio [S1]. Rival airlines sued to stop it for four years. Southwest finally flew on June 18, 1971 [S1]. He became CEO full time in 1981 and led for about 20 years [S1]. He built the airline on low fares, one type of plane, point-to-point routes and quick turnarounds, and on the belief that if you treat employees well, they treat customers well, and the customers keep shareholders happy [S1][S2][S4]. He called culture his biggest edge, because rivals can buy planes but not spirit [S2][S3]. Southwest was profitable every year from 1973 to 2019 [S5].

## Core ideas

### Employees first

Kelleher put employees ahead of customers and shareholders, and explained why. Treat your people well and they treat customers well, which brings customers back and keeps owners satisfied [S2]. He said the business of business is people [S2]. He wanted to value staff as whole people, outside working hours too [S2].

How he applied it: in 2017 he described two leadership rules: put the wellbeing of others ahead of your own, and praise and recognise your people often [S3]. He kept his own pay near the 37th percentile for airline bosses so staff could see he worked for them [S3].

### Culture is the edge no one can buy

He argued that competitors could buy aircraft and lease gates, but they could not buy an outgoing, devoted culture [S3]. In 2004 he said spirit was the most powerful thing of all because it can't be bought [S2]. He insisted culture must be sincere, coming from the heart rather than from a plan [S2]. As the airline grew from hundreds to many thousands of staff, he made protecting the culture "job one" [S3].

How he applied it: he told staff to be themselves, not robots, because they were hired for who they were [S3]. He dressed as Elvis or the Easter Bunny for employees and rode a Harley to work [S4].

### Hire for attitude, train for skill

Kelleher put character ahead of credentials. Southwest asked first what kind of person a candidate was, then taught them the job [S3]. He kept this rule even as the company became very large [S3].

How he applied it: he told stories of staff going well beyond the job, such as a station manager who changed a stranger's tyre after she flew another airline [S3]. He said this spirit was hard to teach, which is why hiring for it mattered [S3].

### More for less, not less for less

He rejected the idea that an airline must choose between low cost and good service [S3]. Southwest aimed to give more for less [S3]. The costs came down through a simple model: high use of each plane, simple rules, and secondary airports [S1][S3]. He liked to point out that a plane only earns money in the air [S3].

How he applied it: Southwest flew only Boeing 737s for most of its history [S5], flew point to point instead of through hubs [S1], and scheduled gate turnarounds of about 15 minutes [S4]. Union contracts let staff cross over: pilots and flight attendants sometimes cleaned planes, and ramp workers sold tickets [S4]. He personally approved spending over $1,000 [S4].

### Grow the market with low fares

Kelleher was sceptical of chasing market share for its own sake. He said he'd rather have a small share and make money than a big share and lose it [S3]. Low fares, he argued, didn't just win customers from rivals. They created new travellers, and could lift traffic on a route by 100% to 300% within a year [S3].

How he applied it: when planes filled up, Southwest added flights rather than raising fares [S2].

### Plan in good times for bad times

He credited Southwest's record of no furloughs to planning ahead. Keep a strong balance sheet and plenty of cash, and manage in good years so you can get through bad ones [S3]. Hiring too freely in a boom, he warned, leads to layoffs in a bust [S3]. The no-layoff promise itself kept the company lean [S3].

How he applied it: he credited Southwest's record of avoiding furloughs to this kind of planning, and told how staff helped stranded crews and passengers after the September 11 attacks [S3].

### Keep it simple, and act

Kelleher distrusted bureaucracy and long plans. He described Southwest's approach as "ready, fire, aim" [S2]. A few clear values, he said, speed up decisions and help people feel like part of things [S2]. His advice to new CEOs was to focus on what matters, avoid red tape and stay focused on results and mission [S2].

How he applied it: he described replacing thick operations manuals with short guidelines for leaders, which said the guidelines could be broken when needed [S3]. When a service problem came up, he preferred to talk with the employee involved rather than write a new rule for everyone [S3].

### Stay humble, and beware complacency

He warned that leaders who think they've reached the top are about to fall [S3]. In 2004 he said humility breeds open-mindedness [S2]. He thought Southwest's main potential enemy was itself, not its competitors [S2].

How he applied it: he pointed out that rivals who copied the low-fare idea often failed because their costs stayed high [S2]. The threat he watched for was Southwest getting comfortable [S2].

### Use humour, even in a fight

Kelleher saw humour as part of the brand and a way to turn conflict into goodwill [S1]. His culture asked people to take themselves lightly and their jobs seriously [S1].

How he applied it: in 1992 a trademark dispute over the slogan "Just Plane Smart" was settled by an arm-wrestling match with the other company's boss, called "Malice in Dallas." He lost, but Southwest kept using the slogan and money went to charity [S1].

## Real decisions

### Fighting four years of lawsuits to fly (1967 to 1971)

After he and Rollin King incorporated the airline in 1967, rival carriers sued to stop it [S1]. Key court wins came in December 1970 and June 1971, and the first flights took off on 18 June 1971 [S1]. He chose to keep going through the delay rather than give up the plan of cheap flights linking three Texas cities [S1].

### One plane type, point to point, fast turns

Southwest flew only Boeing 737s for most of its history, flew point to point instead of through hubs, and aimed for gate turnarounds of about 15 minutes [S1][S4][S5]. He said a plane earns money only in the air [S3]. Union contracts let staff cross over between jobs, and he personally approved spending over $1,000 [S4].

When another company claimed the slogan "Just Plane Smart," Kelleher settled it by arm-wrestling the other firm's boss in a match billed as "Malice in Dallas" [S1]. He lost, Southwest kept the slogan and money went to charity [S1].

### Adding flights, not raising fares

When planes filled, Southwest added flights rather than raising fares [S2]. His reason was that low fares create new travellers, lifting traffic on a route by 100% to 300% in a year [S3]. He preferred a small profitable share to a large losing one [S3].

He described swapping thick operations manuals for short guidelines for leaders, which said they could be broken when needed [S3]. When a service problem came up he preferred to talk to the employee involved rather than write a new rule for everyone [S3].

He credited the airline's record of no furloughs to keeping a strong balance sheet and not over-hiring in a boom [S3]. After the September 11 attacks, staff helped stranded crews and passengers [S3]. He stepped down as CEO in March 2001 and as chairman in 2008 [S1].

## Where he stood

**Who comes first, customers or employees?** Employees. Happy staff make happy customers, who make happy shareholders [S2].

**Should you chase market share?** No. A small, profitable share beats a large, losing one [S3].

**Is low cost the enemy of good service?** No. He aimed for more for less [S3].

**What is the biggest threat?** Complacency inside the company, more than any rival [S2].

**How much planning is enough?** Less than most think. He preferred a few values and fast action [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Fighting rivals in court and politics.** Southwest spent years in legal and political battles with other carriers [S1][S3]. In the early 1990s it lobbied and sued against a planned Texas high-speed rail line. The project was cancelled in 1994, and many observers cite Southwest's role [S5].
- **A founder-centred culture.** The culture leaned heavily on his personality [S1]. By 2017 he joked he was tired of carrying the brand by himself [S3], which hints at how hard it is to pass on.
- **Limits of the model.** The rules that cut costs, such as one plane type and secondary airports, also limited where and how the airline could fly. The 1979 Wright Amendment confined Love Field flights to nearby states, which happened to fit Southwest's short trips [S4].

These are fair points from the record. His interviews give his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- "Employees first" is easy to say. It only works if pay, policy and hiring back it up every day [S3].
- A culture built on one charismatic leader may not outlast him. Plan how it passes on [S3].
- His model suited a high-volume service business. Not every business can win on lowest cost.
- His dislike of rules can go too far in areas where safety or law needs firm procedure.
- His record of legal and political fights shows a tough side. Pair him with a voice on fair play.
