# Herb Lubalin: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Lubalin was a New York art director and type designer who made letters do the work of pictures [S3][S4]. He studied at Cooper Union, spent nearly two decades at the ad agency Sudler & Hennessey, and opened his own studio in 1964 [S1][S2]. He designed the magazines *Eros*, *Fact* and *Avant Garde* for the publisher Ralph Ginzburg, and drew the Avant Garde typeface with Tom Carnase [S1]. In 1970 he co-founded the International Typeface Corporation, known as ITC, with Aaron Burns, and from 1973 he designed and edited its journal *U&lc* [S2][S6]. He didn't like the word "typography" for what he did. He called it designing with letters, and accepted Burns's word "typographics" [S3]. His work showed how the new photo-typesetting of the 1960s let designers squeeze, enlarge and overlap letters to express an idea [S6].

## Core ideas

### Letters can carry the idea

Lubalin's core belief was that type could hold both the message and the picture in a single image [S4]. A word could be shaped so its form showed what it meant. He saw this as a shift in what typography was for: designers were no longer just setting words to sit beside a photo, but using type to give meaning on its own [S4].

How he applied it: his nameplate for *Mother & Child*, a magazine that never printed, is one of his best-known pieces [S2].

### Designing with letters, not setting type

He saw ordinary typography as a mechanical way of putting characters on a page [S3]. What he did was closer to drawing. He worked letter by letter, treating each word as a shape to be designed [S3]. He leaned toward decorative and popular letterforms rather than the plain Swiss-style type that was fashionable then [S3].

How he applied it: he started with fast pen sketches on layers of tracing paper, then wrote detailed notes on typefaces, sizes and weights [S7]. He adjusted letter spacing and heights by hand, and redid work when a client changed something [S7].

### Excitement can earn back lost legibility

Lubalin admitted that playing with type could cost some ease of reading. He knew that researchers thought this was bad [S4]. He also admitted that designers like him distorted and disfigured letters [S4]. His defence was that the excitement of a fresh image could more than make up for the loss, and that the emotional result often justified the distortion [S4].

How he applied it: in his magazine work he pushed letters close together and used bold scale and layouts. Steven Heller later said *U&lc* was where Lubalin tested how far squeezed, expressive lettering could go [S1].

### Simple, direct, with drama from type and space

His designs were often simple and direct. He used type and white space to make content clear, and sometimes dramatic [S2].

How he applied it: a famous cover of *Fact* in 1964 had no pictures at all. It used one line from a story about Barry Goldwater, set in bold type [S2]. Goldwater sued and won, and the case put the magazine out of business [S1][S2]. *Fact* was designed on a tight budget, in a plain, restrained way [S1].

### One idea beats two

When Lubalin judged his own old work, a common fault he found was too many ideas. In 1978 he redesigned a set of his posters from 1966 and wrote down what was wrong with each [S5]. In one, two solutions fought each other, so he cut one [S5]. In another, a quill pen prop was not needed, so he removed it [S5]. In a third, he said turning a letter on its side overplayed an idea that already worked [S5].

How he applied it: on the poster about excess, he removed the drinking straw and let the letter S run off the page, so a single idea did the job [S5].

### The typeface must fit the idea

He chose type for how its shape served the concept, down to small details. In his 1978 critiques he swapped a rounded typeface for one with flat tops and bottoms so stacked lines could butt together. He admitted this was splitting hairs, and argued that this is what good design is about [S5]. He swapped a fancy script that competed with a blunt message for a harsher face that set it off [S5].

How he applied it: he picked a condensed typewriter-style face for a poster called Girls because he felt it suited the span of time [S5].

### Better communication needs more expressive type

Lubalin believed that the better people get at communicating, the more they need expressive typography [S7]. Type was not just a container for words. It was one of the main tools for making a message felt [S7].

How he applied it: through *U&lc* and ITC he showed new typefaces in bold, playful layouts to designers around the world [S1][S6]. Heller has called the journal a forerunner of *Emigre*, the experimental type magazine of the 1980s and 1990s [S1].

### Designers should own their type

ITC was set up to license original typefaces and pay royalties to the people who drew them [S6]. That made type design a business that rewarded its makers.

How he applied it: he co-founded ITC with Aaron Burns in 1970, and as *U&lc*'s designer and publisher he had the final say on its content [S2]. He enjoyed that freedom and said he was his own client [S2].

## Real decisions

### Cutting the quill from the Poet poster (1978 redo)
In his 1966 poster for the word "poet" he had drawn a goose quill. Twelve years later he called it an entirely superfluous prop and took it out, setting the word in ITC Benguiat. He said he was guilty of a sin based on experience: an old habit of adding a picture to a word that already says it [S5].

### Switching to a face that lets lines butt together
For the "Animals" poster he had used a rounded face. He judged it weak for the idea, because he wanted one line to butt against the next to echo the black and white bands. He chose ITC Machine Bold for its flat tops and bottoms [S5].

### Taking the rocking chair out of the old age poster
His main complaint about the old age and death poster was "too much is too little." The chair said old age while the reclining letter said death, and the two ideas diluted each other. He removed the chair and set the leaning "i" in open space in a condensed face [S5].

### Admitting he overplayed the "mouth" poster
For the poster about a bore, he had turned an "O" to spell "mouth." In 1978 he said this overemphasised an already emphatic solution. He also noted that mixed upper and lower case hid the small type that carried the message [S5].

### Running the 1964 Fact cover on one line
For a story questioning Barry Goldwater's fitness for office, he pulled one line from the article, set it in bold type and used no picture. Goldwater sued and won $90,000, which put the magazine out of business [S2].

### Fitting the type to the words, and the words to the type
In a trade ad he set a single paragraph on the baseline beside the word "let," with size and leading chosen to leave room for the descender of a "y." He tracked every line and sometimes changed words so the shape worked. An assistant said you had to do it over and over to make things line up [S7].

### Becoming his own client
When he ran *U&lc*, he had final say on everything in it. He described this as what every designer wants and few get [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask whether the letters alone can show the idea [S4].
- Start with many quick sketches on tracing paper [S7].
- Fit the typeface to the concept, down to the shape of a letter's top [S5].
- Remove props and second ideas that weaken the first [S5].
- Accept some loss of legibility only if the emotional gain is bigger [S4].
- Use white space and bold type for clarity and drama [S2].
- Adjust spacing by hand until the word reads as one shape [S7].
- Judge your own past work as harshly as anyone's [S5].

## Where he stood

**Is typography just setting type?** No. For him it was designing with letters, closer to drawing than to typesetting [S3].

**Must type always be easy to read?** Not always. He accepted that his approach lost some legibility and argued that the excitement could make up for it [S4].

**Should type serve a picture?** It can do more. He wanted type to hold both the message and the picture in one image [S4].

**Is a small detail worth fussing over?** Yes. He said that splitting hairs is what good design is about [S5].

**Who should benefit from new typefaces?** Their designers. ITC paid royalties to the people who drew the faces [S6].

## His work and record

- Born in New York on March 17, 1918. He was colour-blind [S1][S2].
- 1939: graduated from Cooper Union [S1][S2].
- 1945: joined Sudler & Hennessey, where he stayed 19 years. With John J. Graham he created the first NBC peacock in 1957 [S1].
- 1961: designed a trademark for *The Saturday Evening Post* [S1].
- 1962 to 1963: designed *Eros*, which led to an obscenity case that reached the U.S. Supreme Court [S1].
- 1964: opened his own firm, later Lubalin, Smith & Carnase [S1][S2]. Designed *Fact* the same year [S2].
- 1968 to 1971: designed *Avant Garde* magazine. The Avant Garde typeface, drawn with Tom Carnase, came out in 1970 [S1].
- 1970: co-founded ITC with Aaron Burns [S2][S6].
- 1973: began *U&lc* and created the World Trade Center trademark [S1][S2].
- 1974: released Lubalin Graph, and Serif Gothic with Tony DiSpigna [S6].
- Taught at Cornell from 1972 and at Cooper Union from 1976 to 1981 [S2].
- Won more than 500 prizes [S2]. Received the AIGA Medal in 1980 or 1981 (sources differ) [S2][S3].
- Died on May 24, 1981 [S1]. Cooper Union opened the Herb Lubalin Study Center in 1985 [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Legibility.** He admitted that his kind of type play lost some readability, that researchers deplored it and that he distorted letters [S4].
- **Misused ligatures.** The Avant Garde typeface came with many joined letter pairs. Steven Heller said other designers misused them, while Lubalin used them with character [S1].
- **Legal fights.** His magazine work for Ginzburg ran into trouble. *Eros* faced an obscenity case, and *Fact* lost a libel suit to Barry Goldwater that closed it [S1][S2].
- **His own early work.** In 1978 he publicly listed faults in his 1966 posters, from props he didn't need to type that fought the message [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- His approach can sacrifice reading for impact. For long text, forms, signs or anything people must read fast, pair him with a plain, systems-minded designer.
- He worked by hand and by eye. He said less about testing designs with users or about rules that a team could follow.
- His tight, overlapping letters were made for print at large sizes. They may break on small screens.
- His freedom as his own client was rare. Most designers must also win over a client, and he offers less on how to do that.
