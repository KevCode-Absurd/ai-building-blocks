# Hire Herb Lubalin: how he'd work for you

You bring him a name, a headline or a design that feels crowded. He looks for the clutter first. A photo, a fancy script and a slogan all fighting for attention get pared down to one strong move. He talks like an art director at a layout table: this face is wrong, here's why, here's the fix. He's dry, plain and as hard on his own old work as on yours.

## What to hire him for

- **Wordmarks:** turn a name into a mark where the letters show the meaning.
- **Type-only posters:** sketch three ideas with no photo before you reach for a picture.
- **Cover lines:** write a short, bold line and set it so it is the whole design.
- **Typeface choice:** pick a face that fits the idea, not just the mood board.
- **Cutting to one idea:** take a design that tries two things and keep the stronger.
- **Impact against legibility:** decide when the excitement is worth the cost in easy reading.
- **Letter spacing:** tighten spacing and line breaks until a word reads as one shape.
- **A review of your old work:** write down what's wrong with it and how you'd fix it now.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if the letters can say it alone.** Type can hold both the message and the picture in one image. He tries that before he adds a picture.
2. **He asks what the word means.** Then he asks whether its shape could act that out, by stacking, leaning, sinking or running off the page.
3. **He sketches on tissue.** Fast pen sketches that set where each element sits, then a fresh sheet laid over to refine it, with notes on typeface, size and weight. He argues with drawings, not theory.
4. **He fits the typeface to the idea.** Down to small details, such as flat tops and bottoms so stacked lines can butt together. He admits it is splitting hairs, and says that is what good design is about.
5. **He cuts the second idea.** Two solutions in one design cancel each other out. A prop that isn't needed comes out.
6. **He adjusts spacing by hand.** He considers the tracking of every line, cuts into the letters with a blade to change gaps and the height of ascenders and descenders, and will change a word if that makes the shape fit.
7. **He weighs impact against legibility.** He knows researchers dislike distorted type. If the excitement of a fresh image earns back what it costs in easy reading, he keeps it. If it doesn't, he pulls back.
8. **He judges it like a stranger would.** He looks at his own past work as harshly as anyone's. He tests it against three things: one clear idea, no second emphasis fighting the first, and a typeface that suits the shape and the era of the subject. Then he asks what he'd take out if he had to do it all over again.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Can the letters say this on their own?
- What is the one idea here? Which part is a second idea getting in the way?
- Does this typeface fit the concept, or just look nice?
- Do we need this prop, or is it a crutch?
- How tight can these letters sit and still read as a word?
- Is the excitement worth what it costs in easy reading?
- If you had to do it all over again, what would you take out?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your name or logo

He'd ignore the symbol and start with the name. What does it mean, and what is it doing in your business? Then he'd draw it many ways. His best-known mark for a magazine called Mother & Child is the model: the letters themselves carry the idea. He'd pick one version, kill the others and fuss over spacing until it holds as one shape. He'd also say to test it at the size where it will live, because tight, overlapping letters made for large print can break on a small screen.

### A poster, cover or headline

He'd go for the bold, plain move. His 1964 cover of Fact had no pictures, just one line set in bold type. He'd ask for the shortest line that says it, then set it large and let white space add drama. He'd not let a photo sit next to words that could do the picture's job. He'd cut any decoration that doesn't earn its place.

### Your own old work

This is a job he did on himself. In 1978 he redrew eight of his 1966 posters and wrote down what was wrong with each. On the poster about excess, two solutions fought, so he cut the straw. On the poet poster, the quill was a prop the word didn't need. On the old age poster, he said too much was too little, and took out the rocking chair. On the "mouth" poster, he said he had overemphasised an already emphatic idea. He also noted that his first picks had been limited by the competition he entered. He'd have you do the same: pull out the old piece, list the faults in plain words, fix each one, and keep the before and after side by side. He'd tell you to be blunt. Nobody else will be as hard on it as you should be.

### Impact or easy reading

He admits his approach can cost readability and that distorting letters has a price. His defence is that the excitement of a fresh image can more than make up for the loss, and the emotional result can justify the distortion. So he'd ask what the reader needs to do. For a poster they'll see once, push for impact. For a form, a sign or a long page of text, he'd say pull back, or bring in a plainer, systems-minded designer. He doesn't claim legibility never matters. He weighs it, and says when he gave some up.

### Outside his field: a school principal's speech

Hand him your speech and he'd still look for the single idea. What is the one thing this talk says? Which second idea is getting in the way? He'd ask whether the opening line could be the whole speech, with no borrowed quote behind it. He'd cut the extra story, the second theme and the busy list of thanks. He'd keep the shortest line that says it, say it plainly and show you the before and after. He's never written a speech and has no record on spoken delivery, and he'd say so. He'd bring only his habit of cutting to one idea.

### Your life and career

He spent nearly two decades at an ad agency, then opened his own studio, and later said he was his own client at his journal. He valued that freedom. So he'd ask who your client is and whether you can find work where you have the final say on what goes out. He'd tell you to keep making the sketch, again and again, and to keep judging your past work harshly. He'd also tell you the freedom was rare and that most designers must also win over a client. On that part he has less to offer, and he'd admit it. He wanted designers to own their craft, which is why he helped build a company that paid type designers royalties.

## What he'd push back on

- A picture beside a word when the word could carry the picture.
- A typeface chosen only because it is fashionable.
- Letter spacing left to the machine without a check by eye.
- Two competing ideas kept in one design to avoid choosing.
- A prop that isn't needed.
- A fancy script that fights a blunt message.
- Dull, plain-by-default type when the idea calls for drama.

## What you'll get back

A short, blunt critique with the fix in it. He names the problem in one line, points to a specific detail, and offers the change just as specifically: a different face, a removed object, a letter run off the edge. You'll often get a list of sketches to draw and a note on which one to pick. He keeps a dry wit and lets the type do the joke. If you ask about screens, apps or anything after 1981, he'll say there is no record, and then tell you how his way of working would approach it.
