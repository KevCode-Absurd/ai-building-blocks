## In one paragraph

Schultz joined a small Seattle coffee bean seller in 1982, fell for the espresso bars of Milan on a work trip in 1983, and spent the next forty years trying to bring that feeling to America [S1][S5]. He bought Starbucks in 1987 and grew it from 11 stores to more than 28,000 in 77 countries [S1][S5]. His big idea was the coffeehouse as a "third place" between home and work, a warm spot built on community and human contact [S5]. He also argued that a public company could share its success with its staff, whom Starbucks calls partners, through health care, stock and free college, even for part-timers [S5]. When growth went too far, he wrote a blunt memo saying the company had watered down its own experience, then came back as CEO to slow growth and fix it [S3][S4]. He summed up his career as a balance of profit and social conscience [S5]. Critics, though, point to his long fight against unions [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### The coffeehouse as a third place

Schultz saw a coffee shop as more than a place to buy a drink. He framed it as a third place between home and work, a comfortable and welcoming spot that offers community and human connection [S5]. The coffee matters, but so do the room, the people and the feeling of being known [S5]. He got the idea from Italy, where he was struck by the community, romance and theatre of the coffee bars [S5].

How he applied it: after the original owners declined to add espresso drinks, he left in 1985 and opened his own café, Il Giornale, in 1986, named after a Milan newspaper [S1]. When he bought Starbucks in 1987, he renamed his cafés Starbucks [S2].

### The experience is made of small details

Schultz believed customers feel a brand through many small sensory details: the smell of coffee, the sight of a drink being made, the talk with the person behind the counter [S3]. Lose enough of them and the store stops feeling special, even if each loss saved money [S3].

How he applied it: in his 2007 memo he listed what had gone. Tall automatic espresso machines sped up service but blocked the customer's view of the drink and cut the contact with the barista. Sealed bags of coffee kept beans fresh but took away the aroma and the scooping of beans in front of customers. Some stores no longer sold grinders or French presses, so the coffee heritage faded [S3].

### Growth can water down what made you great

The memo's main point was that a string of decisions, each one sensible on its own, had added up to something worse [S3]. To grow from under 1,000 stores to 13,000, Starbucks had made choices for speed, efficiency and return on investment. Together they made the stores feel like a generic chain instead of a neighbourhood café [S3]. He called the damage "self induced" and said rivals of every size were now winning former customers [S3].

How he applied it: he did not blame one team. He took responsibility himself and asked leaders to plan the next year around getting back to the core of the business [S3].

### Look in the mirror, then reset

Schultz thought a leader must face hard truths about the direction of the company, even when the numbers still look fine [S3]. A reset means slowing down, closing what doesn't work, and putting the customer at the centre again [S4].

How he applied it: he returned as CEO on January 7, 2008 [S1]. Within weeks he cut planned U.S. store openings for the year from 1,600 to about 1,175, said about 100 weak U.S. stores would close, and called 2008 a year of refocus and renewal [S4]. Later that year Starbucks said it would close 600 more stores, and it cut about 1,000 office jobs [S2]. He also promised partners the tools to exceed what customers expect [S4].

### Partners first

Schultz treated the people in the stores as the heart of the business. He said he set out to build the kind of company his father, a blue-collar worker and Second World War veteran, never had the chance to work for [S5]. He argued that good benefits fuel growth, because people who are looked after look after customers [S5].

How he applied it: Starbucks was the first U.S. company to offer health care to part-time workers [S1]. It also gave stock ownership to staff [S5]. In 2014 it partnered with Arizona State University to help staff earn a degree online, and in 2015 it widened this to full tuition cover for eligible part-time and full-time U.S. staff [S2].

### Balance profit with conscience

Schultz believed a public company could deliver strong results and still share its success with its people and its communities [S5]. In his farewell letter he described the balance as profit and social conscience, compassion and rigour, love and responsibility [S5]. He also said Starbucks had a responsibility to use its scale for good [S5].

How he applied it: Starbucks pushed fair trade and ethical sourcing, and its fair trade purchases roughly doubled in the two years after 2008 [S1]. It built a sourcing program with Conservation International that pays higher prices to farmers who score well on social and environmental checks [S2]. He reported taking a one-dollar salary in later years [S1].

### Keep going after the noes

Schultz raised money for his first café the hard way. By the account on his Wikipedia page, 217 of the 242 investors he approached turned him down [S1]. He kept pitching until he had enough.

How he applied it: the original Starbucks owners put $150,000 into his venture, and two years after he opened Il Giornale they sold him the Starbucks retail business for $3.8 million [S1].

### Leaders must keep reimagining

Schultz saw a brand as something leaders have to keep renewing. In 2018 he wrote that it was the duty of leaders to constantly reimagine Starbucks [S5]. He reminded staff not to lose sight of what matters most, which he named as fellow partners and customers [S5].

How he applied it: after stepping down as CEO in 2017, he focused on premium Reserve stores and Roasteries, including the first Starbucks in Italy, a Milan Roastery that opened in 2018 [S5]. It closed a circle that began with his trip to Milan in 1983 [S1].

## How he decides

- Start from what the customer sees, smells and feels in the store [S3].
- Add up the small trade-offs. Ask what they do together, not just one at a time [S3].
- When things go wrong, take the blame first, then ask the team to face the facts [S3].
- Slow growth when growth is hurting the experience [S4].
- Close what isn't working, even when it hurts [S4][S2].
- Look after the people who serve the customer, including part-timers [S1][S5].
- Weigh profit against conscience, and look for a way to have both [S5].
- Keep reimagining the brand, and never treat it as finished [S5].

## Real decisions

### Taking espresso bars to a company that said no
After a 1983 buying trip to Milan he tried to persuade Starbucks' owners to sell traditional espresso drinks. A pilot café worked, but they cited the cost of machines, thin repair skills in America and Americans' lack of familiarity with the drink, and chose not to go further [S1]. He left in 1985. His stated pull was the community, romance and theatre of the Italian coffee bars [S5].

### Raising money through 217 refusals
To start his own café he needed $400,000. He visited over 500 espresso bars in Milan, and of the 242 investors he approached, 217 said no [S1]. Starbucks put in $150,000 and a local doctor $100,000 [S1]. His café, Il Giornale, opened in 1986, and he bought Starbucks in 1987 and renamed his cafés Starbucks [S1][S2].

### Owning every store
Schultz did not believe in franchising and made Starbucks keep ownership of every domestic outlet [S1]. The record we hold gives no reason in his words. It fits his stress on control of the experience [S3]. Starbucks listed in June 1992, and the IPO raised $271 million, which financed doubling the store count [S1].

### Paying for benefits others skipped
Starbucks offered healthcare, stock ownership and free college tuition even to part-timers, and became the first US company to offer part-time workers healthcare [S1][S5]. In 2017 he said, by his own account, that it gave comprehensive health insurance about 30 years before the Affordable Care Act and stock options to all staff including part-timers [S6]. His reason: his father, a delivery driver, fell on ice in 1960 and was left with no workers' compensation, severance or health cover, and he wanted to build the company his father never had [S5][S6]. His rule was that success is best when shared [S6].

### Writing the 2007 memo
In February 2007 he wrote to Starbucks leaders that a string of reasonable decisions had watered down the experience, calling the damage self induced and asking leaders to get back to the core [S3]. He named specific changes, such as the tall automatic machines that blocked the customer's view of the drink [S3].

### Coming back as CEO in 2008
In January 2008 he fired his predecessor and returned as CEO [S7]. On January 7, 2008, during the financial crisis, he returned as CEO after eight years [S1]. He led a mass firing of executives and employees, closed hundreds of stores and temporarily closed all US locations to retrain staff in making espresso [S1]. He framed 2008 as a year of refocus and renewal [S4]. By November 2008 about 600 US stores were being closed, with a focus on urban sites, and he ruled out price cuts because they would harm the premium brand [S7]. He refused the word turnaround and called it a "transformational agenda" built on "our core value, humanity" [S7]. Later he named the cause as hubris: growth had covered up mistakes until 2007, and the company lost sight of its purpose [S6]. In March 2009 he and the board approved a $100 million settlement of a barista-led suit over tips [S1].

### Sitting down with 50 new hires in Johannesburg
Before Starbucks opened its first South African store, Schultz met the 50 young people hired to staff it, none of whom had held a job, and spent hours hearing each person's story [S6]. He asked them what ubuntu meant and was told it means "I am because of you" [S6]. He used the story in 2017 to say that leaders should listen first and that not every business decision is an economic one [S6]. It shows his habit of learning a business through its people, not its numbers.

## Where he stood

**What is a coffee shop for?** Community and human connection. It should be a third place between home and work [S5].

**Is efficiency always good?** No. In 2007 he argued that speed and cost savings had stripped away the theatre and romance that brought people in [S3].

**What should a company do when it loses its way?** Look in the mirror, get back to the core, slow down, and fix the experience before chasing more growth [S3][S4].

**What does a company owe its staff?** More than wages. He pointed to health care, stock and college as the things that made Starbucks different and helped it grow [S5].

**Should a company take part in public life?** He thought so. He said Starbucks had a duty to use its scale for good [S5]. This also drew criticism when campaigns misfired [S2].

**Should store staff have unions?** He opposed it. In 2023 he told a Senate committee he was not a "union buster", and he said Starbucks had not broken the law [S1]. Labour boards and critics disagreed [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Unions.** He has been accused of opposing unions since the late 1980s [S1]. After the first company-owned U.S. store voted to unionize in Buffalo in 2021, labour officials accused Starbucks of illegal actions against union workers [S2]. In 2023 a Senate committee questioned him, and he denied being a union buster [S1].
- **Growth he later regretted.** His own 2007 memo admitted the company had diluted its experience while chasing 13,000 stores [S3]. The fix in 2008 and 2009 closed hundreds of stores and cost thousands of jobs [S2].
- **Race Together.** A 2015 campaign asked baristas to write "Race Together" on cups to start a talk about race. It drew heavy criticism and backlash [S2].
- **Sourcing claims.** Some researchers argue the company's ethical sourcing program is closer to greenwashing than real change [S2].
- **Sports.** He bought the Seattle SuperSonics in 2001 and sold them in 2006 to a group that moved the team to Oklahoma City. He later called it one of his biggest regrets [S1].
- **His story.** His account of a poor childhood conflicts with some people who knew him, who recall a more middle-class upbringing (disputed) [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He puts great weight on feeling and romance. Pair him with someone who will check the numbers, or a warm idea can cost too much.
- His love of partners did not extend to unions, so a council should hear from the staff themselves, not only from leaders speaking for them [S1].
- He tends to grow fast and fix later. His own memo shows the cost of that pattern [S3].
- Campaigns built on good intent can misfire in public, as Race Together did [S2]. Test a social message before you launch it.
- His examples come from one big chain with deep pockets. A small business may not afford the same benefits, so look for the spirit, not the exact policy.
