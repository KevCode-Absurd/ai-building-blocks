## In one paragraph

Nooyi grew up in Madras (now Chennai), studied science there, management at IIM Calcutta and Yale, and worked at Boston Consulting Group, Motorola and ABB before joining PepsiCo in 1994 [S2]. As its chief strategist she helped reshape the company: spinning off the restaurant chains in 1997, buying Tropicana in 1998 and merging with Quaker Oats, which brought Gatorade, in 2001 [S2][S3]. She became CEO in October 2006 and chairman in 2007 [S3]. Her strategy, "Performance with Purpose," aimed for long-term growth that was also good for people and the planet, and it split products into "fun for you," "better for you" and "good for you" [S2]. Revenue roughly doubled during her time as CEO [S3]. After stepping down in 2018 she argued, in her memoir, that business can't succeed in a society that fails, and that work and family care must be redesigned together [S1].

## Core ideas

### No business succeeds in a failing society

Nooyi's memoir sums up her core belief: no business can truly succeed in a society that fails [S1]. She sees business as one of the most powerful forces for good in society [S1]. Profit and purpose, in her view, have to work together over the long run.

She also draws a line between purpose and corporate social responsibility. In her 2011 interview she said CSR is an add-on that can be cut, and she called it confession after the mistake, while purpose is built into how the business runs [S6]. In 2010 she described Performance with Purpose as three areas: healthier products, caring for the environment in ways that also cut costs, and staff who can have a life beyond work. She said purpose funds performance and performance funds purpose [S8].

How she applied it: since 2006 PepsiCo has described its mission as "Performance with Purpose" [S3]. Under her it set goals on healthier products, packaging, water use, renewable energy and recycling [S2].

### Reshape the portfolio before you have to

Nooyi's strategy work started long before she was CEO. She argued for leaving businesses that didn't fit and buying ones that did [S2][S3]. A quote attributed to her says a leader's challenge is to look around the corner and change before it's too late [S5].

How she applied it: PepsiCo spun off its restaurants as Tricon, later Yum! Brands, in 1997 [S2][S3]. It bought Tropicana for $3.3 billion in 1998, over doubts from other executives and Wall Street [S2][S4], and merged with Quaker Oats in 2001 [S3].

### Fun for you, better for you, good for you

Nooyi sorted PepsiCo's products into three groups: treats ("fun for you"), lighter versions ("better for you") and nutritious foods ("good for you") [S2]. She moved company spending toward the healthier groups [S2].

How she applied it: PepsiCo said healthier products brought in about $10 billion in 2009, and it aimed for $30 billion by 2020 [S3].

### Tough decisions need rigorous analysis

Nooyi's memoir says good business demands tough decisions, based on careful analysis and firm follow-through [S1]. She also writes that big change has no shortcuts [S1]. Her training as a strategy consultant shows in this [S2].

How she applied it: the portfolio changes took more than a decade, from the 1997 spin-off to the health targets set after 2006 [S2][S3].

### Build for each market, do not export

Nooyi rejects the idea of taking a Western model and adding local flavour. Her example was India’s tiny stores, which need a different way to offer choice [S8]. In 2017 she said she knew she could not export the US model abroad [S10].

How she applied it: she told her team to rethink the business around where it operates, not copy a template [S10].

### Listen more than you speak

Nooyi's memoir praises listening twice as much as you speak [S1]. She describes an early experience where topics were explored in depth and pros and cons debated, which she had never seen before [S1].

How she applied it: as CEO she wrote to the parents of her senior executives and visited their homes, to build a personal bond and keep good people [S2].

### Honesty over false praise

In the 2021 conversation she described feedback as something that can land like a hand grenade, so how you deliver it matters as much as what it says. She also said preparation is a way to show respect, and that a good mentor wants you to soar, not stay under their wing [S7]. Nooyi also writes that she grew up with honesty, not false encouragement [S1]. Her childhood was not one of "great job," she says, but of being asked whether that was the best she could do [S1].

How she applied it: she brings that standard to leadership. Feelings can churn inside, she writes, but a leader shows a calm face [S1].

### Redesign work around care

In her memoir, Nooyi argues that our system puts women's career clock and biological clock in direct conflict [S1]. She calls for workplaces and policies that accept that both men and women work outside the home and need support with children and older parents [S1]. She argues that more women leaders would mean a healthier and fairer society [S1].

How she applied it: after PepsiCo she wrote *My Life in Full* in 2021 to make this case [S2]. Her memoir describes a family setup where grandparents help and children connect with them [S1].

### Use your position to serve

Nooyi has kept working on public problems since leaving PepsiCo [S2]. She sees leaders as people who must give back.

How she applied it: she joined Amazon's board in 2019, co-led Connecticut's economic development centre, co-chaired the state's COVID-19 reopening group in 2020, and holds a leadership chair at West Point [S2].

## Real decisions

### Buying Tropicana and Quaker for the morning

In 2011 Nooyi said the company’s own analysis showed that few of its products were bought before 10 a.m., so Tropicana and Quaker Oats were bought to serve morning occasions [S6]. She also admitted that between 2001 and 2006 the company let both brands stagnate [S6]. The prices and dates are in the record above [S2][S4].

### A portfolio with targets, not a slogan

She sorted products into fun, better and good for you, and in 2011 said the aim was a split near 80 to 20, with good for you possibly reaching about 30% by 2020 [S6]. She judged the shift on brand strength, portfolio share, returns and cash flow, not one or two quarters [S6].

### Cutting deep while raising research spend

In October 2008 PepsiCo began a restructuring with deep cuts to keep breathing room. She raised research spending in the downturn, because cheaper resources meant better hiring and outside partnerships [S8]. She stayed visible, practised what she called realistic optimism, repeated five priorities and let line managers run their businesses [S8].

### Pushing health when colleagues said "soda and chips"

Colleagues pushed back with the point that PepsiCo was a soda-and-chips company. She kept raising the trend until it was acted on, whatever her rank [S10]. In 2021 she named one regret: dropping the health push too early, while still defending the idea [S7].

### Designing plants around water

Her mother rationed water in Madras, and she told that story to explain why PepsiCo designed plants around replacing the water they use. She treated it as part of the business model, not an add-on [S6].

### Writing to executives’ parents

To build a bond with her senior team, she wrote to their parents to thank them [S8]. In 2021 she described letters to the parents of about 400 executives [S7].

### Changing the office, not telling people to cope

On hybrid work she argued for changing office space and fixed schedules, not leaving each person to work it out alone [S7].

## Where she stood

**What is business for?** Profit with purpose. A company can't thrive in a failing society, as of 2021 [S1].

**Should a big company change its products?** Yes, before it's forced to. She pushed PepsiCo toward healthier food and drink [S2][S3].

**Can women have it all?** Not under today's system, which sets career and family clocks against each other. She argues it can be redesigned, as of 2021 [S1].

**How should a leader react to pressure?** Feel it inside, stay calm outside [S1].

**What about praise?** Be honest instead of falsely encouraging [S1].

**How should you keep your best people?** Personally. She wrote to the parents of her top executives and visited their homes, to build a personal bond that made good people want to stay [S2].

**Is a company's name sacred?** Not always. PepsiCo even considered renaming itself and moving away from its namesake cola during the health push [S2].

**Who should lead?** More women. She argues that more female leaders would make society healthier, wealthier and fairer [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **The health push.** Reference sources note that PepsiCo's health push faltered, and one headline said her long-term strategy put her job at risk [S2].
- **Diet Pepsi.** PepsiCo removed aspartame from Diet Pepsi in 2015, then brought it back in 2016 after customers objected [S2].
- **Snacks "for women."** Her 2018 remark that PepsiCo would design snacks for women drew criticism [S2].
- **Tropicana packaging.** The 2009 redesign was dropped after sales fell sharply [S4].
- **The wider company.** In her time, PepsiCo faced criticism over sugary and salty products, plastic waste and water use, including in India [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- A purpose agenda can clash with what shareholders want this quarter [S2].
- Healthy-portfolio goals can be hard to square with selling soda and crisps [S3].
- Big-company tools like long strategy reviews can be too slow for a small team.
- Her ideas on care come from a senior executive's life. Check them against people with fewer resources.
- Product labels like "better for you" can sound healthier than the product is. Check the claim against what is actually inside [S2][S3].
