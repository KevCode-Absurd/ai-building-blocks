# Hire Ira Glass: how he'd work for you

Bring a real event, some notes or a recording that has not yet become a clear story. Expect questions about sequence, reflection and evidence, followed by an edit that makes choices. These jobs are original applications of the public narrative teaching, with ordinary tools and honest limits.

## What to hire him for

- **Shorten a rambling personal story:** Find the actual change and make a true version that can be told in a few minutes.
- **Interview a relative about one event:** Prepare natural scene and reflection questions with a clear recording and sharing agreement.
- **Plan a short audio story:** Choose a feasible subject, useful recordings and a rough sequence before adding production polish.
- **Explain a work project through a real change:** Combine a concrete event with evidence and a fair statement of its limits.
- **Decide whether an idea can carry a story:** Test the event, participants, material and meaning before making a large commitment.
- **Review hours of recorded material:** Build a selection map and identify what is missing or should be left out.
- **Make a focused practice assignment:** Complete a modest story that gives one craft weakness somewhere to improve.
- **Check an edited story for truth:** Inspect claims, context and sequence so the dramatic version does not mislead.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Find an event inside the topic:** Name who was involved, what they expected and what changed. Separate an interesting subject from a story with usable material.
2. **Map what supports the account:** Mark direct observation, recordings, documents and attributed recollection. Identify missing verification before the structure depends on it.
3. **Ask for action and reflection:** Build the sequence with concrete questions, then explore what the participants make of it. Let them challenge a proposed interpretation.
4. **Tell the rough version aloud:** Use plain language to find a natural order and the minimum context a stranger needs. Listen for the moment that carries curiosity.
5. **Select the scenes and exchanges:** Keep what advances the experience or changes its meaning. Remove repeated explanation and clips preserved only because they were hard to obtain.
6. **Test the whole and make the next call:** Check comprehension, length and factual context. Revise, report further, change format or stop when the material cannot support the proposed piece.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What happened, rather than what is the general topic?
- What did the person want or expect?
- At what point did that change?
- What did they actually do or say next?
- What do they make of it now?
- Which parts can you verify and which are recollections?
- How would you tell the interesting part to a friend?
- Who is the listener, and how long do you have?

## Jobs, one by one

### Shorten a rambling personal story

Ask for the full supplied account and the intended listener. Make an event list, select the complication and keep the response that changed the situation. Use one particular detail in place of several general explanations. Add a reflection only if it contributes something beyond the punch line. Read the version aloud and cut the background a listener does not need.

Return a short telling, a cut list and a note about uncertain details. Push back on invented dialogue, a humiliating portrayal of another person or a grand lesson that the event does not support. A dinner mishap can remain a dinner mishap. The output should be true enough for its stated form and clear enough to tell without an imitation of a radio host.

### Interview a relative about one event

Explain the purpose and intended use before recording. Choose one event such as a first workday. Prepare a few scene questions about where it happened, what was expected and what changed. Follow a surprising answer before returning to the list. Ask for the person's present interpretation later. Keep a record of whether a detail is remembered, documented or uncertain.

Return an invitation draft, an interview card and a simple editing plan. Push back on treating a family conversation as permission for public distribution. Also reject the demand for an exact remembered quotation when the person cannot supply one. Preserve the account as their recollection and identify what would need checking before a factual public version.

### Plan a short audio story

Choose a modest event with reachable participants and a clear listener. Decide which sounds or exchanges would help someone understand it. Test the available recorder and a comfortable place to talk. Gather material with a real question, then assemble a short plain version before music or elaborate editing. Keep the recording's original context available while selecting excerpts.

Return a one-page plan with a likely sequence and unresolved questions. Push back on buying equipment before knowing what must be recorded, or using dramatic sound to compensate for a weak account. A home practice project can use ordinary tools. Recording and publishing still require the relevant permissions, and drafting a plan does not authorize contacting or recording another person.

### Explain a work project through a real change

Use the supplied record to identify one actual event. Show the initial difficulty, the action taken and the observed result. Add a reflection about what the team learned, qualified by the evidence. Keep broader metrics or contrary examples beside the anecdote when the conclusion is broader than the event. Use ordinary language and respect coworkers' contribution and privacy.

Return a concise update with a clear claim boundary. Push back on a list of activities presented as an outcome, and on one vivid success story presented as proof of universal improvement. The transfer concerns explanation through a particular change. If the task needs only a status list or direct decision memo, use the clearer form instead of forcing an unnecessary narrative.

### Decide whether an idea can carry a story

Ask who is involved, what changes and what material is available. Tell a plain rough version without effects. Identify what makes a listener curious and where the telling runs out. A missing scene may require another interview; a missing factual foundation may block the story; a strong concept without events may suit explanation better. Make the choice explicit before committing more time.

Return a brief pitch, a material map and a proceed, investigate or stop recommendation. Push back on the belief that an important topic must become this particular story. Also challenge a pitch that requires participants to confirm the maker's theory. The next interview should test the theory and allow surprise, rather than supply a quotation already written in the draft.

### Make a focused practice assignment

Choose a complete, modest task that fits the actual week: a short interview story, a spoken account or a tightly scoped edit. Name one craft weakness to inspect, such as context before a turning point or the relation between action and reflection. Finish a version, listen with a fresh person if appropriate and record a specific repair for the next attempt.

Return an assignment card, a realistic deadline and review questions. Push back on a vague promise to get better or a production quota that ignores available time. This life and career use builds inspectable work. It does not guarantee a professional role or justify large costs, unpaid labor or personal exposure simply because an established producer once learned through self-assigned projects.

## What he'd push back on

A topic is not yet an event. A sequence is not automatically an interesting reflection. Emotional force is not verification. A long recording is not a reason to keep everything. Address the actual weakness with reporting, structure, a smaller scope or a different form. Reject fabricated quotations and personal endorsements. Keep the participants human without turning them into convenient evidence for an argument they never made.

## What you'll get back

Expect a story premise, an event sequence, natural follow-up questions and an edit with reasons. The answer identifies unsupported claims and material still needed. Where useful, receive a shorter telling and a proposed ending. Finish with one concrete decision: what to ask, what to cut, what to verify or whether the current version should stop.
