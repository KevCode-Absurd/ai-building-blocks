# Jack Ma: what he knew and believed

It adds Alibaba's 2014 filing for its U.S. share sale [S5], a report on a 2015 talk [S6], and public reference pages [S3][S4]. Treat each view as "as of" the date of its source, 2011 to 2018. ## In one paragraph

Ma was an English teacher in Hangzhou who failed his college entrance exam twice and was turned down for many jobs before he found the internet on a trip to the United States in 1995 [S3]. In 1999 he and 17 friends and students started Alibaba in his flat, as a website linking small Chinese makers with buyers [S4]. He bet on small private firms rather than big buyers, because China had few large companies [S2]. He then built Taobao to fight eBay, and Alipay to solve the problem of strangers not trusting each other online [S2][S4]. Alibaba's 2014 share sale in New York raised $25 billion, then the largest ever [S4]. He handed the chairman's role to Daniel Zhang in 2019, saying no company should rest on its founder [S1]. His later years were shaped by a clash with Chinese regulators in 2020 [S3].

## Core ideas

### Help small businesses compete

From the start, Alibaba's mission was to make it easy to do business anywhere [S5]. The founders believed the internet would let small firms compete with big ones [S5]. At Stanford in 2011, Ma said U.S. business sites served big buyers, but he chose small private firms. He admitted the model was less exciting than gaming, but said it meant more [S2].

How he applied it: by 2011 he said Alibaba served more than 58 million small businesses around the world [S2]. In 2015 he urged U.S. small firms in New York to use the internet to sell to China's growing middle class [S6].

### Trust has to be built into the system

Ma saw that online trade in China lacked the credit and banking systems that make strangers trust each other [S2]. Alibaba's filing called the trusted status of its ecosystem critical to its success [S5].

How he applied it: Alipay began as an escrow service. It held the buyer's money until the goods arrived. People called it a stupid idea, but he argued that something you improve every day becomes smart [S2]. By 2011 he said it had more than 600 million registered users [S2].

### The local crocodile can beat the ocean shark

When eBay entered China, Ma started Taobao in 2003 to compete [S4]. He described eBay as a shark from the ocean and Taobao as a crocodile in the Yangtze River. In the ocean the shark wins, but in the river the crocodile has the edge [S2]. He also gave luck some of the credit [S2].

How he applied it: Taobao passed eBay in China in 2007 [S4]. He summed up the wider plan as "global vision, local win." Alibaba tested markets in Japan, India and Europe and kept an office in the United States, but it won at home first [S2].

### Data from small firms is the next platform

At Stanford he said Alibaba's cloud work was not about selling hardware or software. The goal was to process the data that came from small firms, shoppers and payments, and turn it into services [S2]. He predicted that China's internet users would soon outnumber America's and that its shoppers would spend more each year [S2].

How he applied it: Alibaba Cloud launched in September 2009 [S4]. By 2015 he was pointing foreign sellers at China's middle class, whose demand for good products he called very strong [S6].

### Build an ecosystem, not an empire

Ma rejected what he called empire thinking [S2]. He wanted a system where firms help each other grow. The 2014 filing describes Alibaba as the steward of an ecosystem of buyers, sellers, service firms and partners [S5].

How he applied it: he split Taobao into separate units to give rivals room and young leaders ownership [S2]. He also chose not to build Alibaba's own delivery network at the time, and supported delivery firms instead [S2].

### Learn from mistakes, more than from success

Ma said Alibaba made many mistakes and joked about writing a book called "Alibaba 1001 Mistakes" [S2]. He argued that failed companies tend to fail for the same few reasons, so studying failure teaches more than studying success [S2].

How he applied it: he admitted that Alibaba handled its purchase of Yahoo China badly, in culture and staffing, and said he would do it again but better [S2].

### Learn by doing

His advice to founders was to test ideas fast and learn as you go. You only learn to swim once you are in the water [S2]. His own path was a string of rejections. He failed the entrance exam in 1982 and 1983 and got into Hangzhou Normal University on his third try [S3]. In 1999 Silicon Valley investors turned him down again and again, and he went home and started Alibaba anyway [S2].

How he applied it: by late 1999 Alibaba had raised $25 million from Goldman Sachs, SoftBank and others [S4].

### Build a company that outlasts its founder

In his 2018 letter, Ma said no one can hold the roles of chairman and CEO forever [S1]. He wrote that Alibaba had moved from leaning on individuals to a system built on culture and the growth of talent [S1]. He had spent about ten years planning the handover [S1].

How he applied it: the founders formed the Alibaba Partnership in 2010, a group that names a majority of the board [S5]. He gave up the CEO job in 2013 and handed the chair to Daniel Zhang on 10 September 2019 [S1][S4].

### A teacher's view of people

Ma trained as a teacher and kept that view of his role. In the letter he said teachers always want their students to do better than them, and that he wanted to go back to education [S1]. At Stanford he said Alibaba's real product was its people [S2]. He also said that past a certain level, money becomes society's trust, and using it well is the hard part [S2].

How he applied it: he founded the Jack Ma Foundation in 2018, focused on education, the environment and public health, and backed a programme for rural teachers [S3].

## How he decides

- Serve the small player the big firms ignore [S2].
- Solve a real social problem rather than chase a hot opportunity [S2].
- Make trust part of the product, so strangers can deal with each other [S2][S5].
- Fight a bigger rival where you know the ground better [S2].
- Try it quickly, then improve it every day [S2].
- Build relationships with customers, not with officials. Be open and follow the law [S2].
- Think global, but win locally first [S2].
- Plan your own succession years ahead [S1].

## Where he stood

**Who should a business serve?** In 2011 he said the small business. His mission was to make it easy for small firms to do business anywhere [S2][S5].

**What matters more, a company's size or its mission?** The mission and the culture. His 2018 letter said they matter more than any one person [S1].

**Should a founder stay in charge forever?** No. He argued a company must not rely only on its founders, and he stepped down while still young [S1].

**What builds a lasting business in a weak system?** Trust between strangers, built into the product, as with Alipay [S2].

**How should a company handle fake goods on its site?** In 2011 he said Taobao spent much of its effort on checks, and that shoppers should learn to compare prices while makers learned to sell direct [S2].

**Should you lean on connections with officials?** No, as of 2011. He said the only connection that counts is the one with the customer [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Long hours.** In 2019 he publicly praised China's "996" culture of very long working weeks, calling it a blessing. Many people criticised this [S3].
- **Clash with regulators.** In late 2020 he criticised regulators and state banks at a Shanghai forum. Soon after, the stock exchange halted Ant Group's share sale, and he was largely out of public view until January 2021 [S3]. In 2023 Ant cut his voting control from 50% to 6% [S3].
- **Market power.** In April 2021 regulators fined Alibaba $2.8 billion for anti-competitive practices. Alibaba did not appeal [S4].
- **Fake goods.** In 2016 the U.S. trade office put Taobao on its list of markets known for fake goods. Alibaba denied wrongdoing and sued counterfeiters [S4]. In 2011 Ma said much of Taobao's effort went into checks on copied goods [S2].
- **Too hopeful about small firms abroad.** A 2015 report noted that his examples of selling to China came from large firms or niche goods, and the same path might not suit a small local shop [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- His trust-first, small-business story sits beside real fights over market power and fake goods. Ask who loses when a platform grows huge [S4].
- Praise for very long hours can wear people out. Pair him with someone who guards the team's health [S3].
- "Build relationships with customers, not officials" assumes the rules are stable. His own 2020 clash shows the risk of ignoring regulators [S3].
- Luck played a part, by his own account [S2]. Don't treat his path as a formula.
