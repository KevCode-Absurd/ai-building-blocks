# Jack Trout: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Jack Trout was an American advertising man who turned positioning into a household word in marketing [S1][S5]. He began in advertising at General Electric and Uniroyal, then joined Al Ries's New York agency, where the two worked together for more than 26 years [S1][S7]. In 1972 they published a three-part series in Advertising Age called "The Positioning Era Cometh" [S4]. Their 1981 book Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind sold millions of copies [S4]. The core idea is simple. People are flooded with messages, so a brand must own one clear place in the customer's mind, set against its rivals [S2][S5]. In 1993 they distilled their work into 22 laws, with a closing warning that managers inside a company would resist them [S15]. Trout later ran his own firm, Trout & Partners [S1][S7]. He died in June 2017, aged 82 [S1].

## Core ideas

### The battle is in the mind

The 1972 articles argued that advertising had moved through a product era and an image era into a positioning era, where strategy matters more than clever ads [S2]. Society is flooded with messages, so people filter out most of them [S2]. The real battleground is the prospect's mind [S2]. The 1993 book said it more starkly: marketing is a battle of perceptions, not products, and truth is only what some expert is believed to hold [S15]. Trout described people as having layers of defence: what they let in, what they notice and what they keep [S10]. In his view the basic approach is not to create something new but to work with what is already in the mind [S7].

### Ladders in the mind

Ries and Trout said people rank brands in each category, as if on a ladder with only a few rungs [S2]. A new brand must climb an existing ladder or start a new one by linking to something familiar [S2]. A name with no positioning idea in it tends to fail [S2]. The book added that strategy depends on the rung you hold [S15]. A leader defends. A number two goes opposite. A weak number three should narrow, because in a mature market the ladder shrinks to two names [S15]. Each brand tends to hold about twice the share of the one below [S15]. He pointed to brands that own one idea, such as Volvo and safety [S3][S10].

### Be first, or make a new category

Leaders usually win a category by getting there first and spending while the market is still open [S2]. In 2013 Trout said it is better to be first than better [S3]. His obituary sums up his core advice as being first, creating new categories and using a strong, clear name [S7]. The book's second law gave the fallback: if you can't be first, set up a category you can be first in [S15]. It also warned that being first to market is not enough. You have to be first in the mind [S15].

How he applied it: he used BMW's long focus on driving as an example of a first position held for decades [S3]. If you can't be first in an existing category, create a new one you can lead [S7].

### Strategy starts with the competition

For Trout, you can't plan your position alone. In 2013 he said strategy starts with the competition, and that it is about what your rivals will let you do [S3]. He also drew on the military thinker Carl von Clausewitz, framing marketing as attack, counterattack and defence [S6]. The book's law of singularity put it in war terms: look for the one bold stroke the enemy least expects, and send your force there [S15].

How he applied it: the 1972 articles held up Avis as the model challenger. It won by placing itself against Hertz as number two, and a challenger should go around or under a strong leader rather than straight at it [S2]. The book called this the law of opposite. Where the leader is strong, turn that strength into a weakness [S15]. He also cited Hyundai and Pepsi as brands that used a rival's weakness [S3].

### Reposition the rival

A brand can gain ground by changing how people see a competitor [S2]. The 1972 articles cited Beck's beer placing itself against Löwenbräu, and Volkswagen admitting its car was ugly to stand out [S2]. The book called the second move candor. Admit a negative buyers already believe, then turn it into the positive. A negative needs no proof, while a boast does [S15].

How he applied it: he told small businesses to find an angle against the big players, and to ask what they bring to the party [S3]. Papa John's linked its pizza to a better sauce supplier, a story the giants could not claim. Trout helped create its line "better ingredients, better pizza" [S1][S3].

### Beware the line extension trap

Putting a famous name on many products blurs its meaning [S2]. In 2013 he said that the stronger a brand gets in one category, the weaker it gets in others [S3]. He preferred separate brands for separate businesses [S3]. He also warned that big changes in the product usually need a new brand and a new plan [S3]. The book called line extension the most violated law, and listed the pull of short-term sales as the reason that managers keep doing it [S15].

How he applied it: he contrasted United Technologies, which kept separate names like Otis and Carrier, with GE's one name on everything [S3][S6]. He saw Kodak's trouble with digital as a case where a new brand was needed [S3]. Wall Street's push for growth, he said, often dilutes brands [S6].

### Keep it simple, own one word

Trout held that complex messages confuse and confused messages get rejected [S10]. He argued that words matter more than pictures [S10]. The book's law of focus said the most powerful idea in marketing is to own a simple word in the prospect's mind, and that a rival's word can't be taken [S15]. A big idea, he said, should hit the mind like a nail, and then the marketing drives it home [S6].

How he applied it: in his last years a friend summed up his advice as needing a position, not just a slogan, and avoiding the common error of trying to appeal to everyone [S8].

### Quality and price are not strategies

In 2013 Trout said that price and quality alone are not a position. Everyone claims quality, so it is hard to own [S3]. The book gave the reason: a claim can only be owned if some rival is willing to say the opposite [S15]. A brand needs a story or a real technical edge [S3].

### The four steps of a position

Asked in a plain Q&A what the work involves, Trout gave four steps. First, know the competition and where it is strong and weak. Second, pick an idea you can own. Third, find your credentials for that idea, such as research or business you have already done, because "I'm here and I'm great" proves nothing. Fourth, tell the story, since he held that good marketing is good storytelling [S11].

How he applied it: his advice to a client was to arrive with the position settled and keep control of it. He said to tell the agency not to be creative but to dramatize the idea [S11]. The book agreed on who owns marketing: the chief executive should go to the front line and see for himself, not hand the work to finance or a junior [S15].

**Attack the weakness that comes from the leader's strength.**

In the Chinese-language Q&A he said every strong rival has a weakness, but the one to hit is the one that comes from its strength [S12]. Avis said its counter lines were shorter, and Hertz could not answer because long lines come with being the biggest [S12]. He added that managers resist this kind of negative idea because they prefer positive thinking [S12].

**Too many choices make one clear idea worth more.**

Trout argued that more choice does not attract buyers, it overwhelms them, and he cited nearly 300 shoe models [S12]. In a flood of options a brand must be easy to grasp, ideally one word, like safety for Volvo [S12].

**Reposition by adjusting perceptions.**

For Trout, repositioning adjusts how people see you or your rival. It does not change their minds from scratch [S12]. His cases: the kiwifruit renamed from Chinese gooseberry, a cider maker that moved to a premium drink, and Lotus rebuilt as a groupware company [S12]. His failures list: Xerox in computers, New Coke, and Cadillac's small cars that looked like Chevrolets [S12].

**Different rules for different ranks.**

A leader reinforces leadership. A number two attacks the leader where it is weak. A company making a new category plays a different game. A small player in a big category acts like a guerrilla and finds a small piece it can hold [S11]. He also stated that over the long run a category in the mind holds about two brands [S12], which the book had called the law of duality [S15].

## How he decides

- Study the competition first [S3].
- Find one open spot in the customer's mind [S2][S3].
- Be first, or create a category you can lead [S3][S7][S15].
- Own one word or idea, and repeat it [S10][S15].
- Give separate businesses separate names [S3].
- Use a new brand for a big change [S3].
- Go around a strong leader, not through it. Hit the weakness that comes from its strength [S2][S12][S15].
- Keep the message simple and spoken plainly [S10].
- Stay consistent for years [S3].
- Keep the top person in charge of the position [S11][S15].

## Real decisions

Trout was an adviser, so these are calls he made or argued for on other people's businesses, as he or his source told them. The business results are not independently checked.

### Avis accepting second place
In the 1972 article, he and Al Ries held up Avis as the model challenger [S2]. Avis lost money for 13 years, they wrote, then turned a profit once it accepted that it was number two and built its message around Hertz [S2]. Their reason: go around the leader and define yourself against it, not through it [S2]. Years later he gave the follow-up rule that the attack works because the leader cannot fix a weakness that comes from its size [S12].

### Papa John's and the sauce
Trout told how he met a tomato sauce maker who supplied small pizzerias, and learned that Papa John's was quietly using it [S3]. That gave Papa John's a better-tasting pizza and an ingredient story to tell, which became "Better ingredients, better pizza" [S3][S12]. His reason: a small business needs an angle against the big players, and a real product fact makes the angle [S3].

### Kodak and a separate digital brand
Trout argued that Kodak invented digital photography but held back because it threatened film, and that it should have launched digital under a separate brand [S3]. His rule: you cannot change a product radically under one name [S3]. In the Mexican interview he listed calls he said he got right: Xerox would fail in computers, Chevrolet would lose share by stretching, and Volkswagen would fail with big fast cars in the US [S13]. The book tells the same Volkswagen story as a failure of one name on every model [S15].

### GE against United Technologies
He contrasted GE, which sold everything under one name and which he called confused, with United Technologies, which kept separate brands such as Otis, Sikorsky, Pratt & Whitney and Carrier [S3]. He said the United Technologies model did better on positioning and shareholder returns, and he blamed Wall Street's demand for growth for much brand dilution [S3][S6]. In the later Q&A he added a limit: long-established leaders like GE and Siemens can hold one shared name longer, but newcomers cannot [S12].

### IBM and the switch from mainframes
Trout advised IBM to move its position from mainframes to integrated computer services [S12]. His reasoning was that IBM ranked only second in hardware, software and chips but was the one firm with the full range of technology [S12]. The book's view of IBM was harsher: it stood for mainframes, and now for everything, which meant nothing [S15].

### Lotus and the groupware reposition
After Microsoft's bundling squeezed Lotus, Trout helped reposition it as a groupware company [S12]. The book backs the logic: Lotus owned the word spreadsheet, could not take a word others held, and so moved to a word nobody owned [S15]. He said the change cost more than a dozen vice presidents, and that it worked only because the chief executive championed it and stayed optimistic [S12]. His rule: a reposition needs the top person to carry it [S12].

### Advising Coca-Cola to drop New Coke, 1993
The book said Coca-Cola was fighting a two-front battle and that its stream of new slogans moved nothing. It advised dropping New Coke, because it blocked the one word Coke owned, the real thing, which Coke could then use against Pepsi's young drinkers [S15]. The stated reason was the law of singularity: in any situation there is one move that works, and a split force cannot make it [S15]. We have not checked what followed.

### AIG, the advice that went unused
Trout named AIG as his main regret. He had advised positioning it as "America's Lloyd's," a specialist insurance marketplace, but it chased financial services and every product for every client instead [S12]. He tied its 2008 collapse in part to derivatives losses in a London unit of about 300 people [S12]. His lesson was that a firm which will not narrow itself pays later.

## Where he stood

**Is creativity enough?** No. In a crowded market, strategy matters more than creativity alone [S2][S4].

**Should a strong brand stretch?** Rarely. Strength in one category weakens it in others [S3][S15].

**How should a small firm fight a big one?** With an angle the big one can't copy [S3].

**Who matters most in marketing?** He thought too few chief executives were closely involved in it, and that weak leadership is worse than a weak market [S9]. The book said the same of success: ego leads a chief executive away from the front line [S15].

**Is positioning still relevant?** In 2007 he said it mattered more than ever, because competition had grown so fierce [S9].

**Can a position last forever?** No. New technology, new habits or a better rival can make a position worthless, as the personal computer did to IBM's mainframe lead [S10]. When the market shifts, a company must reposition and bring its customers along [S10].

**What should a brand name do?** Carry the idea. The 1972 articles called the name the hook that hangs a brand on the mind's ladder, and warned that empty initials and local names can hold a company back [S2].

**How is his advice different from Porter's?** He said many companies do not understand differentiation, and some understand it but cannot put it into practice. He said his work, unlike Michael Porter's, explains how [S13].

**What happens when you have no position?** Price is all that is left, and he called a price fight the road to wreck and ruin [S11][S12].

**What should a manager ask first?** The 1972 articles closed with six questions: what position you own now, what position you want, whom you must outgun, whether you have the money, whether you can stick with it, and whether your ads match the plan [S2]. The book also made money a law of its own: an idea without funding won't get off the ground [S15].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Who invented positioning.** Some scholars say Ries and Trout revived an older idea rather than inventing it. Others credit David Ogilvy in the 1950s [S5].
- **Too narrow at first.** Early positioning was criticised for looking only at the product. Later views add a brand's reputation and its standing against rivals [S5].
- **Politics.** His 2002 work for the State Department's campaign to improve America's image was tied to the run-up to the Iraq War [S1].
- **Firm rules.** His laws are stated as absolutes. Real markets have exceptions, and his own predictions about specific firms were not always tested [S3]. The 1993 book named firms it expected to fail or to be squeezed, and also warned in its law of unpredictability that forecasts mislead. We have not checked those calls [S15].

## Blind spots to watch

- He focuses on the message and the mind. He says less about the product, price or service behind it.
- His rules can be rigid. Some brand extensions work.
- He leans on big, famous brands, and the book's cases are US consumer brands from the early 1990s. Test his advice on a small budget.
- War language can make rivals the focus instead of customers. Pair him with Philip Kotler or Byron Sharp.
- He wrote in the age of TV and print. Digital channels may need other tools.
