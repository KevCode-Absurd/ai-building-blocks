## In one paragraph

Jack Trout was an American advertising man who turned positioning into a household word in marketing [S1][S5]. He began in advertising at General Electric and Uniroyal, then joined Al Ries's New York agency, where the two worked together for more than 26 years [S1][S7]. In 1972 they published a three-part series in Advertising Age called "The Positioning Era Cometh" [S4]. Their 1981 book *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* sold millions of copies [S4]. The core idea is simple. People are flooded with messages, so a brand must own one clear place in the customer's mind, set against its rivals [S2][S5]. Trout and Ries went on to write *Marketing Warfare* and *The 22 Immutable Laws of Marketing* [S1]. Trout later ran his own firm, Trout & Partners, with offices in many countries [S1][S7]. He died in June 2017, aged 82 [S1].

## Core ideas

### The battle is in the mind

The 1972 articles argued that advertising had moved through a product era and an image era into a positioning era, where strategy matters more than clever ads [S2]. Society is flooded with messages, so people filter out most of them [S2]. The real battleground is the prospect's mind [S2]. Trout described people as having layers of defence: what they let in, what they notice and what they keep [S10].

How he applied it: he defined positioning as how you set yourself apart in the mind of the customer [S3]. In his view the basic approach is not to create something new but to work with what is already in the mind [S7].

### Ladders in the mind

Ries and Trout said people rank brands in each category, as if on a ladder with only a few rungs [S2]. A new brand must climb an existing ladder or start a new one by linking to something familiar [S2]. A name with no positioning idea in it tends to fail [S2].

How he applied it: he pointed to brands that own one idea, such as Volvo and safety [S3][S10]. He held that the best messages centre on one word or one attribute [S10].

### Be first, or make a new category

Leaders usually win a category by getting there first and spending while the market is still open [S2]. In 2013 Trout said it is better to be first than better [S3]. His obituary sums up his core advice as being first, creating new categories and using a strong, clear name [S7].

How he applied it: he used BMW's long focus on driving as an example of a first position held for decades [S3]. If you can't be first in an existing category, create a new one you can lead [S7].

### Strategy starts with the competition

For Trout, you can't plan your position alone. In 2013 he said strategy starts with the competition, and that it is about what your rivals will let you do [S3]. He also drew on the military thinker Carl von Clausewitz, framing marketing as attack, counterattack and defence [S6].

How he applied it: the 1972 articles held up Avis as the model challenger. It won by placing itself against Hertz as number two, and a challenger should go around or under a strong leader rather than straight at it [S2]. He also cited Hyundai and Pepsi as brands that used a rival's weakness [S3].

### Reposition the rival

A brand can gain ground by changing how people see a competitor [S2]. The 1972 articles cited Beck's beer placing itself against Löwenbräu, and Volkswagen admitting its car was ugly to stand out [S2].

How he applied it: he told small businesses to find an angle against the big players, and to ask what they bring to the party [S3]. Papa John's linked its pizza to a better sauce supplier, a story the giants could not claim [S3]. Trout helped create its line "better ingredients, better pizza" [S1].

### Beware the line extension trap

Putting a famous name on many products blurs its meaning [S2]. In 2013 he said that the stronger a brand gets in one category, the weaker it gets in others [S3]. He preferred separate brands for separate businesses [S3]. He also warned that big changes in the product usually need a new brand and a new plan [S3].

How he applied it: he contrasted United Technologies, which kept separate names like Otis and Carrier, with GE's one name on everything [S3][S6]. He saw Kodak's trouble with digital as a case where a new brand was needed [S3]. Wall Street's push for growth, he said, often dilutes brands [S6].

### Keep it simple

Trout held that complex messages confuse and confused messages get rejected [S10]. He argued that words matter more than pictures, and that images should support a clear spoken message, not replace it [S10]. A big idea, he said, should hit the mind like a nail, and then the marketing drives it home [S6].

How he applied it: in his last years a friend summed up his advice as needing a position, not just a slogan, and avoiding the common error of trying to appeal to everyone [S8].

### Quality and price are not strategies

In 2013 Trout said that price and quality alone are not a position. Everyone claims quality, so it is hard to own [S3]. A brand needs a story or a real technical edge [S3].

How he applied it: he criticised advertising that leaned on stock formulas over product features and engineering [S3].

## Where he stood

**Is creativity enough?** No. In a crowded market, strategy matters more than creativity alone [S2][S4].

**Should a strong brand stretch?** Rarely. Strength in one category weakens it in others [S3].

**How should a small firm fight a big one?** With an angle the big one can't copy [S3].

**Who matters most in marketing?** He thought too few chief executives were closely involved in it, and that weak leadership is worse than a weak market [S9].

**Is positioning still relevant?** In 2007 he said it mattered more than ever, because competition had grown so fierce [S9].

**Can a position last forever?** No. New technology, new habits or a better rival can make a position worthless, as the personal computer did to IBM's mainframe lead [S10]. When the market shifts, a company must reposition and bring its customers along [S10].

**What should a brand name do?** Carry the idea. The 1972 articles called the name the hook that hangs a brand on the mind's ladder, and warned that empty initials and local names can hold a company back [S2].

**What should a manager ask first?** The 1972 articles closed with six questions: what position you own now, what position you want, whom you must outgun, whether you have the money, whether you can stick with it, and whether your ads match the plan [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Who invented positioning.** Some scholars say Ries and Trout revived an older idea rather than inventing it. One historian said they made it their trademark. Others credit David Ogilvy in the 1950s [S5].
- **Too narrow at first.** Early positioning was criticised for looking only at the product. Later views add a brand's reputation and its standing against rivals [S5].
- **Politics.** His 2002 work for the State Department's campaign to improve America's image was tied to the run-up to the Iraq War [S1].
- **Firm rules.** His laws, such as never stretching a brand, are stated as absolutes. Real markets have exceptions, and his own predictions about specific firms were not always tested [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- He focuses on the message and the mind. He says less about the product, price or service behind it.
- His rules can be rigid. Some brand extensions work.
- He leans on big, famous brands. Test his advice on a small budget.
- War language can make rivals the focus instead of customers. Pair him with Philip Kotler or Byron Sharp.
- He wrote in the age of TV and print. Digital channels may need other tools.
