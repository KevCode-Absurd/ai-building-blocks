# Jakob Nielsen: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Jakob Nielsen is alive, and views can change. This data set rests on six pieces in his own words: four Nielsen Norman Group articles (the ten heuristics, the 2000 piece on five users, Usability 101 and the 2009 look back at discount usability), a 2017 video on Jakob's Law and a 2024 post on his own newsletter. Wikipedia supplies the facts of his career. We did not have his books. Treat each view as "as of" the year of the article it comes from. He wrote for decades and refined his advice over time, so older numbers may have shifted. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after 2024.

## In one paragraph

Nielsen is a Danish usability expert, born in Copenhagen in 1957, with a PhD in human-computer interaction [S1]. He worked at Bellcore, IBM and Sun Microsystems, then co-founded the Nielsen Norman Group with Don Norman in 1998 [S1]. He made his name by making usability cheap. In 1989 he argued that small, quick tests with a handful of people, rough prototypes and expert checks against simple rules could find most problems at low cost [S5]. His 10 usability heuristics are still used to review designs [S2]. His core belief is that you learn what works by watching real people use a design, not by asking them or by trusting your own taste [S4]. In 2023 he founded UX Tigers to focus on usability and AI [S1].

## Core ideas

### Usability is a measurable quality

Nielsen defines usability as how easy a design is to use, and also as the set of methods for making it easier [S4]. He breaks it into five parts: how fast people learn it, how fast they work once they know it, how well they remember it after a break, how many errors they make and how they recover, and how pleasant it feels [S4]. He separates usability from utility. Utility asks whether the design does what people need. Both matter equally [S4].

How he applied it: he argued that on the web, people who struggle simply leave, and that a product shoppers can't find is a product they can't buy [S4]. He suggested spending about 10% of a design budget on usability [S4].

### Watch what people do, not what they say

Nielsen's basic method is user testing. Find people like your real users, give them realistic tasks, and watch them without helping [S4]. He warns that listening to what people say is misleading, so focus groups are a weak substitute [S4].

How he applied it: he said you do not need a fancy lab. An office with a closed door, real users and a notepad will do [S4].

### Test with about five users, many times

In a well-known 2000 article, Nielsen argued that elaborate tests waste money [S3]. With Thomas Landauer, he modelled how many problems each extra test user finds. The first user shows about a third of the problems. Each new user mostly repeats what you saw, and five users find about 85% [S3]. So rather than one big study of 15 people, he advised three rounds of five, fixing the design between each round [S3].

How he applied it: he set limits on the rule. It only holds for similar users. If a site serves very different groups, such as children and parents, test a few from each [S3]. Studies that need numbers, rather than insights, need about 20 users [S3].

### Discount usability

Nielsen first set out "discount usability" in a 1989 conference paper [S5]. It had three parts: simple tests with a few users thinking aloud, rough prototypes, often on paper, and heuristic evaluation, where experts check a design against known rules [S5]. He built it partly out of need, since his university budget was tiny next to a big lab like IBM's [S5]. At the time, he said, the idea was seen as heresy [S5].

How he applied it: he argued that imperfect methods still find real problems, and that bad user testing beats no user testing [S5]. He backed this with a 1988 study of 20 teams testing a drawing program with three users each. Study quality explained 58% of the variation in problems found, and the rest came from team talent and luck [S5].

### Ten heuristics for any interface

With Rolf Molich, Nielsen created a list of usability rules in 1990 and refined it in 1994 [S2]. Among the ten: show people what the system is doing, speak their language, give them a clear way out, stay consistent, prevent errors, let people recognise options instead of recalling them, and keep the design free of anything they rarely need [S2]. Error messages should use plain words, not codes, and the best system needs no extra explanation [S2].

How he applied it: the list became a standard tool for quick expert reviews, one of the three parts of discount usability [S5]. NN/g was still updating the article in 2024 [S2].

### Jakob's Law: follow the conventions

Nielsen's best-known rule of thumb is that users spend most of their time on other sites [S6]. So they expect yours to work the same way as the ones they already know [S6]. Designers should use familiar patterns, so people can apply what they have learned elsewhere [S6].

How he applied it: he used this to argue that design changes should be kept small, and that a new pattern has to be worth the cost of breaking habits [S1][S6].

### Test early and keep testing

Nielsen says usability work belongs at every stage [S4]. Test the current design, test rivals' designs, test paper sketches, then keep testing as you build [S4]. If you wait until the design is finished, deep problems are very hard to fix [S4].

How he applied it: his advice for three small rounds of tests uses the second round to check whether the fixes worked and to find deeper problems with structure and flow [S3].

### Rethink accessibility with AI

In 2024 Nielsen argued that accessibility had failed by usability standards [S7]. After about 30 years of effort, he said, computers were still hard, slow and unpleasant for many disabled users [S7]. He gave two reasons. Full accessibility is too expensive for most firms to build and test, and screen readers turn a two-dimensional screen into a slow, linear stream of audio [S7]. He proposed "generative UI," where AI builds an interface for each user, such as an audio-first design for blind users [S7].

How he applied it: he predicted that designers would shift from drawing exact screens to setting the rules the AI follows, much as responsive web design ended pixel-perfect control [S7].

## How he decides

- Watch real users try real tasks. Don't help them [S4].
- Trust behaviour over opinions [S4].
- Test about five users, fix, then test again [S3].
- Test each very different user group separately [S3].
- Start early, with paper if you have to [S4][S5].
- Use the 10 heuristics for a fast expert review [S2].
- Follow the patterns users already know, unless the gain is big [S6].
- A cheap, imperfect test beats no test [S5].

## Real decisions

### Turning a small budget into a method
At the end of the 1980s Nielsen had a university budget that was tiny next to a lab like IBM's. He worked for about two years on cheaper methods and presented them in 1989 as discount usability: thinking-aloud tests with few people, narrow paper prototypes and expert checks against rules. He says it was seen as heresy at first [S5].

### Choosing five users over fifteen
With Thomas Landauer he modelled that each test user finds about 31% of problems. Rather than one study of fifteen, he advised three rounds of five. Round one finds most problems, round two checks the fixes and finds deeper ones, round three catches the rest [S3].

### Moving from three users to five
His early advice was three users per round. He later settled on five as a practical compromise [S5].

### Spending about a tenth of the budget
He advised spending about 10% of a design project's budget on usability. On his figures that more than doubles a website's quality measures on average, with smaller gains for software and physical products [S4].

### Keeping the heuristics steady
He and Rolf Molich drafted the list in 1990 and he revised it in 1994. He says it has stayed essentially unchanged since, because durable principles should apply to future interfaces too [S2].

### Calling accessibility a failure, and proposing generative UI
In 2024 he judged 30 years of accessibility work against usability and found it lacking, with reasons of cost and slow screen readers. He proposed AI-built interfaces for each user. Readers pushed back in the comments under the piece [S7].

## Where he stood

**How many users do you need to test?** About five per round, for qualitative insight, run several times [S3].

**Is a lab necessary?** No. A closed room and real users are enough [S4].

**Should a site be original?** Mostly no. Users expect it to work like the sites they already use [S6].

**Do focus groups work?** Poorly for usability, because what people say differs from what they do [S4].

**Has discount usability won?** In 2009 he said he could not yet declare a win, since many teams still ran big studies or skipped paper prototypes [S5].

**Has accessibility worked?** In 2024 he said no, judged by usability, and that AI-built interfaces may be the way forward [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Unproven rules.** His heuristics have never been fully validated by research, and a 2008 University of Calgary article asked whether they oversimplify usability [S1].
- **Neglect of visual design.** Visual and graphic designers have said he undervalues typography, readability and visual hierarchy [S1]. Some critics have called his approach puritanical [S1].
- **Self-promotion.** The writer Philip Greenspun argued that his work was more about marketing himself than research [S1].
- **Mobile sites.** His 2012 advice to build separate mobile sites drew opposition from supporters of responsive design, which became the norm [S1].
- **Accessibility.** His 2024 claim that accessibility had failed drew push-back. One reader argued it understated real progress, let firms off the hook, and skipped over how much unproven work AI interfaces would need. Another worried that custom interfaces for each person would break shared conventions [S7].
- **Five users.** His original 1989 paper suggested three users, and he later moved to five as a compromise [S5]. The number depends on similar users, which he notes himself [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- He puts ease of use first. Brand, emotion and beauty can get less weight. Pair him with a visual designer.
- Jakob's Law can make everything look the same. Sometimes standing out is the point.
- Five-user tests find problems but don't measure how big they are. Use larger studies for numbers.
- His advice comes mostly from websites and software. Check it before applying it to physical spaces or print.
- His confident tone can make rules of thumb sound like laws.
