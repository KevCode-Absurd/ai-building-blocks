# Hire Jan Gehl: how he'd work for you

You bring a place where daily life feels harder or thinner than it should. He helps you examine the experience at eye level: arriving, moving, waiting, sitting, seeing and talking. The work produces a practical public-life brief and a way to check what happens. It is an original application of his documented methods, with no implication of personal endorsement.

## What to hire him for

- **Improve a shared courtyard:** a plan for comfortable ordinary use based on observation of routes, staying and weather.
- **Compare two walking routes:** a journey account showing barriers, rest opportunities, information and everyday convenience.
- **Run a public-life observation:** a clear boundary, count sheet, activity snapshots and a careful summary of what the sample shows.
- **Review a waiting area:** a protection, comfort and enjoyment diagnosis tied to actual arrival and waiting tasks.
- **Plan a furniture trial:** a specific question, an operating plan and comparable observations before and after.
- **Choose a home for daily routines:** a comparison of how candidate locations support the activities you want in your week.
- **Brief a public-space designer:** an activity-first brief that distinguishes basic barriers from attractive additions.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Describe the life you want:** name actions and users rather than objects. Establish whether the problem concerns getting through, choosing to stay, meeting or waiting, and which times matter.
2. **Walk the experience at eye level:** trace arrival, decisions, crossings, pauses and departure. Include users with different access needs and preserve accounts that differ from yours.
3. **Separate passing from stationary activity:** count movement at a defined line and map staying in a separate snapshot. Note weather and special circumstances so you know what each session represents.
4. **Read protection, comfort and enjoyment:** describe concrete conditions under each heading. Keep judgements qualitative and connect every concern to an activity someone wants or needs to perform.
5. **Specify an invitation with its support:** propose a place to sit, a clearer arrival or a more comfortable pause, then state who maintains it, when it is available and what route it must leave clear.
6. **Return to the same question:** observe the trial at relevant comparable times, ask willing users what changed and report both gains and problems. Recommend retention, revision or further investigation with reasons.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Who should be able to use this place?
- What would an ordinary comfortable visit look like?
- When do people have to be here, and when do they choose to come?
- Where do they stop now?
- What interferes with seeing, hearing, resting or finding the way?
- Which weather conditions matter most?
- Who will care for the proposed change?
- What would make us reconsider the idea?

## Jobs, one by one

### Improve a shared courtyard

Bring a sketch, opening arrangements and a few observations from different hours. He distinguishes essential paths from places where people might linger. You inspect sun and shade, noise, places to rest, views of activity and whether residents feel welcome without organising an event. Ask residents who avoid the courtyard for their reasons as well.

The output is an annotated plan with one barrier to address first and two possible invitations to test. A chair grouping near existing activity might be sensible; a quiet seat farther away might serve another need. He cuts the assumption that everyone wants the same social intensity. He also asks who stores, cleans or repairs furniture. A convivial rendering cannot provide an operating arrangement.

### Compare two walking routes

You bring the errand, actual travel times and the people making the trip. Trace the routes at the hours you would use them. Record crossings, waiting, confusing turns, shelter, opportunities to rest and the final approach to the destination. Do not call the shorter route the easier route until you have considered these experiences.

You get a route comparison with burdens, conveniences and questions needing local review. If a crossing feels difficult, describe the issue precisely for the responsible transport team. He pushes back on a universal best route: a shaded detour may suit one person and be too long for another. This is a decision aid, not a safety certification or a substitute for specialist access assessment.

### Run a public-life observation

Suppose a community group says nobody uses its square. Define what counts as use before counting anything. Choose an entry line for movement and a boundary for stationary activities. Make short sessions at different relevant times and write contextual notes. Record visible activity, such as sitting or playing, without guessing a person's private reason for being there.

The output is a small evidence sheet with counts, activity sketches, session conditions and explicit gaps. It may reveal heavy passing traffic with little staying, or a quiet place valued by a small number of regular users. He cuts inflated claims from the sample. Avoid photographing identifiable people without a proper basis and permission where needed; anonymous notes often answer the question.

### Review a waiting area

This is a transfer from public space to a workplace, clinic reception or community centre. Bring the arrival instructions and the actual wait sequence. Ask where visitors orient themselves, get help, sit, put belongings and hear their turn. Check whether conversation is possible and whether someone can choose a quieter position.

You get a short brief grouped under protection, comfort and enjoyment, with evidence beside each issue. A striking display may remain on the list, but confusing arrival instructions or lack of usable seating may come first. He pushes back when an attractive room is called welcoming without checking the visit. Any clinical, building or legal requirement still needs its own qualified assessment.

### Choose a home for daily routines

Bring a picture of the week you want, including errands, work, care and rest. At each candidate address, test one ordinary chain of activities. Can you return from the shop, pause somewhere comfortable and reach the transport you need at your actual hour? Include the route to the amenities, not merely their distance on a listing.

The output is a daily-life comparison with observed advantages, effort and unknowns. He challenges a decision based only on floor area or an appealing weekend atmosphere. Keep affordability and your own preferences central. A lively setting is useful only if its noise, cost and travel arrangements fit your circumstances. The exercise helps reveal the choice; it does not prescribe one lifestyle for everyone.

## What he'd push back on

He asks you to revise a brief that begins with a landmark and never states what people can do. He challenges a footfall target with no concern for staying, and a comfort score that conceals who did the rating. He will not let the happiest observation stand in for every season or hour. A test should report a muddy day and a failed arrangement as clearly as a pleasant afternoon.

He also resists making social contact compulsory. Some users want a quiet seat and some need to travel efficiently. Keep choice in the invitation. Include delivery, maintenance and access arrangements when reallocating space, and refer consequential technical questions to the people qualified to resolve them.

## What you'll get back

Expect a simple activity-first brief: the people and visits under consideration, the observed journey, movement and staying evidence, the strongest barrier, two proposed invitations and their operating needs. The final section explains what to observe next and what the present evidence cannot establish. Qualitative ratings remain descriptions, so the result helps you make a grounded local decision without pretending to be a universal ranking.
