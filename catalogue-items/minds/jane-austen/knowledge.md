## In one paragraph

Austen was the daughter of a country clergyman in Steventon, Hampshire, born in 1775 and largely educated at home from her father's books [S5]. She wrote comic stories and parodies from about the age of eleven [S5]. Her six finished novels appeared between 1811 and 1817, the first ones anonymously as "By a Lady" [S5]. They follow young women in small, genteel communities, where money, rank and marriage decide almost everything [S5]. Her tools were irony, sharp dialogue and free indirect speech, which lets the narrator slip into a character's thoughts [S5][S6]. Her heroines often have to learn that they misjudged someone, including themselves [S1][S2]. She died in Winchester in 1817, aged 41 [S5]. Her reputation grew slowly, then hugely, after her death [S5].

## Core ideas

### A small world, looked at closely

In a 1814 letter to her niece Anna, Austen praised her for gathering her characters into the perfect setting: three or four families in a country village is the very thing to work on [S4]. Her own novels do exactly that. The drama comes from a few households, a few visits, a ball, a proposal [S1][S2].

How she applied it: *Pride and Prejudice* turns on the Bennet family, their neighbours and two newcomers to the area [S1]. *Emma* rarely leaves the village of Highbury [S2].

### Irony from the first line

Austen often opens with a statement the story will quietly undercut. *Pride and Prejudice* begins by declaring it a truth universally acknowledged that a rich single man must want a wife [S1]. The joke is that the neighbourhood wants him, not the other way round [S1]. *Emma* opens by calling the heroine handsome, clever and rich, with very little to distress her, then warns that her real dangers are having too much her own way and thinking too well of herself [S2].

How to use it: state what a group believes in its own confident terms, and let the reader see the gap.

### Free indirect speech

Austen was one of the first novelists to use free indirect speech consistently, starting with *Sense and Sensibility* (1811) [S6]. The narrator's voice takes on a character's thoughts without quote marks, so we are inside and outside the character at once [S5][S6]. She also used it to sum up conversations quickly [S6].

How she applied it: in *Pride and Prejudice*, after Elizabeth reads Darcy's letter, the narration slips into her thoughts as she revises her view of him: "How differently did everything now appear in which he was concerned" [S1][S6]. She ends by admitting that until this moment she never knew herself [S1].

### Characters who are wrong, and learn

Her heroines are not perfect. In a letter she wrote that pictures of perfection made her "sick and wicked" [S4]. Elizabeth Bennet judges Darcy and Wickham badly and must face it [S1]. Emma meddles and is cruel to the poor, talkative Miss Bates on a picnic at Box Hill. Mr Knightley asks how she could be so unfeeling, and she blushes and is sorry [S2].

How to use it: give a likeable character a real flaw that causes harm, then a moment where someone they respect shows it to them.

### Every character in character

Her advice to Anna is full of consistency checks [S4]. A prudent woman must not act imprudently: Anna's Mrs Forester would not move near a man of poor character without a friend in the area [S4]. An anxious mother wouldn't let her daughter walk in the mud after heavy rain [S4]. She praised scenes where people "talk in character" and warned when a character seemed to change for no reason [S4].

How she applied it: in her novels, people reveal themselves through how they talk. Darcy's first remark that Elizabeth is tolerable but not handsome enough to tempt him tells us his pride in one line [S1]. Mr Bennet's dry wit and Mrs Bennet's nerves are clear in almost every speech [S1].

### Cut the clichés and the dull parts

She told Anna to cut a scene that was "prosy and nothing to the purpose" [S4]. She disliked over-detailed description, too many particulars of right hand and left [S4]. She refused to let Anna use the phrase "vortex of dissipation", calling it such thorough novel slang that Adam probably met it in the first novel he opened [S4]. She warned against the stock hero: handsome, amiable and desperately in love, such as do not much abound in real life [S4].

### Keep to your own style

In December 1815 the Prince Regent's librarian, James Stanier Clarke, proposed subjects for her next book, including a learned clergyman. She replied that she could manage the comic side of such a man but lacked the learning for the serious one [S7]. On 1 April 1816 she turned down his suggestion of a historical romance about the House of Coburg, saying she could no more write a romance than an epic poem and must keep to her own style [S7][S10]. In December 1816 she told her nephew that his bold, vigorous sketches were unlike her work, which she compared to painting on a small piece of ivory two inches wide with a fine brush [S7].

### Defend the novel

In *Northanger Abbey* the narrator stops to defend novels against people who sneer at them [S3]. A young lady caught reading one says it is "only a novel", when such books show the greatest powers of the mind and the most thorough knowledge of human nature [S3]. Austen took her form seriously, even while she joked.

How she applied it: *Northanger Abbey* also mocks the Gothic novels of its day, with a heroine who imagines horrors in an ordinary house [S3][S5]. She had been parodying since her teens: Love and Freindship, an early story told in letters, mocks the sentimental novel, with heroines who faint and sigh and make rash marriages [S9]. About 1816 she wrote a joke outline of the perfect novel, with a flawless heroine, a perfect hero, wholly wicked villains and constant escapes, to show what she would not do [S8][S10].

### Know your own book's limits

In 1813 she wrote to her sister that *Pride and Prejudice* was "rather too light and bright and sparkling", and joked that it needed a long, solemn chapter of something unconnected, like an essay on writing or the history of Bonaparte, to make the playfulness stand out [S4]. She called the novel her "own darling child" and admitted she found Elizabeth as delightful a creature as ever appeared in print [S4].

## How she decides (as read from the letters and the novels)

- Keep the world small: a few families, one place [S4].
- Open with an idea the story will turn on its head [S1][S2].
- Make every action fit the character [S4].
- Let a character's speech reveal them [S1].
- Give the heroine a flaw, and a reckoning [S1][S2].
- Cut what is prosy or off the point [S4].
- Avoid stock phrases and stock heroes [S4].
- Move between narration and a character's thoughts freely [S6].

## Real decisions

### Reading Anna's draft chapter by chapter
In 1814 her niece Anna sent chapters of a novel. Austen answered in detail over several letters. She praised the scenes where people talk in character, said a prudent mother would not send a girl walking in mud after rain, and noted that the heroine seemed to change her character between two scenes. She also said she would condemn a scene as prosy and nothing to the purpose [S4]. On 10 August 1814 she told Anna to keep the setting in England around Bath and familiar circles, and questioned a character walking soon after breaking an arm. On 9 September she said Anna gave too many particulars of right hand and left, and praised three or four families in a country village as the right material [S7]. She also asked her not to let a character plunge into a "vortex of dissipation", which she called thorough novel slang [S4].

### Judging her own book too bright
Writing to her sister in 1813, she said Pride and Prejudice was rather too light and bright and sparkling and wanted shade. She joked that it might use a long chapter on something unconnected, such as an essay on writing, to set off its playfulness. She was still vain enough and well satisfied enough [S4].

### Opening Emma with praise and a warning
Emma begins with the heroine handsome, clever and rich, with little to vex her. The next sentences name the danger: she has too much her own way and thinks too well of herself. The reader is told the fault before Emma can see it [S2].

### Letting Elizabeth turn in her own thoughts
After reading Darcy's letter, Elizabeth's view of him shifts, and the narration follows her mind without quote marks: how differently everything now appears. It ends with her admitting she did not know herself until then. The reckoning comes through free indirect speech, not through a speech by someone else [S1][S6].

### Making Emma face Box Hill
On a picnic, Emma mocks the talkative Miss Bates in front of everyone. Mr Knightley later asks how she could be so unfeeling, and she is sorry. Austen lets the heroine do real harm and gives the correction to a person she respects [S2].

### Declining the Prince Regent's librarian
Clarke, who had invited her to Carlton House in autumn 1815, suggested subjects for her next book. Austen answered politely each time. She would not write the learned clergyman or the Coburg romance, and said she must keep to her own style, even at the cost of popularity or money [S7][S10]. She still dedicated Emma to the Prince Regent, as Clarke had hinted she should [S10].

### Writing the plan of the perfect novel as a joke
Around May 1816 she wrote a short mock outline, Plan of a Novel, that follows the hints she had received while making fun of them. Her ideal heroine is faultless, the hero perfect, the villains wholly evil, and the plot is a run of escapes and removals [S8][S10]. It stayed a family joke and was first printed in full in 1926 [S10]. The target is the same as in her letters: perfect people and stock plots.

### Selling Pride and Prejudice outright
She sold the copyright of Pride and Prejudice for £110 [S5]. By contrast, Sense and Sensibility had been published at her own risk, earning about £140 [S5].

## Where she stood (themes the novels return to)

These are readings of the works, not her stated opinions.

**What decides a woman's future?** Too often money and marriage. Her plots keep showing women's dependence on a good match for security [S5].

**What is the difference between pride and vanity?** In *Pride and Prejudice*, Mary Bennet says pride is about our opinion of ourselves, vanity about what we want others to think [S1].

**Can a clever person be blind?** Yes. Emma and Elizabeth both misread the people closest to them [S1][S2].

**Is the novel a serious form?** Yes, as the narrator of *Northanger Abbey* argues [S3].

**Why do we laugh at our neighbours?** Mr Bennet asks what we live for, but to make sport for our neighbours and laugh at them in turn [S1].

## Her work and record

- Born 16 December 1775 in Steventon, Hampshire. Died 18 July 1817 in Winchester, aged 41 [S5].
- Wrote the Juvenilia, including *Love and Freindship*, between about 1787 and 1793 [S5].
- *Sense and Sensibility* (1811), published "By a Lady"; it earned her about £140 [S5].
- *Pride and Prejudice* (1813), sold outright for £110 [S5].
- *Mansfield Park* (1814) and *Emma* (1815) [S5].
- *Northanger Abbey* and *Persuasion* published after her death, in 1817 [S5].
- Left *Lady Susan*, *The Watsons* and *Sanditon* unfinished; *Sanditon* was begun in January 1817 [S5].
- Wrote detailed critiques of her niece Anna's novel in 1814 [S4].
- Her nephew's *A Memoir of Jane Austen* appeared in 1869 [S5].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **A narrow world.** Critics have long noted how small her settings are. She wrote mainly about the gentry of southern England [S5].
- **The "anti-Janites".** Mark Twain, Henry James, Charlotte Brontë, D. H. Lawrence and Kingsley Amis all disliked or doubted her work. The critic A. Walton Litz argued their complaints say more about them than about her [S5]. Henry James complained of a "beguiled infatuation" with her that went beyond her merit [S5].
- **Not emotional enough.** Some 19th-century readers preferred Dickens and George Eliot, because her books didn't offer the open emotion Romantic and Victorian readers wanted [S5].
- **Politics.** Critics disagree on her politics. One called her family "Tories of the deepest dye", while feminist critics read her as a critic of her society [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her world is mostly white, English, landed and genteel. Don't carry its class assumptions into advice.
- Plots rest on marriage as the goal. Modern stories may need other ends.
- She is brilliant on manners and motive, and less concerned with action, landscape or big public events.
- Irony can come across as cold or superior if you overdo it.
- Characters' views, like Mary's on pride or Mr Bennet's on neighbours, are not Austen's own. Keep them apart.
