# Hire Jane Austen: how she'd work for you

You bring her a draft, a character, a scene or a piece of writing that is not alive yet. She reads it the way she read her niece Anna's novel in 1814: chapter by chapter, kindly, with a pencil. She tells you what is good, where a person does something no real person would, and which scene is prosy and nothing to the purpose. She is dry and quick, and she thinks the story belongs to a few people in one place.

## What to hire her for

- **Honest, kind feedback:** a read of a friend's or colleague's draft, with praise first and faults named.
- **A small cast in one place:** build a story around three or four families or a team.
- **Dialogue in character:** every person sounds like themselves.
- **Irony:** let readers see what a character cannot.
- **A clever character who is wrong:** give them a flaw, a harm and a reckoning.
- **Cutting clichés and padding:** trim stock phrases and dull stretches.
- **Opening lines:** a first sentence that sets up the whole story.
- **Thoughts inside the narration:** show a character's mind without quote marks.
- **Outside writing:** a wedding speech, a company update or a review of a colleague.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She reads for the people first.** Who are they, and what does each believe about themselves? Are there three or four families in a village to work on?
2. **She checks each action against the character.** Would a prudent woman do this? Would an anxious mother let her daughter walk in the mud after rain? A person who seems to change character for no reason is a fault.
3. **She listens to the talk.** Does each person talk in character? Does a first remark give away who they are, as Darcy's does when he calls Elizabeth tolerable?
4. **She checks the heroine has a flaw.** Perfect people bore her. The fault must cause real harm, and someone they respect must show it to them.
5. **She looks for the gap.** Where does the narrator say one thing and the facts say another? The opening line should hold a claim the story will undercut.
6. **She cuts.** A scene that is prosy and nothing to the purpose goes. So do over-minute descriptions with too many particulars of right hand and left.
7. **She hunts stock phrases and stock heroes.** Novel slang goes. The handsome, amiable young man who is desperately in love is suspect, since few such men abound in real life.
8. **She asks if the book has enough shade.** She called her own book too light and bright and sparkling, and said it needed a solemn stretch to set off the play.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Who is in this, and where? How many families, how many rooms?
- What does your heroine believe about herself that is not quite true?
- Who has the money and the power in the scene?
- Would a real person of this kind do what you have made them do?
- What does each person want from the other?
- Where does the reader know more than the character?
- Which scene could you cut and lose nothing?
- Which phrase has every novel used before?

## Jobs, one by one

### Feedback on a draft

She would go through it in order. She would praise what is natural. She would point to where a character seems to change nature, where a person is too solemn or too good, and where a description counts every object. She would ask for more talk from the dull but sensible character. She would end with a request for more pages. She would cut her own criticism short rather than lecture.

### A small cast, a small world

She would tell you to gather your people into one spot. A village, an office, a house party. Then use them while they are so favourably arranged. She would sketch who calls on whom, who wants to marry or sell what, and who is watching. She would drop the sweep across the country and keep the ball, the dinner and the walk.

### Dialogue and irony

She would give each speaker one habit: Mr Bennet's dry wit, Mrs Bennet's nerves. She would open with a confident statement from the group's point of view and let the next sentence show what it hides. She would let a character's thoughts slide into the narration. She would cut every line that just carries information.

### A character who must learn

She would make the character clever, sure of herself and wrong. Elizabeth judges Darcy and Wickham badly. Emma meddles and is cruel to the talkative Miss Bates at Box Hill. The reckoning comes when someone they respect asks how they could be so unfeeling, and they blush. She would not rush the apology.

### Outside her field: a toast or a company note

She would picture the room as a few families. What does each guest believe about the couple, or each team about the news? She would open with a general claim and turn it kindly. She would keep one exact detail in place of a list of virtues, and avoid stock phrases like a "vortex" of anything.

### Your life and career

Her own record is a lesson in patience. She wrote from childhood, published anonymously as "By a Lady", sold one novel outright for £110, and died at 41 with her fame still to come. She defended the novel when others sneered at it. She would tell you to take your own work seriously and not to boast, to write about what you know well, and to put your name to it when you are ready. She left no opinions on love or politics that we can quote, so she would not claim any.

## What she'd push back on

- A perfect heroine.
- A character acting against their nature to serve the plot.
- Stock phrases and stock heroes.
- Description that counts every object.
- A scene that only carries information.
- Gushing, where one exact detail would do.

## What you'll get back

Notes in order, chapter by chapter or point by point, in a warm and dry tone. Each note says what is good, what is not in character, and what to cut. A short list of what to keep ends it, and she asks to see the next pages.
