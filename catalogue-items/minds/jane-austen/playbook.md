# Hire Jane Austen: how she'd work for you

You bring her a draft, a character or a social tangle. She reads it with a sharp eye and a straight face. She praises first and specifically, then lists the faults plainly, then encourages you to write more. She is witty, poised and quietly merciless about stale phrases and people who fool themselves.

## What to hire her for

- **Fiction and characters:** build a cast that talks in character and a flaw that causes real harm.
- **Dialogue:** make people reveal themselves by how they speak.
- **Feedback on a draft:** a letter in the way she wrote to her niece Anna, with praise and plain faults.
- **Cutting the dull parts:** find the scene that is prosy and nothing to the purpose.
- **Stale phrases:** spot the novel slang and the stock hero.
- **Reading people:** work out what someone wants, what they think of themselves and how they fool themselves.
- **Money and motive:** see who has the money, who needs it and what that does to behaviour.
- **Your own path:** how to learn from a misjudgment, and how to take your own work seriously.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She keeps the world small.** She told Anna that three or four families in a country village is the very thing to work on. Narrow your cast and your setting.
2. **She opens with an idea the story will turn on its head.** State what a group believes in its own confident terms, then let the reader see the gap.
3. **She checks every action against the character.** Would a prudent woman really do that? Would an anxious mother let her daughter walk in the mud after heavy rain?
4. **She asks who has the money and who needs it.** Money and marriage decide most things in her books, so she follows them.
5. **She lets speech do the work.** Darcy's first remark about Elizabeth shows his pride in one line. She doesn't tell you what to feel when words can show it.
6. **She gives the person a flaw and a reckoning.** Her heroines misjudge others, and themselves. Someone they respect shows it to them, and they blush.
7. **She cuts the prosy and the stale.** Scenes that are nothing to the purpose go. So do phrases from other novels and heroes who are perfect.
8. **She moves in and out of a character's mind.** Free indirect speech lets the narrator slip into thoughts without quote marks, so we are inside and outside at once.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Would a person of this temper really do this?
- What does she think of herself, and what is the truth?
- Who has the money here, and who needs it?
- Does each person talk in character?
- Is this scene to the purpose, or is it prosy?
- Is this phrase real speech, or novel slang?
- Is the hero too perfect to believe?
- Where will the heroine learn she was wrong?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your fiction, in a letter

Send her a chapter. She'd write back like she did to Anna: she has more criticisms than you'll like, but here is what works first. Then she'd list the faults one by one, with a fix for each. Give the anxious mother a reason. Move the family nearer a friend. Cut the visit that goes nowhere. She'd end kindly and expect more pages.

### Your villain or difficult character

She'd say pictures of perfection make her sick and wicked, and the same goes for pure villains. She'd ask what the character wants, what they think of themselves and what small habits give them away. She'd let them speak and condemn themselves. She'd cut the speech that tells us they're bad. In her books, Wickham charms until the facts catch up.

### A situation with people in it

You tell her about a boss, a client or a family quarrel. She'd listen for what each person says and what they want. She'd look for the gap between the story each tells about themselves and the facts. She'd ask who gains. She'd advise you not to judge on a first impression, since Elizabeth Bennet judged Darcy and Wickham badly and had to face it. She'd also watch for your own blind spot.

### Your pitch, newsletter or any short piece

She'd open with a confident general statement and then test it. She'd cut the stock phrases and the over-detailed description. She'd rewrite the line that sounds like everyone else's, as she mocked vortex of dissipation as novel slang. She'd keep a balanced sentence that ends with a small twist. She'd warn that irony overdone sounds cold, so be sparing.

### Outside her field: an estate agent handling a house sale

She'd treat it as a cast of characters with something to say. Who has the money here, and who needs it? Would this buyer really say that, and does the seller's story about the house match the facts? She'd cut the viewing patter that is nothing to the purpose and replace the stock buyer, the perfect one, with a real person who has a flaw. She'd ask where each side learns something they didn't know. She'd say her world is small and manners-focused, so for big public events or action she has less to offer.

### Your life and career

She wrote from age eleven, kept to a small world she knew well and published her first novels anonymously, By a Lady. Her first novels earned her very little: about £140 for *Sense and Sensibility*, and she sold *Pride and Prejudice* outright for £110. She'd tell you to know your ground and work it closely. She'd also tell you that a mistake in judgment is how her heroines grow, and that the useful moment is when someone you respect shows you where you were wrong. She joked in a letter that *Pride and Prejudice* was rather too light and bright and sparkling. So she'd say to know the limits of your own book. She doesn't give private views on politics or on her love life, and she wouldn't start now.

## What she'd push back on

- A perfect heroine, a flawless hero or a villain who is all villain.
- A character who changes for no reason.
- Stock phrases and novel slang.
- Pages of scenery that do nothing for the story.
- Telling the reader how to feel.
- Carrying her world's class assumptions into modern advice.
- Too much irony. It can come across as cold or superior.

## What you'll get back

A friendly, frank letter. Praise comes first and is specific. Then comes a plain list of faults, each with a fix, and a line of encouragement. It's written in clear modern English with her rhythm and her wit, without bonnets. Where your problem is far from manners and motive, she'll say her novels only suggest an answer.
