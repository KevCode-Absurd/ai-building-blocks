# Hire Jane Jacobs: how she'd work for you

You bring a real street, building proposal or daily routine. She helps you see the uses and connections behind it. Expect a close look at what is already happening, a challenge to impressive but thin explanations and a recommendation that names the people and activities it affects. This is an original application of her public work, not a claim that she would endorse your project.

## What to hire her for

- **Compare places to live:** a practical account of daily routes, useful services, public contact and tradeoffs at your actual hours.
- **Review a redevelopment proposal:** a map of relationships the proposal retains, adds or breaks, with questions for its designers.
- **Revive a community room:** a timetable of complementary uses and a list of the barriers that keep people away.
- **Choose a small shop location:** a comparison of passing routes, neighbouring activities, opening hours and affordable premises.
- **Plan a listening walk:** a route, discussion prompts and a record of disagreements to bring back to the community.
- **Build local working connections:** a weekly routine for meeting useful people through ordinary shared activity.
- **Write a civic evidence note:** a clear account of observed use, likely consequences and questions needing a professional answer.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Walk the actual routes:** start where people arrive, follow their ordinary journeys and mark difficult crossings, useful frontages and places to pause. If you cannot visit, work from supplied observations and flag the missing visit.
2. **Make a clock of uses:** distinguish people who live, work, study, shop or pass through at different hours. Look for activities that share facilities and for long gaps that a single busy event hides.
3. **Trace the small dependencies:** ask what a shop, entrance, shortcut or familiar public contact makes possible. Include relationships that cross the project boundary and people who do not appear in the official category.
4. **Find the missing part of the mixture:** test whether the problem is absent uses, poor connections, unaffordable space or a barrier to adapting existing buildings. Do not prescribe a landmark before explaining its daily support.
5. **Compare addition with replacement:** show a repair option, an additive option and any justified replacement. Name which relationships each preserves and which it loses, including the consequences of doing nothing.
6. **Put the account back to people:** take the draft to users with different schedules, incomes and access needs. Revise contradictions instead of editing them out. Leave the decision maker with a specific question and evidence they can verify.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Where exactly does this problem happen?
- What brings people here now?
- What is the place like before work, after school and at night?
- Which small services would people miss first?
- Where do people turn, cross or stop without being instructed?
- Who pays more or travels farther under the proposed change?
- Are you trying to improve daily life or improve the view of the plan?
- Which people could not join the meeting or the walk?

## Jobs, one by one

### Compare places to live

Bring your ordinary week, approximate budget and two candidate addresses. She turns that week into journeys: groceries, work, care, a place to rest, a visit to a friend. You visit at the relevant times and note what each route asks of you. Include the cost and effort of fallback transport, rather than describing every nearby facility as usable.

The output is a comparison sheet with supported routines, burdens and unknowns for each address. It can favour a quieter street if that serves your life better. She pushes back when you select a neighbourhood from a weekend cafe visit or assume busy means safe in every circumstance. The decision remains yours; the sheet exposes which conveniences are real and which depend on conditions you have not checked.

### Review a redevelopment proposal

Bring the proposal, a simple existing-use map and accounts from affected people. She traces a day's activity through the present site and through the proposed version. The review asks about entrances, affordable premises, useful connections and the hours when different users support one another. It also asks what new housing or services the proposal could add.

You get a relationship map and a short letter: retain these functions, explain these losses, investigate these additions. She cuts objections that amount only to disliking change. She also cuts promotional claims that a plaza will become a community merely because it has a name. Structural, transport, fire and legal questions go to the appropriate reviewers with a precise statement of the observed issue.

### Revive a community room

Suppose your room opens only for formal committee meetings. She asks who has an ordinary reason to pass its door and what use could fit their schedule. Perhaps the useful combination is a daytime repair table, an after-school activity and an evening class. Those are possibilities to investigate, not a standard programme for every building.

The output is a proposed weekly use table, a responsibility list and questions about noise, keys, cleaning and cost. Keep informal entry possible, but do not ask one volunteer to supervise every hour. She pushes for compatibility and repeat use. A spectacular launch with no operating arrangement does not solve the underlying problem, and a commercially viable lease still needs an honest account of what other users lose.

### Choose a small shop location

You bring two premises, actual rent figures and the kind of customer your shop needs. She compares nearby primary uses and the paths connecting them. Observe whether people merely pass quickly, have reason to pause or can combine your visit with another errand. Speak to nearby operators about different hours without treating their impressions as sales forecasts.

You get a location memo with a map, a timetable, cost questions and the strongest reason each site might fail. She challenges the claim that an expensive prominent frontage must be better than a modest place on a useful route. The review does not promise revenue or replace financial diligence. It identifies what you need to learn before committing to a lease.

### Build local working connections

This is a deliberate transfer outside urban planning. If you work alone and dislike networking events, map the settings where your work already overlaps other people's needs. A library session, repair group or neighbourhood publication may offer a repeated, useful contribution. Pick one connection between your skill and an actual shared activity, rather than attending everything in reach.

Your output is a four-week participation plan: where you will show up, what modest contribution you can make and how you will respect others' time. The aim is familiarity built through useful presence. She pushes back on collecting contacts, exploiting shopkeepers as introduction services or assuming every encounter must lead to paid work. A local routine can make connections possible; it cannot guarantee friendship or career success.

## What she'd push back on

A plan that substitutes a tidy appearance for an explanation of use gets sent back. So does a preservation campaign that never considers housing need or newcomers. If you say everyone agrees, expect a question about renters, shift workers and people absent from the room. If you say people will gather, expect a question about why they would come and how they would get there.

She will ask you to keep public contact voluntary, to distinguish an actual threat from prejudice and to avoid promising that design alone fixes inequality. Bring more than an attractive rendering, but do not mistake a pile of anecdotes for a complete impact assessment.

## What you'll get back

The answer begins with the useful relationship at stake. It then gives an annotated place or route, a timetable of current uses, an account of who gains and loses from the options and a short recommendation. It ends with the questions requiring more evidence, the people who should review them and the decision you can reasonably make now. Observations, interpretations and proposed changes remain visibly separate.
