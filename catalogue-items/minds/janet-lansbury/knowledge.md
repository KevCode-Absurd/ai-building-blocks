## Core ideas

### Observe before directing

Lansbury describes learning to observe children rather than constantly doing for them. [S1]

Original everyday application: Watch a child's attempt with blocks before showing the right way. A pause can reveal an intention you would otherwise miss. Supervision and a safe setting remain the adult's job. Observation is active attention, not indifference, and a child can still ask for help that you provide without taking over the entire task.

### Let the child lead play

Her transcripts describe children inventing and developing their own activities. [S2]

Original everyday application: Sit nearby and let the child choose what to explore instead of proposing a game at each pause. Follow interest without demanding uninterrupted absorption. Children vary in how much proximity and response they need. The useful habit is respecting a developing activity, not meeting a target number of minutes of supposedly perfect independent play.

### Boundaries can support autonomy

Her boundaries episode describes constant adult performance crowding out a child's activity. [S1]

Original everyday application: When asked for endless drawings, say you are finished and leave paper available. Be honest about the limit rather than pretending you cannot draw. Acknowledge disappointment and allow the child to decide what to do next within a safe setup. The adult's rest matters alongside the child's opportunity to create.

### Independent does not mean abandoned

Her 2024 transcript challenges equating independent play with simply leaving a child alone. [S2]

Original everyday application: Provide a safe area and responsive presence suitable for the child's age. The intended distinction is who directs the play, not whether attachment needs disappear. Remain available and attend to practical needs. If a child struggles with separation, respond to that situation rather than treating a play principle as a command to ignore distress.

### Allow feelings about a limit

Her episodes address parental discomfort with maintaining boundaries. [S1]

Original everyday application: Acknowledge wanting another game while keeping your real limit about starting dinner. Do not change the limit solely to obtain a calm reaction, but also check that it is reasonable and safe. A child's disappointment need not become an emergency. You can be sympathetic without entering a fresh negotiation after every protest.

### Treat care as relational time

Her autobiographical account describes full attention during feeding, dressing and other care. [S2]

Original everyday application: Explain what you are about to do and invite participation while dressing. Cooperation develops through ordinary care as well as formal play. Slow down where practical and tell the child what is happening. Time pressure can be real; a brief respectful explanation is still useful when a leisurely routine is unavailable.

### Respect adult capacity

Her play discussions include boundaries around the attention parents can give. [S1]

Original everyday application: Offer a short period of focused connection and a realistic break instead of constant entertainment. A sustainable routine includes both the child's needs and the caregiver's life. Plan the setup, then observe how it works. Avoid turning another parent's capacity or a published success story into a standard that every household must reproduce.

## An everyday worked application

In this original play example, a child demands that the parent draw one more picture again and again. The parent is tired and needs to make dinner. State that drawing is finished for now, acknowledge the child's wish and leave safe materials available. Stay responsive without adding fresh entertainment to prevent every protest. Observe whether the child finds an activity or needs a practical form of support. The experiment is respecting agency and adult capacity, not achieving a set duration of solitary play or withholding connection.

Before trying the example, define what a useful result would look like for the people involved. Separate a change in your own behavior from an outcome requiring someone else's participation. An invitation can be declined; a boundary can be maintained without agreement; a practical obligation may need a direct decision. These are different tasks, so do not judge all of them by whether the conversation feels pleasant immediately.

## Where the method was criticised or needs caution

The sources above primarily present the person's own account and institutional record. They should not be mistaken for independent proof that every application works. The cautions here are editorial limits on this educational adaptation, rather than invented reports of named critics or a claim of scientific consensus.

Independent play varies with child, environment and available support. Do not convert a success story into a timer or use a boundary to ignore distress and practical needs. Respectful parenting includes adult judgment and supervision. It does not require a naturally calm parent or a household able to offer unlimited space and time.
