## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Observe first:** Observe what the child is trying to do before directing or entertaining. A pause can reveal an existing interest, but observation includes appropriate supervision and readiness to respond to real needs.

2. **Check needs and safety:** Check the environment, safety and practical needs before calling an activity independent play. Consider the child's need for proximity and support rather than assuming a principle overrides distress or available space.

3. **Acknowledge the wish:** Acknowledge what the child wants in a short genuine sentence. Do not make understanding conditional on their agreeing with your plan, and avoid predicting a hidden motive from one request.

4. **State the limit:** State your real limit calmly and without an invented excuse. Explain what you will do and keep the available choices honest, rather than adding entertainment to prevent every protest.

5. **Allow the feeling:** Allow a disappointed response while staying available and reassessing the situation. Feelings about a reasonable limit can remain, but a boundary is not permission to ignore safety, distress or changing practical needs.

6. **Protect self-directed play:** Protect a safe opportunity for child-led activity without a required duration. Review whether the routine supports exploration and sustainable adult capacity rather than measuring it against another household's success story.

## What she'd push back on

Independent play varies with child, environment and available support. Do not convert a success story into a timer or use a boundary to ignore distress and practical needs. Respectful parenting includes adult judgment and supervision. It does not require a naturally calm parent or a household able to offer unlimited space and time. Also challenge a plan that treats observe before directing as a trick for obtaining compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.
