# Jason Fried: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Jason Fried is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his company's free book Getting Real, the company's book pages, a 2013 interview, his blog posts from 2021 to 2025, and a public reference page. Getting Real and the other books were published under the 37signals or Basecamp name, so treat their ideas as the company's views, which Fried co-founded and led [S1][S3]. Date each view to the source it comes from, as of 2013, 2021 or 2025. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about later events. His exact birth year isn't in our sources. He was at university from 1992 to 1996, so it is roughly 1974 [S2].

## In one paragraph

Fried co-founded 37signals in Chicago in 1999 as a web design firm [S1][S2]. The team built a tool to manage their own client projects. It became Basecamp, launched in 2004, and by 2005 the company had moved from design work to making its own software [S1][S2]. Over the next two decades he argued, in books and posts, for a different way to run a company: build less, fund it yourself, fix the deadline and cut the scope, hold few meetings, keep the team small and the hours sane [S3][S5][S7][S8][S9]. He put culture ahead of growth and preferred steady gains to big bets [S2]. In 2021 he made a set of changes at the company, including a ban on political talk on the work account, that led about a third of staff to leave [S1][S10]. In recent posts he says business is learned by doing it, and that owners of independent companies should use their freedom to back odd ideas [S12][S13].

## Core ideas

### Build half a product, not a half-baked one

Getting Real argues that trying to include every good idea gives you a weak product [S4]. Take your plan, cut it in half, and keep cutting until only the essentials remain. The ideas you cut aren't thrown away. They wait for later [S4].

How he applied it: Basecamp's team launched with the messages section first, because they saw it as the heart of the app. They left out milestones, to-do lists and other features at first, then decided what to add from how people really used it [S4].

### Fix time and budget, flex the scope

When a project won't fit its schedule or budget, cut features. Don't push back the date or add money [S5]. A fixed deadline forces hard choices about what matters. Trying to fix time, money and scope all at once usually lowers quality. Keeping scope loose lets the team change course as it learns [S5]. A smaller, polished launch beats a bigger, mediocre one [S5].

How he applied it: the rule sits in the opening part of Getting Real, next to "Build Less" and "Fund Yourself", as one of the starting rules for any project [S3].

### Start with no

Every feature costs more than it looks. It has to be designed, built and tested, and once customers rely on it, it's hard to remove [S6]. So the default answer to a request, from a customer or the team, is "not now." A feature has to keep coming back before it gets serious thought [S6]. When users push, remind them they chose the product because it is focused [S6].

How he applied it: the chapter retells how Steve Jobs, at a demo of the iTunes Music Store, turned down a long list of requested features and kept only the crucial ones [S6]. The same part of the book includes chapters titled "Forget Feature Requests" and "Hold the Mayo" [S3].

### Fund yourself

Outside money should be the fallback [S7]. Investors want their money back, often fast, and that pressure can push a company toward selling out instead of building a good product. Software is cheap to start, so ask what you truly need: a smaller team, less money, a shorter timeline, or a side project [S7]. Tight limits force sharper choices early. A quick launch shows within a month or two if an idea has legs [S7].

How he applied it: the chapter compares two companies started by the same founder. The funded one had high expectations and pressure to sell. The self-funded one grew slower but built a better product and stayed independent [S7]. In 2006 Jeff Bezos did buy a minority stake in 37signals, so the company was not free of outside owners [S1].

### Calm is a choice

The book It Doesn't Have to Be Crazy at Work argues that chaos shouldn't be the normal state at work [S9]. Anxiety isn't needed for progress, and a day full of meetings isn't needed for success. The book calls these the side effects of broken models and false "best practices" [S9]. In 2013 Fried said staff worked no more than 40 hours a week, and that overwork was one of his main worries [S2].

How he applied it: he grew the company slowly. After 14 years it had 36 people, the most it had ever had [S2]. He said he kept it small by doing one or two things at a time, and hired slowly because each hire is costly and needs real work to do [S2].

### Meetings are a last resort

Getting Real calls meetings toxic to getting work done [S8]. They break the day into pieces, talk about ideas instead of real work, share little per minute and drift off topic. A meeting is often a sign the idea isn't clear yet. Simplify it and talk it through in writing or chat [S8]. If you must meet, keep it under 30 minutes, invite as few people as possible and have a clear agenda [S8].

How he applied it: the same part of the book has a chapter called "Alone Time", about protecting long stretches of quiet work [S3].

### Decide with what you know now

In 2025 Fried argued against working from a long backlog or a ranked list [S11]. A list records what someone believed earlier. Deciding in the moment uses newer information. Past decisions carry no special weight, and every choice is still a yes or a no [S11].

How he applied it: he wrote the post to answer people who asked how his team chooses what to do next without a backlog. His answer was to decide as you go [S11]. In 2021 he made a related change at Basecamp: make a decision, explain it once and move on, instead of going over old choices again [S10].

### You learn business by doing it

In 2025 Fried wrote that books and threads can inform you, but they can't teach you to hire, build a product or run a company [S12]. You get better at hiring by hiring. He compared it to playing guitar: reading a hundred books won't make you play well. He called business a form of muscle memory [S12].

How he applied it: in 2013 he told the same kind of story about himself. He sold software on the side in college, learned that people would pay for something useful, and decided to work for himself [S2].

### Independence is a duty

In 2025 Fried argued that owning an independent company gives you room to try things others would never allow [S13]. Owners can back strange ideas from their team and protect odd experiments even when success looks unlikely. Big public companies can't do this, which gives small owners an edge [S13]. Skipping that chance means missing one of the best parts of running a company [S13].

How he applied it: 37signals has kept trying new products and models. It launched HEY email in 2020, the ONCE line of software you buy once and run yourself in 2024, and Fizzy in 2025 [S1].

## How he decides

- Cut the plan in half, then in half again [S4].
- Keep the date and the budget. Cut scope to fit [S5].
- Say "not now" to new features until they keep coming back [S6].
- Pay your own way first. Treat outside money as plan B [S7].
- Write it down instead of calling a meeting. If you must meet, 30 minutes and an agenda [S8].
- Put culture ahead of growth, revenue and reach [S2].
- Prefer steady growth to big risks. Don't put too much on the table at once [S2].
- Decide with what you know today, explain it once, move on [S10][S11].
- Give customers more value than they pay for [S2].

## Where he stood

**Should a young company take venture money?** Usually no. Investor pressure can push a company toward a quick exit and a worse product. Start small and cheap, and use outside money only as a fallback [S7].

**Is growth the goal?** No. In 2013 he said he puts culture ahead of more products, more revenue and more influence, and prefers steady growth [S2].

**Do great results need long hours?** No. He argued that anxiety and constant busyness aren't required for progress, and in 2013 his company capped the week at 40 hours [S2][S9].

**How should a team plan?** Lightly. Fix the time and cut the scope, and decide what's next from current knowledge instead of an old backlog [S5][S11].

**Should a software company take public stands?** As of 2021, he said the company makes software and is not a social impact company, and moved political and social talk off the work account [S10].

**Can you learn business from books?** Only a little. As of 2025, he said you learn it by doing it [S12].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The 2021 changes.** After staff objected to an internal list of "funny" customer names, the company banned political and social talk on its work forums. It offered severance to those who disagreed, and about a third of the company left [S1]. Fried's own post said these talks had become divisive and draining [S10]. The reference page lists the episode under criticism and controversy [S1]. Losing a third of the team is a large cost for a company that prized its culture [S2].
- **Cutting benefits.** The same 2021 post ended fitness, wellness and education allowances as "paternalistic". Staff got the cash value for that year, plus a new profit-sharing plan [S10]. Some will see this as respect for adult choices, others as a loss.
- **Fights with big platforms.** He called Google's ad policy a "shakedown" in 2019 and took on Apple in 2020 [S1]. Supporters saw a small company standing up. Others saw a firm using public pressure to get its way.
- **Products that didn't last.** Some products were closed or folded in. Highrise, a contact manager, was spun off, brought back and closed to new sign-ups in 2018 [S1].
- **Fund yourself, with a famous investor.** The company preached self-funding but took a minority investment from Bezos in 2006 [S1][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- His advice comes from a profitable software company with low costs. A business with stock, factories or high startup costs may need outside money to begin [S7].
- "Start with no" protects focus, but it can also miss a real need that customers keep asking for in different words [S6].
- Avoiding meetings works when people write well. A team that doesn't may need more live talk than he suggests [S8].
- Deciding with only current knowledge can drop good ideas recorded earlier [S11].
- He writes as an owner with full control. Employees and managers inside bigger companies may not have the freedom he assumes [S13].
- Pair him with someone who thinks about scale, fundraising or big public companies.
