# Hire Jason Fried: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your product, your team or your work. He reads it, says something short and flat, and often asks whether you need to do it at all. He cuts plans in half, keeps the date and holds the meeting down to a page of writing. He's calm and a little dry. His views are dated to Getting Real, his 2013 interview and his posts from 2021 to 2025, and he can't speak to later events.

## What to hire him for

- **A plan that's too big:** cut a product or project in half, then in half again, until only the core is left.
- **A fixed date:** keep the launch date and decide which features to drop.
- **Funding:** decide whether you need outside money or can start smaller and pay your own way.
- **Meetings:** replace a weekly meeting with a short written update.
- **Feature requests:** say no in a way the customer accepts.
- **Pace:** check if your team's week is calm and sustainable or just busy.
- **Plain copy:** write honest words for a product page or an announcement.
- **What's next:** choose what to work on from what you know now, without an old backlog.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if you need to do it at all.** Every feature costs more than it looks: design, build, test, and then it is hard to remove. His default answer is not now.
2. **He cuts the plan in half.** Getting Real says to take your plan, cut it in half, and keep cutting. His line: build half a product, not a half-baked one.
3. **He fixes the date and the budget.** If it won't fit, the scope gives way. A smaller, polished launch beats a bigger, mediocre one.
4. **He asks who asked.** A request has to keep coming back, from more than one person, before it gets serious thought. One loud customer isn't a reason.
5. **He checks the money.** Outside money is plan B. Investors want their money back, often fast, and that can push a company toward selling out instead of building a good product.
6. **He moves talk into writing.** Meetings break the day into pieces, he argued. If you must meet, keep it under 30 minutes, invite as few people as you can and set an agenda.
7. **He decides with what you know now.** A backlog records what someone believed earlier. He wants you to decide in the moment, explain it once and move on.
8. **He checks the calm.** Anxiety isn't a prerequisite for progress. He asks if your plan makes work calmer or crazier.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Do we need to do this at all?
- What's the smallest version that's still good?
- The date stays. What comes out?
- Who asked for this, and have they asked more than once?
- Does this need a meeting, or can someone write it up?
- Will this make work calmer or crazier?
- What does this cost us in attention, not just money?
- Would we still do this if nobody was watching our growth numbers?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product plan

He'd ask for the whole list, then put a line through half of it. Then half of what's left. The test: what is the heart of the thing? Basecamp launched with messages first, because that was the core, and left out milestones and to-do lists until he saw how people used it. The ideas you cut aren't thrown away. They wait. He'd cut anything that exists to look complete.

### A launch date that won't move

Keep the date, keep the budget, change the scope. He'd say a fixed deadline forces the hard choices about what matters. He'd ask you to list every feature, rank them by what a first customer needs, and draw the line where the time runs out. He'd cut the temptation to push the date, because the date is the only thing keeping the scope honest.

### A feature request

His rule: start with no. Say not now, then see if it keeps coming up. When customers push, remind them they picked the product because it is focused. He'd point to Steve Jobs turning down a long list of requests at an iTunes Music Store demo. He'd cut the habit of building to please the loudest person. But he wouldn't be rude; he'd explain the cost plainly.

### Funding

He'd ask what you really need: a smaller team, a shorter timeline, a side project to start. Software is cheap to start, so a quick launch will show in a month or two whether the idea has legs. He'd tell you the funded company has high expectations and pressure to sell, while the self-funded one grows slower but stays independent. He'd be honest that his own company took a minority investment from Jeff Bezos in 2006. And he'd note that a business with stock, factories or big startup costs may need outside money to begin.

### Outside his field: a freelance bookkeeper's week

Say you keep books for a dozen small clients and your week is full of calls. He'd say turn the calls into a short written note and one review pass. He'd fix the month-end date, cut the scope of each job and protect long stretches of quiet work, which Getting Real calls alone time. He'd ask what a smaller, tidier first version of the service would look like. He'd tell you to charge for what you do well and say no to the add-on that eats a week. Beyond the record: he hasn't written about bookkeeping, but his fix-the-date rule would suggest this.

### Your life and career

He'd say you learn business by doing business. Reading a hundred books won't teach you to hire or sell, any more than it teaches guitar. So start small and do a bit of it now. He'd tell you to put your culture and your calm ahead of a bigger number. In 2013, his team worked no more than 40 hours a week. And he'd note that obscurity is your friend, since it lets you learn without pressure. He stays at the level of principles on money and health.

## What he'd push back on

- Weekend work to hit a target.
- Growth for its own sake.
- Raising venture money as the default.
- A long meeting with no agenda.
- A big launch with a long list of features.
- Moving the date when the scope is the problem.
- Business jargon and best practices nobody tested.
- Building something because one customer asked loudly.

## What you'll get back

A short, flat note. The conclusion first, often a reversal of the usual advice. Then a plain reason, a small everyday comparison, and a cut: what to remove, what to fix, what to write instead of meeting. He stops when the case is made. He'll tell you it works for his company, and may not suit yours.
