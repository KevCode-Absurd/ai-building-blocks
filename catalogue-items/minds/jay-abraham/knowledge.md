# Jay Abraham: what he knew and believed

Jay Abraham is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from two podcast interviews with published transcripts, his own website and guides page, a reference page and two people who summarise his ideas [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6][S7]. Neither interview shows a date, so we can't date those views precisely. We read them as his views in recent years, up to 2026. We could not read his books in full. Many of the results he cites come from his own company and are not independently checked. Nothing here covers his private life or plans.

## In one paragraph

Abraham is a marketing consultant from Indianapolis who made his name in direct response marketing in the 1970s, then founded The Abraham Group [S1]. *Forbes* listed him in 2000 as one of the top five executive coaches in the US [S1]. His best-known idea is that there are only three ways to grow a business: more buyers, bigger sales and more frequent buying [S2][S4][S7]. Small gains in each lever multiply together [S4]. He also teaches risk reversal, where the seller takes on the buyer's risk, and the strategy of preeminence, where you act as the buyer's most trusted advisor [S2][S3][S6]. His deeper claim is that most businesses are sitting on assets they don't use, like old leads, past customers and possible partners [S2][S4]. He learned by working across many industries and borrowing what worked in one to use in another [S3].

## Core ideas

### Three ways to grow

Abraham says a business can grow in only three ways. Increase the number of buyers, increase the size of each sale, and increase how often they buy [S4][S7]. He adds a fourth, "utility value," meaning other fair ways to earn from a buyer or prospect relationship [S4]. Most owners chase new customers, when the other two levers are often cheaper to pull [S6].

How he applied it: he described how a 10% gain in each of three levers produces about 33% growth, because the gains multiply [S4]. He said Tony Robbins built part of his business teaching on this idea [S4].

### Growth is geometric

Abraham argues that many small gains beat waiting for one big breakthrough [S4]. Improving lots of activities by 10% to 15% each compounds, because the effects multiply instead of add [S4]. You don't have to double sales to multiply profit [S4].

How he applied it: he contrasted this with chasing ten times more sales, which often needs more cash, staff and stock, and can shrink margins. He said many small improvements can multiply the bottom line far more safely [S4].

### Audit every way money comes in

He tells owners to list every activity that brings in revenue, formal or informal, and ask whether each one reaches the right market with the strongest message [S4]. Then ask how leads become buyers, how buyers come back, what else they could buy, and whether non-buyers can still be served for profit [S4]. He calls his system Revenue System Optimization and keeps a list of around 40 overlooked assets, such as follow-up, leads and sales channels [S4].

How he applied it: he points out that sales teams vary. Finding what the best people do differently and teaching it to the rest can lift results by 10% to 20% [S4].

### Know what a customer is worth

Abraham wants owners to know the lifetime value of a buyer by type and by source [S4]. From that you can set an "allowable acquisition cost," how much you can spend to win a customer and still profit [S4]. He said that when he started, almost nobody understood lifetime value [S3].

How he applied it: he argues that money spent on ads is lost unless you recover it by converting leads, selling more to buyers, and finding value in people who didn't buy [S4].

### Risk reversal

Most buyers hesitate because they carry the risk. Abraham teaches that the seller should take on that risk with strong guarantees, trials or pay-for-results deals [S2][S6]. He calls this lowering the "hurdle rate" for starting a relationship [S4]. His site calls risk reversal the most powerful conversion tool in business [S2][S5].

How he applied it: he has taken part of his consulting fee as a share of the extra profit he helps create, rather than only a flat fee [S2][S6].

### The strategy of preeminence

Preeminence means being seen as the only real choice in your category and the buyer's most trusted advisor [S3]. It starts with a mission focused on the people you serve, not on making money [S4]. He tells owners not to fall in love with their company or product, but with the people they serve, and to picture the product at work in the buyer's life [S4]. He says it needs an outward focus and a clear point of view, explained with metaphors buyers can grasp [S3].

How he applied it: he describes preeminence as advising buyers on what they really need, even when that is not what you sell [S6].

### Referrals should be a system

Word of mouth is good, he says, because it shows buyers like you. But it is erratic, coming one month and not the next [S4]. Referred buyers buy sooner, haggle less, buy more and stay longer, so it pays to generate referrals on purpose [S4].

How he applied it: he teaches host-beneficiary partnerships, where a business that already serves your buyers introduces you to them, often for a share of the revenue [S5][S6].

### Borrow from other industries

Abraham built his method by working in many industries and noticing that what was normal in one was unknown in another [S3]. He calls himself a synthesiser, someone who combines ideas from many fields into something new, which he separates from copying [S3]. He tells marketers to study outside their own field [S3].

How he applied it: he learned direct response basics from a mentor, Dan Rosenthal, then studied old copywriters like Claude Hopkins, Victor Schwab and Robert Collier [S3].

### Concept beats copy

As a former copywriter, he says a strong concept can carry mediocre copy, while great copy can't save a weak concept [S3]. A breakthrough can come from a new way of delivering, a new offer or a new analogy [S3]. He doesn't think free offers carry much weight now, and prefers to offer valuable ideas or experiences [S3].

How he applied it: he researches buyers' language by reading the headlines, chapter titles and one-star and five-star reviews of the top books in a field [S3].

## How he decides

- Pull all three levers: buyers, sale size, frequency [S4].
- Aim for many 10% gains, not one miracle [S4].
- Audit every source of revenue and every unused asset [S4].
- Know lifetime value and the most you can pay for a customer [S4].
- Take the risk off the buyer [S2][S4].
- Serve as the buyer's trusted advisor [S3][S4].
- Make referrals a system, not luck [S4].
- Test small, model best and worst cases, then commit [S4].

## Real decisions

### Learning from a mentor and the old masters

Abraham learned direct response basics from a mentor, Dan Rosenthal [S3]. He then studied older copywriters, among them Claude Hopkins, Victor Schwab and Robert Collier [S3]. His reason was that proven methods from other eras still work when moved to a new field [S3].

### Becoming a synthesiser

He noticed that what was normal in one industry was unknown in another [S3]. He chose to combine ideas from many fields into something new, which he separates from copying [S3]. He tells marketers to study outside their own field [S3].

### Taking a share of the extra profit

Instead of only a flat fee, Abraham has taken part of his consulting pay as a share of the extra profit he helps create [S2][S6]. His reason is that buyers hesitate because they carry the risk [S2][S6].

### Researching the buyer's own words

To learn what buyers want, he reads the headlines, chapter titles and one-star and five-star reviews of the top books in a field [S3]. He looks for strong feeling, because it shows what people want and fear [S3]. He says this feeds the concept, which he thinks matters more than the copy [S3].

### Stumbling into seminars

He says he entered the seminar business by chance, when an event he expected to draw 15 people drew 350 [S3]. This is his own account, and the numbers are not independently checked.

### Preferring value to free offers

Abraham says free offers carry less weight now [S3]. He prefers to offer valuable ideas or experiences [S3]. He holds that a great concept can carry weaker copy [S3].

## Where he stood

**Is more advertising the answer to growth?** Usually not first. He looks for unused assets and the other two levers before more spend [S4][S6].

**Should you give things away free?** Less and less. He thinks free has lost value [S3].

**What matters more, concept or copy?** Concept [S3].

**Is debt bad for growth?** Not if the model produces enough extra profit to pay it back. He calls financing a key lever for growth [S4].

**Who is the copywriter's real client?** The audience, not the company paying the bill [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

We found no detailed independent critique of his work in the sources we read. Some fair limits:

- **Unchecked results.** His site claims more than $75 billion in client profits and lists big case results without naming clients [S2]. These are self-reported.
- **Loose maths.** He said doubling all three levers gives 800% growth [S4]. Doubling three factors gives eight times the revenue, which is a 700% rise. The idea holds, but check his numbers.
- **High price.** His paid programmes start at $25,000 and run past $350,000 a year, as of 2026 [S2]. Most small firms will use his ideas, not his services.
- **Hard to date.** His interviews and site pages are often undated [S3][S4]. Treat his figures with care.

## Blind spots to watch

- His levers assume you already have a working product and buyers. A brand-new business may need product work first.
- Small gains everywhere can spread a small team thin. Pick the two or three that matter most.
- Upsells and "utility value" can tip into squeezing customers. Keep his word "ethically" in front.
- He gives little on brand or creative ideas. Pair him with George Lois or Donald Miller.
- Many of his examples come from his own clients, with names hidden.
