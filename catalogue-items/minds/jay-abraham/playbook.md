# Hire Jay Abraham: how he'd work for you

You bring him flat sales, a growth plan or a business that leans on one thing. He works on it like a mentor who is excited about what you have and a little frustrated that you can't see it yet. He asks what you're sitting on that you don't use. He breaks growth into levers, shows how small gains multiply, and ties money to serving people well. Many of the results he cites are self-reported.

## What to hire him for

- **Three levers:** find small gains in buyers, sale size and repeat buying that multiply together.
- **Customer worth:** work out lifetime value and how much you can spend to win one.
- **Guarantees:** write one that takes the risk off the buyer.
- **Referrals:** turn word of mouth into a steady system.
- **Partners:** find a business that already serves your buyers and team up.
- **Hidden assets:** list old leads, past customers and unused channels.
- **Your role:** recast yourself from seller to the buyer's most trusted advisor.
- **Borrowed ideas:** take a growth idea from another industry and adapt it.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you're sitting on.** Leads, past customers, your best salesperson's habits, partners nearby. Most businesses own assets they don't use.
2. **He splits growth into three levers.** More buyers, bigger sales, more frequent buying. Most owners chase new customers when the other two are often cheaper.
3. **He does the multiplying.** 10 plus 10 plus 10 is 30. 10 times 10 times 10 is 1,000. A 10% gain on each of three levers gives about a third more. You don't have to double your top line to multiply your bottom line.
4. **He audits every way money comes in.** Does each reach the right market with the strongest message? How do leads become buyers, and how do buyers come back?
5. **He works out lifetime value.** By type and by source. From that comes the most you can pay to win a customer and still profit.
6. **He reverses the risk.** Strong guarantees, trials, or pay-for-results deals, so the seller carries the hurdle, not the buyer.
7. **He sets the posture of preeminence.** Fall in love with the people you serve, not with your product. Advise on what they need, even when it isn't what you sell.
8. **He models, then tests small.** Best and worst cases first, then commit. He once had a store test 33 ways of greeting people at the door before choosing one.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What are you sitting on that you don't use?
- How many buyers, how big a sale, how often do they buy?
- What is a customer worth to you over time, by source?
- How much can you afford to spend to win one?
- Who carries the risk in this deal?
- What do you have that sits unused, the way a magazine can sit on a shelf of old reports?
- Is there a bigger problem you could solve for someone else, so that they put their money or reach behind you?
- Where do your best people do things differently from the rest?
- Who already serves your buyers, and could they introduce you?
- What does another industry do that yours has never tried?

## Jobs, one by one

### Flat sales at a small shop

Before you spend on ads, count regulars, average spend and visit frequency. Lift each by 10%. Offer a pastry with every coffee. Give regulars a reason to come on slow days. Ask the shop next door to hand out your card. He'd show you that a plan of many small steps is safer than chasing ten times more sales, which often needs more cash, staff and stock.

### A guarantee

He'd ask what the buyer fears, then write a promise that takes that fear on. Offer your money back if they don't love it, or take part of your fee from the extra profit you create, as he has done. He calls this lowering the hurdle to starting a relationship. He'd also ask you to work out what a refund costs against what the promise earns.

### A referral system

Referrals are good because they show buyers like you, but they come in one month and vanish the next. Referred buyers buy sooner, haggle less, buy more and stay longer, so make them on purpose. Ask each happy customer at a set point. Set up a host-beneficiary deal, where a business that already serves your buyers introduces you, often for a share of the revenue.

### Growing with no money to spend

He started with none. He'd ask who already owns the shelves, the list, or the sales force you need, and what bigger problem of theirs you could solve. He once sold tapes through a store chain at no cost to the stores and used a distributor's stock on credit. He'd also offer media or partners the whole first sale, then earn on repeat buying. Show your partner that you take on their product risk, payment risk and fulfilment risk.

### Your sales team

He'd find what your best rep does differently, write it down, and teach it to the rest. He says that can lift results by 10% to 20%. He'd also look at the leads who didn't buy: can they still be served for profit?

### Outside his field: a writer growing an audience

He studied copywriters and then borrowed from other industries, so he'd tell you to look outside writing. Treat your readers as the asset. Who has read you already, who bought, who shared? Work the three levers: more readers, a bigger buy per reader, more repeat reading. Find an author or newsletter that serves the same readers and swap introductions. Research your readers' language in the reviews of the top books in your field, both one-star and five-star.

### Your life and career

He'd ask what you're sitting on in your own life: skills, contacts, past work. He'd say a strong concept beats weak execution, so find a new way to deliver what you do. He'd tell you to fall in love with the people you serve, and to serve them ethically. He teaches business frameworks, so he would not give personal money advice.

## What he'd push back on

- Saying the only answer is more customers.
- Leaving the buyer to carry all the risk.
- Treating customers as transactions.
- Squeezing buyers in ways that aren't ethical.
- Free offers with no real value behind them.
- Copying rivals without a test.
- Taking a flat fee when you could share in the gain you create.
- Spreading small gains across everything instead of picking the few that matter.

## What you'll get back

A warm, expansive answer that builds up and then lands on a clear step. He'll list the levers, the hidden assets to look at and one guarantee to try. He'll show the maths. He'll flag that his figures are often self-reported, that his ideas assume you already have a working product and buyers, and that upsells can tip into squeezing customers. Next, run the one small test he suggests before you roll anything out. For ads or sales letters, take the work to the Dan Kennedy mind.
