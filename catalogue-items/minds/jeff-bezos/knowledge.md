# Jeff Bezos: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Jeff Bezos is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his letters to Amazon shareholders, written from 1997 to 2021, and from public reference pages. He stepped down as Amazon's CEO in July 2021 [S8]. Treat each view as "as of" the letter it comes from. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after that.

## In one paragraph

Bezos built Amazon around one habit: start with the customer and work back to what you should build [S7]. His first letter to shareholders, in 1997, said the company would judge itself by value created over the long term, would focus hard on customers, and would choose cash flow over flattering accounts [S1]. Over the next two decades he added a small set of tools that people outside Amazon still use: sort decisions into one-way and two-way doors [S2], write the press release before you build [S6], read six-page memos instead of watching slides [S4], and treat failure as the price of invention [S2]. His last letter as CEO said a company, like a living thing, has to keep spending energy to stay different from its surroundings, and that it must create more value than it takes [S5].

## Core ideas

### Obsess over customers, watch competitors

Bezos argued that a company can center itself on competitors, products, technology or customers, and that the customer is the best choice. Customers are never fully satisfied, so they keep pulling the company forward [S3]. In the 2012 letter he said Amazon's energy should come from wanting to impress customers, not from wanting to beat rivals [S1]. Amazon's leadership principles still open with this idea: leaders start with the customer, watch competitors, but don't fixate on them [S7].

How he applied it: Amazon built systems that spot when a customer got a poor experience, such as a video that played badly, and refund them before they complain [S1].

### It's all about the long term

The 1997 letter set out how the company would make choices. It would invest for long-term market leadership rather than short-term profit or Wall Street's reaction. When good-looking accounts and real cash flow pulled in different directions, it would take the cash [S1]. In 2012 he reprinted that letter to show the approach had not changed [S1].

How he applied it: Amazon sold its own devices close to cost and aimed to earn money when people used them, not when they bought them [S1].

### Day 1 versus Day 2

"Day 1" is his name for the energy of a young company that is still experimenting. "Day 2" is the slow decline that follows when a company stops [S3]. He said the defences against Day 2 are customer obsession, a skeptical view of proxies, eager adoption of outside trends, and fast decisions [S3].

How he applied it: he warned in 2016 that a process can become the thing people serve, instead of the outcome it was built to protect. Teams should own the result, not just follow the steps [S3]. The same goes for surveys and metrics. He wanted leaders to understand customers through stories and judgment, and use research to check blind spots [S3].

### One-way doors and two-way doors

In the 2015 letter Bezos split decisions into two types. Type 1 decisions can't be undone, so they deserve slow, careful thought. Type 2 decisions can be reversed, so a small group or one person with good judgment should make them quickly [S2]. He warned that big companies tend to use the slow process for everything, which kills speed and invention [S2].

How he applied it: in 2016 he said most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had. Waiting for 90% usually means you are being slow, and being good at fixing mistakes makes speed cheaper [S3]. He also used "disagree and commit": say you disagree, then back the decision fully. He gave an example of doing this himself, when a team wanted to make an Amazon Studios show he doubted [S3].

### Invention needs failure

Bezos treated failure as part of invention. Experiments fail, and if you know in advance it will work, it isn't an experiment [S2]. Because a few huge wins can pay for many losses, it makes sense to take bold bets. He used baseball to explain it: swing for the fences and you strike out a lot, but in business one hit can score far more than four runs [S2].

How he applied it: he named Amazon Web Services, the third-party marketplace and Prime as bets that paid off [S2]. By the 2015 letter, nearly half of the units sold on Amazon came from outside sellers [S2].

### High standards are learned, and they need realistic scope

In the 2017 letter he wrote that customers keep raising the bar, so yesterday's delight becomes today's normal [S4]. He argued that high standards can be taught, that they spread through a team, and that they belong to a field. Being excellent at one thing doesn't make you excellent at another [S4]. Two parts matter: you must recognize what good looks like, and you must know how long it takes to get there [S4].

How he applied it: he told the story of a friend who hired a coach to learn a perfect handstand. The coach said most people think it takes two weeks, when it really takes about six months of daily practice. Wrong expectations about scope, not lack of taste, are why people give up [S4].

### Narrative memos instead of slides

Amazon meetings started with everyone reading a six-page memo in silence, like a study hall, instead of a slide presentation [S4]. Bezos said a great memo can take a week or more, because it needs drafts, feedback, time away and a fresh edit [S4].

How he applied it: when a memo was weak, he blamed wrong expectations about scope more often than a writer who couldn't tell good from bad [S4].

### Working backwards

The product process at Amazon begins with the customer and works back to the least technology needed. Werner Vogels, Amazon's chief technology officer, described the steps in 2006: write the press release first, then a list of likely questions and answers, then a description of the customer's experience, then the user manual [S6]. Writing the press release forces a team to say plainly why the product should exist [S6]. "Start with the customer and work backwards" also appears in Amazon's first leadership principle [S7].

### Create more than you consume, and stay different

In his final letter as CEO, Bezos argued that a business must create more value than it takes from the people it touches. He put rough numbers on it: about $301 billion created in 2020, spread across shareholders, employees, outside sellers and customers [S5]. He also added a new goal for Amazon: to be the best employer and safest place to work on Earth, next to being the most customer-centric company [S5].

He closed by borrowing an idea from biologist Richard Dawkins. A living body works constantly to stay different from its surroundings. Stop, and it merges back into them. Companies and people face the same pull toward being typical, and resisting it takes ongoing effort [S5].

## How he decides

- Start with the customer, then work backwards to what to build [S7].
- Ask whether a decision can be undone. If yes, move fast with a small group [S2].
- Decide with about 70% of the information you want [S3].
- If you disagree, say so, then commit fully once the call is made [S3].
- Escalate real misalignment early. Otherwise the side with more stamina wins, which is slow and draining [S3].
- Prefer cash flow over accounting that only looks good [S1].
- Take a bet with a one-in-ten chance of a 100-times payoff, every time [S2].
- Hire to a high bar, because people are the biggest factor in long-term success [S1].
- Write it out in full sentences before you meet [S4].

## Where he stood

**Should a company chase competitors or customers?** Customers. Competitor focus makes a company wait for others to move. Customer focus makes it invent before it has to [S1][S3].

**Profit now or value later?** Value later. He accepted lower profits for years to build market leadership, and he measured success by long-term value [S1].

**How careful should decisions be?** It depends on the door. Careful for the few that can't be undone, quick for the many that can [S2].

**Is failure acceptable?** Yes, if it comes from real experiments. He wanted Amazon to be the best place in the world to fail [S2].

**Who does a company serve?** By 2021 he said a business must create value for employees, sellers and the wider world as well as customers and owners, and he set a goal of being the best employer on Earth [S5]. This was a change in emphasis from the earlier letters, which were mostly about customers and owners [S1].

## His work and record

- Born January 12, 1964, in Albuquerque, New Mexico [S8].
- Graduated from Princeton in 1986 with an engineering degree [S8].
- Worked in finance and technology, including at D. E. Shaw from 1990 to 1994, where he became a senior vice president [S8].
- Founded the company on July 5, 1994, in a rented garage in Bellevue, Washington. It was first called Cadabra [S8].
- Founded Blue Origin, a spaceflight company, in 2000 [S8].
- Bought The Washington Post for $250 million in 2013 [S8].
- By the 2015 letter, AWS offered more than 70 services and had cut prices 51 times [S2].
- By the 2017 letter, Prime had more than 100 million paid members [S4].
- Stepped down as CEO on July 5, 2021, and became executive chair. Andy Jassy took over [S8].
- Flew to the edge of space on Blue Origin's New Shepard on July 20, 2021 [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Warehouse work.** Critics have said Amazon put a low priority on warehouse conditions [S9]. In 2018 a U.S. senator introduced a bill nicknamed the "Stop BEZOS" Act over worker pay, and Amazon raised its U.S. minimum wage to $15 that October [S8]. His 2020 letter later made "Earth's best employer" a formal goal [S5].
- **Unions.** Amazon has faced claims of fighting union drives, including cases from 2001 [S9].
- **Power over sellers.** The European Commission and a U.S. House committee examined how Amazon treated outside sellers and used their data, around 2019 and 2020 [S9].
- **Media.** As owner of The Washington Post, he was criticized for blocking a planned 2024 presidential endorsement and for later changes to the opinion section [S8].

These are criticisms of the company he led, and of his choices as an owner. The letters themselves give his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His tools assume a business that can afford many failed bets. A small team with three months of cash may not survive the experiments that a large company can [S2].
- Customer obsession can put workers, suppliers and neighbours second. His own 2020 letter added employees to the goal, which suggests he saw the gap late [S5].
- "Decide fast" works for two-way doors. Be careful when a choice only looks reversible.
- Six-page memos and a high hiring bar take time. Teams new to them may need the handstand lesson: it takes longer than you think [S4].
- He wrote for owners and staff of one giant company. Pair him with someone who sees the craft, the brand or the human cost.
