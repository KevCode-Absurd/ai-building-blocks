# Jeff Bezos: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-21. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Jeff Bezos is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his letters to Amazon shareholders, written from 1997 to 2020, and from public reference pages. Honesty note: this rebuild used a compiled copy of every shareholder letter from 1997 to 2020, and all 24 were read in full. Claims tagged [S1] to [S5] and [S10] to [S28] rest on his own text. The Vogels post [S6] and the leadership principles page [S7] are second-hand or reference pages that were not on disk this time. A letter's year is the year it reports on, and it was sent the following spring. He stepped down as Amazon's CEO in July 2021 [S8], so these are views as of the letter they come from. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after that.

## In one paragraph

Bezos built Amazon around one habit: start with the customer and work back to what you should build [S7][S21]. His first letter, in 1997, said the company would judge itself by value created over the long term, would focus hard on customers, and would choose cash flow over flattering accounts [S10]. Over the next two decades he added tools: free cash flow per share as the master measure [S17], goals built on inputs [S22], one-way and two-way doors [S2], working backwards [S21], six-page memos instead of slides [S4], and failure as the price of invention [S2][S27]. His last letter as CEO said a business must create more than it consumes, and that a company, like a living thing, has to keep working to stay different from its surroundings [S5].

## How his thinking changed

- **1997 to 1999: grow and own the market.** Sales grew 838% in 1997, and he said the company would pick market leadership over short-term profit [S10][S11]. He told staff to be afraid of customers, not rivals [S11]. By 1999 he called Amazon a platform that could launch new stores faster and cheaper [S12].
- **2000 to 2001: the crash, then balance.** Shares fell more than 80%. He listed the progress anyway and owned the losses on Pets.com and living.com [S13]. After years on growth and then cost, he said the company could afford both, and added low price as a third pillar next to selection and convenience [S14].
- **2002 to 2004: cash as the yardstick.** He argued that customer experience can become a mostly fixed cost, so quality and low prices need not trade off [S15]. He named free cash flow per share as the ultimate measure [S17].
- **2005 to 2006: math versus judgment.** He split decisions into those math can settle and those that need judgment, and put price cuts in the second group [S18]. He set tests for new businesses [S19].
- **2007 to 2011: invention beyond retail.** Kindle showed he would build new skills rather than shrink the vision [S20][S21]. In 2010 he said the advanced technology behind Amazon's services is not idle: it leads to free cash flow [S23]. Goals moved to inputs [S22], and self-service platforms beat gatekeepers [S24].
- **2012 to 2014: proactive delight and the flywheel.** Improve things before rivals force you to [S1]. Marketplace, Prime and AWS became the three big bets that lock together [S26].
- **2015 to 2017: speed and standards.** Doors in 2015 [S2], Day 1 defences in 2016 [S3], high standards in 2017 [S4].
- **2018 to 2020: wandering, scale and obligation.** Wandering guided by curiosity, and failures that grow with the company [S27]. Then pandemic pay and safety steps [S28], and in 2020 a tally of value for every group and a duty to staff [S5].

## Core ideas

### Obsess over customers, and work backwards from a need

A company can center itself on competitors, products, technology or customers. Bezos picked customers because they are never fully satisfied and keep pulling the company forward [S3]. In 1998 he told staff to wake up afraid of customers, not rivals [S11]. In 2012 he said Amazon's energy comes from wanting to impress customers, not from wanting to beat competitors, and that this lets a company act early: lower prices, add benefits and invent before it has to [S1]. By 2015 he said 90 to 95% of what AWS builds comes from what customers ask for [S2]. In 2018 he added the limit: asking customers is not enough, because the biggest needle movers are things they don't know to ask for. No one asked for AWS or Echo [S27]. In 2017 he wrote that yesterday's wow becomes today's normal [S4].

**Working backwards and wandering.** Working backwards means starting with a customer need and acquiring whatever skills it demands. The opposite, "skills-forward," asks what else the company can do with what it is already good at [S21]. Bezos wrote that skills-forward is rewarding but, used alone, never pushes a company to build fresh skills [S21]. Werner Vogels later described the habit as writing the press release first, then the questions and answers, then the customer experience [S6]. Bezos added in 2018 that wandering, guided by hunch and curiosity, is a needed counterbalance to efficiency because the biggest discoveries are non-linear [S27].

How he applied it: Amazon kept negative reviews despite vendor complaints, and shipped a warning about repeat purchases that slightly lowered sales [S16]. It refunds customers who had poor video playback before they complain [S1].

### It's all about the long term

The 1997 letter set out how the company would choose. It would invest for long-term market leadership, not short-term profit or Wall Street's reaction, and when accounts and cash pulled apart it would take the cash [S10]. He reattached that letter every year. In 2003 he compared owners to tenants: a long-term view is a requirement and an outcome of true ownership [S16]. In 2008 he said long-term thinking lets a company work for years on a need that is meaningful and durable [S21]. His test of a durable need was whether anyone could picture customers wanting higher prices, less selection or slower delivery ten years on [S21]. In 2012 he said it squares customers' and shareholders' interests [S1].

How he applied it: Amazon sold its own devices close to cost and aimed to earn money when people used them, not when they bought them [S1].

### Cash flow, not earnings, and inputs, not outputs

He said a share is worth the present value of future cash flows, so the figure to drive is free cash flow per share [S14][S17]. He showed that a business can post a 10% margin and 100% earnings growth while burning cash, because the machines it must keep buying cost more than the profit they make. Slower growth would even have been better in that case [S17]. He warned that EBITDA misleads in the same way [S17]. He also counted share dilution, aiming for about 3% a year from staff options [S14]. In 2020 he repeated the point: a stock price is a prediction of future cash flows, discounted back to today [S5].

**Inputs, not outputs.** Newer leaders were surprised how little time Amazon spent discussing financial results. The company focuses on controllable inputs and trusts the outputs to follow [S22]. Of the 452 goals set for 2010, 360 bore directly on customer experience. The word revenue appeared eight times, free cash flow four, and net income, margin or operating profit not once [S22]. In 2013 he called growth in selection, and how much people want it, the key inputs for Prime Video, and called new customers, repeat use and streams the outputs that show the inputs are right [S25]. He named the same split when judging Prime Video: do free trial starts, conversion and renewals rise [S26].

### Price, volume and the flywheel

**Price against the math.** When you cut prices, short-term math nearly always says you should have raised them. He knew elasticity well enough to predict that the extra volume never pays for the cut in the short term. But the math cannot see five or ten years of building customer trust, so he made the call by judgment [S18]. His stated rule for pricing: earn customer trust, not optimise this quarter's profit dollars [S21].

**The flywheel.** He used the word for a loop where each part feeds the next. Lower costs allow lower prices, which bring more volume, which spreads fixed costs, which allow lower prices again. He told shareholders to expect that loop to repeat [S14]. In 2014 he showed the three-way version: selection and price drew customers, customers drew third-party sellers, sellers added selection and scale, and Fulfillment by Amazon tied Marketplace to Prime [S26]. By 2018 third-party sellers made up 58% of physical sales, growing about 52% a year against 25% for Amazon's own sales. He credited the tools Amazon built for them [S27].

### Day 1 versus Day 2

"Day 1" is the energy of a young company that is still experimenting. In 1997 he said it was Day 1 for the Internet and, if Amazon executed well, for Amazon [S10]. In 2016 he defined the other end: Day 2 is stasis, then irrelevance, then painful decline [S3]. The defences are customer obsession, a skeptical view of proxies, eager adoption of outside trends, and fast decisions [S3]. A proxy is a stand-in for the goal: a process followed for its own sake, or a survey average used in place of understanding the customer [S3].

### One-way doors and two-way doors

In 2015 Bezos split decisions into two types. Type 1 decisions can't be undone, so they deserve slow, careful thought and wide advice. Type 2 decisions can be reversed, so a small group or one person with good judgment should make them quickly [S2]. He warned that large firms drift into using the heavy process for everything, which brings slowness, timid risk and too few experiments. A footnote added that firms that treat one-way doors lightly fail before they grow large [S2].

In 2016 he added four rules for speed: do not use a one-size-fits-all process; decide with about 70% of the information you wish you had, since waiting for 90% usually means being slow; use "disagree and commit"; and recognise real misalignment early and escalate it, because otherwise the side with more stamina wins [S3]. He committed against his own doubts when the Amazon Studios team wanted to make a show [S3].

### Failure and invention

Bezos treated failure as part of invention. Experiments fail, and if you know it will work it isn't an experiment [S2]. He wrote that with a ten percent chance of a 100 times payoff you should take the bet every time, even though you will be wrong nine times in ten. Baseball caps a swing at four runs, but business can pay 1,000 [S2]. In 2013 he said failure is not optional; experiments start small, so failures stay small and winners get doubled down on [S25]. In 2018 he added that failure needs to scale too: if the size of failed bets isn't growing, a large company isn't inventing at a size that moves the needle. Multibillion-dollar failures are acceptable if the bets were good ones [S27].

### High standards are learned, and they need realistic scope

In 2017 he wrote that high standards are teachable, spread through exposure, and belong to a field. Being excellent at one thing doesn't make you excellent at another [S4]. Two parts matter: recognising what good looks like, and knowing how long it takes. He told of a friend whose handstand coach said most people expect to learn it in two weeks, when it takes about six months of daily practice [S4]. He used the same lens on the memo: a great six-page memo is rewritten, shared, set aside for a couple of days and edited again, and takes a week or more [S4]. The name of the author doesn't appear on it, since it is the team's [S4].

### Create more than you consume, and stay different

In his last letter as CEO, Bezos argued that a business must create more value than it takes from the people it touches. He put rough numbers on 2020: $21 billion for shareholders, $91 billion for employees, $25 billion for third-party sellers and $164 billion for customers, for a total of $301 billion [S5]. For customers he valued only time saved, using 75 hours a year at $10 an hour minus the cost of Prime [S5]. He added a goal: to be Earth's best employer and safest place to work, next to the most customer-centric company [S5]. He closed by borrowing an idea from biologist Richard Dawkins. A living body works constantly to stay different from its surroundings. Stop, and it merges back in. Companies and people face the same pull to be typical, and resisting takes continuous effort [S5].

## How he decides

- Start with the customer need, check it will still hold in ten years, then work backwards [S21].
- Ask whether a decision can be undone. If yes, move fast with a small group [S2].
- Decide with about 70% of the information you want [S3].
- If you disagree, say so, then commit fully once the call is made [S3].
- Escalate real misalignment early. Otherwise the side with more stamina wins [S3].
- Let math decide the questions math can answer. Use judgment, and say so, where it can't [S18].
- Prefer cash flow over accounting that only looks good, and watch the cash per share [S10][S17].
- Set goals on inputs you control, not on profit [S22].
- Measure programs, drop those that don't earn their keep, and back those that do [S10].
- Take a bet with a one-in-ten chance of a 100-times payoff, every time [S2].
- Write it out in full sentences before you meet [S4].
- Tally what you create for every group you touch, using cautious guesses [S5].

## Real decisions

### Marketplace, after two misses and an internal fight
Amazon tried Auctions and zShops first, and both missed. He says the team learned from the failures and stayed stubborn on the vision. In 2000 it put outside sellers on the same product page as its own goods, which made buyers worry about stuck stock and cannibalised sales. His stated reason was simple: if a third party offers a better price or availability, the customer should get easy access to that offer. Outside sellers rose from 6% of units sold in 2000 to 28% in 2005, and above half by 2017. He later said many well-meaning people were not aligned with the plan and the big decision needed escalation. [S2][S3][S18][S26]

### Prime, with no simple math to prove it
Amazon launched all-you-can-eat fast shipping for a flat fee and gave up many millions in shipping revenue in the first year. He says people called it risky and no simple math showed it would pay. The reasons were the earlier results from free shipping, an intuition that customers would see it as a great deal, and analysis that scale would cut the cost of fast delivery. By 2017 Prime had more than 100 million paid members. A small team also built the one-hour Prime Now service in 111 days. [S2][S4][S26]

### Staying the course after the 2000 crash
Shares fell more than 80% in a year. Amazon had held stakes in smaller online sellers such as Pets.com and living.com, and both shut down. He wrote that putting in more of Amazon's own money to keep them alive would have been a bigger mistake, and that the "land rush" idea that had guided the company had stopped being useful. He kept the customer focus, set a goal of a pro forma operating profit in the fourth quarter of 2001, and met it with $59 million. [S13][S14]

### Building Kindle with no hardware skills
Amazon had never designed a device. He says that rather than shrink the vision to fit existing skills, the company hired hardware engineers and learned. The vision was every book ever printed, in any language, in under 60 seconds. It took three years to launch and the first run sold out in about five and a half hours. In 2018 he said the Fire phone failed and its learnings sped up Echo and Alexa. [S20][S21][S27]

### Running AWS as a "what does this have to do with selling books" bet
Many called the move into cloud services odd. He wrote that critics asked what it had to do with selling books, and that he decided the real link was culture: customer obsession, invention, long-term thinking and care for operations. The services were built on Amazon's own large-scale systems, which he said it had to invent [S23]. Ten years on, AWS passed $10 billion in annual sales, and he said no one had asked for it. Its prices were cut 51 times, many before rivals pushed. [S2][S19][S23][S27]

### Taking cash over tidy accounts, and cutting prices against the math
In 1997 he told owners that when the look of the accounts and real cash pulled apart, Amazon would take the cash. Seven years later he showed with a worked machine example why. In 2005 he explained that the company lowers prices even though its own math says to raise them, because trust compounds over years that no elasticity model can reach. [S10][S17][S18]

### Setting a $15 minimum wage, then admitting a gap
In 2018 Amazon raised its U.S. minimum wage to $15. He said the company expected it to help the business, but that this wasn't why it did so: it seemed the right thing to do, and he challenged rivals to match. In the 2020 letter, after a lopsided union vote in Bessemer, he wrote that the company needed a better vision for how it creates value for employees, and set the goal of being Earth's best employer. [S27][S5]


## Where he stood

**Competitors or customers?** Customers. Competitor focus makes a company wait for others to move [S1][S3]. He did not claim it was the only way [S1].

**Profit now or value later?** Value later, measured in long-term cash [S10][S17].

**How careful should decisions be?** It depends on the door [S2].

**Is failure acceptable?** Yes, from real experiments and good bets [S2][S27].

**Who does a company serve?** By 2020, employees, sellers and the wider world as well as customers and owners [S5]. Earlier letters were mostly about customers and owners, though he linked their interests from 2001 [S14].

## His work and record

- Born January 12, 1964, in Albuquerque, New Mexico. Graduated from Princeton in 1986 with an engineering degree. Worked at D. E. Shaw from 1990 to 1994 [S8].
- Founded the company on July 5, 1994, in a rented garage in Bellevue, Washington. It was first called Cadabra [S8].
- Took the company public in May 1997 [S10].
- Founded Blue Origin, a spaceflight company, in 2000 [S8].
- Launched Prime in 2005 and Kindle in 2007 [S21][S20].
- By the 2015 letter, AWS offered more than 70 services and had cut prices 51 times [S2].
- By the 2017 letter, Prime had more than 100 million paid members [S4].
- Stepped down as CEO on July 5, 2021, and became executive chair. Andy Jassy took over [S8].
- Flew to the edge of space on Blue Origin on July 20, 2021 [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Warehouse work.** Critics have said Amazon put a low priority on warehouse conditions [S9]. In 2018 a U.S. senator introduced a bill nicknamed the "Stop BEZOS" Act over worker pay, and Amazon raised its U.S. minimum wage to $15 that October [S8]. His 2020 letter later made "Earth's best employer" a formal goal [S5].
- **Unions.** Amazon has faced claims of fighting union drives, including cases from 2001 [S9]. His 2020 letter said the Bessemer vote showed more work was needed [S5].
- **Power over sellers.** The European Commission and a U.S. House committee examined how Amazon used sellers' data, around 2019 and 2020 [S9]. His letters credit seller tools for their growth [S27].
- **Failed bets he named himself.** He wrote that Pets.com, living.com and the Fire phone lost money [S13][S27].
- **Media.** As owner of The Washington Post, he was criticized over a blocked 2024 endorsement [S8].

These are criticisms of the company he led, and of his choices as an owner. The letters themselves give his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His tools assume a business that can afford many failed bets. A small team with three months of cash may not [S2][S27].
- Customer obsession can put workers, suppliers and neighbours second. His own 2020 letter added employees to the goal, which suggests he saw the gap late [S5].
- "Decide fast" works for two-way doors. Be careful when a choice only looks reversible.
- The value-created tally in the 2020 letter uses generous guesses, such as a flat hourly value for saved time. It is a way to think, not an audit [S5].
- Six-page memos and a high hiring bar take time. Teams new to them may need the handstand lesson: it takes longer than you think [S4].
- He wrote for one giant company. Pair him with someone who sees the craft, the brand or the human cost.
