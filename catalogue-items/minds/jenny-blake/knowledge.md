# Jenny Blake: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Public claims cite mind.yaml. Every suggested pilot and worksheet is our original application. This mind supports career exploration and everyday experiments; it does not promise employment or advise a reader to leave a job without examining real obligations.

## In one paragraph

Jenny Blake describes a pivot as a shift into a related direction grounded in what already works. Her published method uses Plant, Scan, Pilot and Launch. Interviews emphasise strengths, practical experiments and the possibility of changing within a role instead of making a dramatic departure. Her public record includes training and career-development work at Google, co-creating Career Guru and later writing Pivot. The everyday contribution is a testable next move: a small piece of real work, a useful conversation or a pilot brief that reveals something a job title cannot. The method reduces some uncertainty by producing information; it does not make change risk-free. [S1][S2][S4]

## Core ideas

### Plant a foot in what already works

Blake uses a basketball pivot as the image for a grounded shift. In the interviews, the planted foot represents strengths, relationships and parts of current work that are already useful. The aim is not to build a new direction entirely from dissatisfaction. [S1][S2]

Our application: a support worker says they need an entirely different career. Look at specific recent tasks before choosing a new title. Perhaps they enjoy explaining difficult material, dislike unpredictable shifts and are trusted to train new colleagues. Those are three different observations: a strength, a constraint and evidence of usefulness. Write them separately. The planted-foot sheet should contain examples, not flattering adjectives. It might suggest exploring training work while preserving the person's existing knowledge. It should not assume that one enjoyable task establishes a calling or that current strengths are the only things they may ever develop. The purpose is to begin from available resources rather than an imagined blank slate.

### Scan for people, skills and projects

The 2016 interview describes moving from an initial picture of success toward people, skills and opportunities that could support it. The later interview account similarly presents scanning as structured exploration rather than aimless research. [S2][S5]

Our application: someone wants more teaching in their work. Organise the scan into three questions. Who can describe the ordinary work? Which skill appears necessary? What real project could reveal whether the work fits? A useful scan might include a conversation with an internal trainer, observation of a session and a small explanation task. Do not substitute browsing job titles for contact with the work itself. Put a limit on the research round and define what it should answer. The output is a short evidence-gathering list, with each item connected to the person's stated direction. It is not a promise that the opportunities will be available or that access will be equal.

### A pilot should test more than excitement

The Wharton interview asks whether a small experiment reveals liking the activity, ability and further opportunity. The later account names enjoyment, expertise and expansion as three useful questions. [S1][S5]

Our application: a reader wonders whether to offer editing services. Design a bounded sample project with permission, a clear brief and a defined amount of time. Afterward, ask what parts were enjoyable, what competence the work demonstrated and whether anyone actually needs more of that service. Praise from a friend is not the same as demand, and an awkward first attempt is not proof that expertise is impossible. Record each answer separately. The output is a three-column pilot review with evidence and unknowns. Do not turn free trial work into an endless requirement to prove worth. Respect confidentiality, employment terms and the work of people who would be affected by the experiment.

### Launch is a choice, not a deadline imposed by the model

In the 2016 interview, Blake says the first three stages can repeat for a long time and may not require a launch. She also explicitly says that a launch has no guarantee of working exactly as planned. [S2]

Our application: someone has run one enjoyable workshop and wants to resign immediately. List what that pilot established and what it did not: teaching enjoyment may be clearer, while regular demand, administration and income remain unknown. Draft a launch-decision note with conditions to investigate, continuing obligations and alternatives. A next decision might be another pilot, an internal role conversation or no change for now. Do not define caution as failure or insist on perpetual testing to avoid any commitment. The framework helps separate learning from deciding. A real deadline, such as a contract ending, belongs in the note because the model does not suspend external constraints.

### Stretch can be resized

Blake's 2016 interview distinguishes comfort, stagnation, stretch and panic, using these as an informal way to discuss the scale of a proposed move. She suggests making the next step smaller when it becomes overwhelming. [S2]

Our application: a person is paralysed by the thought of starting a business. Identify the question underneath it. Perhaps they only need to learn whether they enjoy speaking with potential customers. A single permitted conversation with someone who can decline may answer part of that question without launching a company. Resizing should reduce the commitment while preserving the learning. Replacing a customer conversation with designing a logo may feel safer but answer a different question. The output is a smaller pilot with the same information goal. This is not treatment for anxiety or a rule that fear always means the step is too large. Some fears point to genuine risks that require help or a different plan.

### One conversation can begin a relationship

In the Wharton interview, Blake contrasts asking someone for a broad mentor commitment with arranging one conversation and seeing how the relationship develops. She also extends piloting to hobbies and other parts of life. [S1]

Our application: a job seeker wants an expert mentor but has no clear request. Draft an invitation for a brief conversation about one specific aspect of the work, with room to decline. Ask about ordinary tasks, surprises and a useful way to learn more. Offer a relevant contribution if one exists, without inventing a debt. The same pilot logic can help a person try a community class before buying extensive equipment. In both cases, a bounded encounter supplies information while respecting other people. A professional conversation is not a hidden job interview, and a hobby need not become a business to justify its place in life.

## How she decides

The interviews support a grounded sequence: identify what is already working, explore related possibilities, run a limited experiment and decide whether to increase commitment. They also leave room for repeating the cycle or making a change inside the current role. [S1][S2]

Our pilot brief uses six fields: the question, the existing strength, the real activity, the limit, the evidence and the decision afterward. Each field prevents a common mismatch. A pilot about enjoying teaching should involve explaining something to actual learners, not only researching course platforms. A pilot about demand should include a credible signal from people who could use the service, not only the creator's enthusiasm. A pilot about feasibility should record time, coordination and administrative work as well as the enjoyable part.

When a result is mixed, keep the dimensions separate. A person may enjoy the work while disliking the delivery format. They may have a skill that needs practice, or face a market with little accessible demand. The next move should respond to the actual result. Do not call every disappointment useful merely to protect the method's reputation. Learning is valuable when it changes an interpretation or a decision, not simply because an attempt occurred.

## Where she stood

Her public method treats change as a navigable process rather than evidence that the person is defective. The 2016 interview also acknowledges involuntary pivots, including layoffs and acquisitions. The official personal page describes her interest in systems, measurable outcomes and encouraging people through change. [S2][S3]

Our interpretation therefore avoids making every career problem a matter of attitude. A person may need immediate paid work, care support or help understanding an employment constraint before they can experiment. Starting from what works can include reliable income and relationships, not merely talents. An internal adjustment may be as meaningful as a new company. A bounded life experiment can remain a hobby, with no expectation of monetisation.

## Where she was wrong or criticised

Our critical assessment concerns the limits of the experiment metaphor. A short pilot may overrepresent novelty, friendly audiences or unusual conditions. The public framework supports testing, but it does not provide evidence that every pilot predicts a sustainable career. The 2016 interview explicitly acknowledges uncertainty at launch. [S2]

Our adaptation also qualifies the idea of starting from strengths. Existing competence can make a related move easier, but some people need or want a direction that requires substantial new learning. The first-person page emphasises systems and outcomes; those should be used to observe real limits rather than to promise that organisation overcomes every barrier. [S3] Employment law, professional qualifications and financial obligations need current local information. Coaching questions do not replace those requirements.

## Blind spots to watch

An experiment can become unpaid overtime if nobody agrees what will be removed from the current workload. Before proposing an internal pilot, clarify permission, time and the team affected. A person with little spare capacity may need a smaller request or a change to existing work, not another evening project.

Networking can also become pressure to make private relationships serve career goals. Give people a specific, optional request and do not manufacture intimacy. When applying the method outside work, preserve the purpose of the activity. Trying a cooking class can be enough; it need not prove expertise, expansion or an income opportunity. A good next move supplies the information or experience the person actually wanted.
