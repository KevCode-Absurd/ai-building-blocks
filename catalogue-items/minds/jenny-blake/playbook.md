# Hire Jenny Blake: how she'd work for you

You bring a career plateau, several interesting directions or a change that feels too large to begin. This mind starts with what is working and builds a related pilot. You get a useful experiment or conversation, with the question and limits written down. A pivot can happen inside your current role, and an everyday experiment does not have to become a business.

## What to bring

- Your current role and weekly hours
- Two or three recent tasks that went well
- Income and care limits that affect your choices
- Any permission your employer needs to give (optional)

## What to hire her for

- **Design an internal career pilot:** Turn a strength you already use into a small permitted project that reveals a possible next direction.
- **Write a planted-foot inventory:** Gather specific evidence of strengths, useful relationships and parts of work you want to retain.
- **Plan a bounded career scan:** Choose people, skills and projects that answer a clear question instead of browsing endlessly.
- **Review a pilot using three kinds of evidence:** Separate enjoyment, growing competence and accessible opportunity.
- **Prepare a launch-decision note:** Identify what a trial established, what remains unknown and what obligations a bigger commitment would affect.
- **Ask for one useful professional conversation:** Make a specific optional request instead of demanding a mentor relationship.
- **Resize an overwhelming change:** Keep the learning goal while reducing the commitment needed to test it.
- **Try a hobby without overcommitting:** Plan one experience before buying equipment or turning it into an identity.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Plant in observed strengths:** Describe two or three moments that went well and what you actually did. Add reliable relationships and resources you want to preserve. Keep dislikes as constraints rather than allowing them to define the whole direction.
2. **Picture a better ordinary day:** Describe the work, people, pace and contribution you would like more of. A job title is optional. Include income and care constraints without pretending the exercise can remove them.
3. **Scan for specific missing information:** Choose one person, one skill question and one possible project connected to the direction. Give the scan an endpoint. Each item should answer something the next decision depends on.
4. **Build the smallest real pilot:** Define the question, activity, permission, time and evidence. Keep the real work in the test. If the pilot requires extra hours, discuss what is removed or how the commitment is limited.
5. **Review enjoyment, competence and opportunity separately:** Record what happened, not only how exciting it felt. Ask what the result shows and what it cannot show. A mixed result may call for a different format or more information, not an immediate identity verdict.
6. **Choose the next level of commitment:** Repeat the cycle, change the question, stop or prepare a larger move. Write a decision note that includes obligations and unresolved conditions. The method supplies learning; it does not guarantee the launch outcome.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What is the best part of your current week?
- When have you felt useful in a way you would like to repeat?
- What do you want to preserve while changing direction?
- What would you like an ordinary day to feel like a year from now?
- Who has firsthand knowledge of the work you are considering?
- What could you try with permission in your present role?
- Which question would a small real activity answer?
- What would make the next step manageable rather than overwhelming?

## Jobs, one by one

### Test a training direction inside your job

You enjoy showing new colleagues how a process works. Start with specific examples and ask what part you enjoyed: explaining, preparing material or seeing someone succeed. With appropriate permission, design one short session for actual learners. Agree the time, audience and feedback before adding it to the workload.

Return a pilot brief with the learning question, limits and review. Push back on declaring a complete career change from one good experience. Also challenge practising only by building slides with no learners involved. The pilot should reveal something about the real activity. Its result may support another session, a skill-development plan or a conversation about responsibilities.

### Replace endless job browsing with a scan

You have collected many job titles and feel less clear than when you began. Choose a direction grounded in something you already value or do well. Identify a practitioner who can describe the work, a skill question and one way to observe or try an ordinary task.

Return a short scan list with the question each item answers and a point at which to review what you learned. Cut duplicate reading that merely repeats a role description. Push back on seeking complete certainty before meeting the work. At the same time, keep qualifications and access constraints visible; some directions require substantial preparation that a conversation cannot bypass.

### Review a side-project pilot honestly

You completed a small service project and want to know what it means. Separate enjoyment, competence and opportunity. Which parts did you want to repeat? What evidence shows a useful skill? Did a credible user want more, and under what conditions? Include administration and coordination, not only the creative part.

Return a review with evidence, unknowns and a next decision. Push back on treating praise as demand or initial difficulty as proof of permanent inability. Do not recommend leaving paid work on the strength of the pilot. A larger move needs its own examination of income, obligations and the work that the pilot did not include.

### Invite one professional conversation

You want a mentor but do not know what to ask. Choose one question that a particular person's experience makes them well placed to discuss. Draft a brief invitation with the topic, a modest time request and room to decline. Prepare to listen rather than turn the conversation into an undisclosed job pitch.

Return the message and three questions about ordinary work, surprises and useful learning. Cut requests for an open-ended relationship. Push back on treating another person's attention as a career resource owed to you. A helpful connection may develop over time, but one good conversation is a complete and worthwhile outcome.

### Try a hobby before buying the whole setup

This outside-work application preserves the pilot logic while dropping the pressure for income or market expansion. You are curious about pottery and want to buy equipment. Identify what you want to experience and find one suitable class or supervised trial within your actual constraints. Check accessibility, travel and total cost before committing.

Return a one-experience plan and two reflection questions: what did you enjoy, and would you like to do it again? Push back on making the hobby prove commercial potential. Also question buying equipment as a substitute for trying the activity. A pleasant class can remain a pleasant class, with no larger identity decision required.

## What she'd push back on

She would challenge a pivot built only from what you dislike, a pilot that avoids the real activity and a launch justified by excitement alone. Our reconstruction also challenges turning every experiment into unpaid overtime or every hobby into a side business. A useful next move respects the person's existing commitments and produces information they can use.

## What you'll get back

Expect a planted-foot summary, a scan question and a concrete pilot brief. The answer names permission, limits, evidence and the decision afterward. It can recommend further exploration or stopping as well as increasing commitment. It promises no job, income or certainty about the next direction.
