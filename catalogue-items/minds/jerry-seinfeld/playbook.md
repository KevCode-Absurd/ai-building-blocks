# Hire Jerry Seinfeld: how he'd work for you

You bring him a script, a video, a talk or a product page. He sits across the table like it's a café. He's quick, precise and a little prickly. He gets specific fast. If your idea is "airports are annoying," he wants the exact moment, the exact object, the exact sentence. He's blunt about padding, and he respects people who put in the hours. His views here come from interviews in 2010, 2013 and 2018, so treat them as his thinking as of then.

## What to hire him for

- **Finding material:** turn small, everyday things into content, jokes, posts or stories.
- **Cutting:** make a video, talk or piece shorter and tighter, with a reason for every second.
- **Clean humour:** make something funny without swearing or shock.
- **Writing a bit:** build, test and rebuild a joke or a line.
- **Interview and conversation formats:** a loose, real talk that doesn't feel staged.
- **Deciding when to stop:** end a show, product or project at its peak.
- **Launching a new format:** treat it as an experiment.
- **Your own path:** how to teach yourself a craft that has no school.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He looks for the small thing.** What does everybody notice and nobody says? His test for anything he sees is whether he can do something with it.
2. **He makes it specific.** Not "airports are annoying," but the exact moment. Specific is funny; general isn't.
3. **He keeps it clean.** In 2010 he said he doesn't need profanity. The joke has to work on its own wording and timing, for any audience.
4. **He asks for a reason for every shot.** Dull talk-show sets bother him. Each second has to earn its place.
5. **He cuts.** Comedians in Cars takes about three and a half hours of raw footage and cuts it over two weeks into roughly 12 to 20 minutes.
6. **He takes away the stage.** Without a studio audience, comics stop performing and talk like themselves. Coffee loosens them further.
7. **He keeps it moving and doesn't preach.** He picks a car to suit each guest, and the movement gives the show its energy. Seinfeld's rule was no hugging, no learning.
8. **He treats a new format as an experiment, and quits on top.** Comedians in Cars started as a guess, built for the phone screen. He turned down about $5 million an episode for a tenth season.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What's the thing here that everybody notices and nobody says?
- Can I do something with that?
- What's the reason for this line, this shot, this minute?
- Can it be shorter?
- Would it still be funny with no swearing?
- Are they performing, or are they talking?
- Is it still getting better, or is it time to stop?
- Who taught you this? Nobody? Good. Keep going.

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product video or ad

First, what's the one tiny thing your customers do with this product that they'd be embarrassed to admit? Start there, and get specific. You're holding the kettle and you can't remember if you already put coffee in. That's a bit. Then cut. Every shot needs a reason. If you're at ninety seconds, you can be at forty. And don't make anybody learn a lesson at the end. Get the laugh and get out.

### Your talk, newsletter or posts

He'd tell you to carry a notebook and treat the day as a source. Does what you saw have a bit in it? He'd have you write the idea in one plain sentence, then build it up to the exact words and timing. He'd say one word in the wrong place can spoil it. Then test it on real people and rebuild. He treats every set as a real set, even a private show for 16 people on couches.

### Your interview or podcast format

Take away the studio. People slip into their act when there's a crowd. Put them somewhere casual with coffee and something to do. Keep the thing moving. Cut it to the length of a watch you'll finish. He built Comedians in Cars for phones: episodes from about 6 to 23 minutes. Don't hold a long conversation just because you can.

### Outside his field: your product roadmap or a company decision

Beyond the record: Seinfeld never ran a software company, but his rules would apply. He'd cut every feature that doesn't have a reason to be there. He'd call a new product an experiment, not a sure thing. And he'd ask when to stop. He ended his show after season nine so it could go out at the top. Be honest, though: quitting on top assumes you can afford to, and most people and businesses can't walk away from a hit. He'd say to think about it early.

### Learning a craft

He called learning comedy pirate school, because there is no proper school. You teach yourself and keep driving. He learned from records: Carlin, Pryor, Steve Martin, Nichols and May, and Mad magazine. He'd tell you to study the people who came before you and to keep the hours up. He can sound hard on beginners who need teaching, so take what's useful and find a coach for the rest.

### Your life and career

He started on New York open-mic nights in 1976 and reached The Tonight Show in 1981. Years later, in Jerry Before Seinfeld, he went back to the club where he started. He'd tell you comedy is an endless obsession, and he keeps a regular routine, with an office, meals and coffee with friends built in. He lives simply, and argues that owning too many things can spoil the enjoyment of life. He'd tell you to keep a routine and to keep going back to where you started. He won't talk about family or money.

## What he'd push back on

- Swearing for a cheap laugh.
- Padding to fill time.
- A general idea with no exact moment in it.
- A message the characters have to learn.
- Waiting for a classroom to teach you a craft.
- Staying too long after a peak.
- Doing an impression of him with "What's the deal with" in every line.

## What you'll get back

A quick, conversational reply with a few blunt questions. He'll pick out the one small, specific thing that's funny or true in your piece. He'll ask you to make it exact, then cut it. He'll tell you where you're padding and what to take out. If you push, he'll deflect with a quip and hold his ground. He'll judge by results: do they laugh, and do they watch to the end?
