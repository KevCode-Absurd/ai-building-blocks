# Jill Konrath: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Konrath began as a high school teacher, moved into sales at Xerox and then computer sales, and in 1987 started her own sales consulting firm [S11]. She became known for books on getting through to buyers who have no time: *Selling to Big Companies* (2005), *SNAP Selling* (2010), *Agile Selling* (2014) and *More Sales, Less Time* [S11][S9]. Her central picture is the crazy-busy buyer who makes a snap decision about whether a seller is worth any time at all [S7]. To win that decision, she argues, sellers must talk about the business results a customer gets, not the product [S1]. The real rival is usually the status quo, so the offer must be clearly better than what the buyer does now [S1]. Her methods are practical: interview customers who recently switched, tailor the message to one buyer, slow down the first meeting, and focus on what you can control [S5][S4][S3][S6].

## Core ideas

### Buyers are crazy-busy and decide in a snap

*SNAP Selling* is written from the view of a busy, overloaded customer [S11]. Its page describes crazy-busy prospects who make a quick decision that decides a seller's fate [S7]. The book covers how prospects judge salespeople, how to get access to busy buyers, and how to keep decisions from stalling [S7]. Her value proposition article opens on the same note: overwhelmed buyers don't care what you sell, only what it does for them [S1].

How she applied it: her book page frames the goal as becoming a valuable resource whose messages won't be deleted [S9].

### The status quo is your biggest competitor

Konrath argues that the hardest rival is the buyer's current way of doing things [S1]. Changing takes effort, education, analysis, agreement, justification and rollout [S1]. People won't change unless the new offer is clearly better than what they have [S1]. A strong value proposition creates enough curiosity to get past that inertia [S1].

How she applied it: she told sellers to list the three most likely ways a buyer solves the problem today without them, and how each of those hurts the buyer's goals [S4].

### Say what results customers get

She defines a value proposition as a clear statement of the tangible business results that similar customers have gained [S1]. It has three parts: a business driver the buyer is measured on, a verb showing change, and a specific number [S1]. Drivers include productivity, turnaround time, conversion rates, costs, uptime and profit [S1]. Verbs include increase, cut, reduce and eliminate [S1]. Numbers make it believable, and she advised against rounding them, because exact figures seem more credible [S1].

How she applied it: her own line was that she helped companies crack big accounts and shorten sales cycles, backed by a client's 87% success rate in reaching the largest U.S. companies. She said it made meetings easier to book [S1].

### Learn your value from customers who just switched

When responses are poor, or prospects say they already have it handled, the value proposition is weak [S5]. Her fix was to interview customers [S5]. Skip long-time fans, who may have forgotten life before. Talk to people who switched in the past 6 to 12 months, because they can describe the change [S5]. Listen and don't sell. Plan your questions, ask several people at each customer, and look for patterns [S5].

How she applied it: she told the story of a printing system whose sales stalled after early adopters. One printer said it cut his prepress staff by a third for the same work, shortened turnaround by two to three days, and let him delay buying a new press. The company relaunched with those results and, she said, reached 50% market share within a year [S5].

### No numbers? Talk business anyway

Some offers can't be measured. Konrath said her own consulting business grew for years without clients measuring her value [S2]. She suggested four moves. Describe the work in business terms. Use credible industry research. Agree on metrics with new customers early and track them together. Brainstorm with colleagues about the problems you solve and their knock-on effects [S2].

How she applied it: she used a "So what?" test. Keep asking it until the answer reaches money, time or competitive edge [S2]. She stressed that the customer's view of value counts more than the seller's own [S2].

### One message for one buyer at one moment

In another article she said the perfect value proposition does exist, but only for a specific buyer at a specific company at a specific time [S4]. Generic messages fail because they talk about the seller, aim at one buyer when several decide, or miss the company's real priorities [S4].

How she applied it: her steps were to focus on one industry first, map the main buyers and their goals, research each company's priorities, watch for trigger events like a reorganisation, and write a main message plus two or three backups. Then send them across email, voicemail and social media, and repeat with other contacts [S4].

### Slow down the first meeting

In 2019 she wrote that sellers cram too much into a first meeting, and prospects push back [S3]. She cited Forrester research finding that only 15% of executives felt sales meetings met their expectations [S3]. Signs of this backlash include a prospect who says all is fine, raises small objections, asks about price then says it's too high, or promises to follow up and never does [S3]. The first meeting should create curiosity and agree on a sensible next step, not close the deal [S3].

How she applied it: she told sellers to research the company, set a realistic goal for the meeting, bring one relevant customer story or piece of research, and prepare at least ten questions [S3].

### Focus on what you can control

In a tough market, she warned against both denial and false cheer [S6]. Accept the conditions and work on what you can change [S6]. Reframe problems as challenges, which pushes the mind to look for ideas [S6]. Fill the pipeline through higher-leverage work such as webinars with colleagues, co-marketing with related firms and alliances with sellers who reach the same buyers [S6]. Improve each conversation through better preparation, a sharper value proposition and more provocative questions [S6].

How she applied it: she suggested asking how a famous athlete or scientist would handle the situation, as a way to break out of a stuck mindset [S6].

### Keep learning, and protect your time

*Agile Selling* argues that sales keeps getting more complex, so sellers need learning agility to keep up [S9]. *More Sales, Less Time* is about cutting distractions, focusing on priorities and freeing up one to two hours a day [S8]. Its first chapter is a confession of her own crazy-busy life [S8].

How she applied it: in 2013 she surveyed more than 3,000 sales professionals about LinkedIn, found only 5% used it fully and 55% not at all, and turned the results into an ebook [S11].

## How she decides

- Ask what the busy buyer gets, not what you sell [S1].
- Name the status quo you are up against [S1][S4].
- Put a driver, a verb and a number in the message [S1].
- Ask recent switchers what changed [S5].
- Keep asking "So what?" until you hit business impact [S2].
- Aim the first meeting at curiosity and a next step [S3].
- Spend energy only on what you can control [S6].

## Where she stood

**What do buyers care about?** Results for their business, not your product [S1][S5].

**Who is the main competitor?** Usually the status quo [S1].

**Can you sell without hard numbers?** Yes, with business language, outside research and shared metrics, though she still urged sellers to quantify wherever they can [S2].

**Should you try to close in the first meeting?** No. Sales takes patience, and rushing makes prospects pull back [S3].

**Is there one perfect pitch?** Only for one buyer at one moment, which is why research matters [S4].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Little criticism on record.** Wikipedia's page on her has no criticism section [S11]. Treat the points below as limits rather than settled faults.
- **Results claims are hers.** Figures like the 50% market share story and the 87% success rate come from her own articles, with clients unnamed [S5][S1].
- **Built for business-to-business sales.** Her examples are mostly large companies with several buyers [S4][S3]. Selling to the public may need other tools.
- **Value can be overdone.** Her own article admits many offers can't be measured, and her advice to quantify everything can push sellers toward shaky numbers [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- Deep research on one buyer at a time is slow. A seller with a huge list may need a simpler, wider approach.
- Her focus is getting in the door and starting well. Pair her with a closer or a negotiator for the late stages.
- Exact numbers persuade, but only if they are true. Never invent a figure to fit her formula.
- Her advice on tough markets is about mindset and leverage. It won't fix a product nobody needs.
- Her newest views are not in this data set. She has moved on to other work [S10].
