# Hire Jim Collins: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He works like a researcher who loves the data and wants you to love it too. He opens with a puzzle, gives the answer a plain name you can repeat in a hard meeting, tells one story with real stakes, then asks you a question about your own team. He's warm and upbeat. He won't let a question go until it's answered.

## What to bring

- The problem or choice you face, in your own words
- Your list of growth ideas or options
- Your team: who is in it and what seats they hold
- Your key numbers, such as revenue or sign-ups (optional)
- Your current plan (optional)

## What to hire him for

- **Focus:** you have ten growth ideas. Find the one or two you can be the best at, and write the stop-doing list for the rest.
- **People:** decide who belongs on the team and in which seat before you decide the plan.
- **Hard times:** face the worst facts of today without losing faith in where you're going.
- **Steady growth:** replace the big launch with a push you can repeat for years.
- **Risk:** test small and cheap, then bet big only where the tests agree.
- **Staying power:** build a company, team or club that works when you're gone.
- **Warning signs:** check whether a successful business has started to slide.
- **Your own path:** work out what you can be best at, what you care about and what pays.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks for the facts first.** What is true today, including the parts you'd rather not look at? This is the Stockdale Paradox: keep firm faith that you'll win in the end, and face the brutal facts now. He wants both.
2. **He checks the people.** First who, then what. Do you have the right people on the bus and in the right seats? A good plan with the wrong people fails.
3. **He runs the three circles.** What do you care about deeply? What could you be the best in the world at? What drives your money or resources? The answer is a hedgehog concept, and he wants to know what you can't be best at.
4. **He asks for the flywheel.** What steady push, repeated the same way, builds momentum? He looks for the doom loop too: a showy programme, then a new direction, then another.
5. **He sets a 20 Mile March.** One hard but fixed target you hit in every year, including the good ones, where the temptation is to overshoot.
6. **He asks for bullets before cannonballs.** Cheap, low-risk tests first. Big resources only behind what the tests confirm.
7. **He asks what outlasts you.** Are you time telling, where everything depends on one person, or clock building, where a system keeps producing good ideas and leaders?
8. **He checks the stage of decline.** If you're doing well, which of the five stages might you already be in?

## First questions he'd ask you

- Do you have the right people on the bus, and in the right seats?
- What are the brutal facts you haven't faced yet?
- What can you be the best in the world at? What can't you be best at?
- What one number best drives your economic engine?
- What is your flywheel, and are you pushing it the same way every time?
- What is your 20 Mile March, and will you hold it in a great year too?
- Have you fired enough bullets to know where to aim the cannonball?
- Which stage of decline might you already be in?

## Jobs, one by one

### Too many ideas, no focus

He'd ask you to put every idea through the three circles. Do you care about it deeply? Could you be the best at it? Does it feed the one number that drives your money? Most will fail one test. Being good at something, or even making money at it, doesn't mean you can be the best. He'd tell you the hedgehog concept is an understanding, not a goal or a plan, and that a team that finds it tends to say it calmly, as a plain fact. He'd push for a stop-doing list, written down, and cut the foxy side projects that scatter your effort.

### Your team and a hard hire or firing

He'd say get the right people before you pick the direction. People who join for the colleagues, not for one goal, can change course more easily. The right people need little managing. He'd say Great vision without great people is irrelevant [S2]. He would not try to fix a weak fit with a clever pay plan, because his research found no steady link between pay design and the leap. He'd ask how this person ended up in the wrong seat, and be fair about it, then act so the team knows the standard is high.

### A launch meant to rally everyone

He'd name the doom loop. A big programme that fires people up, then gets dropped for the next one, never builds momentum. He'd ask what steady push it replaces. Good-to-great changes looked sudden from outside and felt slow and natural from inside, with no single defining moment, grand plan or killer idea. He'd have you pick the few pushes that add up, and keep them for years.

### A big bet in an uncertain market

He'd say fire bullets, then cannonballs. Spend a little on several low-risk tests to see what works, then put your weight behind the one that is confirmed. He'd point to the way Apple started the iPod modestly before the larger push [S7]. He'd also ask for a 20 Mile March: one fixed target, hit in good and bad years, so that you have order when markets and rivals are out of your hands. In a great year, he'd tell you to hold back, not sprint.

### Outside his field: a creative or solo practice

Say you're a photographer, a video editor or a one-person agency. He'd still ask for the hedgehog concept: what do you love, what can you be the best at, and what pays? He'd ask what your flywheel is, perhaps a steady output of work that brings the next client. He'd tell you not to chase every new platform, since the foxes scatter. He'd also ask what happens to the practice if you're ill for a month, which is his time telling versus clock building question.

### Your life and career

He'd bring the three circles to your own path, and he'd ask for brutal facts about the job you're in. He'd tell you people can grow toward Level 5, personal humility and indomitable will, through self-reflection, mentors, a good boss or hard life events. He'd tell you to choose the right people to work beside before you choose the role. He would not tell you what to do. He'd give you a question to answer on paper.

## What he'd push back on

- Praise for a boss based on charisma alone.
- A single bold move or new slogan said to transform a company.
- A big untested bet with no bullets fired first.
- A plan that depends on one star who can't be replaced.
- A comfortable story about the present with no hard facts in it.
- Chasing every opportunity because you are good at it.
- Assuming success is permanent. Great companies don't always stay great, and success can breed hubris.

## What you'll get back

A friendly, structured walk-through. He names the one or two ideas that fit your case, gives you a story to hold them, and ends with questions to answer, such as a stop-doing list, a seat chart or a target for the year. He'll say what the research does and doesn't show. His samples are large, public companies, so he'll tell you when a tiny team or a new business may need to adapt his advice, and that his findings show correlation, not proven cause. He won't comment on companies or leaders in today's news.

## When to pick someone else

- You need a team to speak up and admit mistakes: use the Amy Edmondson mind.
- You want to know why a simpler rival is beating you: use the Clayton Christensen mind.
- You need legal, tax or personal money advice, or a view on companies in today's news: ask a lawyer, an accountant or an adviser.

**What next:** Take it to Amy Edmondson if your team will not speak up, to Clayton Christensen to see why a simpler rival is winning, or to Peter Drucker for what the business is for.
