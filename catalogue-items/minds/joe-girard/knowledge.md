# Joe Girard: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Girard grew up poor on Detroit's east side, shined shoes in bars at nine, and left school in the eleventh grade [S2]. After his building business failed and left him deep in debt, he talked his way into a job selling Chevrolets at 35 [S1][S2]. He went on to sell 13,001 cars between 1963 and 1978, and Guinness World Records listed his 1,425 cars in 1973 as the most sold by one salesman in a year [S1]. His secret, as his writing tells it, was simple and tireless: treat the sale as the start of a long relationship, thank every buyer, serve them after the deal, keep files on what matters to them, and hand out business cards to everyone [S3][S4][S6]. He retired from selling at 49 and spent his later years writing books such as *How to Sell Anything to Anybody* and speaking to companies [S1].

## Core ideas

### The sale is the start, and a thank-you comes with it


Girard saw the bond with a customer as long-term, like "a long term marriage" [S4]. Most salespeople disappear after the deal. He kept working for the customer, because repeat business drives long-term success [S4]. The same rule, he said, applies to selling anything, not just cars [S4].

How he applied it: he wrote a personal thank-you letter to every customer each night. It congratulated them on the purchase, said they had made a smart choice, invited them to call any time and promised long-term service [S4].

He noticed that most salespeople never thank customers [S4]. A sincere thank-you after every sale was his rule [S4]. Many buyers have second thoughts after a purchase, so telling them they decided well eases the doubt [S4]. People like praise, and he believed congratulations could turn a buyer into a loyal customer or a friend [S4]. Without that attention, customers feel used, as if the salesperson only wanted a quick commission [S4].

### People buy you, not just the product

Another dealer could sell the identical car, so the difference had to be him [S4]. He told of a buyer who chose him over a rival who was $100 cheaper, because Girard himself was the one thing nobody else could offer [S3]. He called you the most important product you sell, and said time and money spent improving yourself pays off [S6].

### Service builds repeat business and referrals

Girard believed lasting relationships come from never letting up on service [S3]. Bad service stops you building a solid customer base [S3]. He saw a problem as a chance: where other salespeople avoided upset customers, he treated fixing the problem as the first step to the next sale, and promised to be there whenever they needed him [S3]. Keeping a customer costs far less than finding a new one, and one customer may buy many cars over a lifetime and send friends and family too [S3].

How he applied it: he told salespeople to make time for current customers, not just chase new ones, and to stay in regular contact [S3].

### Know your customer

After each sale, record what you learn about the customer: family, hobbies, travel [S6]. Then you can talk about what matters to them, and they can see that you care [S6]. As he put it, "Satisfied customers are the best bet for future sales" [S6].

### Prospect everywhere

He called the business card one of his best tools and handed it out everywhere, because "wherever there are people, there are prospects" [S6]. He also used the phone to stay in touch and to find new buyers by cold calling [S6]. In his later teaching he added any tool that tells people who you are and how you can help, including networking and social media [S6].

How he applied it: his site says that after his third year he hired staff to screen and pre-qualify prospects, so he could spend his time selling, and he ran his appointments like a doctor's office [S2].

### Listen, prepare and match the need

Top salespeople, he wrote, are excellent listeners who know which questions to ask [S8]. Do your homework so you can answer the buyer's questions, and if you can't, admit it and call back fast with the answer [S8]. Know their problems, have solutions ready, and explain the specific reasons the product helps them, because a buyer who doesn't see the value won't buy [S8].

### Time is the gift

Everyone gets the same 1,440 minutes a day [S7]. He compared yesterday to a cancelled check and tomorrow to a promissory note [S7]. His rules: get organised in advance, cut time-wasters like gossip and meetings that run long, plan the week and the day, put first things first, and finish what you start [S7].

### Faith over fear

Girard set two words against each other: faith in yourself and your future, and fear of failure, which makes people avoid risk [S5]. Mistakes are fine if you study them [S5]. His five rules: believe in yourself, spend time with confident people, build confidence like a muscle, face fears instead of running, and keep busy so fear has no room [S5].

### Work beats talent

He credited his early success to using every free moment for prospecting and planning [S2]. He said people who failed often weren't willing to work and didn't want it badly enough [S2]. A spark of inspiration, he believed, leads to results, because "sparks create fires" [S2].

## How he decides

- After every sale, thank the customer the same day [S4].
- Serve existing customers before chasing new ones [S3].
- Treat every complaint as the start of the next sale [S3].
- Keep a file on every customer [S6].
- Hand your card to everyone [S6].
- Prepare answers before you meet the buyer [S8].
- Plan the day and put first things first [S7].
- When afraid, act anyway and keep busy [S5].

His day was run by habits, not moods. He used every free moment for prospecting and planning [S2]. He hired staff to pre-qualify prospects so his own hours went to selling [S2]. When a customer was upset, he moved toward the problem, fixed it, and treated that as the first step to the next sale [S3]. Before a meeting he did his homework, so he could answer questions and, if he could not, call back fast with the answer [S8].

He also kept the long view. A customer kept costs far less than a new one found, and one buyer may purchase many cars and send friends and family [S3].

## Where he stood

**Is the job to close deals?** Closing is only the beginning. The real job is keeping the customer for life [S3][S4].

**Does price win?** Not always. Service and the person selling can win even against a cheaper offer [S3].

**Are great salespeople born?** No. He was a school dropout who failed at business first [S2]. Work and planning made the difference [S2].

**Is fear normal?** Yes, but faith in yourself should run your actions, not fear [S5].

**Should you avoid an upset customer?** No. He faced the problem and promised to be there whenever the customer needed him [S3].

**Is selling yourself part of the job?** Yes. He called you the most important product you sell, and said time and money spent improving yourself pays off [S6].

**What do top salespeople do best?** They listen and know which questions to ask [S8].

## His work and record

- Born Joseph Samuel Girardi in Detroit on November 1, 1928, the son of a poor Sicilian-born father [S1].
- Changed his working name to Girard to avoid bias against Sicilian and Italian customers [S1][S2].
- Joined a Detroit dealership at 35 and was fired after other salesmen complained about how much he sold [S1].
- Sold at Merollis Chevrolet in Eastpointe, Michigan, for 15 years and retired at 49 [S1].
- Sold 13,001 cars from 1963 to 1978. Guinness listed his 1,425 cars in 1973 as a one-year record [S1]. His site says his figures were audited by Deloitte and Touche [S9].
- Ali Reda beat his one-year record in 2017, with 1,582 cars [S1].
- Books include *How to Sell Anything to Anybody*, *How to Sell Yourself* and *How to Close Every Sale* [S1][S9].
- Spoke for General Motors, Hewlett-Packard and Kmart. Inducted into the Automotive Hall of Fame in 2001 [S1].
- Died February 28, 2019, aged 90 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Too pushy for some.** He was fired from his first dealership, and his site says it was for being too aggressive [S1][S2]. Some of his early style would not suit every workplace.
- **Records are claims to check.** His site calls him the world's greatest retail salesman and cites Guinness and an audit [S9]. His one-year record was later beaten [S1].
- **Posts after his death.** Articles under his name on his site were posted years after he died [S3]. They match his teaching, but they are not new statements from him.

## Blind spots to watch

- His world was face-to-face, one big purchase at a time. Low-price or online businesses need to scale his personal touch.
- "Keep busy" can crowd out time to think about strategy, pricing or product.
- Personal files on customers must respect their privacy and the law where you live.
- Pair him with someone who sees the numbers and the system, so service doesn't depend on one heroic person.

- A nightly thank-you letter to every buyer works at his volume of one person doing it by hand. A larger team needs a shared habit, not one hero.
- His stress on faith and hard work can make failure look like a lack of effort, when sometimes the market or the product is the problem.
