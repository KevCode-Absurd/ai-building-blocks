# John Caples: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

John Caples was an engineer who became one of the best-known copywriters in America [S3]. He took his first advertising job in the autumn of 1925, at a mail-order agency, and his ad headed with a piano line made his name within a year [S9][S3][S5]. He joined BBDO in 1927 and stayed for decades [S9][S3]. Mail order let him count every reply, and from that he drew one rule: nothing is true about what works in advertising until it has been tested [S9]. He taught that the headline carries most of the weight, that the reader's own gain is the strongest thing it can offer, and that what an ad says matters more than how well it is written [S9]. His books became a standard guide, with David Ogilvy among those who said so [S3][S4][S9].

## Core ideas

### Facts, not opinions

The first thing he asked of any claim about advertising was whether it had been measured [S9]. He said opinions are easy to get and facts are hard, and many a "rule" he heard in his first week turned out to trace back to someone's pet taste, such as a favourite colour or a first boss's habit [S9]. In one check, mail-order ads of known results were shown to fourteen advertising clubs. About half the experts' picks were wrong when set against actual sales [S9]. He listed six reasons a jury picks poor ads: they read the copy before judging the headline, they want to lift the image of the trade, they favour the noble-sounding line, they reward fine writing, they judge in a meeting room, and they are critics, not buyers [S9].

How he applied it: copy is judged by what it sells. Opinion tests with a few people can still help, if each person sees at least two choices, votes in private and is a real prospect [S9]. He warned that people vote for clever ads, dislike picking an ad that makes them look bad and assume an ad needs a picture. He also warned that a three-way vote can reverse a two-way vote [S9].

### The headline is most of the ad

Caples quoted Don Belding's line that inquiry returns put the headline at 50 to 75 percent of an ad's pull [S9]. A newspaper headline competes with hundreds of other items, and for most readers it is the only thing they see [S9]. So he judged a headline alone, with the copy hidden. Judging it after reading the copy is wrong, because readers do not go in that order [S9]. He also counted the company name at the bottom as part of the headline, because the reader's eye goes there next [S9].

How he applied it: he told writers to read candidate headlines to several people without saying what is being sold, and to try again if they would not read on. The fifth edition adds the story of Ogilvy writing over a hundred headlines before choosing one [S9].

### Self-interest first, then news, then curiosity

He sorted good headlines into the reader's self-interest, news, and curiosity, then added a fourth quality: a quick and easy way to a result [S9]. Of ten winners he analysed, every one offered the reader something wanted, two were news, four used curiosity and four suggested ease [S9]. He called self-interest by far the strongest. Curiosity on its own seldom pulls and works best with news or a gain inside it [S9]. Ten failures he analysed were mostly curiosity-only lines, none offered a gain, and seven were gloomy [S9].

His five rules: get self-interest in; put real news in big; avoid curiosity alone; take the cheerful side; suggest quick and easy, but only if it is believable [S9]. His extra pointers include: decide what would make you buy and say that; do not shorten a headline so far that it loses the idea; avoid dead, too-smart and meaningless headlines; suggest useful information; put the main point in the headline and not in the last paragraph; and do not run an ad with no headline [S9]. A mere statement of fact is weak, because the reader knows what the copy will say, though it can still tell a headline-only reader something [S9].

### Thirty-five formulas, used as a springboard

The fifth edition lists 35 headline formulas. They run from news starters (Introducing, Announcing, New, Now, At last, a date, news style), through price, reduced price, special offers, easy payments and free offers, to useful information, a story, How to, How, Why, Which, Who else, Wanted, This, Because, If, a testimonial, a test for the reader, one-word, two-word and three-word headlines, a warning to delay buying, the maker speaking to the reader, a headline aimed at a named group, a question, and benefits through facts and figures [S9]. He called them a springboard, not a crutch, and said new ones keep being invented [S9]. The headline formulas also work for the outer envelope of a mailing and the opening of a broadcast ad [S9]. Many sample headlines in this edition are from later decades and were added by Hahn [S9].

### Find the right appeal

An appeal is the reason you give the reader to buy. Caples said the headline and the appeal are the same thing in a good ad [S9]. He had seen one mail-order ad sell nineteen and a half times as much as another for the same product, in the same space and place, because the first had the right appeal and the second the wrong one. He said he would take a hastily made ad on the right appeal over twenty beautiful ones on a weak appeal, and that eleven months of a twelve-month campaign could rightly go to finding it [S9].

Hahn's revision adds a list of basic appeals from a supervisor of his: sex (taken widely, to include affection), greed, fear, and a fourth he added from fire-department work, duty or professional honour. Hahn notes that all four are about what is best for the buyer and none for the seller [S9]. Caples's own list of working appeals included advancing at work, being one of the in-group, a bargain, popularity, outshining neighbours and, strongest of all, money [S9]. An appeal can work for one product and look odd on another. When a client's pet idea sounds clever in a meeting, the test is still the replies [S9].

How he applied it: lay a set of ads in order of replies, mark each with its number, and write a checklist (headline and appeal, picture, layout, copy, offer, size, medium, placement). In one set of eleven ads for a client, the five that pulled well all carried the same appeal in the headline, and the losers did not [S9].

### Specific and believable

A claim a reader can picture beats a bigger one they cannot. Caples repeated Bruce Barton's case of a correspondence-course ad, where a modest sale of a first script pulled far more than a huge yearly income, because readers could see themselves in it [S9]. Specific figures, dates and facts make a headline easier to believe [S9]. The ad praised by advertising professionals in its day, a long letter from an imagined wife to a failing husband, did not pull, because no husband wanted another lecture [S9]. Copy that opens with general words about everyone's right to enjoy life did worse than copy that gave the facts at once [S9]. A used-car dealer who changed from elegant general ads to ads with make, year and price saw three times as many customers [S9].

Hahn adds that today's reader is more doubtful of easy-money promises, so claims must be more believable and more honest than they used to be [S9].

### Plain copy that starts fast

Caples said what an ad says counts for more than how it says it [S9]. The first paragraph should be short, continue the headline's thought and name the main benefit. Many an ad is improved by cutting its warm-up opening [S9]. He liked present tense, the second person, subheads that tell the story to a skimmer, captions under pictures, simple words and facts in place of adjectives [S9]. On length, advertisers who could trace results used long copy because it pulled better. Long copy reaches those already interested, and short copy suits an ad whose picture says it all [S9]. He wrote that every ad should be a complete sales talk [S9]. He also set out how to dramatise a dull product with a story or a human moment, and listed cleverness, humour and poetry as copy to use with care [S9].

### Make the reply easy

Chapter 13 lists 32 ways to get more inquiries. The simplest: put the offer in the headline or a subhead, say "free" clearly, show the booklet, describe it so it is wanted, use a coupon, repeat the address, add a phone number, say there is no obligation, give a reason to act now, test several offers, then several ads, media, sizes, seasons and positions, and keep records [S9]. He split these into methods that make the whole ad better and methods that only raise the count without improving the ad [S9]. Hahn added toll-free numbers and fax [S9].

He trusted inquiries as an early guide to sales and cited a study of five million inquiries to 165 firms. The exception is a big free-gift offer, which brings many replies and few real leads. He told advertisers to keep that offer identical and quiet in every ad of a test and to qualify leads by phone before a salesperson visits [S9].

### Test small, key everything

A key is a code in the address or link of an ad. The part naming the ad never changes while the ad stays the same, and always changes when anything in it changes, even a trivial word [S9]. Letters for versions (44A, 44B) make the history easy to read [S9]. He recommended three stages: test ads against each other, then expand the test to many publications, then learn from the results about whole media [S9]. If testing is not possible, learn from those who have tested [S9]. The Hahn-era preface sums his stance in three steps: accept nothing as true until tested, build on what you learn, and treat every ad as a new test of what you think you know [S9].

## How he decides

- Ask what the ad sold, not whether it was liked [S9].
- Judge the headline alone, with the copy hidden [S9].
- Write many headlines, test the best few, and let an outsider vote privately [S9].
- Lead with what the reader gets [S9].
- Use curiosity only with a promise or news attached [S9].
- Prefer the believable figure to the bigger one [S9].
- Put the winning appeal in the headline [S9].
- Key every ad so each reply can be traced [S1][S9].
- Run two versions at once, to matched halves, and compare replies [S9].
- Test on a small scale before a big spend [S9].
- Take the hopeful angle when a gloomy one would do the same job [S9].

## Real decisions

### Telling the piano ad as a story

The headline sets up a public humiliation and turns on "But" [S6]. Caples wrote the copy as a first-person tale and kept the sell to the end: a free booklet and demonstration lesson [S6][S3]. The copy ran to four typed single-spaced pages. His stated reason elsewhere was that readers skip formal copy but follow a story with a happy ending [S1][S9]. It made his name, and within a month he reworked the formula for a French course [S9]. Sources date the ad to 1925 or 1926 [S3][S5].

### Moving his mail-order habits into general advertising

Caples began at Ruthrauff & Ryan in 1925 and moved to BBDO on April 8, 1927 [S3]. He said he wanted to see his work in the better magazines, not a physical-culture title [S9]. The foreword to his 1938 book says he took the hard-boiled mail-order viewpoint into general advertising, used mail-order tests where they fitted and made new ones where they did not [S1]. In 1936 two advertising trade bodies named him to a committee on ways of testing ads [S1].

### Choosing which ads to put in the book

For *Advertising Ideas* he looked at hundreds of ads that had won reader surveys and kept 79. He threw out a car ad that topped the readership list because it had about ten words, so reading it proved little. He threw out an insurance ad built on a huge photo of a cat's whiskers, because a stunt like that had never made sales in his keyed work. He dropped winners on the back cover or opposite the contents page, since he could not separate the copy from the spot. His rule: an ad must draw readers and follow methods tests had tied to sales. He also read surveys by cost, so a cheaper page with the same score won [S1].

### Testing in one magazine before going national

He praised a life insurer that tested each new ad for sales in a single publication. Only ads that drew many coupons and sales ran in national magazines. He picked the case partly for its cheerful pitch, about money you receive at retirement, and for its very long copy, which he defended because the readers who stay are real prospects [S1].

### Hormel and the double-money-back offer

In 1932 a soup maker offered double your money back if the buyer was not satisfied. Caples tells it as the ad that started a school, copied by more than forty makers. The firm feared hungry people would buy soup only to claim the refund, so it tested in small cities first and then bigger ones. In six weeks in Chicago only twelve women claimed. He credited the result to a stopping headline and an offer that showed the maker's confidence [S1].

### Choosing the hardest kind of copy

Caples called mail-order ads his favourite kind of copy and the hardest, because each new ad tried to beat a proven winner [S7]. His reason, as the Hall of Fame records it, was that results are counted, so you cannot hide behind taste [S7]. In his casebook he noted a memory-course ad that ran for years, with nothing written afterward beating it, because every run had its own key number and its cost per sale was known to the penny [S1].

### Putting the appeal in the headline after analysing eleven ads

For one client he had eleven full-page ads in a year. He tallied the coupon returns and laid the ads out from poorest to best. They fell into two groups. Every ad in the better group carried one specific appeal in its headline, and none of the poorer group did, though those had other strong appeals. He concluded the appeal must be in the headline, not just the copy. The best of the losers had it only in a subhead. The winning appeal kept working for several years [S9].

### Learning from the "jail" headline

On an early job for a hair-growing treatment, he reasoned that the cure would sell if the public knew the maker would be liable for fraud if it failed. He wrote a headline saying the maker would be in jail if the treatment did not grow hair, with a picture of the maker behind bars. A fellow copywriter laughed and read it as the maker being locked up. He took this to mean readers snap-judge and that the headline and the picture must tell the same story, because the picture is read first [S9].

## Where he stood

**Is advertising an art or a science?** Closer to a science. Creative ideas matter, but tests should decide between them [S9].

**Does clever writing sell?** Rarely on its own. What the ad says counts for more than its style [S9].

**Long copy or short?** Long copy pulls better where results can be traced and the readers are prospects. Short copy suits ads where the picture carries the message [S9].

**What should a headline do first?** Give the reader a reason to read, usually a benefit to them [S9].

**Can you trust inquiries as a measure?** Mostly. They tend to track sales, except for large free offers, so check sales too [S9].

**Do awards show merit?** Not by themselves. Only measured results show how well an ad sells [S9].

## His work and record

- Born in New York City on May 11, 1900 (some sources give May 1) [S3].
- Entered the U.S. Naval Academy and studied engineering, then took an electrical engineering degree and worked at New York Telephone [S3][S9].
- Took evening classes in advertising at Columbia, and began at a mail-order agency in autumn 1925 at $25 a week, six dollars less than his engineering pay [S9].
- His first printed ad, on October 3, 1925, was for a dance studio [S9].
- Wrote the U.S. School of Music ad (dated 1925 or 1926 by different sources) [S3][S5].
- Joined BBDO in 1927, worked on mail-order accounts and became a vice president [S3][S9].
- Published *Tested Advertising Methods* in 1932, then *Advertising for Immediate Sales* (1936) and *Advertising Ideas* (1938) [S1][S3].
- Published *Making Ads Pay* (1957) and *How to Make Your Advertising Make Money* (1983) [S3].
- Taught at Columbia University for a year from 1952 [S3].
- Inducted into the Copywriters Hall of Fame in 1973 and the American Advertising Federation Hall of Fame in the late 1970s [S3].
- The Caples Awards were founded in his name in 1978 [S4].
- Retired in 1983 after a back injury and died in New York in June 1990, aged 90 [S3][S4][S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Narrow proof.** His evidence came mostly from mail order and coupon replies [S1][S3]. That measures quick response well and says less about brand-building over years.
- **Fear and embarrassment.** One campaign he praised sold through fear of social embarrassment [S1]. It worked, but many readers now find the appeal manipulative.
- **Easy to imitate.** His piano formula was copied for decades [S5]. A formula used without fresh testing goes stale, as he warned [S9].
- **Facts that drift.** Sources disagree on several of his dates and on his title at BBDO [S3][S4].
- **Dated examples.** Some tests and figures in the book come from his era. The fifth edition keeps them beside newer cases, and the book does not always say which is which [S9].

## Blind spots to watch

- He measured what could be counted fast. Pair him with someone who thinks about brand, memory and long-term trust.
- His rules came from print ads and coupons. They carry over to emails and landing pages, but his numbers came from his era's tests, not yours.
- He can make testing sound simple. Small samples give noisy answers, and he wrote before modern statistics were common in advertising.
- A focus on self-interest can crowd out humour, beauty or a strong brand voice.
