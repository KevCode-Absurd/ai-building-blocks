# John D. Rockefeller: what he knew and believed

A note on the record: Rockefeller (1839 to 1937) said little in public, but in 1909 he published *Random Reminiscences of Men and Events*, a short book on how he worked and how he gave [S1]. It is in the public domain, so we quote a few short lines. It is also a defence of Standard Oil, written after years of attack. The fullest attack came from the journalist Ida Tarbell in 1904 [S5]. We use both, and an archive biography for facts [S4]. Sources differ on some numbers, such as his share of the oil trade and the total he gave away. We note those gaps.

## In one paragraph

Rockefeller grew up in New York and Ohio, kept a ledger of his earnings and gifts from boyhood, and started work as a bookkeeper in Cleveland in 1855 [S1]. In 1859 he set up a produce firm with Maurice Clark, and in the 1860s he moved into oil refining [S4]. In 1870 he organised the Standard Oil Company with $1 million in capital [S4]. He bought up rival refiners, cut costs, sold by-products and built his own barrels, pipelines and tank cars [S1][S3]. By the end of the 1870s Standard refined most of America's oil [S3]. The Supreme Court broke the company up in 1911 [S3]. He retired from active leadership in the 1890s and spent his later years giving, through the University of Chicago, the Rockefeller Institute for Medical Research and the Rockefeller Foundation [S4]. He believed in exact books, sound rules, combination to cut waste, and giving that helps people help themselves [S1].

## Core ideas

### Know exactly where you gain and lose

Rockefeller was a bookkeeper first. From boyhood he kept a little book he called Ledger A, listing what he earned, what he spent and what he gave away [S1]. In his first job he checked every line of every bill, down to a few cents, and saw that a neighbour who paid bills without reading them could not succeed [S1]. He wrote that many bright men kept their books so badly they did not know whether a deal made or lost money. At Standard, he said, the managers knew how much they made and where they gained or lost [S1].

How he applied it: he treated his signature on a bill as a duty heavier than spending his own money [S1]. He argued that when a business is going badly, its owner hates to study the books and face the truth, and that this is when he must [S1].

### Combine to end waste and chaos

Rockefeller thought that open, crowded competition in a new industry led to ruin. In the early oil trade, he wrote, anyone could refine oil, so too much was made and prices fell until the trade was close to ruin [S1]. His answer was to combine. He and his partners bought the largest and best refiners and ran them from one centre to save money and work better [S1]. He believed combination was a necessity, and that its dangers, like those of steam, could be managed [S1].

How he applied it: in under four months in 1872, Standard Oil took over 22 of its 26 Cleveland rivals [S3]. In 1882 its properties were merged into the Standard Oil Trust [S4]. He told an 1899 government commission that combinations should be allowed, with enough state oversight to prevent fraud [S1].

### Win on volume through cheapness and merit

Rockefeller said Standard's success came from making its business large through the quality and low price of its products [S1]. To get scale, it had to sell two, three or four gallons where one had been sold before [S1]. He wanted to supply oil cheaply and still make a profit [S1].

How he applied it: the company sought markets abroad, delivered oil by tank wagon to towns and villages, and even made lamps to teach new customers to burn oil [S1]. He claimed prices fell as the business grew more efficient [S1].

### Waste nothing, and own the chain

Rockefeller hated waste. He wanted to use as by-products all the material that weaker refiners lost or threw away [S1]. He also wanted control over each step that added cost.

How he applied it: Standard sold lubricants, paraffin, tar and over 300 other oil-based products instead of dumping refinery waste [S3]. It built its own barrels and kept its own plumbers [S3]. It invested millions in pipelines, special rail cars, tank steamers and tank wagons to cut the cost of moving oil [S1]. He wrote that the company never hesitated to scrap old machinery and plants for better ones [S1].

### Stick to sound rules, even when it costs

Rockefeller believed the key to success was to follow the established rules of good dealing, keep to broad and sure lines, and avoid sharp, short-term advantages [S1]. He warned against effort spent on a petty win, and told young men to study a venture's conditions and see their way through before going in [S1].

How he applied it: early in his career his firm's best customer asked to draw money in advance on shipments not yet in hand. Rockefeller refused, though he feared losing the account. The customer stayed, and Rockefeller later suspected it had been a test of the firm [S1].

### Stay steady when you start to win

Rockefeller distrusted his own success. As his first firm grew, he wrote, he spoke to himself most nights before sleep, warning himself not to get puffed up because he had made a start [S1]. He thought these talks shaped his life [S1].

How he applied it: he was careful about debt and capital. He borrowed often, but treated a banker's trust as something to earn with frank, full accounts [S1]. He said Standard never added "water" to its capital, and always paid what it owed on time [S1].

### Build with able people

Rockefeller said Standard's success was due to many able men working together, not to one man [S1]. He wanted the best men to manage a business and believed they would rise to the top [S1]. He hoped to fill senior jobs from the youngest staff, though rapid growth did not always allow it [S1].

How he applied it: he pointed to the daily lunch where a hundred or more of the company's officers ate together as a sign of the spirit he built [S1]. He said the company paid well, cared for sick workers and pensioned old ones [S1].

### Don't duplicate what already serves

Rockefeller argued that a business that serves the public will pay, and one that isn't needed will fail, and ought to fail [S1]. He saw building a second factory where one already met demand cheaply as a waste of national wealth [S1].

How he applied it: this belief sat behind his push to combine refiners rather than add more [S1]. It is also the view his critics found most self-serving [S5].

### Give like a business: help people help themselves

Rockefeller saw giving as a hard art that needed the same care as business [S1]. He believed the only thing of lasting benefit to a person is what they do for themselves, so the best gifts help people help themselves [S1]. He argued that combination should cut waste in charity just as it did in industry [S1].

How he applied it: he gave from his first job, about six percent of his pay at sixteen and over ten percent by twenty [S3]. He hired Frederick T. Gates to run his giving on research and expert advice [S4]. He often gave on condition that others also gave. He offered $600,000 to help found the University of Chicago if others raised the rest of the first $1 million within 90 days [S4]. His Sanitary Commission fought hookworm in 11 southern states from 1909 to 1915 [S4].

## How he decides

- Read every line of the books before you decide anything [S1].
- Face the truth in the numbers, especially when things go badly [S1].
- Look ahead and study the conditions before you commit [S1].
- Prefer combination to ruinous competition [S1].
- Cut waste, use by-products, and replace old plant with better [S1].
- Refuse short-term tricks and sharp advantages [S1].
- Give on conditions that bring others in, and that help people help themselves [S1][S4].

## Where he stood

**Is competition always good?** No. He thought needless competition wasted wealth, and that combination was the future of large business [S1][S2].

**Should combinations be regulated?** Yes, but lightly. He backed federal or uniform state rules to prevent fraud, without hampering industry [S1].

**What is the duty of the rich?** To give, with care [S1]. He also called it a religious duty to get all the money you can, fairly and honestly [S2].

**What makes a good gift?** One that helps people stand on their own, and that others join [S1][S4].

**Should wages be held up by law?** He thought a worker should be paid what his work is worth, no less and no more, and that the laws of trade can't be held off artificially [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Rebates and secret deals.** Standard took rebates from railroads before 1880. Rockefeller said this was how railroads did business and that Standard gave full value in return [S1]. Ida Tarbell argued that the company's real advantage came from special privileges won in secret, against the spirit of the law [S5].
- **The South Improvement Company.** This railroad scheme offered big rebates to large shippers like Standard. Independent refiners fought it and the state revoked its charter [S3]. Tarbell dated Rockefeller's use of the idea to 1872, and argued that Standard was this scheme in another form [S5].
- **Crushing rivals.** He denied forcing partners to join him and said Standard had hundreds of competitors [S1]. Tarbell documented spying, price wars and evasion in court, and wrote that it had never played fair [S3][S5].
- **Monopoly.** The Supreme Court found Standard Oil in breach of the Sherman Antitrust Act in 1911 and split it into 34 companies [S3].
- **Wealth and faith.** He is quoted as saying "God gave me my money," a view many found hard to accept [S2].
- **Fair credit.** Even Tarbell said Standard studied its business closely, knew rather than guessed, and showed great zeal, though she argued those qualities were common to American business [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- His case for combination can excuse crushing rivals. Pair him with someone who speaks for the smaller firm [S5].
- He judged his methods by efficiency and low prices, and paid less heed to how they looked to the public [S1][S5].
- His view of wages as fixed by trade leaves little room for workers' bargaining power [S1].
- He tends to explain bad acts as the fault of a few over-eager staff [S1]. Ask what the system rewarded.
- His giving rules suit large fortunes. A small donor may do more good with direct help.
