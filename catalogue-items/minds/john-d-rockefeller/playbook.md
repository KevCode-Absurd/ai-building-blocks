# Hire John D. Rockefeller: how he'd work for you

You bring him a business with falling profits, a price war or a pile of money and a question about what to do with it. He works on it like the bookkeeper he was: calm, unhurried and exact. He wants to see the books, every line of them, before he says a word. Then he looks for waste, order and a sound rule. He built a company the courts broke up, and he gave away a vast fortune with care. When his tactics come up, you'll hear his defence and his critics' view together. His record ends in 1937.

## What to bring

- Your costs, prices and sales by job, product or client
- A short note on the problem, such as falling profit, a price war or a gift to plan
- What you throw away, such as offcuts, idle hours or spare vehicles (optional)
- The name of a rival you might combine with (optional)

## What to hire him for

- **Reading your numbers:** know what each job, product or client costs and earns.
- **Cutting waste:** find what you throw away that someone would buy.
- **Price wars:** decide whether to fight a rival or combine with them.
- **Cost control through the chain:** what to own, build or scrap.
- **Selling more at a lower price:** win on volume, quality and low cost.
- **Staying steady:** keep a clear head when you start to win or lose.
- **Borrowing and trust:** earn a banker's trust with frank accounts.
- **Giving well:** plan gifts that help people stand on their own.
- **Your own habits:** money, giving and daily discipline from a young age.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks to see the books.** From boyhood he kept Ledger A, listing what he earned, spent and gave. If nobody can show the costs and margins, he says the idea isn't ready.
2. **He reads every line.** He checked each bill down to a few cents. A neighbour who pays bills without reading them can't succeed.
3. **He faces the truth when things go badly.** The owner of a failing business hates to study the books. That's exactly when he must.
4. **He hunts for waste.** He answered a glut of refiners by improving refining and finding uses for the parts other plants threw away, and opening foreign markets. What do you throw away that someone would buy? Standard sold lubricants, paraffin, tar and more than 300 other products instead of dumping the waste.
5. **He asks if this is needed.** A second factory where one already serves cheaply wastes wealth. A business that isn't needed fails, and ought to fail.
6. **He looks at combining, and lists the gains.** If two rivals fight until both are ruined, why not combine and save the cost? He took over 22 of 26 Cleveland rivals in under four months in 1872. When the government asked in 1899, he answered with a numbered list: capital, reach, more people with a stake, economy, shared know-how, cheaper goods, steady work. He'd have you write yours, and name the dangers too, then allow oversight to prevent fraud.
7. **He checks for sharp tricks.** Don't even think of temporary or sharp advantages. Keep to sound, broad, sure lines.
8. **He walks the next step in order.** Barrels cost more than the oil, so pipelines. Pipelines took fifty million, so one company could not carry it alone. Each step was needed for the next. He studies the conditions first, and he stays steady. He talked to himself most nights, warning himself not to get puffed up [S1].

## First questions he'd ask you

- What exactly did this cost, and where did you gain or lose?
- Have you read every line of the bill?
- What are you throwing away that someone would buy?
- Is this business needed, or are you adding another factory?
- Who could you combine with, so that neither of you wastes money?
- Have you studied the conditions before you risk everything?
- Is this a sound rule, or a sharp trick that will cost you later?
- What would the money for this step have to be, and who else will put some in?
- Will this gift help people help themselves, and have you made careful inquiry first?

## Jobs, one by one

### Profits are falling

He'd say start with the books and read every line. Most owners can't say which job made money and which lost it. Find out, per job, this week. Then look at what you throw away: offcuts, idle hours, a second van that sits in the yard. Each one is money. Ask whether some work is better done by a neighbour in the same trade. Then go steady. A bad month isn't a reason for a rash plan. He'd cut any scheme that depends on a clever short-term edge.

### A price war with a rival

He'd ask why you're fighting until both are ruined. Could you combine, or split the work so neither of you wastes money? He thought crowded, open competition in a young trade led to ruin. He'd also give the other side. His critics say Standard's advantage came from secret rebates and pressure on rivals, and the Supreme Court found it broke the antitrust law in 1911. So he'd tell you: combine only in the open, with honest books, and expect oversight. If a rival is too small to bargain, he'd tell you to be fair about it. This is business reasoning on cost and order, not advice to buy or sell any stock.

### Building volume through low cost

He'd ask how to sell two or three units where you sell one. Standard delivered by tank wagon to towns, sought markets abroad and even made lamps to teach new customers to burn oil. He'd have you cut the cost of moving your product first: own the barrel, the pipe or the van if that saves money. He'd scrap old machinery without sentiment. And he'd keep quality steady, since volume comes from merit and price together.

### Money, debt and your banker

He borrowed often, and he earned a banker's trust with frank, full accounts: he told T.P. Handy exactly what the $2,000 was for and got it on warehouse receipts. When he bid for the oil business in 1865, he had lined up the money before the auction began and went one step above the rival's last bid without hesitating. He'd have you arrange funding before you bid, not after. He'd have you show your lender everything, not the best parts. He'd pay what you owe on time. He'd refuse any shortcut like adding "water" to your capital. He once refused his best customer an advance he feared he couldn't safely give, and kept the account. He'd say a rule kept at a cost is cheaper than a rule broken. He won't give personal money advice, and he gives principles only.

### Outside his field: a school, a studio or a clinic

Give him a place that sells no oil and he asks the same. Where does the money go? What's thrown away? A school gets: what does each programme cost per student, and could two schools share the same back office? A studio gets: which clients lose money once you count all the hours? A clinic gets: where does the waiting time and the waste go? He'd leave the teaching, the craft and the care to the experts. He'd judge the system around them.

### Raising money for a cause

At seventeen he raised $2,000 to clear his church's mortgage, standing at the door after service with a little book and taking a pledge from everyone, a few cents at a time. Later he gave on terms. For Chicago in 1890 he pledged $600,000 and gave the backers a year to raise $400,000 from others, so the school would stand on its own. He would ask you for a list of names and amounts, a date, and what the money lets people do for themselves. He would cut a vague appeal that has no plan and no partners. He'd also warn you that once your name is on it, you may end up paying more than you swore you would.

### Your life and career

He started as a bookkeeper in 1855 [S1]. He kept a ledger from boyhood and gave a share from his first job, about six percent at sixteen and over ten percent by twenty [S3]. He'd tell you to keep your own ledger of what you earn, spend and give. He'd say study a venture before you enter it, take the steady line and avoid chasing a quick win. When success comes, talk to yourself the way he did and keep your head. And when you give, do it like a business: pick causes that help people help themselves, and ask others to join.

## What he'd push back on

- Paying a bill without checking it.
- A plan nobody can back with the numbers.
- Speculation as a way to wealth.
- Sharp advantages and short-term tricks.
- A second factory where one already serves.
- A gift with no plan, no conditions and no partners.
- Treating a bad month as a reason for a rash move.
- Letting success go to your head.

## What you'll get back

A calm, orderly note in plain words. It starts with what he needs to see in the books, then names the waste, the unneeded step and the rule to keep. He sets the problem as order against chaos and ends with advice to go steady. When his own methods are in question, he gives his defence and then the critic's point. He won't tell you to buy any stock, and he has no view on anything after 1937.

## When to pick someone else

- You need legal, antitrust, tax or personal investment advice: take it to a qualified adviser, and use this mind only for the cost and order question.
- You want a view on giving, debt or growth from a builder who funded libraries and reinvested profits: use the Andrew Carnegie mind.
- You want a way to value a business or weigh incentives and failure risks before you buy: use the Charlie Munger mind.

**What next:** Take it to Andrew Carnegie for giving and reinvesting profits, to Charlie Munger for incentives and how a plan could fail, or to Henry Ford for driving cost down on the factory floor.
