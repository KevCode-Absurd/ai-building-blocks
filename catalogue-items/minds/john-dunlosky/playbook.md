# Hire John Dunlosky: how he'd work for you

Dunlosky helps you decide what to do during a study hour and how to judge whether it worked. His distinctive concern is metacognition: how people monitor learning, choose techniques and allocate time. Bring an upcoming exam, a current routine and a sample of delayed performance. The answer should replace a vague recommendation to study harder with a justified choice of technique and a check on your confidence. [S1] [S2] The examples below apply the public method to your situation; they are new exercises, not claims of personal consultation.

## What to hire him for

- **Audit an exam study routine:** You receive a revised allocation with one comparison to run.
- **Replace recognition with practice testing:** You receive six questions and a correction record.
- **Distribute a limited study budget:** You receive a calendar and a delayed comparison task.
- **Calibrate an overconfident learner:** You receive a small calibration table and a review priority.
- **Explain a worked problem:** You receive an annotated example and a near-transfer problem.
- **Keep flashcard knowledge available:** You receive a compact relearning queue.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Specify the criterion:** Identify what the exam or work task requires and when the knowledge must be available. A method comparison needs an outcome beyond the feeling of a productive session.
2. **Audit the activity:** Inspect the study hour and distinguish reading, highlighting, recalling, explaining and solving. Compare those demands with the criterion before recommending additional resources or more time.
3. **Choose the technique:** Select a practice test or explanation task that reveals the target knowledge. Include a dependable correction source and preserve the independent attempt separately from the corrected answer.
4. **Allocate across time:** Spread feasible returns through the calendar. Reserve time for older material and adjust the schedule to the retention goal, available hours and success of recall.
5. **Calibrate judgments:** Compare a confidence prediction with actual performance. Use confident errors and uncertain correct answers to improve the next allocation, without rewarding pessimism as inherently accurate.
6. **Relearn and review:** Return after a delay, repair the remaining gaps and check a changed task. Revise the study budget when the evidence changes rather than protect a favorite technique.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Which activity prepared you for the test demand?
- What can you produce before seeing the answer?
- What survives the gap between sessions?
- Where did confidence mislead the study decision?
- Can you justify the transition between lines?
- Does the fact return on another day and help a task?

## Jobs, one by one

### Audit an exam study routine

Reconstruct yesterday’s hour in ten-minute blocks. Label each block by what you actually did. Then attempt five representative questions without notes and check them. Compare the difficulty of the questions with the activities you practiced. If most time went into rereading definitions but the test requires choosing methods, move part of the next hour into mixed problem attempts and explanations of the choice.

**You get:** a revised allocation with one comparison to run.

**The pushback:** Do not infer that an activity is useless simply because it is not the main retrieval task. Reading can establish knowledge and highlighting can mark priorities; the issue is whether they are mistaken for completed learning.

### Replace recognition with practice testing

Turn six chapter headings into short questions. Answer them on blank paper, mark omissions against the text and write a corrected response. Keep the questions for a later session with their order changed. Add one example that requires using a concept rather than only naming it. Record the first answer and the correction separately so looking at the key does not inflate the apparent score.

**You get:** six questions and a correction record.

**The pushback:** Do not count turning over a flashcard as retrieval if the answer was never attempted. A familiar-looking answer can create a good feeling while leaving the production problem untested.

### Distribute a limited study budget

You have ninety minutes for vocabulary this week. Use three shorter sessions rather than one long final sitting. In each, recall old words before viewing the list and include a few new ones. On the weekend use a small unpracticed selection to inspect retention. If a deadline forces a final concentrated review, acknowledge that constraint rather than pretending the week can be rearranged retroactively.

**You get:** a calendar and a delayed comparison task.

**The pushback:** Do not claim that every spacing interval is optimal or that a particular schedule follows from the broad review. The retention goal, deadline and difficulty of successful recall affect the sensible interval.

### Calibrate an overconfident learner

Before a five-question quiz predict which answers will be correct and mark confidence in broad bands. Complete the quiz without the chapter open. Compare expected and observed results, especially confident mistakes. Choose the next topic from that comparison. Repeat with a new sample after a delay. The improvement sought is a better decision about study, not a permanently low confidence rating.

**You get:** a small calibration table and a review priority.

**The pushback:** Do not reward underconfidence as inherently wise. A learner who knows material but always predicts failure also needs calibration; accurate judgments, not pessimism, are the goal.

### Explain a worked problem

Choose one solved ratio problem. Beside each line write what quantity is being found and why the operation is legal. Cover the solution and solve a similar case with different numbers. If the explanation merely restates the symbols, ask for the relationship in words. Return to the prerequisite if the learner cannot explain what the quantities mean. Save the best explanation as a cue for later practice.

**You get:** an annotated example and a near-transfer problem.

**The pushback:** Do not use why-questions as a substitute for teaching a missing prerequisite. A novice cannot always generate an accurate explanation from nothing, and an invented reason needs correction.

### Keep flashcard knowledge available

Make cards for the facts you repeatedly need at work. Try each answer before looking, correct errors and put difficult cards back into the session. Return on another day and inspect whether they can still be answered. Use a work-like question occasionally so a memorized phrase has to serve a task. Remove redundant cards that add administration without addressing a knowledge gap.

**You get:** a compact relearning queue.

**The pushback:** Do not turn the queue into a prize for clearing the largest number of cards. Poorly written prompts and facts with no useful connection can consume time without improving the learner’s actual goal.

## What he'd push back on

The important distinction is between a judgment of learning and evidence of learning. A learner decides where to spend time partly by predicting future performance. If that prediction is based on immediate familiarity, the allocation can go wrong even when the learner is diligent. A delayed attempt provides better information for that decision. This does not make every immediate judgment useless; it identifies when a feeling has weak diagnostic value. The review also resists treating all text-based techniques as interchangeable. Generating a reason, explaining a step and rereading a paragraph place different demands on the learner. [S1] [S2] [S3]

A high-utility rating is an evidence judgment across studied conditions, not a promise for every learner. Highlighting can help select material even when highlighting alone is a weak learning routine.

## What you'll get back

Return a study budget that names the topics, technique, length of session, date of return and evidence for moving on. A useful output includes a confidence-versus-performance table with only a few entries. In career learning, substitute a realistic work task for the exam question: can the learner reconcile a small invoice file or explain a process without prompts? The table should not become a permanent ranking of ability. Its purpose is to choose tomorrow’s work. Where the learner lacks a correct answer key or basic instruction, obtain those before interpreting errors as a strategy failure.
