# Hire John Gottman: how he'd work for you

You bring a recurring argument about who organizes household work. This mind helps you notice bids for attention and replace destructive argument habits with daily repairs. Expect a practical educational application of the public work, with original examples and language you can adapt.

## What to bring

- A recurring argument or a recent moment that went badly
- What happened in the last argument, one exchange at a time
- What you want the other person to do, in positive terms
- Which concern from the other person could change your plan
- When you could return to the talk if you need a pause

## What to hire him for

- **Respond to a missed bid for attention:** When a partner points out a bird while you cook, briefly look and respond instead of attending only to urgent requests.
- **Rewrite an accusatory complaint:** Describe dishes left in the sink, your overload and a request to alternate cleanup, instead of issuing a verdict about laziness.
- **Repair an interrupted conversation:** After interrupting, stop and offer the floor back, then check whether the other person is ready to restart.
- **Plan a weekend with different needs:** Before arguing about a weekend booking, separately name a wish for adventure and a wish for rest.
- **Build a small daily check-in:** Set aside a brief evening check-in and ask about something your partner found difficult without immediately offering advice.
- **Life and career:** Use the method on one real commitment, disagreement or request for help, and keep your choice separate from the other person's reaction.
- **Outside the usual field:** For an adult team, examine a meeting where requests for input receive no response. Invite people to name the input they need and agree how an unavailable colleague can acknowledge it. Avoid calling silence contempt or assuming a marriage finding predicts team performance. The useful transfer is noticing small exchanges and repairing a missed response, with normal workplace roles and practical obligations kept explicit.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Notice bids:** Look for one small attempt to share attention that tends to pass unnoticed. Describe what a response would look like without demanding constant availability or treating every missed bid as rejection.

2. **Soften the opening:** Turn the recurring accusation into one observed event, a feeling and a positive request. Read the opening aloud and remove any verdict about the other person's character or permanent intentions.

3. **Pause escalation:** If the exchange becomes too heated to hear, acknowledge the escalation and agree a workable pause. Name how you will return, so the break does not become unexplained disappearance.

4. **Accept influence:** Ask which concern from the other person should change your proposal. State the revision explicitly and keep your real constraints visible, making influence something observable rather than a ceremonial promise.

5. **Offer repair:** Notice an interruption, harsh phrase or missed response you can repair. Offer a small acknowledgment and a restart, then check whether the other person can receive that repair now.

6. **Practice friendship:** Choose one daily friendship practice small enough for a busy week. Review whether it creates actual attention and warmth rather than becoming a count of who performed more caring actions.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What happened in the last argument, one exchange at a time?
- What was the small request for attention you may have missed?
- What do you want your partner to do, in positive terms?
- How did the conversation begin?
- What repair have you offered, and was it received?
- Which concern could reasonably change your proposed plan?
- When can you return if you need a pause?
- What small moment of friendship would fit this week?

## Jobs, one by one

In every job, check your words against what actually happened. Don't add a motive you can't know, and don't expect the other person to feel a certain way.

### Respond to a missed bid for attention

Connection can be a small acknowledgment. You need not drop every task, but make a genuine response and say when you can offer more attention. Distinguish distraction from deliberate rejection before interpreting a missed bid. The working output is a conversation map, a repair sentence and a seven-day connection experiment.

### Rewrite an accusatory complaint

Ask for the positive change you want. Keep the complaint about one event, because a catalogue of past failures makes a practical discussion harder. A calmer opening still permits a firm account of the problem. The working output is a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

### Repair an interrupted conversation

A repair needs a receiver as well as a sender. Notice whether the attempt actually reduces tension. If it does not, acknowledge that without insisting the other person reward you for trying. The working output is one clear action and the condition under which you will take it.

### Plan a weekend with different needs

Knowing the meaning can reveal options beyond competing bookings. Ask which parts are essential and which can move. Understanding the dream beneath a preference does not require buying the preferred solution. The working output is two feasible options and a question that helps you choose.

### Life and career: the next commitment

You and a partner keep arguing about the bin. In this original practice case, the bin is one visible part of a larger issue: one person notices the work, asks for help and feels alone; the other hears only criticism. Start with one recent evening. Rewrite the complaint around the actual task and the burden of noticing. Ask the other person to describe what they heard. Agree that responsibility includes noticing, doing and checking completion, then try one clearly owned task for a week. Separately practice acknowledging ordinary bids, so every conversation is not an overdue work request. When using this for a life or career choice, name the decision due now and the support you can reasonably ask for. Keep your own values and responsibilities visible. A conversation can clarify a choice without allowing this mind to choose your relationship or career for you.

### Outside the usual field: an everyday collaboration

For an adult team, examine a meeting where requests for input receive no response. Invite people to name the input they need and agree how an unavailable colleague can acknowledge it. Avoid calling silence contempt or assuming a marriage finding predicts team performance. The useful transfer is noticing small exchanges and repairing a missed response, with normal workplace roles and practical obligations kept explicit. The output is a short written plan with a review date. This is an analogy from the method, not proof that it works in this new setting.

## What he'd push back on

Observation of couples can inform a question without providing a verdict about an individual relationship. Do not quote a divorce probability from a short chat, turn conversational counts into a score of love or assume a mutual communication exercise fits intimidation. A small repair is worth practicing, but it cannot make another person safe or willing. Also challenge a plan that treats "small bids deserve an answer" as a trick to get compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.

## What you'll get back

You receive a conversation map, a repair sentence and a seven-day connection experiment. It starts with the situation as understood, names the central distinction and gives original wording or a small action to try. It also states what remains uncertain and what would make the exercise inappropriate. Bring back what actually happened if you want a revision; a difficult response is information for the next decision, not proof you performed the method incorrectly.

## When to pick someone else

- You feel unsafe or controlled by a partner: contact a local helpline or a trained professional, because this mind cannot make another person safe.
- You want a diagnosis of a partner or child who is not in the chat: ask a licensed professional, since this mind does not diagnose people.
- You want to work on courage, shame and being seen: use the Brené Brown mind instead.

**What next:** Take it to Julie Schwartz Gottman for the clinical side of couples work, to Sue Johnson for the attachment pattern behind a stuck argument, or to Esther Perel for questions of desire and closeness.
