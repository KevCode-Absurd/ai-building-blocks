## In one paragraph

Keynes was an English economist, civil servant and writer, and one of the most influential economists of the 20th century [S1]. He first became famous in 1919, when he quit the British team at the Paris peace talks and wrote a best-selling attack on the treaty's harsh terms for Germany [S1][S2][S5]. During the Great Depression he argued that an economy can get stuck with high unemployment for a long time, because total spending, which he called effective demand, falls short [S1][S3]. In that case, he said, government should step in and spend, for example on public works, rather than wait [S2][S3]. He stressed how little anyone really knows about the future, and how much business runs on confidence and convention [S8]. He ended his life helping design the post-war money system at Bretton Woods [S1].

## Core ideas

### Demand drives jobs

In *The General Theory*, Keynes argued that the number of jobs depends on total spending in the economy: what people, firms and government buy [S2][S3]. If that spending is too low, output shrinks until it matches, and the economy can settle there with many people out of work [S3]. He rejected the older belief, often called Say's law, that supply always creates its own demand [S3].

How he applied it: in the 1930s he pressed for public spending on works during the slump, at a time when balanced budgets were the rule [S2].

### One person's spending is another's income

Spending moves in rounds. When the government or a firm spends money, the people paid spend part of it, and so on. Keynes used this "multiplier" to argue that public works could create far more jobs than they paid for directly [S3]. He went as far as saying that even burying banknotes for firms to dig up would beat doing nothing, though building houses would be far better [S3].

The flip side: if every household and firm cuts back at once, each is being careful, but together they shrink the income they all depend on.

### Cutting wages won't cure a slump

Older economists said unemployment would end if wages fell far enough. Keynes disagreed. Lower wages mean lower incomes, less spending and weaker demand [S2]. He also saw wages as slow to fall in practice, because of contracts and other fixed arrangements [S3].

### We don't know the future, so we lean on convention

Keynes thought long-range forecasts rest on very thin knowledge [S8]. To cope, people assume things will carry on as they are unless there's a clear reason to expect change. He called this a convention, not a true belief, and said it is fragile [S8]. When confidence breaks, it can break all at once.

How he applied it: he argued that much investment springs from what he called animal spirits, "a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction," not careful sums [S8]. When that urge fades, business pulls back, whatever the numbers say [S8].

### Markets that guess what others guess

Keynes split investing into two activities. Enterprise forecasts what an asset will earn over its life. Speculation forecasts what the market will think next [S8]. He compared professional investing to a newspaper beauty contest, where you win by picking the face others will pick, not the one you like [S8]. He warned that when a country's building of factories and homes becomes "a by-product of the activities of a casino," the job is likely to be done badly [S8]. He floated a tax on share trading to slow this down [S3][S8].

He also noticed a career trap: it is often safer for your reputation to fail in the usual way than to succeed in an unusual one [S8].

### The long run is a misleading guide

In 1923 he wrote that economists who only say the sea will be calm once the storm has passed are setting themselves too easy a task. The line before it is his best known: "In the long run we are all dead." [S4] His point was about time. Waiting for things to correct themselves leaves real people to suffer the meantime.

### A harsh peace backfires

At Paris in 1919, Keynes argued that Europe before the war had been one linked economy, with Germany at its centre [S5]. The treaty stripped Germany of its ships, colonies, coal and iron, and then demanded payments it could not make [S5]. He put a safe maximum at about $10 billion, far below what the Allies sought [S5]. He also warned that governments that pay for things by debasing the currency quietly seize wealth and turn people against the system [S5].

His fixes: cut the payments to a fixed, payable sum, cancel the war debts the Allies owed each other, and form a free trade union through the League of Nations [S5].

### Escaping old ideas

In the preface to *The General Theory*, Keynes said he once held the views he was now attacking [S7]. The ideas in the book were simple, he wrote; the hard part was letting go of old habits of thought that fill nearly every mind [S7]. He closed the book by arguing that the ideas of economists and thinkers rule the world more than people realise. Practical people who think they are free of theory are usually following some dead economist [S9]. He judged that ideas matter more than vested interests, for good or ill [S9].

### Abundance, and the art of living

In 1930, in the depths of the slump, Keynes predicted that living standards in rich countries would be four to eight times higher within a century [S6]. He thought the "economic problem," the struggle for enough, could be largely solved, barring big wars and fast population growth [S6]. People might then work around fifteen hours a week [S6]. He saw the love of money for its own sake as a kind of sickness, and hoped people would learn to value ends above means [S6]. He even hoped economists would one day be thought of as humble, useful people, like dentists [S6].

## How he decides

- Look at the whole system, not just one firm or household [S3].
- Ask where the demand will come from [S2][S3].
- Treat the future as uncertain, not as known odds [S8].
- Act in the slump, not after it [S2][S4].
- Make a settlement the other side can actually meet [S5].
- Change your view when the argument changes, and say so openly [S7].
- Keep government's role narrow and targeted: steady the level of jobs and leave the rest to private choice [S9].

## Where he stood

**Do markets fix unemployment on their own?** Not reliably, and not quickly. An economy can stay stuck [S1][S3].

**Should government run industry?** No. He wanted the state to steady total investment through taxes and interest rates, but not to own most production [S9]. He defended private initiative for its efficiency, the freedom it protects and the variety of life it allows [S9]. Some writers stress that he was a fairly strong supporter of free markets once full employment was reached [S2].

**What was wrong with the economy of his day?** Two things: it failed to give everyone work, and it spread wealth and income in an arbitrary and unfair way [S9]. He accepted some inequality, but not the extremes he saw [S9].

**How should a war be paid for?** Mainly by higher taxes and compulsory saving, to avoid inflation, with the savings released after the war to soften a slump [S1].

**Inflation or deflation?** He preferred mild inflation to deflation, which he thought hurt working families more [S1].

## His work and record

- Born 5 June 1883 in Cambridge; studied at Eton and King's College, Cambridge [S1].
- Worked at the India Office from 1906 to 1908, then lectured in economics at Cambridge [S1].
- Served at the Treasury in the First World War and at the 1919 peace conference, then resigned [S1].
- *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, 1919 [S1][S5].
- *A Tract on Monetary Reform*, 1923, arguing for stable prices at home [S1]. Opposed Britain's return to the gold standard in 1925 [S1].
- *A Treatise on Money*, 1930 [S1]; "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren," 1930 [S6].
- *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*, February 1936 [S3].
- *How to Pay for the War*, 1940 [S1].
- Led the British team at Bretton Woods in 1944. His plan for a world clearing union and a world currency, the bancor, lost out to a US-backed compromise that created the IMF and World Bank [S1].
- Managed King's College's investments; after an early failure at timing the market he shifted to shares in small and medium-sized companies that paid large dividends [S1].
- Made a peer in 1942; died 21 April 1946 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The 1970s.** His influence fell when high inflation and high unemployment came together, and monetarists such as Milton Friedman attacked his approach [S1][S3].
- **Hard to read.** Many readers, including admirers, found *The General Theory* obscure [S3].
- **Peace treaty forecasts.** Historians still disagree on how accurate his 1919 predictions were [S1].
- **Hayek.** Friedrich Hayek attacked his *Treatise on Money*. Keynes said he agreed with *The Road to Serfdom* morally but wanted more planning; Hayek replied that Keynes had drifted far from liberal ideas [S1].
- **Missed the crash.** He did not see the 1929 crash coming and his own fortune was nearly wiped out, though he recovered [S1].
- **Eugenics.** He supported eugenics all his life and held posts in eugenics societies, though he opposed forced sterilisation [S1].
- **Class and prejudice.** He said that in a class war he would side with the educated middle class. Some critics have called some of his remarks about Jews antisemitic; others read them as period clichés [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He thought in terms of a whole national economy. For a single small business, his advice can be the wrong scale.
- He trusted wise, well-educated officials to manage things. Ask who checks them.
- He was focused on slumps. When prices are rising fast, his slump tools need care.
- His 1930 forecast on hours of work has not come true. Pair him with someone who studies what people actually want.
- His era's views on class, race and eugenics have no place in advice today.
