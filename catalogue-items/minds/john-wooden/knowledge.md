## In one paragraph

Wooden was a high school English teacher who became the most successful coach in American college basketball [S1][S5]. At UCLA he won 10 national titles in 12 years, seven of them in a row from 1967 to 1973, and his teams once won 88 games straight [S5]. He cared less about the score than about how his players prepared and behaved. In 1934, frustrated that parents only wanted top grades and fans only wanted wins, he wrote his own definition of success: peace of mind from knowing you made the effort to become the best you could be [S1][S4]. Around that idea he built a Pyramid of Success, a set of character blocks from hard work and friendship at the base to competitive greatness at the top [S2]. He taught small things with great care, including how to put on socks [S6], and he believed that discipline should correct people, not punish them [S6].

## Core ideas

### Success is doing your best, not beating others

Wooden rejected the dictionary idea of success as wealth, power or status [S1]. His definition was peace of mind that comes from knowing you made the effort to become the best you are capable of becoming [S1][S4]. Only you can judge that, he said, because only you know if you gave your best [S1]. He traced the idea to his father, who told his sons never to try to be better than someone else, to learn from others, and never to stop trying to be the best they could be, because that part was under their control [S1].

How he applied it: he judged himself and his players by effort and preparation, which they could control, rather than by results, which they could not fully control [S1].

### The Pyramid of Success

The pyramid is a set of building blocks for character and performance [S2]. Its base holds industriousness, friendship, loyalty, cooperation and enthusiasm. The next tier holds self-control, alertness, initiative and intentness. Then come condition, skill and team spirit, then poise and confidence, and at the top, competitive greatness [S2]. Hard work and enthusiasm are the two cornerstones, and he said there is no real substitute for hard work [S1][S2]. In his talk he added faith and patience near the top: young people mistake change for progress, and older people give up too soon, so everyone needs patience and the belief that things will work out if they do what they should [S1].

How he applied it: he defined competitive greatness as performing at your best when your best is required [S5], and saw it as loving a hard challenge because it brings out your best [S2].

### Teach the small things first
In the 1996 interview he added that he taught players to fit their shoes snugly, about a size smaller than they wanted, and to be courteous to staff, which he saw as building team spirit [S7]. 

On the first day of practice, Wooden spent around half an hour showing new players how to put on a sock, because wrinkles lead to blisters, and blisters hurt play [S6]. The lesson stood for a larger habit. Skill sits at the centre of his pyramid as the mastery of details, a lifelong job [S2].

How he applied it: his practices focused on conditioning, fundamentals and team play, and he spent little time scouting rivals [S6].

### Prepare, then trust it
He scouted rivals less than almost any college coach, and put his effort into his own team and being ready for every case [S7]. He said self-imposed pressure is healthy and pressure from outside is a weakness [S7]. He avoided hyping teams up emotionally, because peaks are followed by valleys [S7]. His family's page adds that, if he had prepared players well in practice, he could watch the game from the stands [S9]. 

Confidence, on his pyramid, is self-belief built on thorough preparation, kept in check so it doesn't turn into arrogance [S2]. Poise means staying steady and true to your principles under pressure [S2]. His family's site keeps the maxim that failing to prepare is preparing to fail [S3].

How he applied it: one of his best-known sayings was "Be quick but don't hurry" [S6], which asks for speed with control. He also warned that if emotions take over, you will be outplayed [S3].

### Discipline corrects, it doesn't punish

He held that the purpose of discipline is to correct, not to punish or humiliate, and that yelling did no good [S6]. He was patient and fair on small things and firm on big ones [S6].

How he applied it: when a centre turned up with a full beard against team rules, Wooden praised the player for standing by his beliefs and calmly kept the rule. The player shaved within twenty minutes [S6]. He dropped a coat-and-tie rule for road trips as fashions changed, but kept his core rules [S6].

### A few simple rules

In his TED talk, he listed the rules he used with players: be on time, starting and ending practice on the dot; be neat and clean; no profanity; and never criticise a teammate, because criticising was the coach's job [S1]. He wanted players to put education first, basketball second and social life third, since basketball paid for their schooling [S1].

How he applied it: Bill Walton, one of his star players, was once not allowed on the team bus because he wasn't neat and clean. He had to go home and change before heading to the airport [S1].

### Team before self
He saw reserves as essential, using the picture of an engine and its wheels, and spent extra time with players who did not play much [S7]. 

Team spirit, on his pyramid, means putting the group's success ahead of personal glory [S2]. Cooperation means sharing ideas, tasks and credit [S2]. Loyalty flows to leaders who care for and respect their people [S2]. He disliked showboating and coaches who sought attention [S6].

How he applied it: his teams were built to find the open teammate and to avoid putting the whole scoring load on one shooter [S6].

### The teacher is the lesson

Wooden saw himself as a teacher first [S1]. He quoted a verse in his talk saying that teachers shape young people more by who they are than by the books they use [S1]. His father read poetry to the family by an oil lamp, and Wooden quoted poems all his life [S1]. His father also gave him a seven-point creed when he finished grammar school, which included being true to yourself, making each day your masterpiece, helping others and making friendship a fine art [S5].

How he applied it: he took pride that his former players became lawyers, doctors, dentists and teachers [S1].

## Real decisions

### Writing his own definition of success, 1934

As a young English teacher in South Bend, Wooden was frustrated that parents wanted only A's and B's and that alumni judged coaches almost only by wins and margins [S1]. He found the dictionary idea of success, built on possessions, power and prestige, too narrow, and wrote his own: peace of mind from knowing you made the effort to do your best [S1]. The base of it came from his father, who told him not to measure himself against others and not to fret over what he could not control [S1]. In 1996 he said he then spent 14 years working on the Pyramid, keeping industriousness and enthusiasm fixed while changing the other blocks [S7]. His family's scrapbook dates the Pyramid to 1931 and his definition to the same period, so the dates differ by source [S9].

### Refusing the tournament over a Black player, 1946 to 1948

At Indiana State his team earned a place in a post-season tournament, but the NAIB would not allow a Black player on his team to take part [S7][S8]. Wooden declined the first invitation, and when the rule changed in 1948 he accepted, taking Clarence Walker, who became the first Black player in that tournament [S8]. The player still faced segregation on the trip [S7]. Wooden credited his father's teaching that no one should expect special privileges, and said he never saw color [S7].

### Taking the UCLA job, then staying, 1948 onward

In 1948 Minnesota was his first choice, but a snowstorm disrupted its call and UCLA's offer arrived first, so he accepted it [S8]. Early on he nearly left for Purdue because of poor facilities and a two-year contract, but stayed when UCLA agreed to three years [S7]. Later he preferred one-year renewals that worked as an ongoing commitment [S7]. UCLA won the Southern Division in his first year, and the first national title came in 1964 [S8].

### Keeping the full-court press, 1963

Wooden said he had given up a full-court press in the 1950s too early. In 1963 he kept it because his players had the speed for it, and he tied the 1964 undefeated title to that choice [S7]. His recruiting followed from it: he would trade some height for quickness at every position [S7]. He also scouted rivals less than most coaches, and spent his time on his own team and on preparing for any case [S7].

### Treating players as different people

He said he first gave every player the same pregame routine and meal, then learned that they needed different things, such as a player who wanted pancakes and another who ate chili and never tired [S7]. He called himself a slow learner on this [S7]. He spent extra time with reserves, using the picture of an engine and its wheels to say every role mattered [S7].

### Changing a word in his book after Wilt Chamberlain

Wooden had written about "handling" players, and at a press conference Chamberlain said he was a person, not a thing [S7]. Wooden changed the book to say "working with" players [S7]. He told it as an example of being willing to admit a mistake and change.

### Enforcing the rule, then letting it go

He kept his few rules firm, but dealt with a breach on the spot and did not carry it into the next day [S7]. When two players came in with facial hair against his standard, he gave them a deadline to fix it, they did, and he talked with them afterwards [S7]. He also sent Bill Walton home to change before boarding the team bus, and the bus left without him [S1].

### Announcing his retirement before the 1975 final

Two days before UCLA's last game for the 1975 title, Wooden said he would retire whatever the result [S8]. The family page gives no reason for the timing [S8]. UCLA won, and he went on to teach through clinics, books and speeches [S8].

## Where he stood

**Is winning the goal?** No. Doing your best is the goal. Winning can follow, but it is not the measure [S1].

**Who decides if you succeeded?** Only you, because only you know if you gave your best [S1].

**What comes first for a student athlete?** Education, then the sport, then social life [S1].

**How should a coach treat players?** With care, fairness and firm standards. Discipline is for correction [S6].

**What makes a great performer?** Hard work and enthusiasm at the base, built up through character to competitive greatness [S2].

- A three-time consensus All-American guard at Purdue, 1929 to 1932, then a pro in the National Basketball League [S5].

- First person in the Basketball Hall of Fame as both player (1960) and coach (1973) [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The booster Sam Gilbert.** A wealthy UCLA supporter gave players cars, clothes, flats and trips. A 1981 *Los Angeles Times* investigation found Wooden knew Gilbert was close to players and should have known much more [S5]. Wooden said he chose not to ask players to cut ties and that he may have trusted too much [S5]. A 1981 NCAA case put UCLA on probation for two seasons and ordered it to cut ties with Gilbert [S5].
- **Not every player loved him.** Edgar Lacy, benched early in the famous 1968 game against Houston, quit the team and said he had never enjoyed playing for Wooden [S5].
- **A lost lead.** In the 1974 loss to North Carolina State, UCLA led by 11 and by 7 in overtime. He said he should have called a timeout sooner [S7].
- **Credit for tactics.** His assistant Jerry Norman's zone press, adopted in 1964, helped UCLA win its first title [S5]. The Wooden story can hide the work of the people around him.
- **A hard act to follow.** Of the nine coaches after him at UCLA, only Jim Harrick won a title [S5]. The weight of his record made life hard for those who came next.

## Blind spots to watch

- His world was a top college team, with gifted recruits and a strong school behind him. A volunteer group or a new business may not have that talent or support.
- "Don't think about the score" can be a luxury when results decide whether a team survives. Pair him with someone who watches the numbers.
- His rules come from a different time (dress codes, haircuts). Keep the reason behind a rule, and update the rule.
- He trusted people deeply, which helped his teams and also let a problem like Gilbert grow [S5]. Trust, then check.
- He speaks from a Christian and Midwestern frame of faith and family [S5]. Don't push that frame on people who don't share it.
