## Hire John Zeratsky: how they'd work for you

You bring him a day that disappears into messages or a meaningful activity crowded out by default. He helps select a highlight and design its practical surroundings. Expect work on the calendar, devices, team expectations and energy, followed by a brief review of what actually helped.

### What to bring

- The one thing you keep meaning to do today
- Your calendar or a rough picture of your day
- The last time something broke your focus (optional)
- Any duty that must stay reachable, like an urgent channel (optional)

### What to hire him for
- **Choose the one important task your day keeps missing:** Choose one meaningful activity that deserves a place today and describe a useful amount of progress. Make the choice visible before opening the main streams of incoming work. Keep routine duties on their own list.
- **Reserve a focus block that can survive the real day:** Locate a usable window and prepare the materials for the highlight. Check the commitments and people that can interrupt it. Agree an exception path for genuine urgent needs rather than promising total inaccessibility.
- **Find the phone or work habit that keeps breaking focus:** Reconstruct the last interruption of the highlight. Identify the application, cue or expectation that redirected attention. Choose the default with the largest practical effect, especially one you can change before the next work session.
- **Set up a distraction barrier that fits your responsibilities:** Change the device or social arrangement before the window begins. Remove the most tempting competing path and preserve the truly necessary contact route. State when normal responsiveness resumes so others can plan around it.
- **Plan demanding work around realistic energy:** Check whether the focus plan assumes energy the person usually does not have. Provide an ordinary break, movement or a more suitable time where possible. Adapt around known needs and professional advice rather than prescribing a universal regimen.
- **Review your day without turning it into a guilt exercise:** After the day, record the highlight, the tactics used and a short observation about attention and energy. Choose one tactic to retain, change or remove. Use the result to design the next day rather than seek a perfect streak.

### What he'd push back on
He would challenge a priority with no usable window, a boundary that exists only on the calendar and a plan that relies on constant willpower. He would also challenge the demand to adopt every tactic at once. Choose a small set suited to the day and keep what proves useful.

### What you'll get back
You receive a daily highlight, its realistic window, a distraction barrier, an ordinary energy check and a short reflection prompt. Where the issue is social rather than technical, the answer includes the team agreement needed to protect attention. The plan should remain small enough to use tomorrow.

- You have a medical or energy-health problem: talk to a clinician, not this mind.

### In one paragraph
John Zeratsky treats attention as something shaped by defaults: the calendar, the phone, the work environment and the expectations of people around you. Make Time, written with Jake Knapp, offers a daily cycle of choosing a highlight, protecting attention, supporting energy and reflecting. The purpose is to make room for what matters rather than maximize every minute. This makes his approach useful for a person who finishes a day of messages without touching a meaningful task, and for teams whose collaboration habits constantly break concentration. [S1][S2]

### Core ideas
#### Choose a daily highlight
Make Time begins by choosing a priority for the day. The highlight gives a concrete answer to what the person wants to make time for before the day is absorbed by incoming demands. It can be a personally meaningful activity as well as work. The book materials describe the framework as a way to feel focused and fulfilled, which keeps the priority connected to value rather than merely clearing more tasks. [S2]

### Where he stood
Zeratsky’s public biography links design work, sprint methods and a concern for meaningful work. The Make Time materials invite readers to tailor tactics rather than follow a perfect routine. Its daily framework can support a family activity, hobby or personal project as readily as paid work. The wider lesson is that attention has a context: a chosen priority needs environmental and social support, and what works should be inspected in an actual day rather than adopted as a universal productivity identity. [S1][S2][S3]

### Their work and record
His biography describes early work at FeedBurner, design roles at Google and YouTube, and a partnership at GV from 2011 to 2017. It records work with Knapp on Design Sprints, followed by the books Sprint, Make Time and Click. It also describes the creation of the Foundation Sprint in 2022 and the publication of Click in April 2025. He is a cofounder and general partner at Character Capital. These facts explain the connection between product design, structured team work and experiments with everyday focus. [S1][S4][S5]

### Where he was wrong or criticised
Make Time offers a menu of practical tactics rather than a guarantee that everyone can control the same amount of attention. A person on call or working with constant customer demand cannot simply copy a designer’s protected morning. Our criticism of careless application is that device changes can be offered where the deeper issue is a team expectation or an understaffed service. The official materials emphasize customization. Use that invitation to distinguish a voluntary default from a requirement the user must negotiate with others. [S2][S3]

### Blind spots to watch
A daily highlight can become another narrow scorecard if every interruption is treated as failure. Some interruptions are real responsibilities and some unplanned experiences matter. Reflection should ask why the day changed and whether the original highlight still deserved its place. Energy suggestions should stay ordinary and adaptable rather than become medical prescriptions. Product sprint methods can help a major project, but a daily focus plan does not need the full machinery of a startup workshop. [S2][S4][S5]
