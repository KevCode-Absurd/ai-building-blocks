# John Zeratsky: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that John Zeratsky advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
John Zeratsky treats attention as something shaped by defaults: the calendar, the phone, the work environment and the expectations of people around you. Make Time, written with Jake Knapp, offers a daily cycle of choosing a highlight, protecting attention, supporting energy and reflecting. The purpose is to make room for what matters rather than maximize every minute. This makes his approach useful for a person who finishes a day of messages without touching a meaningful task, and for teams whose collaboration habits constantly break concentration. [S1][S2]

## Core ideas
### Choose a daily highlight
Make Time begins by choosing a priority for the day. The highlight gives a concrete answer to what the person wants to make time for before the day is absorbed by incoming demands. It can be a personally meaningful activity as well as work. The book materials describe the framework as a way to feel focused and fulfilled, which keeps the priority connected to value rather than merely clearing more tasks. [S2]

**An original application:** A person has a full message queue and wants to prepare for a career conversation. Choose drafting the three points for that conversation as today’s highlight. The inbox remains part of the day, but it no longer silently defines success. State what a useful draft would contain and when it can happen. A clear highlight is smaller and more observable than the broad aim of improving a career.

**Where this goes wrong:** A highlight that contains an entire project cannot guide a normal day. Choose a coherent piece that can be recognized when it has happened.

### Give the priority a real window
A priority needs a practical opportunity to happen. Make Time’s broader framework joins choosing the highlight with tactics for protecting attention and supporting energy. A visible choice alone does not remove calendar conflicts. The useful adaptation is to reserve a window that fits the person’s obligations, materials and available concentration, rather than wait for an ideal gap that the day may never provide. [S2]

**An original application:** The career-conversation draft needs twenty quiet minutes and access to recent project notes. Choose a realistic slot before a routine meeting, gather the notes beforehand and set a limited finish. If a caregiving duty makes that window unreliable, choose a smaller version or an alternative. The calendar should represent an executable arrangement rather than an optimistic claim on someone else’s time.

**Where this goes wrong:** A calendar block can look protected while the team expects immediate replies. Address the expectation instead of repeatedly moving the block.

### Identify the default that captures attention
The Make Time site frames the work as rethinking defaults of busyness and distraction. That directs attention to the environment and repeated habits rather than relying entirely on resistance in the moment. A default might be opening email first, leaving a feed beside the document or accepting every meeting invitation. The first useful diagnosis is specific: what reliably redirects attention when the person intended something else? [S3]

**An original application:** A writer opens a browser to find one fact and spends the next ten minutes on a news feed already present in another tab. The relevant default is the starting browser state. Close unrelated tabs before the next session and keep a note of research questions so searching has a clear purpose. The diagnosis is more useful than a general instruction to be disciplined.

**Where this goes wrong:** A distraction list can become an inventory of everything enjoyable. Focus on the default that conflicts with the chosen activity, not on judging all leisure.

### Create a barrier around the highlight
The Laser part of Make Time uses tactics to reduce distraction during the chosen activity. The book page explicitly mentions adjusting devices and applications. The goal is to make attention easier by changing the path into a competing activity. The appropriate barrier depends on the job: removing a feed, changing notification settings or agreeing response windows may help without cutting off the channels a person genuinely needs. [S2]

**An original application:** A support worker cannot silence the urgent service channel, but can close optional social tabs and turn off nonurgent notifications for a short drafting block. A colleague covers routine questions until the block ends. Keep an urgent contact path open. The barrier now fits the work’s real duty of care while reducing the distractions that do not serve it.

**Where this goes wrong:** An extreme barrier that breaks a real responsibility will not remain credible. Design the boundary around the user’s actual role and exceptions.

### Support attention with ordinary energy
Make Time includes Energize because focus is connected to the body and the conditions of the day. Its public framework places energy alongside attention rather than treating concentration as a disembodied act of will. This supports ordinary planning around rest, movement, food and breaks. The catalogue applies the principle as a practical check of conditions, without claiming an individualized health protocol or a guaranteed boost. [S2]

**An original application:** Someone plans their most demanding writing immediately after a long sequence of calls and skips lunch to preserve the slot. Try a different arrangement: a break and ordinary meal before a shorter session, or move the work to a period when they tend to be more alert. Record what happens. The point is to notice a bodily constraint rather than buy a complicated performance routine.

**Where this goes wrong:** Energy advice can become another optimization project. Keep the change simple and avoid interpreting every tired day as failure to manage the body correctly.

### Reflect and keep only useful tactics
The Reflect stage asks what worked, what did not and what to try next. The official book page presents the tactics as a customizable menu for imperfect people. This makes a day information about the design, rather than a permanent verdict about personal ability. Reflection can retain, adjust or drop a tactic. It also allows the original highlight to be reconsidered when new circumstances made another activity more important. [S2]

**An original application:** At the end of a day, note whether the career-conversation draft happened, whether the notification boundary helped and what interrupted it. If a real urgent issue displaced it, plan a new window instead of adding guilt. If the boundary worked but the task was too large, shrink the finish. Keep the observation brief enough that it supports tomorrow rather than becoming another report.

**Where this goes wrong:** Changing every tactic at once makes it difficult to see what helped. A reflection should guide a practical adjustment, not grow into a second task-management system.

## How he decides
- **Choose a daily highlight:** Choose one meaningful activity that deserves a place today and describe a useful amount of progress. Make the choice visible before opening the main streams of incoming work. Keep routine duties on their own list.
- **Give the priority a real window:** Locate a usable window and prepare the materials for the highlight. Check the commitments and people that can interrupt it. Agree an exception path for genuine urgent needs rather than promising total inaccessibility.
- **Identify the default that captures attention:** Reconstruct the last interruption of the highlight. Identify the application, cue or expectation that redirected attention. Choose the default with the largest practical effect, especially one you can change before the next work session.
- **Create a barrier around the highlight:** Change the device or social arrangement before the window begins. Remove the most tempting competing path and preserve the truly necessary contact route. State when normal responsiveness resumes so others can plan around it.
- **Support attention with ordinary energy:** Check whether the focus plan assumes energy the person usually does not have. Provide an ordinary break, movement or a more suitable time where possible. Adapt around known needs and professional advice rather than prescribing a universal regimen.
- **Reflect and keep only useful tactics:** After the day, record the highlight, the tactics used and a short observation about attention and energy. Choose one tactic to retain, change or remove. Use the result to design the next day rather than seek a perfect streak.

## Where he stood
Zeratsky’s public biography links design work, sprint methods and a concern for meaningful work. The Make Time materials invite readers to tailor tactics rather than follow a perfect routine. Its daily framework can support a family activity, hobby or personal project as readily as paid work. The wider lesson is that attention has a context: a chosen priority needs environmental and social support, and what works should be inspected in an actual day rather than adopted as a universal productivity identity. [S1][S2][S3]

## Their work and record
His biography describes early work at FeedBurner, design roles at Google and YouTube, and a partnership at GV from 2011 to 2017. It records work with Knapp on Design Sprints, followed by the books Sprint, Make Time and Click. It also describes the creation of the Foundation Sprint in 2022 and the publication of Click in April 2025. He is a cofounder and general partner at Character Capital. These facts explain the connection between product design, structured team work and experiments with everyday focus. [S1][S4][S5]

## Where he was wrong or criticised
Make Time offers a menu of practical tactics rather than a guarantee that everyone can control the same amount of attention. A person on call or working with constant customer demand cannot simply copy a designer’s protected morning. Our criticism of careless application is that device changes can be offered where the deeper issue is a team expectation or an understaffed service. The official materials emphasize customization. Use that invitation to distinguish a voluntary default from a requirement the user must negotiate with others. [S2][S3]

## Blind spots to watch
A daily highlight can become another narrow scorecard if every interruption is treated as failure. Some interruptions are real responsibilities and some unplanned experiences matter. Reflection should ask why the day changed and whether the original highlight still deserved its place. Energy suggestions should stay ordinary and adaptable rather than become medical prescriptions. Product sprint methods can help a major project, but a daily focus plan does not need the full machinery of a startup workshop. [S2][S4][S5]
