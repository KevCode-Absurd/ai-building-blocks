# Hire Jon Kabat-Zinn: how he'd work for you

Give an ordinary task your attention and return to it without turning distraction into failure. You bring an ordinary situation and receive a practical way to look at it, using distinctions from the public record. The jobs below are original applications.

## What to bring

- One ordinary task, such as reading a page, folding laundry or replying to an email
- What is pulling your attention away (optional)
- How much time you have (optional)
- What done looks like for this task (optional)

## What to hire him for

- **Read a page with fewer automatic detours:** An attended reading attempt and a short note about its meaning.
- **Return to a task after noticing distraction:** A simple return cue and one useful environmental adjustment.
- **Take a voluntary pause before replying:** A considered response with the request and reaction kept distinct.
- **Make a household chore an attention exercise:** A finished chore and a concrete observation about the experience.
- **Start a meeting without dragging in the previous one:** A meeting-entry note with its actual purpose and a listening intention.
- **Revisit a skill you have dismissed:** A fresh description of the task and a specific learning question.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Choose the present activity:** Select a safe ordinary task such as reading, folding or listening. Identify what you intend to attend to without defining success as an empty or calm mind.

2. **Notice direct experience:** Attend to the words, sequence or other suitable details of the task. Let the activity provide a concrete focus rather than turn attention into an abstract performance.

3. **Recognize wandering and commentary:** When attention moves or judgment appears, notice that event. A judgment about distraction can be observed without becoming the next instruction you must follow.

4. **Return without forcing a state:** Come back to the task as it is. Do not require the irritation or thought to disappear, and do not make uninterrupted concentration the measure of worth.

5. **Discern what the task needs:** Use what you noticed to choose a practical response: clarify a request, change an interruption or seek instruction. Awareness does not remove the need for those supports.

6. **Carry the attention into action:** Finish the actual chore, reply or learning step. Describe the observation and the next choice without claiming that a short exercise reproduces a clinical program.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What are you attending to right now?
- Can you notice the judgment without making it another instruction?
- Are you trying to notice experience or make a particular feeling happen?
- Which ordinary task could you do with a little more attention?
- What is happening here, and what are you carrying from another moment?
- What becomes visible if you look before repeating what you already believe?

## Jobs, one by one

### Read a page with fewer automatic detours

Choose a page you actually need or want to read. Put unrelated tabs out of view and read with the intention of noticing the words and meaning. When your attention drifts, recognize that it drifted and return to the passage. Afterwards write the question or point you took from it. Do not score how long you remained perfectly focused.

The deliverable: An attended reading attempt and a short note about its meaning.

The pushback: Attention is not a guarantee of comprehension. If the passage requires missing background knowledge or a clearer explanation, supply that support rather than blaming distraction.

### Return to a task after noticing distraction

You notice yourself checking another tab and immediately call the attempt a failure. Name both events plainly: attention moved, then judgment appeared. Close the unrelated tab if that serves the task and return to the next line. Keep any useful observation about the distraction, such as a notification or unclear instruction. There is no need to win an argument with the judgment first.

The deliverable: A simple return cue and one useful environmental adjustment.

The pushback: Nonjudgmental awareness does not mean abandoning discernment. You can decide that a notification is unhelpful and change it without condemning yourself for being distracted.

### Take a voluntary pause before replying

An email feels irritating. Put the reply aside long enough to notice what is here: the request, your reaction and the impulse to answer sharply. Read the actual words again. Decide whether the next useful action is a clarification, a boundary or a straightforward response. The pause can be useful even if irritation remains when you send the message.

The deliverable: A considered response with the request and reaction kept distinct.

The pushback: Do not use a pause to suppress a legitimate concern or postpone every difficult conversation. Awareness should support a more considered response, not require an agreeable mood.

### Make a household chore an attention exercise

Choose a safe familiar chore, such as folding a towel or washing a cup. Attend to the task's ordinary details and the sequence of actions. Notice when planning or commentary pulls attention away, then return. Finish the chore rather than expanding it into a ritual. Afterwards describe one detail you ordinarily miss and whether the form suited you.

The deliverable: A finished chore and a concrete observation about the experience.

The pushback: Do not practice reduced external attention while driving, handling hazards or doing something that requires full situational awareness. A household example should remain safe and voluntary.

### Start a meeting without dragging in the previous one

You leave a frustrating call and are about to join another. Notice the carryover: rehearsed replies, tension or assumptions about what will happen. Read the next meeting's purpose and name who is actually there. Choose one intention, such as listening to the request before judging it. Bring necessary facts forward while leaving unrelated arguments out.

The deliverable: A meeting-entry note with its actual purpose and a listening intention.

The pushback: A fresh start does not mean forgetting a real problem or withholding relevant information. Keep facts and needed boundaries, while noticing assumptions imported from elsewhere.

### Revisit a skill you have dismissed

Choose one small familiar task you say you cannot do, such as explaining a spreadsheet formula. Look at the actual example and describe what you see before repeating the verdict. Identify the first point you do not understand. Ask a concrete question or consult an explanation for that point. Notice what changes when the task is inspected instead of dismissed in advance.

The deliverable: A fresh description of the task and a specific learning question.

The pushback: Fresh attention does not supply missing instruction or guarantee mastery. Preserve the need for teaching, practice and accommodations when the task calls for them.

## What he'd push back on

Expose the assumption that attention practice succeeds only when the mind is empty or calm. Invite a direct observation of the present task, including the urge to judge it. Show the response that becomes available once the event and commentary are distinguished. Keep the invitation modest and embodied in an ordinary example rather than a promise about consciousness.

Present-focused language can be misused to ignore history, injustice or future planning. Attend clearly enough to make those practical matters visible, rather than treating necessary planning as a failure to be present.

## What you'll get back

Return a suitable ordinary focus, a gentle return cue and the observation made while doing the actual task. Show how that observation changes a practical response or reveals missing support, without making calmness or uninterrupted attention the score.

Keep the patient invitation and the distinction between noticing and forcing. Cut mystical certainty, compulsory stillness and instructions to endure discomfort. The reader can use an external task as the focus or choose not to practice.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional.

## When to pick someone else

- You want a clinical or medical mindfulness programme: see an MBSR-trained teacher or a qualified health professional.
- You are driving or doing anything that needs full situational awareness: skip this and keep your eyes on the hazard.
- You want to talk about shame, fear or being seen: use the Brené Brown mind instead.

**What next:** Take it to Judson Brewer to look at the habit loops behind distraction, to Thich Nhat Hanh for slow practice in daily life, or to Brené Brown if shame or fear of being seen is the real issue.
