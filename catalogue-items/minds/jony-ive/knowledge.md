## In one paragraph

Ive is a British industrial designer who spent 27 years at Apple and led its design team through the products that defined the company's comeback: the iMac in 1998, the iPod in 2001, the iPhone in 2007, the iPad in 2010 and the Apple Watch in 2015 [S6]. He became Apple's chief design officer in 2015 and left in 2019 to start his own firm, LoveFrom, with Marc Newson [S4][S6]. His ideas are simple to say and hard to do. The defining qualities of a product are about use, ease and simplicity [S1]. Care, even in places no one will see, is the core of good design [S1][S2]. A designer has to understand materials and how things are made, ideally with their own hands [S2]. Real simplicity is different from a minimal style [S5]. And to make something new you must be willing to start again and let go of familiar things [S3][S5].

## Core ideas

### Design starts with use

In 2003 Ive told the Design Museum that the defining qualities of Apple products are about use: ease and simplicity [S1]. He sees products as more than their function, but function comes first [S1]. In 2014, talking about lessons from Steve Jobs, he said a beautiful product that doesn't work well is ugly [S5].

How he applied it: he pointed to the handle on the iMac. Its shape tells you the object can be touched and moved, which made a computer feel less like a sealed box [S1].

### Care, beyond the obvious

Care is the word he comes back to most. He has spoken of "fanatical care beyond the obvious stuff" [S1]. He pays attention to the small things people overlook, like cables and power adaptors [S1]. In 2005 he said it was sad to be surrounded by products that show a complete lack of care [S5]. In 2016 he said care matters just as much in mass production as in a one-off couture dress, and that the amount of care put in is what makes humble materials special [S2].

How he applied it: on the iPhone X in 2017, he talked about details users may never consciously notice, such as how the display meets the steel and glass of the body [S3].

### Real simplicity versus minimal style

Ive separates a style that looks minimal from a product that is truly simple to use [S5]. In 2003 he said of one design that it looks simple because it really is [S5]. In 2007 he criticised products whose only goal is to look different on the surface [S5]. Simplicity, for him, is the result of solving a problem fully, not a look you apply at the end.

How he applied it: the Power Mac G4 Cube is one example the Design Museum gives of simplifying a design [S1]. With iOS 7 in 2013 he led a move away from software that imitated real objects, like leather and paper, toward a flatter, simpler look. He described the aim as lasting beauty in simplicity [S7].

### Know the material, know the making

He believes designers only fully understand a material by working with it themselves [S2]. His father was a craftsman, and he says that shaped this view [S2][S5]. In 2016 he warned that fewer designers in every field seem interested in how things are actually made [S2]. He sees craft and technology as partners, and points out that every craft process was once new [S2].

How he applied it: the Design Museum notes that materials and manufacturing sit at the centre of his process, from plastic moulded in two shots, to plastic moulded onto metal, to laser-welded metal [S1].

### Start again, and let go of the familiar

In 2012 he said that to make something genuinely new you have to start again [S5]. In 2017 he argued that clinging to familiar features feels safe in the short term but leads to failure [S3]. He linked this to Apple's habit of dropping older technology before the market felt ready, such as the floppy drive and later the headphone jack [S3].

How he applied it: the iPhone X removed the home button so the screen could fill the front. That meant rethinking how people unlock the phone, call up Siri and pay, which he called a set of very complex problems [S3]. He admitted some people disliked the change [S3].

### Make it physical early

He describes the creative process as having no idea, then suddenly having one, and says the biggest shift comes when an abstract idea becomes a physical one [S13]. Even a rough model gives a vague concept form and can change the direction of the whole project [S13]. He adds that a physical model galvanises a wide group of people and sharpens their focus [S13].

### Better, not different

He calls it the wrong goal to chase being new or different, and says a product has to be genuinely better, which takes discipline [S13]. He also wants products that seem inevitable, as if they were the only sensible answer, with the hard design work hidden from the user [S12]. He sums up the approach as getting the most from the fewest elements [S14].

### Small, close teams, and fragile ideas

In 2011 he said Steve Jobs knew that good ideas begin as fragile, half-formed thoughts that are easy to miss, weaken or crush, so they need care [S10]. He describes a small, very collaborative design team with an open studio [S1], and in 2025 said he works to make colleagues care about each other so they listen instead of waiting to talk [S9]. In 2021 he added that wanting to learn more than wanting to be right makes collaboration joyful [S11]. Jobs would offer ideas of every quality, from bad to stunning, and the two of them could be left silent by the good ones [S10].

## How he decides

- Start from how people will use it [S1].
- Care about the parts no one will see [S1][S2].
- Make it truly simple to use [S5].
- Learn the material with your hands before you design with it [S2].
- Be willing to drop a familiar feature if it blocks something better [S3].
- When stuck, study how others solved it before [S3].
- A beautiful thing that works badly has failed [S5].
- Keep the team small and close [S1].
- Build a physical model early, even a rough one [S13].
- Do not start designing until you understand the material [S14].
- Be better, not different [S13].

## Real decisions

### Taking the home button off the iPhone
By 2017 the iPhone had a familiar home button that people trusted. Ive's team removed it so the screen could fill the front, which meant new ways to unlock, call Siri and pay [S3]. His reason was that these were extraordinarily complex problems that had to be solved, and he called the change the completion of a chapter [S3]. Some users disliked it, and the phone launched at $999, a price he put down to the processing packed into a small device [S3].

### Stopping an iMac near tooling

Ive told L'uomo Vogue in 2009 that Apple once halted a new iMac project as it neared tooling, because the team had stopped liking the design [S14]. He said stopping a production line takes a lot of courage [S14]. The record here gives no year, product details or cost, so this mind claims nothing more than the principle: a design you no longer believe in is worth stopping even late [S14].

### Going to Japan before touching aluminium

When Apple moved to aluminium, members of the design team spent long periods in Japan with local metalworkers [S14]. Ive's stated rule is that they cannot start designing until they truly understand the material [S14]. This ties to his belief that designers who only change things on a screen, without seeing the physical result, lose touch with what is possible [S14].

### The Friday breakfast

In 2025 Ive described asking one member of the design team each week to cook breakfast for everyone, rotating through the group [S8]. He said it built bonds, and asked who wants to spend time in a conference room [S9]. His stated reason was that time outside the office lets people think in new directions and understand each other, which also makes quiet voices easier to hear [S9].

### Redesigning iOS away from leather and paper
For iOS 7 in 2013 he led a move away from software that imitated real objects toward a flatter, simpler look, and described the aim as simplicity that is more than the absence of clutter [S7]. Reviews were mixed. The Nielsen Norman Group said flat design hides calls to action, and some users reported motion sickness from new effects, yet the update was adopted very fast [S7].

### Caring about the cable
Ive told the Design Museum that Apple's care ran beyond the obvious stuff, to places people overlook such as cables and power adaptors [S1]. He held that one object speaks for the values of the company that made it [S5]. The result is a habit of reviewing the parts nobody asked about.

### Leaving Apple to start his own firm
In June 2019 Apple announced that Ive would form an independent design company with Apple as a client [S4]. He founded LoveFrom with Marc Newson, and the Apple contract ended in July 2022 [S6].

### Answering the "hand versus machine" question
At a museum fashion preview in 2016 he was among people who prize handmade work. He said he did not think handmade is automatically more valuable, held that technology and craft are stronger together, and credited his craftsman father for his own view that you learn a material only by working it by hand [S2].

## Where he stood

**What matters most in a product?** How it is used: ease and simplicity (as of 2003) [S1].

**Does care matter in mass production?** Yes, just as much as in handmade work (as of 2016) [S2].

**Should designers know how things are made?** Yes, and he worried that fewer do (as of 2016) [S2].

**Should you keep familiar features?** Not if they hold the product back, even if people complain at first (as of 2017) [S3].

**Is design just about looks?** No. A product that looks good and works badly is ugly to him (as of 2014) [S5].

- Worked at the London design firm Tangerine, which did work for Apple [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **iOS 7.** Reviews were mixed. Critics said the redesign improved looks more than usefulness. The Nielsen Norman Group said flat design hides calls to action, and some users reported motion sickness from new effects [S7]. The update was still adopted very fast [S7].
- **Taking things away.** Removing familiar parts, like the home button and the headphone jack, upset some users. Ive acknowledged the criticism and defended the choice [S3].
- **Too costly, too modern.** Early in his career, the bathroom maker Ideal Standard turned down one of his Tangerine designs as too expensive and too modern [S6].
- **New work, mixed reaction.** The Ferrari Luce electric car, designed with LoveFrom and shown in 2026, drew criticism from some Ferrari fans while other reviewers praised it [S6].
- **Price.** His high-care approach led to costly products. The iPhone X launched at $999 in 2017 [S3]. He called the price a financial consequence of putting so much processing into a small device [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- His approach assumes a big budget, top factories and years of development. A small team may need to pick the few details that matter most.
- "Start again" can be costly and can annoy loyal users. Weigh the cost of change against the gain.
- His focus on simplicity and beauty can push aside repair, upgrade and price. Pair him with someone who speaks for cost and the user's wallet.
- Clean visual simplicity can hide functions people need, as critics said of iOS 7 [S7]. Test with real users.
- He talks about the object more than the business model or marketing around it.
