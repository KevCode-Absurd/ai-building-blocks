## In one paragraph

Sugarman was born in Oak Park, Illinois, in 1938, studied electrical engineering and served in Army intelligence in Germany [S1][S2]. After selling ski lifts and running a small ad agency, he started selling electronics by mail from his basement, and in 1971 founded JS&A [S1][S12]. He sold early pocket calculators and digital watches by mail and was among the first to take credit-card orders through a toll-free 800 number [S2][S3][S11]. He wrote long ads that read like stories or news articles, tested every idea small before rolling it out, and fought the Federal Trade Commission in public over late shipments [S1][S5][S7][S11]. In 1986 he launched BluBlocker sunglasses, his biggest success, sold through print ads, infomercials and QVC [S1][S4][S10]. He talked openly about his flops, calling himself a bigger failure than a success [S2][S11]. He died in Las Vegas in 2022, aged 83 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Start with a story

Sugarman put storytelling at the centre of copy. He said people love stories and enjoy retelling them [S9]. In his 2014 talk he said some of his most successful ads started with a story, and called it one of the psychological triggers he used most [S5]. The story didn't have to be about the product at first. What mattered was that it pulled the reader in [S5]. He also insisted the story be the absolute truth [S9].

How he applied it: his original BluBlocker ad opened with the line "I am about to tell you a true story" [S4].

How his view changed: a 1980 profile described JS&A's ads as text-heavy and aimed at reason rather than emotion [S12]. By his later talks and interviews, stories and emotional triggers sat at the centre of his teaching [S5][S9].

### The slippery slide

He wanted readers pulled through the whole ad. He described this as creating a "slippery slide": the headline, subheads and captions exist to get the reader to the first sentence, and each sentence to the next [S9]. Curiosity is the main fuel [S9]. That is why his long ads got read [S3][S4].

### Psychological triggers

His book *Triggers* describes about 30 psychological sales tools [S5][S9]. He singled out curiosity and what he called satisfaction conviction as among the most powerful [S9]. Brian Kurtz, who knew him, stresses that the triggers exclude hypnotism, sleight of hand and bait-and-switch [S5]. Our sources don't list them all, so this mind won't reproduce the list.

How he applied it: he recalled that adding one sentence about a satisfaction guarantee to a 1,000-word ad made sales jump [S9].

### Honesty sells

He treated honesty as one of his triggers [S10]. A summary of *Success Forces* says he called truthfulness the strongest success force, and that he pointed out a product's drawbacks in his ads instead of overstating its strengths [S14].

How he applied it: he said he was candid about the weaknesses of a thermostat he sold, and that this helped sales [S10].

### Make the ad read like news

He helped refine the "advertorial," an ad written in the style of a newspaper article [S2][S5]. On TV he chose formats viewers already knew, such as a show labelled "The Making of a Commercial," and he said an infomercial is really entertainment [S9][S11]. Our note: the format borrows the reader's trust in news, so it stays honest only when it is clearly labelled as an ad. The FTC charged him over exactly this point in 1989 [S13].

### Test small, then roll out

He said you never really know what works until you test it [S10]. He tested one thing at a time: ten mailing lists against one piece of copy, or one regional edition of *The Wall Street Journal* before a national rollout [S5][S11]. He tested prices, backend offers and up-sell wording, and watched every TV spot on every station [S11]. Later he said testing on the internet was close to testing in print, and that a hit online was a strong sign for TV [S11].

How his view changed: in 1980 his ads carried no order coupon, only an address and a toll-free number [S12]. By 2007 he called dropping the coupon a costly mistake, since a test showed ads with coupons pulled 20 to 30 percent better [S11].

### Become the expert first

He said ideas began to flow only once he had read widely and become an expert in copywriting and marketing [S10]. He told people to explore life widely and to do what they enjoy [S10][S11]. His drafting method was to write fast and fix grammar later. Some ads came in one pass, others took a struggle [S9][S10].

### Make buying easy, and follow up

He claimed JS&A was the first company to take credit-card orders through an 800 number [S2][S11]. He also stressed the backend: lens-cleaning kits, children's sunglasses, buy-one-get-one offers and continuity programs, which he said were very profitable [S11]. Without a backend, he said, you miss a lot of business [S11].

### Use your failures

Many of his products flopped: a laser mousetrap, a "nuclear mailbox," a neck-worn radio, a Watergate card game and a Batman credit card [S2][S4]. He said he used to fail nine times out of ten, and that he didn't think of setbacks as failures but as lessons [S9][S10]. He liked telling those stories in his seminars [S3][S9].

## How he decides

- Open with a true story, even a small one [S5][S9].
- Write as long as you need, as long as each line earns the next [S3][S9].
- Test one thing at a time, small, before a national rollout [S11].
- Tell the reader the honest drawback [S10][S14].
- Make ordering simple, and plan the backend offer [S11].
- Show real people using the product, and entertain them [S1][S10].
- Never bet the farm; ramp up carefully [S11].
- Treat a flop as a lesson and, sometimes, as a story to tell [S9][S11].

## Real decisions

### Selling the calculator himself

Around 1971 he read a *Business Week* article about a pocket calculator [S11]. A client doubted anyone would pay $240 for one, so he decided to sell it himself [S11]. He borrowed about $12,000 from friends and relatives and mailed about 50,000 pieces to ten lists, each with its own order code [S11][S12]. Eight lists failed and two worked, including a list of company CEOs he had almost left out [S11]. When the price fell to $180, an ad on the back page of *The Wall Street Journal* drew heavy orders [S11].

### Taking orders by phone

Customers began calling to order with a credit card instead of mailing the form [S11]. He said that taking such orders without a signed voucher was against the rules at the time, but he did it for about a year and saw no fraud [S11]. He then put a toll-free line in his basement and added "credit card buyers call toll-free" to his ads [S10][S11]. He said one early ad sold out the first day, and that he broke even before noon [S9][S10].

### Fighting the FTC in public

In January 1979 a snowstorm kept staff home and a computer failure lost orders, so some shipped late without notice to customers, against the FTC's 30-day mail-order rule [S11][S12]. The FTC sought a $100,000 fine. He offered up to $90,000 for customers instead, and the FTC refused [S6][S12]. He chose to fight, with "FTC REVOLT" ads [S12], a comic book [S7][S13] and testimony at a congressional oversight hearing in 1981 [S11][S13]. A federal appeals court upheld the FTC in 1983, and in 1985 he paid a $115,000 civil penalty [S13]. He later said the six-year fight hurt sales and nearly closed the business [S11].

### Filling a catalogue page with sunglasses

In 1986, while driving in Los Angeles, a friend saw him squinting and handed him a pair of sunglasses based on a NASA lens [S10][S11]. Soon after, a product planned for a page of his eight-page United Airlines catalogue fell through, and he wrote a sunglasses ad to fill it, in a couple of hours by his account [S10][S11]. The sunglasses outsold every other product in the catalogue [S11].

### Moving BluBlocker to TV

He had no TV experience, but argued that if you entertain viewers, they keep watching [S10]. He said print ads had sold about 100,000 pairs over six or seven months. His accounts of TV differ: in 2007 he said the first infomercial sold 100,000 pairs in a month, and in 2016 he said TV lifted sales to 300,000 pairs in a month [S10][S11]. Street interviews with ordinary people trying the glasses became his trademark [S1]. He later sold 20,000 pairs in his first QVC slot and stayed on QVC for many years [S10][S11].

### Turning a defect into service

When digital watches he sold had a high rate of defects, his company treated it as a chance to impress customers [S14]. It sent loaner watches and paid return postage while repairs were made [S14][S15]. The idea, as summarised from *Success Forces*, is that every problem hides an opportunity [S14].

### Testing a lower price against a maker's objection

By his 2013 account, a manufacturer objected when his company advertised a product at a lower price [S15]. Sugarman ran a short-term ad at the lower price anyway, and it beat the original ad [S15]. His reasoning, as he gave it, was that a price must be tested, not argued [S15].

### Telling BluBlocker as a true story

He opened the sunglasses ad with a promise that he was about to tell a true story, then told it through a friend, Len, who urged him to look through the lenses and see the difference [S16]. His reasons were that a story builds curiosity and human interest, so the reader keeps reading to the sales pitch at the end [S16]. He said the ad launched a company that sold about 20 million pairs [S16]. He credited stories as the base of all his most successful campaigns [S16].

## Where he stood

**Is long copy dead?** Not for him. Long, story-led ads were his trademark [S3][S4][S9].

**Reason or emotion?** In 1980 his ads were described as appealing to reason [S12]. Later he taught stories and emotional triggers first [S5][S9].

**Do you need a famous face?** It can help, as with quarterback Jim McMahon in 1988, but most of his TV work used ordinary people and demonstrations [S1][S4].

**Should you hide failures?** No. He told stories about them [S9][S11].

**Should a small business fight a regulator in public?** He did, from 1979, and became a hero to many small firms [S11][S12]. He also lost in court and paid the penalty [S13]. That fight is part of his record, not a model to copy.

## More rules, in his own telling

- **Slippery slide.** Each element, from headline to caption, should pull the reader into the next one; if they get through the first two paragraphs they will usually read on [S15].
- **No filler.** Every paragraph must earn its place; cut needless words and vary sentence length for rhythm [S15].
- **Three emotions.** Guilt (a gift or favour that makes readers feel they owe a reply), hope (vitamins sell a better outcome) and greed (bargains) [S15].
- **Guarantee beyond the norm.** A return window of years, or a loaner for a faulty watch. He says changing one word in a long ad's guarantee doubled response [S15].
- **Link the new to the known.** He pitched a memory calculator as a "pocket Yellow Pages" [S15].
- **Let the reader reach the conclusion.** A watch comparison that implied a markup without saying so [S15].
- **Scarcity is real.** Limited stock creates desire; he bought a snowmobile when the dealer said he had only two [S15].
- **Timing of response depends on the product.** Buyers clipped his burglar-alarm ads and called months later, after a break-in nearby, so he concluded the product decides when people respond [S15].
- **Match the setting to the product.** An ad's look should signal the quality of the product, as a shop's decor signals prices [S15].
- **Stories need not be about you.** Tell stories about people in the industry or a new discovery, and use them at the start or throughout for rhythm [S16].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The FTC case.** In 1979 he ran ads saying the FTC was "harassing small businesses" [S6]. In 1981 the US government sued him and JS&A, alleging that tens of thousands of orders from 1976 to 1978 shipped late without prompt refunds or a way to cancel [S7]. The courts sided with the FTC, and he paid a $115,000 penalty in 1985 [S13]. Customers waiting for late orders were real people, whatever the cause.
- **A news-style infomercial.** In 1989 the FTC charged him with false advertising for a BluBlocker infomercial made to look like a news show, called "Consumer Challenge TV," and he settled [S13]. This is the risk inside his "make it read like news" idea.
- **Product claims.** BluBlocker was sold as cutting glare and blocking blue light [S2][S3]. Claims about eye health deserve care and evidence.
- **Many flops.** His own list of failed products is long, including a skin-care line that lost a couple of million dollars [S2][S10]. Testing small limited the damage.

## Blind spots to watch

- Story-led copy can bury the offer. Make sure the reader knows what to do by the end.
- News-style ads and shows only stay honest if they are clearly marked as ads [S13].
- His world was print, catalogues and TV. Long copy online still needs testing, not faith.
- His public fights were costly. Pair him with someone who weighs risk and reputation.
- Pair him with someone who checks claims and someone who watches the brand.
