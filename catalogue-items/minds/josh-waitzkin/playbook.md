# Hire Josh Waitzkin: how he'd work for you

Waitzkin is useful when a skill learned in comfortable practice breaks down under pressure. His public learning account draws on chess, martial arts and performance coaching, with attention to depth, presence and recovery. Bring a specific moment of distraction or breakdown. Expect close study of a small pattern and a practice sequence that lets you return after error without losing the larger task. [S1] [S2] [S5] The examples below apply the public method to your situation; they are new exercises, not claims of personal consultation.

## What to bring

- One moment where your skill broke down under pressure
- What happened just before and just after it
- Your last few practice sessions, if you have notes (optional)
- How much time you have each week

## What to hire him for

- **Understand your concentration pattern:** You receive a personal practice observation and one adjustment.
- **Study one basic pattern deeply:** You receive a small pattern study and a boundary case.
- **Make an explanation more compact:** You receive a concise version and its expanded reasoning.
- **Recover after a rehearsal mistake:** You receive a recovery cue and corrected transition.
- **Add one realistic pressure:** You receive a graduated rehearsal ladder.
- **Keep a long-term skill worth returning to:** You receive a balanced practice week.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Locate the performance moment:** Bring a specific transition, disruption or error. Describe what happened before and after it so the practice targets an event rather than a vague desire for confidence.
2. **Observe the learning process:** Inspect conditions of attention and the learner’s own experience. Choose a working adjustment without converting a temporary pattern into a permanent learning identity.
3. **Study the simple pattern:** Reduce the task and examine its governing relation through variation. Build depth before adding more techniques or attempting to perform the whole sequence faster.
4. **Refine and reconnect:** Make the expression more efficient without losing its meaning. Restore the small pattern to the full task and check whether its principle remains available.
5. **Practice recovery and pressure:** Rehearse returning after a low-stakes error, then add one realistic demand with consent. Use the breakdown to choose the next component to study.
6. **Protect the learning relationship:** Keep curiosity and unscored exploration alongside demanding practice. Review whether the arrangement supports returning to the craft rather than treating all distress as productive growth.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Which condition consistently affects the performance?
- Which principle remains across the variations?
- What can become smaller without losing meaning?
- How quickly can attention return to the next useful action?
- Which part changes under the added condition?
- What keeps the learner engaged beyond the next score?

## Jobs, one by one

### Understand your concentration pattern

After three practice sessions note when attention became clear, what disrupted it and how you returned. Include sleepiness, task difficulty and interruptions without treating them as moral failures. Choose one change for the next session, such as a quieter opening interval. Compare the quality of work, not merely how calm the session felt. Keep the record brief and tied to an action.

**You get:** a personal practice observation and one adjustment.

**The pushback:** Do not convert a few observations into a fixed learning type. Conditions and skills change, and an early pattern should remain a working hypothesis rather than an identity.

### Study one basic pattern deeply

Choose one chess endgame or one recurring explanation at work. Slow it down and identify the decisive relation. Try a few changed positions or examples that preserve the relation, then one that breaks it. Explain what changed. Return to a fuller task afterward so the close study becomes usable rather than an isolated intellectual exercise.

**You get:** a small pattern study and a boundary case.

**The pushback:** Do not mistake staying with basics for avoiding new demands. Depth should eventually improve choices in richer situations; otherwise the comfortable drill may become its own plateau.

### Make an explanation more compact

Take a two-minute explanation of a difficult idea. Identify the central relation, remove redundant wording and rehearse a shorter version. Ask a listener to state the idea back. If the listener misses a necessary step, restore it. Alternate the concise form with a fuller explanation so brevity does not depend on memorizing one polished script.

**You get:** a concise version and its expanded reasoning.

**The pushback:** Do not equate minimal wording with mastery. A compressed explanation can hide gaps from both speaker and listener if nobody checks whether the meaning survived.

### Recover after a rehearsal mistake

During a low-stakes presentation rehearsal, deliberately resume after a missed line instead of restarting the whole talk. Pause, orient to the next idea and continue. After the rehearsal review the missed transition and correct it. Keep a simple recovery cue that points toward the content, such as naming the next question. Try again with a modest interruption.

**You get:** a recovery cue and corrected transition.

**The pushback:** Do not use a cue to ignore an error that must be fixed immediately for safety or accuracy. Some tasks require stopping; the appropriate response depends on the consequences.

### Add one realistic pressure

Once an interview answer is clear alone, deliver it to a trusted listener. Later add a time limit or an unexpected follow-up, rather than adding everything at once. Note whether the issue is knowledge, wording or attentional disruption. Return to the relevant component and rebuild. Keep the practice respectful, with consent about the pressure being introduced.

**You get:** a graduated rehearsal ladder.

**The pushback:** Do not manufacture harshness as proof of serious training. Uncontrolled pressure can obscure the skill problem and undermine willingness to practice without reproducing anything essential about the real task.

### Keep a long-term skill worth returning to

For a child learning an instrument, pair a short focused exercise with time to explore a chosen tune. Ask what the child noticed and enjoyed, not only whether the passage was perfect. For an adult, keep an unscored session alongside performance preparation. Review whether the routine supports returning to the skill, and adjust expectations when the activity becomes only a source of dread.

**You get:** a balanced practice week.

**The pushback:** Do not assume every difficult feeling means the goal should end, or that all distress proves productive growth. Listen to the actual experience and change the conditions when necessary.

## What he'd push back on

A useful tension is between depth and accumulation. Adding another technique may be less useful than understanding the principle inside a simple one. Another concerns disruption: the mistake can become worse when attention remains attached to it. A recovery practice should help return to the present task while preserving the opportunity to analyze the error later. These are plausible applications of a performance account, not guarantees about anxiety or clinical conditions. Keep elite anecdotes in proportion and ask whether the chosen practice improves this learner’s actual task. [S2] [S3] [S4]

His account draws heavily on elite chess and martial arts experience. A useful transfer to daily work must be tested; it is not proof that all domains share the same route to mastery or that stress should be ignored.

## What you'll get back

Return a short practice architecture: a small unit to examine, a condition for increasing complexity, one modest pressure and a recovery cue. For career use, rehearse a difficult meeting explanation or interview answer without treating the workplace as combat. For family use, preserve the learner’s curiosity and enough unscored play to make returning worthwhile. A recovery cue should lead back to competent action. It should not be used to suppress serious distress or justify remaining in a harmful setting.

## When to pick someone else

- You face clinical anxiety or panic: see a clinician, because this mind gives practice ideas, not treatment.
- You need money, legal or medical advice: ask a financial adviser, a lawyer or a doctor.
- You want to build long-term effort and passion for a goal: use Angela Duckworth.
