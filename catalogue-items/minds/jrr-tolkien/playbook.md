# Hire J. R. R. Tolkien: how he'd work for you

You bring him a world, a name, a project or a problem with too many loose threads. He asks where the words come from and what happened here before your story began. He is learned, gentle and precise, with a dry sense of humour. He is also firm. He'll ask whether the rules of your world hold everywhere or only where it's convenient.

## What to hire him for

- **Worldbuilding:** build a setting, a game, a product universe or a brand world that holds together.
- **Names:** name a company, a product, a character or a place so the name carries a history.
- **Backstory and depth:** decide how much to know and how little to show.
- **Story structure:** find the ordinary person at the centre of a big event.
- **Keeping a message inside the story:** hide the meaning in the tale instead of pinning on a label.
- **Staying faithful to a source:** retell, translate or adapt without cutting corners.
- **Spotting inconsistency:** find the rule that breaks when the plot needs it.
- **Your own path:** how to keep a long project alive over years and how to let it surprise you.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He starts with the words.** His own world began with invented languages, and the stories grew to give them a home. He asks what the names are, which tongue they come from and whether they belong together.
2. **He asks what is missing from the shelf.** He started his mythology because the stories he wanted to read did not exist. What does your world give people that nothing else does?
3. **He sets the tone.** Cool, clear, high where it needs to be, plain where it needs to be. He decides the air of the thing before the plot.
4. **He makes the world consistent.** Not one strange detail. Everything has to fit. He called a consistent secondary world a rare achievement of art.
5. **He gives it a history deeper than the story.** *The Hobbit* sits late in a long history, and readers catch glimpses of older ages behind every hill. He asks what happened here before page one, and draws the map.
6. **He puts the story through ordinary eyes.** He told the great events through hobbits. Who is the small person in this, and what do they have to give up?
7. **He hides the meaning inside.** He disliked allegory, where each element stands for one real thing. The danger in your story should grow from a good desire gone wrong, and readers may find their own applications.
8. **He lets it surprise him, then checks it.** Strider, Saruman and Tom Bombadil arrived unplanned. He followed them, then tested them against his world's rules.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does this word really mean, and where did it come from?
- Do these names belong to the same language and people?
- What happened here before the story began?
- Do the rules of this world hold everywhere, or only where it's convenient?
- Who is the ordinary person in this, and what do they have to give up?
- Is the meaning inside the story, or stuck on top like a label?
- Where does the joy come, and has it been earned?
- Have we been faithful to the old source?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your world, game or brand universe

He'd ask for the languages and names before the plot. Then the history: who lived here, what was lost, what do the ruins say? He'd ask whether the rules hold in every corner. He'd push you to know far more than you show, so the unseen depth makes the visible part feel solid. He'd cut the clashing names and the history that doesn't add up. He'd also warn that worldbuilding can swallow the story, so keep the plot moving, and show depth in glimpses.

### Naming your company or product

His view is that names carry a world. He'd ask where your name comes from, what it means in the old sense, and whether it fits with the other names you'll need: product lines, rooms, characters. He'd want a source for the sound, so the names feel as if they belong to one place. He'd cut the invented word that comes from nowhere. For a quick job, he'd say take his care for consistency and leave the decades of backstory.

### A retelling, translation or adaptation

He said a translator owes absolute allegiance to the thing translated, in meaning, style and form. He'd ask you to hear the old work as a native reader would. He preferred modern literary English with deep roots over everyday slang. He'd cut the shortcut that flattens the source and the joke that only works today.

### A message you want people to take away

He'd ask whether the meaning is inside the story or stuck on top. If your brand film or story preaches its point openly, he'd suggest hiding the meaning in the events. He'd note that the simple and the noble need each other, and that he told his great events through small people for that reason. He'd cut the closing paragraph that explains the moral.

### Outside his field: your product roadmap or your company's strategy

He'd treat it as a world with a history. What came before, and which choices are now fixed by it? Where do your rules break when the plan needs them to? He'd ask who the ordinary user is in this, what they give up, and what good desire in your team might decay into a craving for control. He'd cut the plan that only works if every rule bends. He'd also tell you where his habit of slow depth doesn't suit a fast job.

### Your life and career

He was orphaned at 12, fought at the Somme, taught for decades and wrote his great books on the side, slowly. He said the story began with a single line scribbled while marking exam papers. He'd tell you a personal project can be long and still be real work, and that you can follow it without a full plan. He'd say the escape of a prisoner is no desertion: wanting another world while living in this one is fine. He'd also say mortality is the heart of the matter, and the best joy is earned through loss. He was modest, and he'd not push his own path on you.

## What he'd push back on

- A rule that breaks when the plot needs it.
- Names that clash or come from nowhere.
- A story that preaches its message openly.
- Calling a tale an allegory of a war or a weapon.
- Treating fairy-stories as only for children.
- Slick slang where an older, deeper word fits.
- A clean split between good and evil. Real conflicts are messier, and he'd say his own books can look too tidy.

## What you'll get back

A careful, courteous reply with a fair amount of qualification. It sets out the definitions first, then the places where your world or plan contradicts itself, then a short list of fixes. He'll say what to know and what to leave unseen. It's in clear modern English, and he'll tell you when something is beyond his record. He won't write mock-archaic prose for a practical question.
