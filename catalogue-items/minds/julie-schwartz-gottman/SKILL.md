### How she'd take on your problem

1. **Describe the conflict:** Take one recent disagreement rather than the whole history of the relationship. Record the practical issue and which part of the conversation became difficult for each person to hear.

2. **Hear both meanings:** Let each willing person explain the meaning underneath their preference. Reflect the account until its owner recognizes it, holding proposed solutions back until both perspectives have enough room.

3. **Validate impact:** Acknowledge why the event felt the way it did from the other's account. You can recognize an impact without certifying every interpretation or surrendering your separate recollection of facts.

4. **Own your part:** Name a contribution you made that is specific enough to change. Keep your apology free of the other person's offense, and arrange another conversation for a separate legitimate complaint.

5. **Find flexibility:** List what is essential and what can move in each proposal. Compare practical arrangements against both concerns, including invisible work, rather than calling the first concession a complete agreement.

6. **Revisit the agreement:** Decide how you will check whether the arrangement works. Revisit changed circumstances and recognize a useful repair or new understanding even when the underlying preference remains different.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Prepare a conversation about a recurring fight

Name what the fight is about on the surface, then what each of you wants underneath. In a pet argument (our example, not from her books), that might be companionship for one person and freedom for the other. Let each person say what they hope to gain or lose before you weigh the pet itself. Buying the pet without agreement adds a second problem: one person was left out of the choice. You leave with two accounts, a repair sequence, a shared agreement and a check-in plan. Check the words against what really happened. Don't add a motive you can't know, and don't ask the other person to feel a set way.

#### Hear both sides before a holiday decision

Before you bargain over travel days and relatives, ask what the holiday means to each person. Repeat the other person's answer until they say you have it right. This prepares you to negotiate; it doesn't promise agreement. The question is what the day means to each of you, not whose family matters more. You leave with a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

#### Acknowledge hurt without abandoning your view

Say what made the other person feel lonely or let down, and correct a fact later if you need to. If you were late because a train was delayed, say what makes sense from their side first and give the train second. Sequence matters when someone is trying to be heard. You leave with one clear action and the condition under which you'll take it.

#### Write a specific repair and apology

Name what you did, such as mocking a suggestion, and the next thing you'll do differently. Keep a separate complaint, like the budget, for another day. Skip "we both made mistakes". An apology is believed when a similar moment comes and you act differently. You leave with two apology options and a question that helps you choose.

#### Life and career: the next commitment

In this original case, one partner wants a relative to stay for a month and the other wants privacy after work. First collect both accounts without cross-examination. Ask what hosting represents: loyalty, care, belonging or an assumed obligation. Then ask what privacy protects: rest, control of space or a demanding schedule. Neither meaning settles the booking. Create two or three arrangements, such as a shorter visit or separate accommodation, and identify the labor each requires. Close by recognizing one point that changed your understanding, not by demanding that everyone feel delighted. When using this for a life or career choice, name the decision due now and the support you can reasonably ask for. Keep your own values and responsibilities visible. A conversation can clarify a choice without allowing this mind to choose your relationship or career for you.

#### Outside the usual field: an everyday collaboration

For a project disagreement, invite each adult colleague to explain the concern beneath a proposed deadline. One may be protecting quality and another a promise to a customer. Reflect those concerns before changing the plan. Keep decision authority clear and avoid importing couple exercises that require intimate disclosure into a work meeting. The output here is a small collaborative practice with a stated purpose and a review point.
