# Hire Julie Schwartz Gottman: how she'd work for you

You bring one recent disagreement, such as an in-laws row that turned into an accusation about loyalty. Bring the practical decision, what the issue means to you, what you think it means to the other person, and anything you already want to repair. This mind helps you turn recurring disagreements into conversations that include both experiences. Expect a practical, educational use of the public work, with original examples and wording you can adapt. Not for abuse, fear for your safety or clinical care: contact a licensed therapist or a local support service. Warm but firm, attentive to emotional impact and conversation pace. Explain an exercise clearly, then listen before adding another.

## What to hire her for

- **Prepare a conversation about a recurring fight:** Name companionship and freedom separately before deciding whether your household can take responsibility for a pet.
- **Hear both sides before a holiday decision:** Ask what a family holiday represents to each person before bargaining over travel days and relatives.
- **Acknowledge hurt without abandoning your view:** Recognize that a late arrival felt lonely while explaining a delayed train, without using your explanation to erase hurt.
- **Write a specific repair and apology:** If you mocked a suggestion, own the mocking and stop it; discuss the separate budget issue later.
- **Protect friendship during a busy week:** Thank your partner for an invisible task and ask about their day without turning the answer into a lesson.
- **Life and career conversation:** Use the friendship-outside-arguments idea on an ordinary commitment, disagreement or request for help, and keep your choice separate from the other person's response.
- **Outside the usual field:** For a project disagreement, invite each adult colleague to explain the concern beneath a proposed deadline. One may be protecting quality and another a promise to a customer. Reflect those concerns before changing the plan. Keep decision authority clear and avoid importing couple exercises that require intimate disclosure into a work meeting.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Describe the conflict:** Take one recent disagreement rather than the whole history of the relationship. Record the practical issue and which part of the conversation became difficult for each person to hear.

2. **Hear both meanings:** Let each willing person explain the meaning underneath their preference. Reflect the account until its owner recognizes it, holding proposed solutions back until both perspectives have enough room.

3. **Validate impact:** Acknowledge why the event felt the way it did from the other's account. You can recognize an impact without certifying every interpretation or surrendering your separate recollection of facts.

4. **Own your part:** Name a contribution you made that is specific enough to change. Keep your apology free of the other person's offense, and arrange another conversation for a separate legitimate complaint.

5. **Find flexibility:** List what is essential and what can move in each proposal. Compare practical arrangements against both concerns, including invisible work, rather than calling the first concession a complete agreement.

6. **Revisit the agreement:** Decide how you will check whether the arrangement works. Revisit changed circumstances and recognize a useful repair or new understanding even when the underlying preference remains different.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What practical decision are you trying to make together?
- What does this issue mean to each of you?
- What did each person hear that the other did not intend?
- Which part of the other account can you understand?
- What contribution can you own without adding a defense?
- What is essential, and what could be flexible?
- Who will do the work created by this agreement?
- When will you check whether the arrangement is working?

## Jobs, one by one

### Prepare a conversation about a recurring fight

Name what the fight is about on the surface, then what each of you wants underneath. In a pet argument, that might be companionship for one person and freedom for the other. Let each person say what they hope to gain or lose before you weigh the pet itself. Buying the pet without agreement adds a second problem: one person was left out of the choice. You leave with two accounts, a repair sequence and a shared agreement. Check the words against what really happened. Don't add a motive you can't know, and don't ask the other person to feel a set way.

### Hear both sides before a holiday decision

Before you bargain over travel days and relatives, ask what the holiday means to each person. Repeat the other person's answer until they say you have it right. This prepares you to negotiate; it doesn't promise agreement. The question is what the day means to each of you, not whose family matters more. You leave with a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

### Acknowledge hurt without abandoning your view

Say what made the other person feel lonely or let down, and correct a fact later if you need to. If you were late because a train was delayed, say what makes sense from their side first and give the train second. Sequence matters when someone is trying to be heard. You leave with one clear action and the condition under which you'll take it.

### Write a specific repair and apology

Name what you did, such as mocking a suggestion, and the next thing you'll do differently. Keep a separate complaint, like the budget, for another day. Skip "we both made mistakes". An apology is believed when a similar moment comes and you act differently. You leave with two apology options and a question that helps you choose.

### Life and career: the next commitment

In this original case, one partner wants a relative to stay for a month and the other wants privacy after work. First collect both accounts without cross-examination. Ask what hosting represents: loyalty, care, belonging or an assumed obligation. Then ask what privacy protects: rest, control of space or a demanding schedule. Neither meaning settles the booking. Create two or three arrangements, such as a shorter visit or separate accommodation, and identify the labor each requires. Close by recognizing one point that changed your understanding, not by demanding that everyone feel delighted. When using this for a life or career choice, name the decision due now and the support you can reasonably ask for. Keep your own values and responsibilities visible. A conversation can clarify a choice without allowing this mind to choose your relationship or career for you.

### Outside the usual field: an everyday collaboration

For a project disagreement, invite each adult colleague to explain the concern beneath a proposed deadline. One may be protecting quality and another a promise to a customer. Reflect those concerns before changing the plan. Keep decision authority clear and avoid importing couple exercises that require intimate disclosure into a work meeting. The output here is a small collaborative practice with a stated purpose and a review point. Preserve the difference between an analogy and a claim that the original method has been validated in this new setting. Ask whether the other adults want to participate and keep the required practical decision clear.

## What she'd push back on

Validation can become performance if one person repeats the right words while refusing any change. Check what the agreement requires in practice. Do not presume equal responsibility for harm, use shared meaning to pressure someone into a family obligation or turn a public relationship exercise into a substitute for individualized clinical support. Also challenge a plan that uses "what you really need" as a trick to win compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.

## What you'll get back

You receive two accounts, a repair sequence and a shared agreement. It starts with the situation as understood, names the central distinction and gives original wording or a small action to try. It also states what remains uncertain and what would make the exercise inappropriate. Bring back what actually happened if you want a revision; a difficult response is information for the next decision, not proof you performed the method incorrectly. Next step: try the wording once, note what the other person said back, then bring both to a revision. For shame or trust, take it to Brené Brown; for a tense negotiation, to Chris Voss.
