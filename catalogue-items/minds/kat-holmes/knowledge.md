# Kat Holmes: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. This living-person profile dates views to the checked essays and interviews. Practical examples are written by us. No birth year is asserted where the checked sources do not establish one.

## In one paragraph

Kat Holmes is a designer and author of Mismatch: How Inclusion Shapes Design, first published in 2018. The publisher records her earlier leadership of inclusive design at Microsoft. Her practical focus is the interaction between people and designed environments: a product can exclude someone because it assumes the wrong body, resources, context or way of acting. The useful response is to investigate the mismatch and the expertise it conceals, rather than treating the person as an awkward exception. [S5][S1]

## Core ideas

### A mismatch is a design constraint you can examine

In Who Gets to Play?, Holmes uses playgrounds and technology to examine what happens when a design admits only people matching its requirements. She asks whether designers have used their own abilities as the presumed centre. [S1]

Our sign-up exercise names the interaction precisely. A resident wants to join an activity, but the process requires a smartphone, a particular payment method and rapid completion. List each requirement separately and ask which is essential to the activity rather than merely convenient to the organiser. A different route may preserve necessary information without preserving every assumption. Do not begin by declaring that some people lack digital skills. First inspect what the process actually demands and who has been given a way to meet it.

### Excluded people often have relevant expertise

In the 2019 Interior Design interview, Holmes describes learning from human personal assistants and from people with disabilities who had long developed ways to interact with technology. She emphasises expertise absent from conventional processes. [S3]

Our research brief asks participants to show or describe how they already solve the task, if they choose. Someone using an alternative input method may understand the workflow's hidden dependencies better than its designer. Record the adaptation, the effort involved and what the person would change. Do not convert a workaround into a requirement that the participant continue compensating for a poor system. The point is to learn from expertise and reduce an unnecessary burden, not to admire resilience while leaving the exclusion intact.

### Participation can have several forms

Holmes's playground essay argues for different routes into an experience rather than a single solution for an imagined normal user. Her examples distinguish the desired activity from a particular piece of equipment. [S1]

For our family-gathering plan, name the shared aim: enjoying time together, hearing news or contributing an idea. Speaking rapidly in a large room is only one possible means. Smaller conversations, written contributions or time to respond may support the same purpose differently. Ask the people involved what they want rather than assigning them to an alternative. Keep the options connected to the common experience so they do not become an inferior side event. A variety of participation is useful only if people can choose and be heard.

### Context changes what someone can do

The Microsoft guide distinguishes permanent, temporary and situational limitations, and presents recognition of exclusion, learning from diversity and extending a specific solution as its three principles. It credits Holmes as part of a larger team. [S4]

Our shared-tool review tests the actual settings of use. Can someone read the information in noise or poor light? Can they complete the task while caring for another person? What happens on a slow connection or when speech is unavailable? These questions should not erase the distinct experience of permanent disability. They expose how a design's demands interact with circumstances. Keep a record of the particular need that prompted a change, then examine any broader usefulness without claiming that every user now benefits in the same way.

### Inclusion needs a continuing practice

Asked about the future in the 2019 interview, Holmes presents inclusive design as something to practise consistently. She acknowledges forms of exclusion that are not yet well understood. [S3]

Our process review therefore includes a recurring question at the point where a decision is made: whose expertise is missing here? For a community form, inspect a new required field, payment route or deadline when it is introduced. A successful review six months ago does not establish that the revised process still works. Record the exclusion, the proposed response and who can verify it. The aim is a durable habit of noticing and correcting mismatches, not an annual celebration of an inclusive identity.

### Curiosity can guide learning across boundaries

In the CoDesign interview, Holmes describes seeking people who are curious about a topic, including at intersections between interests, rather than thinking only in terms of the next career level. [S2]

Our career application is a question-led learning project. If you are interested in design but unsure where to specialise, choose a mismatch you can investigate respectfully: a local booking process, a shared tool or a community activity. Identify people who understand the task from different perspectives. Agree on a bounded contribution and a way to learn together. The output is a question, a small project and a record of what changed your thinking. It is not a promise that curiosity alone produces a job or removes financial constraints.

## How she decides

The checked toolkit distinguishes inclusive design as a method from accessibility as an attribute or discipline, and explicitly says that the method does not itself establish compliance with all accessibility standards. [S4] That is an important limit on what this profile returns.

Our working order is to define the participation someone seeks, expose the assumptions in the current route and learn from people encountering the greatest mismatch. Then design a specific alternative, preserve meaningful choice and test who can use it. Examine whether the response helps in other contexts while keeping the original excluded user's needs visible. Finally, identify the decision or habit that produced the mismatch so the same pattern is less likely to return.

For a concrete original example, a residents' association accepts requests only through a short online form that times out. Ask what information is genuinely needed, how long different people need and what other route could collect it. A saved draft or an assisted submission might help, but either must be evaluated in the actual process. Check who receives the request and whether the alternative has the same status and response time. Adding a phone number that nobody answers merely moves the exclusion to another place.

## Where she stood

In the CoDesign interview, Holmes links the makeup of a design team to the outcomes it creates and describes an interest in human purpose within complex technology. [S2] The publisher describes Mismatch as an exploration of inclusion across disciplines, rather than a narrow software instruction manual. [S5]

Our corresponding stance is to ask about people, methods and outcomes together. A diverse group can still use an exclusionary template, and a well-intentioned organiser can still overlook the person least able to complete a task. Invite expertise early enough to change the criteria. Ask participants how they want their input used, credit their contribution and avoid turning one account into a claim about an entire category of people. Inclusion in the conversation must carry some influence over the result.

## Where she was wrong or criticised

The checked sources do not support inventing a personal controversy. They do support limits: the toolkit is a starting point and evolving practice, while Holmes says in the 2019 interview that it was too early to gauge the impact of her recently published book. [S4][S3]

Our critical safeguard is that an elegant method does not prove an inclusive outcome. A solution for one person may fail someone with a conflicting requirement. Broader usefulness is something to investigate, not a universal result to announce. A business case may help secure attention, but the practical task also concerns people who are not profitable customers. Do not make their participation conditional on a forecast of revenue. These are boundaries of our application, not attributed quotations from Holmes.

## Blind spots to watch

An organiser can mistake adding options for making them usable. Check the complete route, the response received and the authority attached to an alternative. A written contribution that is never read is not meaningful participation. A different payment process that carries a penalty may preserve the original exclusion. Ask the affected person what the difference means in practice.

Do not treat people as a collection of impairments or scenarios. Their goals, expertise and preferences matter. Nor should a situational example imply that a brief inconvenience is equivalent to a persistent access barrier. The final output should identify the mismatch, the knowledge needed, the alternative ways to participate, the criteria for checking them and the unanswered technical or policy questions. Keep the method useful by making its results inspectable.
