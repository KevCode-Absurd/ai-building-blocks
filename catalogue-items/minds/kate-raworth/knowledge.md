# Kate Raworth: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. This living-person profile uses the dated interviews and checked organisational material. Household and community exercises are our qualitative adaptations, not validated personal Doughnut accounts. No birth year is asserted without an established source.

## In one paragraph

Kate Raworth created the Doughnut of social and planetary boundaries and co-founded Doughnut Economics Action Lab, or DEAL. Her 2017 book brings different economic perspectives into a framework for meeting human needs within the living planet's means. Its practical invitation is to change the goal, widen the picture of value and examine how an arrangement distributes opportunities and environmental pressures. It is a compass for questions and design, rather than a promise that one diagram supplies every policy or measurement. [S5][S3]

## Core ideas

### Give progress a purpose beyond getting bigger

In Dollars to Doughnuts, Raworth describes the Doughnut as a visual compass between a social foundation and an ecological ceiling. DEAL presents a change from growth as the goal toward thriving within those conditions. [S1][S3]

Our community-event exercise asks what a successful event is meant to provide: connection, useful learning, a welcome to new neighbours or support for local work. More tickets sold could accompany success, but could also make the room crowded and the organisers exhausted. Write the purpose before choosing measures. Include access to the experience and the resources required to provide it. The point is not to make every small event responsible for solving planetary problems. It is to stop treating a larger number as a complete explanation of why the activity is worth doing.

### The economy lives within society and nature

Raworth's embedded-economy account places economic activity within society and the living world. Her interview contrasts that picture with a diagram that leaves material flows and unpriced consequences outside the main story. [S1]

Our household-week map records what comes in, what is used and what leaves: food, goods, energy, time, packaging and effort. Money matters, but the cheapest visible arrangement may create costs elsewhere. For instance, a delivery could save a caregiver an important trip while creating other resource demands. Describe both rather than assigning a moral score from the label alone. Ask what evidence would improve the comparison. The output is a wider account of the arrangement, including constraints the household cannot simply choose away. Do not invent footprint totals from a rough list.

### Care and commons belong in the picture

The Thought Economics interview distinguishes market, state, household and commons as forms of provisioning. Raworth highlights unpaid care and collectively created goods that a narrow money measure can miss. [S2]

Our family review lists the work that makes a week possible: planning, cooking, cleaning, accompanying, remembering and helping. Ask who does each task, who notices when it is missing and who gets time to rest. Do not translate every relationship into a price merely to prove that it matters. A shared library or garden also depends on care, knowledge and upkeep. The practical question is whether a proposed saving transfers work to someone already stretched. A useful review can recognise that dependency and change responsibilities without pretending affection provides an unlimited free resource.

### Distribution is designed into an arrangement

Raworth's interviews argue for distributive design rather than assuming growth will automatically share value. Her discussion of enterprises examines purpose, networks, governance, ownership and finance as conditions shaping what an organisation can do. [S1][S2]

Our sharing-initiative brief asks who owns the equipment, who can use it and who can alter the rules. A cupboard of shared tools might reduce purchases for some residents while remaining inaccessible to people without storage space, transport or a convenient schedule. Include the organising labour and authority to change the service. Keep the question concrete: who has access to the useful thing and who controls it? A friendly mission statement cannot answer that alone. For a formal enterprise, ownership and financial decisions require appropriate professional advice; this exercise identifies questions rather than prescribing a legal structure.

### Regeneration needs a changed flow, not a green label

DEAL describes a shift from degenerative to regenerative dynamics and from divisive to distributive ones. It also emphasises interdependence and systems thinking rather than a purely mechanical view of an economy. [S3]

Our repair-project plan traces an object's use beyond the repair table. Can it return to service? Are suitable parts available? Who checks it, and what happens to items that cannot be repaired? Count extra travel, storage and volunteer effort rather than equating every collected object with avoided waste. The group can aim to keep useful goods in service while acknowledging uncertainty about net effects. A successful repair is a concrete outcome. A claim of universal environmental benefit needs evidence the event alone does not provide. Choose wording that matches what the group can actually establish.

### Local aspirations and wider responsibilities interact

The collective Doughnut Unrolled tool brings local and global questions together, both social and ecological. Its credits name Raworth, Rob Shorter, Leonora Grcheva and Andrew Fanning, collaborating with Ruurd Priester and building on earlier partnerships. [S4]

Our community-purchase discussion uses four prompts: what it offers people here, what it changes in the local environment, what it asks of people elsewhere and what pressures may travel beyond the area. Use them to reveal missing information, not to fabricate a complete assessment. A locally convenient product may involve distant labour or materials; a local environmental improvement may shift a burden elsewhere. Ask which consequences are known, plausible or unknown. The output is a question map and evidence plan, with priorities for investigation. It is not an official DEAL workshop or a certified place portrait.

## How she decides

These working rules interpret the documented compass, embedded economy and design questions. They are our small-scale synthesis, with the limits of transfer kept explicit. [S1][S2][S3][S4]

- Define the needs and qualities of life the activity should support before treating growth or spending as success.
- Name social shortfalls and resource pressures separately. A gain in one does not erase a serious problem in another.
- Include market purchases, public provision, household care and commons in the map of how needs are met.
- Ask how access and authority are distributed before assuming the benefits will spread later.
- Trace flows and consequences beyond the point of purchase or disposal. Mark missing evidence instead of inventing precision.
- Use several relevant measures and a discussion of trade-offs. Keep the measures subordinate to the purpose.

Our event scorecard might include whether intended participants could join, what they valued, volunteer hours, actual spending and materials left over. Choose only measures the group can gather honestly. Explain what each says and what it misses. Attendance can indicate reach but not belonging. A bag of leftover supplies records a practical fact but not a full environmental impact. Add participant accounts where counts are inadequate. The exercise should make the next decision better, such as changing the format or purchase plan, rather than produce a decorative dashboard that no one uses.

## Where she stood

Raworth's public biography presents her work as making economics fit contemporary social and ecological realities. DEAL describes Doughnut Economics as a mindset drawing on several schools of thought, not a fixed set of policies and institutions. [S5][S3]

Our career reflection applies that distinction carefully. List what work needs to provide: a viable income, time for care, learning, relationships and a meaningful contribution. Examine which conditions each possible direction supports or undermines. This is a structured conversation, not financial advice or a promise that a preferred job can meet every need. A person with little room to manoeuvre should not be blamed for accepting necessary work. Use the richer picture to identify a feasible learning project, a conversation with an employer or an available support, while naming constraints that require collective changes.

## Where she was wrong or criticised

In Dollars to Doughnuts, Raworth states that her own model is not the true version of the world and asks whether models are useful relative to goals and context. That provides a documented caution against treating the diagram as self-validating. [S1]

The checked sources do not supply an independent criticism that needs to be invented. Our application has an important limit: choosing local indicators, setting thresholds and assigning a place a share of global pressures involve evidence and judgments. A household conversation cannot settle those questions scientifically. Likewise, a richer scorecard does not decide every conflict between time, affordability, participation and resources. Make disagreements and assumptions visible, then decide what can responsibly change. Calling an initiative regenerative or distributive does not establish that it achieves those qualities.

## Blind spots to watch

A broad compass can overwhelm a small decision. Select the few questions most likely to change the choice and record what lies outside the exercise. Do not ask a household to compensate individually for every failure in infrastructure or public provision. Notice differences in resources and realistic options.

Positive stories can also outrun measurement. Reuse, sharing and repair have specific conditions and costs. State what was observed and what remains uncertain. The profile can help a group ask better questions, but it cannot certify sustainability, calculate an exact personal planetary entitlement or guarantee that a particular action produces an environmental outcome everywhere.
