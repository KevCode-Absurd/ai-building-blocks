### In one paragraph

Katherine Johnson was a mathematician who spent 33 years at NASA's Langley center in Virginia, from 1953 to 1986 [S1]. She started as one of the "computers," the women who did calculations by hand, and moved within weeks into a flight research team of engineers [S1]. She worked out the path for Alan Shepard's flight in 1961, the first American in space [S1]. Before John Glenn orbited the Earth in 1962, he asked for her to check the new electronic computer's numbers by hand [S1][S6]. Her habits are simple to state and hard to keep. Learn to work the problem yourself before you use the machine [S5]. Start from where you need to end up and work backwards [S3]. When there's no textbook, go back to basics and write one [S3]. And always plan how to get home [S2].

### Core ideas

#### Work the problem yourself first

Johnson believed you should know how to solve a problem before you hand it to a computer. She told her grandchildren that you learn to work the problem first, and only then go use the machine [S5]. The point is that you can't judge an answer you couldn't have reached yourself.

How she applied it: when NASA moved to electronic computers for John Glenn's orbital flight in 1962, officials asked her to check the computer's numbers [S3]. She worked through the equations by hand, to many places past the decimal point [S5]. She recalled simply that she checked it and it was correct [S3]. Her reputation for accuracy helped build trust in the new machines [S2].

Her 1962 Echo I note with Gertrude Westrick shows the same habit on a different kind of job. It gathers 500 days of orbit data on the Echo I balloon satellite and its rocket casing from two tracking groups [S8]. It says plainly that the two groups list their data in different forms, with different reference times, and it plots their numbers uncorrected instead of quietly merging them [S8]. It notes that both programs go erratic when the orbit is nearly circular, and it uses the heavier rocket casing as a baseline to compare with the light balloon [S8].

#### Work backwards from where you need to land

Johnson's favourite approach was to begin at the end. If engineers needed a capsule to come down at a set place, she would start from that point and work out when to launch. She called this her strong suit [S3].

How she applied it: her 1960 report with Ted Skopinski gives a method for finding the direction a rocket must be heading at the moment its engine cuts off, so that the satellite later passes over a chosen spot on Earth [S4]. The reason was recovery. A spacecraft had to be over a known point when it began its return [S4]. The report works through two examples, one launched east and one west, each passing over the target after three orbits [S4].

#### When there's no textbook, go back to basics

Space flight was new, and there were no books to look things up in. Johnson said the team had to go back to geometry and work it all out [S3]. They wrote their own textbook, because there was nothing else on space [S3]. She counted herself lucky to be there at the beginning [S3].

How she applied it: she did math for *Notes on Space Technology*, a 1958 NACA document on the new field [S1]. Her 1960 report starts from the basic geometry of an orbit on a rotating Earth [S4]. It then adds the small correction for the Earth not being a perfect sphere, and checks the results against a computer program that included that bulge [S4].

#### Ask questions, and ask to be in the room

Johnson was curious and asked a lot of questions. When her engineering team held briefings, she asked if she could go. She was told the women didn't usually attend. She asked whether there was a law against it. There wasn't, and her boss let her go [S6].

How she applied it: being in the room led to her name on the work. When she and Ted Skopinski were writing the 1960 report, Skopinski said she should finish it, since she had done most of the work [S2]. It was the first time a woman in the Flight Research Division was credited as an author of a research report [S1]. Over her career she wrote or co-wrote 26 reports [S1][S2].

#### Trust the machine only after you've checked it

Johnson lived through the change from human computers to electronic ones. She didn't fight the change, and she didn't take it on faith either. She later worked directly with digital computers [S2]. The machine earned trust when its answers matched careful work done another way.

How she applied it: Glenn's request is the clearest case. Accounts of his exact words differ [S1][S5][S6]. In all of them, he wanted Johnson to confirm the computer's answers before he flew [S1][S5][S6].

#### Plan the way home

Getting a crew up is half the job. Johnson spent much of her work on the return. She recalled that during Apollo 13 in 1970, everyone had worried about the crew getting there, and her team worried about getting them back [S2].

How she applied it: for Shepard's flight she worked out the path and the launch window, and plotted backup navigation charts in case the electronics failed [S2]. Her backup procedures and charts helped set a safe return path for the damaged Apollo 13 [S2].

#### Do your best, as if someone is watching

Johnson's advice to young people was plain. Follow your passion, do your best at all times, and work as if someone is watching you [S5]. She liked to work problems, and she pointed out that if you do your best, nobody can ask you to do it again [S5].

How she applied it: she said she went to work every day for 33 years happy [S3]. NASA's biography quotes her saying she loved going to work every day [S1].

#### Know your worth, and don't overrate it

Johnson said she never felt inferior. Her father taught her that she was as good as anybody in town, but no better [S3]. When people asked how she coped with being treated as less, she said, in our paraphrase, that she didn't have time for that [S3].

How she applied it: in her own account, as these sources report it (a paraphrase), she knew segregation existed but didn't feel it at NASA, because everybody there was doing research [S2][S7]. She kept her focus on the work. This is her own account of her own experience. Other sources show that the facilities she worked in were segregated (see below) [S2].

#### Pass it on

In her later years she tutored young people and talked with children about careers in math and science [S2][S3]. She counted as a child: stairs, dishes, the steps to church [S5]. She wanted children to see that this kind of curiosity could lead somewhere.

### Real decisions

#### Doing the return trip backwards

For early flights she was asked to find the launch time that would bring a capsule down at a set spot. She started from the landing point and worked back, and said that was her strength. Early flights followed a simple arc, so this worked [S3].

#### Writing the report as a method with two worked cases

Her 1960 report with Skopinski sets out expressions for the burnout direction that puts a satellite over a chosen place on a rotating, bulging Earth. The bulge enters as small corrections inside a repeated solution. It includes an eastward and a westward example, a comparison with a computer program, and a check of how small changes in direction and latitude shift the result [S4].

#### Laying out two sets of tracking data side by side

In the 1962 Echo I note she and Westrick set out to present 500 days of orbit data in a form useful to others. The two tracking groups gave different reference times and units, so the authors listed the differences point by point and converted everything to statute miles [S8]. They said they plotted the data uncorrected and did not try to settle apparent conflicts, and that both programs became unreliable below an eccentricity of about 0.01, so they marked those stretches [S8]. They also included the rocket casing for comparison, because its weight to drag area ratio is about 900 times higher [S8]. An errata page issued in October 1962 fixed one number in the closing remarks, 0.80 to 0.08 [S8].

#### Checking the computer before Glenn flew

NASA asked her to verify the electronic computer's trajectory for the 1962 orbital flight. She confirmed it was correct. In 2018 she said she worked the calculation several places past the decimal point, and in 2008 that a computer could do much more and much faster but still had to be checked, so the machine was checked by a person [S3][S5].

#### Asking for a seat in the briefings

When told women did not attend, she asked whether there was a law, then joined and pressed on until she was in the room [S6].

#### Ignoring the sign

At work she and her colleagues dealt with segregated seating in the cafeteria by ignoring the sign and later eating at their desks. She said her father taught her that nobody is better than you, and that at work people were treated as equals [S5]. Records show segregated facilities in the computing pool until 1958 [S2].

#### Staying nervous but sure before Apollo 11

She had checked her lunar calculations and was still nervous during the landing, because outcomes can be unpredictable. She wanted nothing to go wrong, and nothing did [S3].

The book's author, Margot Lee Shetterly, said timelines had to be merged for the film [S7]. Johnson herself praised the film and its actors [S2].
- **"I didn't feel it" versus the record.** Johnson said she didn't feel segregation at NASA [S2][S7]. The computing pool had segregated facilities until NACA became NASA in 1958, and discrimination went on after that [S2]. Both can be true. Her account is one person's experience, not the whole history.
- **The lone-genius story.** Popular versions can make her sound like she worked alone. She was part of a large group of women computers and engineering teams, and some of the film's characters are blends of several real people [S1][S7].
- **Who said what to Glenn.** His words appear in several versions [S1][S5][S6]. Treat any exact quote of Glenn as uncertain.

### Blind spots to watch

- Her method is careful and slow by design. It suits work where an error costs a lot. For small, cheap, reversible choices, it can be more checking than the job needs.
- She worked inside a large, well-funded agency with clear goals. Her habits assume someone will give you time to check.
- She spoke little about the harm of discrimination. Pair her with someone who speaks directly about fairness and power in a team.
- Hand-checking scales badly. Today, the lesson is "check against an independent method," which may be another program, not a pencil.
- She was an orbital mathematician. On people, sales or design, use her as the person who checks the numbers, not the person who sets the vision.
