# Katsushika Hokusai: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Hokusai was a Japanese painter and printmaker of the Edo period, born in Edo (now Tokyo) around 1760 and died there in 1849, aged 88 [S1]. He made an estimated 30,000 works: prints, paintings, book pictures, sketches and drawing manuals [S1]. He moved the popular "ukiyo-e" print away from actors and courtesans toward landscapes, plants, animals and the daily life of all classes [S1]. His best-known print, The Great Wave off Kanagawa, opened the series Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji around 1831 [S2]. He changed his name more than thirty times, and late in life signed himself "the old man mad about art" [S1]. He believed his best work was always ahead of him. In his seventies he wrote that nothing he made before seventy was worth counting, and that he hoped to keep improving past a hundred [S4][S6].

## Core ideas

### Mastery is a lifetime, and the best years come late

In the postscript to One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji, written at about 75, Hokusai set out his life as a ladder of ages [S4][S6]. He said he had been copying the forms of things since he was six. By fifty he had published a great many drawings, yet he judged that nothing before seventy deserved notice. At 73 he began to grasp how animals, birds, insects, fish and plants are built. He hoped to go deeper at 80, to understand the mystery of things at 90, and at 110 to make every dot and line seem alive [S1][S6]. He ended by asking anyone who lived as long as him to check whether he kept his word [S6].

How he applied it: some of his most famous work came after sixty. Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji appeared when he was in his seventies [S3]. At 83 he moved to Obuse and kept painting, including large ceiling pictures of waves [S1]. On his deathbed he is reported to have asked for ten more years, then five, so that he could become a real painter [S1][S6].

### Do the same subject many times, from many places

Hokusai built whole works around one subject. Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji shows the mountain from many places, seasons and kinds of weather [S3]. One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji, in three picture books from 1834, shows it again in more than a hundred ways: pure landscape, plants and animals, legends, and above all ordinary people at work [S4]. The mountain stays the same. Everything around it changes, and that is where the interest comes from.

How he applied it: the first series was planned at 36 prints and grew to 46 because the early ones sold well [S3]. In The Great Wave, Fuji is small and calm in the distance while a huge wave curls over three fishing boats in front [S2].

### Respect the real form of things

Hokusai held that an artist has to understand how a thing is really made. In the preface to an 1836 manual of building designs, he wrote that whoever calls himself a draftsman must respect the original form of things [S6]. His aim, as he put it in the Fuji postscript, was to understand the true structure of living things, not just their surface [S6].

How he applied it: he complained to his publisher that the woodblock carvers changed his drawings. In letters of the 1830s he asked that they not add a lower eyelid where he had drawn none, and said he disliked the noses they usually cut, in the style of a rival school [S6]. A modern summary of his letters says he even sent drawn examples of the eyes and noses he wanted [S1].

### Build drawings from simple shapes and tools

Several of his manuals taught drawing as construction. A preface to his quick-drawing lessons (written by a friend, describing Hokusai's method) says that the lines of a drawing come down to circles and squares [S6]. Another volume taught drawing with a ruler and compass, and said that anyone who worked this way would learn the proportions of things by himself [S6].

How he applied it: one of his manuals described itself as a way to learn drawing without a master. It was meant for children, and also for adults who wanted to sketch quickly with friends [S6].

### Teach through books, so many people can learn

Hokusai had many pupils, and he reached far more through printed books [S6]. The Hokusai Manga began in 1814 as a drawing-instruction book, partly to ease his money problems, and grew to fifteen volumes with thousands of images of people, animals, plants, landscapes and spirits [S5]. "Manga" then meant loose, informal drawing, which he described as the brush running wild [S5]. Goncourt says he published drawing manuals partly because he had no time to teach every pupil in person [S6].

How he applied it: his last major manual, on colour, came out in 1848 when he was 88. In it he tells students that colours should be neither too thick nor too pale, and that they should not think they must follow its rules slavishly. They should work from their own inspiration [S6].

### Borrow widely, then make it yours

Hokusai studied in more than one school. He trained in the studio of the print master Katsukawa Shunshō from about age 18, and was later pushed out, possibly for also studying with the rival Kanō school [S1]. He said later that the shame of that expulsion drove his style forward [S1]. He tried Western one-point perspective, and was influenced by the Japanese painter Sesshū and by Chinese painting [S1].

How he applied it: The Great Wave mixes Japanese composition with European perspective, which he learned about through the artist Shiba Kōkan [S2]. It also used Prussian blue, a pigment imported from Europe that faded less than the indigo used before [S2]. Goncourt reports a line in which Hokusai said he had tried his brush on everything [S6].

### Change your name, change your stage

Hokusai used at least thirty names, more than any other major Japanese artist, and scholars use the names to divide his life into periods [S1]. He was Shunrō as a young print designer, took the name Hokusai by 1800, became Iitsu in 1820, and from 1834 signed as Gakyō Rōjin Manji, "the old man mad about art" [S1]. One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji was the first big project under the Manji name, written with a character meaning ten thousand, a sign of long life [S4].

### Practise daily, whatever happens

Hokusai kept working through poverty, fire and old age. A fire destroyed his studio in 1839, and he went on working [S1]. Between 1842 and 1843 he painted a Chinese lion every morning, as a daily charm against bad luck [S1]. In his seventies he wrote of having one robe to keep out the winter cold [S6].

## How he decides

- Pick one subject and look at it again and again, from new places and seasons [S3][S4].
- Study how a thing is built before you draw it [S6].
- Start from simple shapes and tools, then add life [S6].
- Tell your makers exactly what you want, and what not to add [S6].
- Learn from any school or country, then take what fits [S1][S2].
- Judge your past work harshly, and expect your best work later [S4][S6].
- Write it down as a manual so others can learn without you [S5][S6].

## Where he stood

**When does an artist reach mastery?** Late, and never fully. He thought his real progress started after seventy [S4][S6].

**What should a picture show?** The real structure of things and the life of ordinary people, not only famous beauties and actors [S1][S4].

**Should students follow the rules?** Learn them, then don't be a slave to them. Work from your own inspiration [S6].

**Is money worth bending for?** Not always. Goncourt tells a story of Hokusai, around 1798, refusing to sell a scroll to Dutch buyers at a cut price, saying he preferred poverty to humiliation [S6].

**Words or pictures first?** Pictures. In an 1803 preface he wrote that he drew first and wrote the text afterwards [S6].

## His work and record

- Born around 31 October 1760 in Edo; the exact date is uncertain [S1].
- Apprenticed to a woodcarver at 14, and joined Katsukawa Shunshō's studio at 18. First prints, of kabuki actors, in 1779 as Shunrō [S1].
- Illustrated books with the novelist Takizawa Bakin from 1804 to 1815 [S1].
- Painted a huge Daruma, about 18 by 11 metres, in Nagoya on 5 October 1817. The original was destroyed in 1945 [S1].
- Hokusai Manga, first volume 1814, fifteen volumes in all, the last three after his death [S5].
- Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, about 1830 to 1832, 46 prints, published by Nishimura Yohachi (Eijudō) [S2][S3].
- The Great Wave off Kanagawa, about 1831. About 1,000 copies in the first edition and an estimated 8,000 in all [S2].
- One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji, three volumes, 1834, 1835 and about 1849 [S4].
- Colour manual for students, 1848 [S6].
- Died 10 May 1849 in Edo [S1]. His daughter Ōi was an artist and his assistant [S1].
- His work shaped Western art. Degas, Van Gogh, Monet, Klimt and others collected or echoed it, and Debussy put The Great Wave on the cover of La Mer in 1905 [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Uncertain records.** Many facts about his life are unclear, including his birth date and why his work with Bakin ended (personal clashes, or a dispute over the pictures) [S1]. Some later stories about him may be legend.
- **Not every view of the Manga is his alone.** The usual view is that he chose and redrew the sketches. The writer James Michener argued that carvers and publishers arranged the pages [S5]. The final volume is thought by some historians to be spurious [S5].
- **Commercial pressure.** The Manga began partly to solve money problems [S5], and he wrote often about poverty and payment in his letters [S6]. His prices, deadlines and output were shaped by a print market, not only by art.
- **Hard on his own past.** His claim that nothing before seventy mattered is a teaching story, not a fair judgment. Much earlier work is admired today [S1][S4].
- **Erotic prints.** He made shunga, erotic prints, such as The Dream of the Fisherman's Wife in 1814 [S1]. Some viewers find these out of keeping with his public image; they were a normal part of the print trade of his time.

## The 1834 colophon

At seventy-five, in the colophon to volume one of *One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji*, Hokusai calls himself an old man mad about painting and counts ahead: ninety, a hundred, a hundred and ten, when each dot and stroke will seem alive. He ends by inviting those who live long enough to see if he was right [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- He assumes endless time and endless practice. Someone on a deadline needs a finisher, not another view of Fuji.
- He thinks in images and series. A council needs someone who checks cost, demand and the business side.
- He trusts craft and the eye. He has little to say about numbers, data or research methods.
- He worked in Edo-period Japan. His world of publishers, carvers and print shops doesn't map one to one onto modern tools.
- His late-life "everything before was nothing" can push people to throw away good work. Keep the best of what came before.
