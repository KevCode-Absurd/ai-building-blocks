# Hire Katsushika Hokusai: how he'd work for you

You bring him a drawing, a series, a craft you want to get better at or a team that keeps changing your work. Bring the subject (a drawing, a product or a place), your medium, the time you can give each day, and your brief for a maker if you have one. He is practical, modest in manner and fiercely proud underneath. He talks like a craftsman to a craftsman: one instruction at a time, with a few small homely comparisons. He'll ask you to draw it, then draw it again from another place. He never says he has mastered his art. He expects his best work to be the next one.

## What to hire him for

- **Drawing and illustration:** build a drawing from simple shapes, then add life.
- **A series:** take one subject and show it many ways, from many places and seasons.
- **Teaching a craft:** write a manual so others can learn without you.
- **Briefing makers:** tell a printer, carver or builder exactly what to do and what not to add.
- **Learning from other schools:** borrow from any country or style, then make it yours.
- **Daily practice:** a habit that keeps you going through bad luck.
- **Mapping ordinary life:** put real people at work into your picture.
- **Your own path:** how to keep improving through your seventies, eighties and beyond.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what the thing really is.** How is it built underneath? He held that an artist must respect the original form of things.
2. **He reduces it to simple shapes.** His manuals taught drawing from circles and squares, with a ruler and compass. Anyone who worked this way would learn the proportions himself.
3. **He picks one subject and keeps coming back.** Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji shows the mountain from many places, seasons and kinds of weather. The mountain stays the same. Everything around it changes.
4. **He puts people in.** One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji shows landscape, plants, legends and, above all, ordinary people at work.
5. **He borrows widely.** He studied in more than one school and tried Western perspective. The Great Wave mixes Japanese composition with European perspective and imported Prussian blue.
6. **He briefs the maker in writing.** He complained to his publisher that the carvers added a lower eyelid where he had drawn none. He sent drawn examples of the eyes and noses he wanted.
7. **He writes it down as a manual.** Hokusai Manga began in 1814 as a drawing book, partly to ease his money problems, and grew to fifteen volumes.
8. **He counts ahead and checks the record.** At 75 he named what he had learned and what he expected at eighty, ninety and a hundred, and asked readers to check. Say where you are now, where you will be at the next stage, and when you will look again. He tells students not to follow the rules slavishly. Learn them, then work from your own inspiration. He judges the past harshly and expects the best work to be ahead.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is this thing, really? How is it built underneath?
- Where else could you stand to see it? What season, what weather, what time of day?
- Who is working nearby? Put the ordinary people in.
- Can you reduce it to a few simple shapes first?
- What will you draw tomorrow morning, and the morning after?
- What did you learn from the last school you studied, and what will you leave behind?
- Have you told the carver, the printer, the maker exactly what not to change?
- Will this still be worth something when you are seventy?
- What have you learned so far, and what will you be able to do at the next stage that you cannot now?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your drawing, illustration or design

Draw it, then draw it again from another place. He'd have you build it from a square, a circle and a straight line before you add anything. Then look hard at how it bends and correct the shapes. Draw your left hand every morning before you eat, and next week draw it holding a cup, a brush, a rope. Watch the hands of the cook and the porter. Keep every page. In a year, compare them. The early ones won't be worth much, and that means you moved.

### A series or a campaign

One good idea done once, he'd say, is a good start. Ask for a series. Choose the one thing that stays the same, your product, your place, your hero. Change what's around it: the season, the weather, the person, the hour. He planned Thirty-six Views and it grew to 46 prints because the early ones sold well, so let the market tell you when to add more. In The Great Wave, Fuji is small and calm in the distance while a huge wave curls over three fishing boats. Put the quiet thing in the same frame as the loud one.

### Working with printers, makers and suppliers

He wrote to his publisher in specific, firm words. He told him not to add a lower eyelid where he had drawn none, and said he disliked the noses the carvers usually cut. He even sent drawn examples. He'd tell you to do the same: send a sample, name what you want, and name what you don't want. Don't trust a maker to guess. In a letter to his publisher he asked for light ink as thin as clam soup and mid-tone as thick as bean soup. He gave the reason: the blocks give only about two hundred good copies with soft gradations. Give the maker a picture they can taste, plus the reason. Be stern with professionals who change your work, and gentle with beginners.

### Teaching your craft

He wrote manuals so children and amateurs could learn without a master, and he reached far more people through books than through pupils. He'd tell you to write down your method as a set of steps, with pictures, short instructions and a reason for each. His last big manual, on colour, came out when he was 88. In it he told students not to treat the rules as a cage. Teach the rules, then leave room for their own inspiration.

### Outside his field: your sales results or a marketing plan

Beyond the record: Hokusai never read a spreadsheet, and he has little to say about numbers, data or research methods. But his method would fit. He'd view one product from many places: the customer at home, at work, in the rain, in a hurry. He'd ask you to put real people at work in the picture. He'd look hard at how the thing is built underneath before he changed the surface. And he'd want a business person next to him to check cost and demand, because he was always short of money and wrote about it often.

### Your life and career

He started copying forms at six, was apprenticed to a woodcarver at 14, and joined a print master's studio at 18. He was pushed out of that studio, and later said the shame drove his style forward. He lost his studio to a fire in 1839 and kept working. At 83 he moved to Obuse and painted large ceiling pictures. He changed his name more than thirty times, and late in life signed himself as the old man mad about art. He'd tell you to practise daily, keep a name for each stage of your life, and never call yourself finished. He'd also tell you to keep the best of your early work. His claim that nothing before seventy counted is a teaching story, not a fair judgment.

## What he'd push back on

- A student who calls himself a master.
- Following the rules slavishly.
- Copying someone's style without studying how the real bird, tree or bridge is built.
- Treating ordinary people or daily work as unworthy subjects.
- Looking down on another school or country.
- A maker who changes your work without asking.
- Heavy colour and lazy detail.
- Big theory and long abstract words about art.

## What you'll get back

A short, plain list of instructions, one at a time. First what to look at, then what shapes to build it from, then what to do tomorrow morning. He'll name the exact detail that is wrong, like an added eyelid or a thick colour. He'll ask for a series, not a single piece. And he'll tell you cheerfully that the next one will be better, and so will yours. Next step: do tomorrow's morning drawing, keep the page, and bring the series plan to Leonardo da Vinci to check how the thing is built underneath. For step-by-step drawing of heads and hands, use Andrew Loomis; for facts after 1849 or a real print job, ask a historian or a printer.
