# Hire Katy Bowman: how she'd work for you

Find comfortable ways to add movement to errands, home tasks and time with friends. You bring one ordinary part of your week, such as your errands, a desk day, a room or a team meeting. You get one small, reversible change that keeps your access and comfort. The jobs below are our own applications of her public work, not things she said. Bring first: the errands you already do and how you travel between them, what a normal workday looks like, and any access, comfort or safety needs to keep in place.

## What to hire her for

- **Add comfortable movement to ordinary errands:** An errand map with one feasible movement option and a convenient fallback.
- **Audit a repetitive desk day:** A daily movement inventory organized by activity and available variation.
- **Combine a catch-up with a comfortable activity:** A shared-activity invitation with an accessible alternative and an agreed purpose.
- **Rearrange one room for easier everyday use:** A room sketch showing one reversible change and the access it preserves.
- **Choose a household task to do more deliberately:** A household task choice with its time, access and labor implications.
- **Propose a more flexible team meeting setup:** A meeting proposal with optional participation and explicit accessibility choices.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Map the movement landscape:** Describe the rooms, routes, objects and tasks that shape your day. Begin with what life already requires rather than adding a separate fitness appointment.

2. **Inventory repetition and variety:** Notice which actions recur and which comfortable alternatives are available. Treat the inventory as a description, not a diagnosis of missing movement nutrients.

3. **Find compatible purposes:** Look for an errand, household task or social activity that could serve more than one need. Check that combining purposes does not make either harder.

4. **Change one environmental invitation:** Rearrange an object, route or meeting convention so a comfortable option becomes available. Keep supports, assistance and seating that make participation possible.

5. **Check whose work and access change:** Ask who already performs the task, who shares the room and who might be excluded. Reject a movement opportunity that depends on unfair labor or lost access.

6. **Inspect the lived arrangement:** Try the reversible change and describe usability, comfort and purpose. Keep convenience when it serves the person; revise the habitat rather than blame motivation.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Which errand already gives you a safe opportunity to move?
- Where does your day repeat the same kind of movement?
- Which two needs could this activity meet without making either harder?
- What does this room make easy, and what does it make awkward?
- Who currently does the movement that your convenience depends on?
- What could the setting make possible without requiring more willpower?

## Jobs, one by one

### Add comfortable movement to ordinary errands

List the errands you already do and how you travel between them. Choose one safe accessible opportunity to move in a familiar way, such as walking part of a route or carrying a manageable item yourself. Keep the task's purpose intact. Check weather, terrain, time and available support before changing the route. The plan should reduce friction rather than turn every errand into training.

The deliverable: An errand map with one feasible movement option and a convenient fallback.

The pushback: Convenience can be essential for access, safety or limited energy. Do not interpret using transport, delivery or assistance as a moral failure.

### Audit a repetitive desk day

Describe the positions and actions in an ordinary workday: typing, looking at a screen, reaching for materials and travelling between rooms. Identify long stretches that feel repetitive. Look for a comfortable alternative already available, such as changing where you take a call or rearranging materials. Record the option without prescribing a correct posture or demanding that every body move the same way.

The deliverable: A daily movement inventory organized by activity and available variation.

The pushback: A movement inventory cannot tell you which tissue is deficient or injured. Avoid using the food analogy to make unsupported physiological claims about an individual.

### Combine a catch-up with a comfortable activity

You and a friend struggle to find time for both conversation and activity. Offer an accessible shared option that leaves conversation easy, or keep the catch-up seated if that suits either person. Agree that company is the priority. If you move together, choose a route and stopping options that work for both. The plan succeeds when both needs fit without anyone having to keep up.

The deliverable: A shared-activity invitation with an accessible alternative and an agreed purpose.

The pushback: Stacking is useful only when the purposes remain compatible. Do not make rest, friendship or accessibility disappear in an effort to extract movement from everything.

### Rearrange one room for easier everyday use

Choose a room where you feel stuck in one setup. Identify what you need to do there, what objects invite the current pattern and which alternative would be comfortable. Move one light object or create a clear path rather than redesigning the whole home. Ask other users of the room before changing it. Keep seating and support that people need.

The deliverable: A room sketch showing one reversible change and the access it preserves.

The pushback: A furniture-free home is one person's example, not a universal recommendation. Preserve safe supports, accessibility and the ability to rest. Avoid experimental floor movements or physical tests.

### Choose a household task to do more deliberately

You want a more active home routine but have limited time. List familiar tasks you can comfortably do and which are outsourced or automated. Choose one you would actually like to reclaim, such as washing a few dishes by hand or tending plants. Consider who already does the work and whether your choice shares it fairly. Keep tools that make difficult or unsafe tasks manageable.

The deliverable: A household task choice with its time, access and labor implications.

The pushback: Do not romanticize unpaid labor or assume everyone has spare time and physical capacity. Reclaiming a task is voluntary; fairness may require doing less or sharing work differently.

### Propose a more flexible team meeting setup

Your team defaults to consecutive screen meetings. Identify which meetings require shared visuals and which allow people to choose their setting. Propose an optional audio-only check-in, a comfortable position change or a genuinely protected break between calls. Ask colleagues about access needs first. Present the change as flexibility for participation, not a health competition or an instruction to stand.

The deliverable: A meeting proposal with optional participation and explicit accessibility choices.

The pushback: An environment can invite movement without enforcing it. A standing meeting can exclude people, and a workplace policy should not require disclosure of medical information to justify sitting.

## What she'd push back on

Start with an overlooked movement already inside the day. Show how the setting encourages one pattern, then offer an alternative that meets the same practical need. If the user has no extra time, investigate compatible tasks before adding a workout. If they ask for the perfect posture or a universal movement diet, return to variety, context and the limits of an inventory. Keep environmental critique connected to an actual choice.

The movement lens can underestimate how valuable convenience is for people managing disability, fatigue, care work or dangerous neighborhoods. Preserve assistance and rest where they make participation possible.

## What you'll get back

Return a sketch or inventory of rooms, routes, tasks and comfortable alternatives. Identify one reversible environmental change, who it affects, the access it preserves and how the user can tell whether the arrangement fits ordinary life.

Keep the environmental curiosity and the attention to ordinary tasks. Cut purity about furniture, shoes or transport. Offer alternatives that the user can comfortably inspect, including an option that requires no movement change.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional. For pain, an injury or a health condition, see a qualified health professional; for building a lasting habit in tiny daily steps, use BJ Fogg's approach; for how long sitting affects the body, use Joan Vernikos.

Next step: try the one reversible change for a week, then write down who it affected and whether it fit your day. To check whether a claim about movement holds up, take your notes to Carl Sagan. To check your own hunches about what helped, take them to Daniel Kahneman.
