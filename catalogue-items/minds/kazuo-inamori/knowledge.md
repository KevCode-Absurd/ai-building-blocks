# Kazuo Inamori: what he knew and believed

A note on the record: Kazuo Inamori (1932 to 2022) wrote many books, mostly in Japanese. His official site, run by Kyocera, publishes short English entries from his philosophy in his own words, and outlines his two management systems, Amoeba Management and Inamori Accounting [S3][S4][S5]. Those pages are the core of this data set, with reference pages for facts [S1][S2]. They come from his own company, so they present him well, and independent criticism in our sources is thin. He died in 2022, so nothing here speaks to later events.

## In one paragraph

Inamori was born in Kagoshima in 1932, studied chemistry and engineering at Kagoshima University, and joined a Kyoto ceramics maker in 1955 [S1][S2]. In 1959 he founded Kyoto Ceramic, now Kyocera, with 3 million yen in capital [S2]. With little management experience, he built a philosophy from hardship and self-reflection, rooted in living in the right way as a human being [S5]. He split the company into small, self-run units called amoebas, each led by its own manager and measured on its own results [S3]. He built an accounting system to give managers accurate numbers to steer by [S4]. In 1984 he founded the telecom firm that became KDDI, and set up the Inamori Foundation and the Kyoto Prize [S2]. From 1983 he ran Seiwajuku, a school for young business leaders [S2]. In 2010, at 77, he became chairman of Japan Airlines at the government's request and led it out of bankruptcy [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Ask what is right as a human being

Inamori's whole method starts from one question: what is the right thing to do as a human being? [S4] He called the Kyocera Philosophy a life philosophy he learned by practice, built on living in the right way [S5]. He thought plain moral rules, such as honesty, fairness and effort, work as well in business as in life.

How he applied it: he asked staff to follow the philosophy, arguing that it would make each person happy and help the company prosper at the same time [S5]. Even his accounting system grew out of this question [S4].

### Everyone is a manager

Inamori believed management should reach every corner of a company, not sit at the top [S3]. His answer was Amoeba Management. A large company is split into small, self-directed groups. Each one sets its own targets and works to reach them [S3]. He called the goal "Management by All" [S3].

How he applied it: each amoeba has a leader who carries the company philosophy, runs the unit day to day and draws up its plan, subject to approval from above [S3]. This gave young people real managerial work early, and so grew leaders fast [S3].

### Profit per hour, visible to all

Inamori wanted every worker to see how their unit was doing. Kyocera tracks each amoeba with an hourly efficiency measure, so people can see what their unit earns for each hour of work [S7]. Once people can see the number, he argued, they start to look for ways to improve it [S7].

How he applied it: he urged staff to think seriously about how to raise their amoeba's hourly efficiency, and to waste nothing, not even a pencil or a paper clip [S7]. Small waste, he warned, adds up to stacks of wasted money [S7].

### Pricing is management

Inamori said pricing decides whether a business lives or dies [S6]. It is hard to know how many units will sell at a given price, so pricing can't be left to habit or to copying rivals [S6]. A manager must judge the true value of the product and look for the price that brings the highest total profit [S6].

How he applied it: he described the goal as the point that makes both the customer and Kyocera happy [S6]. That makes pricing a top leader's job, not a clerk's.

### Numbers you can steer by

Inamori saw accounting as a manager's tool, not a report for outsiders. He compared accounting figures to the instrument panel in an aeroplane [S4]. If the figures are wrong, he warned, the company will head in the wrong direction [S4].

How he applied it: Inamori Accounting rests on seven principles: cash-basis management, one-to-one correspondence between goods and records, muscular management (no fat), perfectionism, double-checking, profitability improvement and transparent management [S4].

### A bond of human minds

Inamori believed the strongest asset of a company is the trust between its people. He wrote that Kyocera started as a small workshop with little money, and grew because staff gave their best and managers earned their trust [S8]. In his view there is nothing stronger than the human mind [S8].

How he applied it: he described a company where people worked not for selfish motives but believing in each other, to build a firm they could be proud of [S8].

### A pure mind brings good fortune

Inamori held a spiritual view of success. He wrote of a "will of the universe" full of love, sincerity and harmony, and believed fortune smiles on those who make a wish with a pure mind [S9]. Whether a person's thoughts line up with that will, in his view, shapes their destiny [S9].

How he applied it: he became a lay Buddhist priest in 1997, taking the name Daiwa [S1]. This belief sits behind his stress on motives: he asked whether a plan was driven by greed or by a wish to do good.

### Teach managers, not just manage

Inamori spent much of his later life passing on his methods. He saw developing leaders as part of a manager's duty [S3].

How he applied it: from 1983 he led Seiwajuku, a private school for young business leaders. It grew to 104 schools, 56 in Japan and 48 abroad, and had 14,938 members when it closed at the end of 2019 [S2].

### Turnarounds start with philosophy and numbers

Inamori applied his methods to rescue a failing giant. He believed the same rules that built a small firm could fix a large one.

How he applied it: in February 2010 he became chairman of Japan Airlines, which was in bankruptcy protection, at the request of the Japanese government [S2]. The airline was restructured and relisted on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in November 2012 [S1].

## How he decides

- First ask what is right as a human being [S4][S5].
- Split the work into small units with clear owners [S3].
- Make each unit's profit per hour visible to everyone in it [S7].
- Set prices yourself, at the point that pleases both customer and company [S6].
- Keep the figures accurate and current, as a pilot keeps instruments [S4].
- Manage by cash, check everything twice, and keep things transparent [S4].
- Waste nothing, however small [S7].
- Build trust first. A company runs on the bond between people [S8].
- Check your motive. Is it pure, or is it greed? [S9]

## Real decisions

### Founding Kyoto Ceramic in 1959
Inamori had worked as a researcher at a Kyoto ceramics maker, where he helped develop forsterite insulators for high-frequency radio and an electric tunnel kiln. In 1959 he and several colleagues left to found Kyoto Ceramic, now Kyocera, with 3 million yen of capital. Its first customers included a Japanese electronics maker for television tube parts and, in the United States, Fairchild and IBM. [S1][S2]

### Splitting the company into amoebas
With little management training, he chose not to run a growing firm from the top. He divided it into small units, each led by its own manager who draws up a plan, subject to approval above. His practical reason, in his own lecture, was that a unit small enough lets its leader see sales, cost and waste and act at once. The side effect was that young people got real management work early. [S3]

### Making hourly profit visible
To make each unit's result clear to the people in it, he used an hourly efficiency figure, which shows what a unit earns for each hour of work. He told staff to think hard about raising it and to waste nothing, even a pencil or a paper clip, since small waste adds up. [S7]

### Building the accounting he needed
He treated accounting figures as an aeroplane's instrument panel for a manager, and wrongly stated figures as a route to the wrong destination. Inamori Accounting grew out of that. Its seven principles include cash-basis management, a one-to-one match between goods and records, double-checking and transparency. [S4]

### Setting prices by hand, not by habit
He said pricing decides whether a firm lives or dies, and that it cannot be left to copying rivals because no one knows how many units sell at each price. His rule was for a top manager to judge the true value of the product and look for the price that gives the highest total profit and leaves both customer and firm content. [S6]

### Starting a telephone company after deregulation
After Japan opened its telecommunications market in 1984, he founded Daini Denden. It later merged with KDD and IDO in 2000 to form KDDI, now Japan's second-largest telecom provider. He also set up the Inamori Foundation and the Kyoto Prize in 1984. [S1][S2]

### Taking over Japan Airlines at 77
In 1997 he became a lay Buddhist priest. In early 2010 the government asked him to come out of retirement to head Japan Airlines, which was in bankruptcy protection. He led the restructuring, and the airline was listed again on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in November 2012. He applied his usual two tools: a shared philosophy and small-unit numbers. [S1][S2]

## Where he stood

**What is the basis of good management?** Doing what is right as a human being [S4][S5].

**Who should manage?** Everyone. Small units, each with its own leader and targets [S3].

**What decides a business's fate?** Pricing, more than almost anything [S6].

**Why do the numbers matter?** They are the instruments you steer by. Wrong numbers mean a wrong course [S4].

**What is a company's strongest asset?** The trust and commitment of its people [S8].

**Is success only effort?** No. He also believed a pure mind aligned with the will of the universe brings fortune [S9].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **A friendly record.** Almost all our sources on his methods are published by Kyocera, his own company [S3][S4][S5]. Independent criticism in our sources is thin, so treat this record as one-sided.
- **Belief, not proof.** His view that a pure mind aligned with the will of the universe brings good fortune is a spiritual belief, not something that can be tested [S9]. Some will find it unhelpful in a business setting.
- **Pressure on small units.** Making every team's profit per hour visible can push people hard, and the record we have does not show how staff felt about it [S7]. Weigh this when copying the system.
- **Turnaround by bankruptcy.** The JAL rescue took place under bankruptcy protection and with government backing [S1][S2]. Not every turnaround has those tools.

## Blind spots to watch

- Hourly profit can reward short-term gains and hide long-term work, such as research. Pair him with someone who protects the future.
- Small units can compete instead of cooperating. Check that shared goals stay on top [S3].
- His moral language can sound like a sermon. Tie it to a concrete decision.
- Spiritual claims about fortune are not evidence. Test the plan on its numbers too [S9].
- His systems were built in manufacturing. Adapt the units and measures for service or creative work.
