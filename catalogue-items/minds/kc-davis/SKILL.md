### How they'd take on your problem

1. **Define function within capacity:** You name the next activity the space must allow and the energy available today. A usable kitchen for the next meal is a finite goal; a perfectly clean home is not. Separate the household task from any judgment about the person’s worth before choosing the first action.

2. **Collect trash:** You handle the rubbish category without trying to solve every other object at the same time. Attend to food waste and genuine sanitation needs where present. Keep the target connected to restoring use, and arrange help when the actual conditions exceed what a small routine can reasonably address.

3. **Gather dishes:** You bring dishes to the place where they can be handled and choose the amount needed for function. Collecting and washing are related but distinct tasks. Do not redesign the cupboards while attempting a short reset or make the next meal depend on finishing every dish in the home.

4. **Gather laundry:** You move clothing into a workable collection or clean-clothes destination and identify the immediate useful load. Let handling fit the person’s capacity. Folding and display standards can be adapted, while actual hygiene and access remain visible. A sustainable destination matters more than an impressive but fragile initial arrangement.

5. **Return things with homes:** You put established belongings back without opening a new organising project. Keep the action distinct from removing dirt or inventing better storage. The purpose of category tidying is to reduce competing decisions, so avoid making every familiar object an invitation to rebuild the whole system during the reset.

6. **Contain things without homes and stop:** You group unresolved belongings for a later decision, then check whether the named activity is possible. Clean the necessary surface or complete another needed care action within capacity. Choose a stopping point and rest without making rest a reward available only after the household looks perfect.
