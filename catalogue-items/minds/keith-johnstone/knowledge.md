# Keith Johnstone: what he knew and believed

Public record checked 2026-10-23. This profile uses Johnstone's actual book passages, a published interview and his later theatre essay. It draws a narrow practical line between a willingly shared fictional exercise and real decisions about consent, responsibility or evidence. The exercises here are our adaptations and do not reproduce a licensed performance format.

## In one paragraph

Keith Johnstone developed improvisation through attention to spontaneity, relationships and narrative. His book distinguishes the status a person plays from their social position, examines accepting and blocking offers and explains returning earlier material to give a story shape. [S1] His later interview asks performers to attend to one another and develop what is already present rather than compete to sound clever. [S2] The everyday value is a way to make a shared scene, revise dialogue or rehearse a response to a small interruption. The point is an available next action, not a demand that the user become fearless, obey every suggestion or treat social life as a contest for dominance.

## Core ideas

### 1. Status is something played in a relationship

In Impro, Johnstone distinguishes social rank from what someone does moment by moment. A socially senior character can play lower status, and a subordinate can play higher. His exercises explore changes through behavior and relation to a partner. [S1] Our application writes the same harmless exchange twice: a visitor asks for a meeting room, first as though expecting to command the room and then as though uncertain of the rules.

Choose observable changes rather than labels of personal worth. The visitor may interrupt, wait, occupy space or ask permission. Let the receptionist's response affect the next action. A shift becomes interesting when the relationship changes, not merely when one actor performs a collection of confident gestures in isolation.

Do not use the exercise as a universal body language decoder. A person's gaze, movement or pauses can have many causes. The theatrical lens helps invent or inspect a fictional exchange. It does not prove that a real colleague is weak, deceptive or socially inferior. In a practical rehearsal, ask what your own behavior communicates and allow the other person to describe their experience.

### 2. Accept an offer so the shared fiction can develop

The book examines how performers prevent action by denying a partner's invention and how accepting a premise can let a scene move. [S1] Our simple exercise begins in a lost-property office. One player establishes that a parcel is making a noise. The other treats that as part of the fictional world and contributes a response that changes what they do next.

Acceptance need not mean a character agrees with another character's wishes. A character can object to opening the parcel while accepting that the parcel exists and is making a noise. The players continue the shared reality even when the characters disagree inside it. This distinction keeps a scene from collapsing into a contest over who is allowed to invent its facts.

Outside fiction, use a smaller transfer: acknowledge what someone actually proposed before evaluating it. You can understand a request and still refuse it. You can accept that a person feels disappointed without accepting their interpretation of events. Neither an improvisation exercise nor a slogan can remove real consent, budget limits or the need to check a claim.

### 3. Let the understandable next move be enough

In the Hugh Tebby interview, Johnstone connects trying to impress with thinking ahead instead of listening. He favors making contact and developing what is inherent in a scene rather than requiring a stream of witty conversation. [S2] Our adaptation asks two willing players to make a thirty second scene with no duty to be funny.

Give one person an ordinary task, such as packing a picnic. Let the other notice and contribute something obvious within that situation. If there is a leaking bottle, deal with the bottle before inventing an unrelated disaster. The next action can reveal a relationship: who notices, who takes charge, who wants to hide the mistake and who offers help.

The exercise lowers the demand for display so attention can move outward. It does not reject imagination or careful craft. An obvious contribution to you may be new to a partner. After the scene, describe what became clear rather than ranking who had the most original idea. A useful response can be ordinary and still create a scene worth continuing.

### 4. Keep a developing world coherent

Johnstone's theatre essay explains a developing scene as narrowing its range of plausible possibilities. An idea can seem clever while damaging the world already established. He presents staying within that world as useful advice for beginners. [S3] Our exercise names the scene's ordinary frame and two established facts before adding a new event.

If the scene is a small bakery preparing for opening, a missing key or a mistaken order belongs naturally to its world. A spaceship may also belong if the players have established a bakery on a space station. The problem is not whether an idea is strange. It is whether the audience has a way to understand its relationship to what came before.

Use this lens to revise a short story too. Circle an incident that asks the reader to accept a new rule only at the moment it solves a problem. Either prepare that rule earlier, choose a consequence already supported or deliberately build a different kind of story. Do not call surprise a mistake when the work has actually made room for it.

### 5. Return earlier material to give the story shape

Impro describes remembering shelved incidents and bringing them back into the ongoing story. [S1] Our adaptation keeps a small list of established objects or concerns while two people make a story. When a problem arrives, look backward for something that can now matter differently instead of adding another unrelated invention.

A character leaves a bright umbrella in a waiting room. Later, when they need to identify someone who passed through, the umbrella can become a recognizable clue. The return should alter the current situation. Merely mentioning every object again is a roll call, not necessarily an ending. Choose the earlier detail whose new use makes a consequence understandable.

For a factual anecdote, this remains a selection and emphasis tool. An object may return only if it actually did. You cannot fabricate a satisfying callback and retain the claim that the account is strictly true. A fictional exercise offers more freedom, provided the user and audience understand the form.

### 6. Respond to what happened rather than the cue you expected

The theatre essay criticizes actors who reproduce a prepared response while failing to notice a changed scene. Its examples are recollections and illustrative accounts, not independent records of every production detail. [S3] The useful craft question is whether a performer is attending to the present event or waiting for the world to match an internal script.

Our ordinary rehearsal is a club introduction. A willing partner adds a harmless interruption, such as dropping a pen or asking for a repeated name. The speaker pauses, notices the actual need and returns to the next essential point. They do not have to turn the interruption into a joke. The aim is to remain in contact with the room while preserving the task.

Agree on the rehearsal beforehand. Do not sabotage a real presentation to teach someone spontaneity. A broken microphone may require practical repair rather than a creative response. The exercise should expand a speaker's options for a small interruption, not make them responsible for handling every failure without help.

## How to make a shared scene move

Start with willing participants, an ordinary location and a short time limit. Choose a situation with no physical contact or personal disclosure required. One player establishes a task or fact. The partner responds within the shared world and adds an action or consequence. Keep attention on what changes between the characters rather than on preparing a clever line while the partner is speaking.

If the scene stalls, review the actual exchange. Did someone deny a fact, replace the premise or avoid every action? Try it again with the established fact retained. If nothing changes between the characters, alter a task or a relationship. If the scene becomes incoherent, name what is already true and choose a next move that fits. Different problems call for different repairs.

Finish by looking backward. Select one earlier detail that can help resolve the current situation. Stop when the return changes the relationship or completes the action. A thirty second exercise can end simply. It does not need a full theatrical plot or a laugh to count as an inspectable attempt.

## Where this lens helps

### Dialogue on a page

Two characters discuss a misplaced key. Write the exchange so the person who initially controls the interaction gradually needs the other's knowledge. Mark the action that changes their relation. Avoid assigning a permanent high or low status identity. The resulting dialogue should show a shift a reader can follow.

### A collaborative idea session

During a clearly designated exploration phase, restate a colleague's proposal accurately before adding a possible development. Then move to evaluation with the real constraints visible. The transfer concerns listening and extending an idea long enough to understand it. It does not require approving a proposal, making a commitment or avoiding necessary criticism.

### A gentle family story game

Two willing people tell a clearly fictional story about a lost package. Keep the content suitable for everyone present and permit passing a turn. Use one earlier object in the ending. The output is a small piece of shared play, not a test of imagination or a diagnosis of who blocks others in life.

## Blind spots to watch

The approach can be criticised when status becomes a crude ranking system or when accepting offers is used to pressure participants. A theatrical distinction should expand choices, not narrow a person's right to refuse. Keep the fictional frame clear, permit a pause and do not ask for unwanted touch or disclosure. The exercise's value depends on the actual people being able to participate willingly.

Johnstone's later essay includes speculative claims about the brain and stage fright. Those remarks are not used here as medical or scientific guidance. [S3] Nor does a lively workshop establish that one teaching method suits every learner. Test the small craft result and adapt the activity to the participants, rather than treating discomfort as proof that the teacher must be right.
