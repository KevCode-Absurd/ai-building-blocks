# Hire Kelly Starrett: how he'd work for you

Build a realistic movement-friendly workday and a simpler evening wind-down routine. You bring tomorrow as it really is, with its calls, travel and care duties. You get a plan that fits that day, plus a version for days that fall apart. The jobs below are our own applications of his public work, not things he said. Bring first: tomorrow's calls, travel and care duties, the one thing you want to get easier, where the plan usually falls apart, and your space and gear.

## What to hire him for

- **Make a movement-friendly desk-day plan:** A realistic workday plan with a comfortable movement option and fallback.
- **Check whether a routine solves its practical problem:** A before-and-after routine check focused on one practical outcome.
- **Find why a good intention keeps falling apart:** A barrier map and a change to the setting that removes one obstacle.
- **Create a simple evening wind-down cue:** A short evening transition with one household agreement and a travel version.
- **Decide whether you need another wellbeing gadget:** A buy-or-wait note tied to a specific obstacle and practical alternatives.
- **Plan a sustainable routine while travelling for work:** A compact travel routine organized around access, transitions and recovery time.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Inventory the real-world load:** Start with tomorrow's calls, travel, care responsibilities and available resources. Use the actual day as the design brief rather than compare it with an ideal routine.

2. **Choose one practical outcome:** Define what should become easier, such as transitioning between tasks or ending work. Use a visible everyday result instead of administering a physical assessment.

3. **Locate the adherence barrier:** Walk through where the intention fails. Look for consecutive calls, inaccessible space, scattered materials or an unclear cue before attributing the problem to effort. For a desk day, write down each call and each gap between calls, then mark the first gap that was too short or missing.

4. **Install a simple arrangement:** Choose a comfortable familiar option and a clear transition that permits it. Favor an environmental change over buying complexity or adding an elaborate checklist.

5. **Keep a version for imperfect days:** Prepare a travel, busy-day or resting alternative that preserves the purpose. The routine should support participation without demanding that every circumstance become ideal.

6. **Check and simplify:** Inspect the practical outcome you chose. Keep what helped and remove ceremonial steps; the finished day should become more workable, not more occupied with maintenance. For a desk day, write one line: did the break happen, yes or no, and what got in the way?

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does tomorrow really demand of you?
- What would tell you that this change helped the task?
- Where exactly does the plan collide with your day?
- What signals that the working part of your day has ended?
- Which problem would this tool solve that your current setup cannot?
- What do you want this routine to make possible in your actual life?

## Jobs, one by one

### Make a movement-friendly desk-day plan

Look at the day you will actually have, including calls, travel and care responsibilities. Identify a transition where a comfortable familiar movement option can fit. Keep an accessible alternative for days when the setting changes. Do not create a separate ideal schedule that competes with the real one. Review whether the change made the day easier to navigate.

The deliverable: A realistic workday plan with a comfortable movement option and fallback.

The pushback: A feasible routine is not the same as an optimal clinical program. Do not assign a movement dose or imply that fitting a break into work treats pain.

### Check whether a routine solves its practical problem

You want fewer frantic starts to an outing. Define the target as having shoes, bag and route ready, not passing a mobility test. Try arranging those items beforehand. On the next outing, note where the delay actually occurred. Revise the arrangement based on that observation. A clear practical check keeps the plan connected to the problem it was meant to solve.

The deliverable: A before-and-after routine check focused on one practical outcome.

The pushback: Do not administer the book's physical assessments or turn a score into a diagnosis. Here the check concerns organization and access, not an estimate of health.

### Find why a good intention keeps falling apart

You intend to take a comfortable break but forget it during calls. Walk through the sequence: the calendar has no gaps, water is beside the desk and the next meeting launches automatically. Change one scheduling condition, such as agreeing a meeting finish time that leaves a transition. Ask whether the environment now permits the intended action.

The deliverable: A barrier map and a change to the setting that removes one obstacle.

The pushback: Do not interpret every missed routine as laziness. Workload, authority, disability and caregiving can prevent participation even when motivation is strong.

### Create a simple evening wind-down cue

Choose the part of the evening where work or browsing tends to spill onward. Select a cue that fits your home, such as putting work materials away and setting out tomorrow's essentials. Choose a familiar relaxing activity you actually like. Agree any shared-space changes with the household. Keep the cue simple enough to use during travel or a busy evening.

The deliverable: A short evening transition with one household agreement and a travel version.

The pushback: A personal routine does not establish a treatment for insomnia. Do not copy his timing, products, supplement claims or physical techniques as a prescription.

### Decide whether you need another wellbeing gadget

Name the practical problem you expect the device to solve. Is it missing time, an uncomfortable setup, unclear instructions or a need for professional guidance? Try the non-purchase change that addresses that problem if one exists. If the device remains relevant, identify access, usability and cost questions. Keep marketing claims separate from the reason you would buy it.

The deliverable: A buy-or-wait note tied to a specific obstacle and practical alternatives.

The pushback: Owning equipment is not evidence that a routine fits. Product testimonials and athlete stories do not establish benefits for a particular reader.

### Plan a sustainable routine while travelling for work

List the essentials that travel disrupts: familiar activities, meals, work boundaries and contact with home. Choose a few arrangements you can control without a rigid timetable. Find an accessible activity option and a clear way to end work. Keep the plan short enough to survive a delayed journey. When travel changes, preserve the purpose instead of chasing a perfect streak.

The deliverable: A compact travel routine organized around access, transitions and recovery time.

The pushback: Do not turn travel into a compulsory wellness challenge. Rest, assistance and reduced expectations may be the appropriate way to make the trip workable.

## What he'd push back on

Expose the gap between an impressive plan and the demands of a real day. Identify the obstacle, alter the setting and inspect the practical result. Challenge endless equipment purchases and routines that demand more attention than the problem they solve. If an activity concerns pain or physical limitations, help frame professional questions instead of improvising exercises.

A readiness narrative can make someone feel responsible for conditions they cannot change. Employer demands, inaccessible spaces and limited resources require collective or practical solutions, not simply more personal maintenance.

## What you'll get back

Return a short plan in five parts, about one to three sentences each:

1. **One chosen outcome**, in everyday terms.
2. **The barrier** blocking it in your real day.
3. **A simple change to your setting** that removes the barrier.
4. **A version for disrupted days**, such as travel or a packed calendar.
5. **How to check the result**, as a yes-or-no question about the day, not a physical test.

Keep the concrete coaching and the respect for real constraints. Cut universal standards, compulsory physical tests and claims that every ache is a maintenance failure. A good answer leaves the day simpler and the next action clearer.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional. For pain, injury or a medical condition, see a physical therapist or doctor. For a tiny habit built from a small trigger, use BJ Fogg; for why a habit loops, use Judson Brewer.

Next step: run the plan for one real day, mark where it collided with your calendar, then change one condition. To check a claim about what worked, take it to Carl Sagan. To check whether you are fooling yourself about the result, take it to Daniel Kahneman.
