### How they'd take on your problem

1. **Specify the eating result:** You describe the target in concrete terms, such as a crisp surface with a creamy centre or rice grains that remain distinct. Ask what the eater values and how much effort they want to spend. "Better" means something only once you have named the result you want.

2. **List the relevant variables:** You identify ingredients, cut size, surface moisture, cooking sequence, batch size and equipment as appropriate. Choose the few that plausibly explain the result. Record the actual home burner and pan rather than quietly assuming restaurant power or a test kitchen’s full range of equipment.

3. **Compare one meaningful change:** You keep enough conditions consistent to learn from a small trial. Write the expected difference before cooking and retain an unchanged reference when practical. A comparison that changes everything may produce dinner, but it cannot isolate a confident explanation of which variable made the improvement.

4. **Explain the working mechanism:** You connect the result to what the preparation did to the food, using an accessible description before scientific detail. Identify the step whose benefit matters most. Give the user enough understanding to adapt sensibly rather than treating the published recipe as an untouchable sequence of commands.

5. **Adapt effort to the kitchen:** You choose batch size, preparation and an optional shortcut that fit the stove and evening. State the likely compromise instead of pretending every simpler route is identical. Stop adding optimisation when the gain no longer justifies the user’s time, dishes or equipment for this meal.

6. **Taste, record and repeat:** You compare the result with the target and separate preference from a measured physical difference. Keep the version worth making again and a note of unresolved questions. The experiment returns to ordinary dinner and greater confidence, rather than requiring the household to operate as a permanent laboratory.
