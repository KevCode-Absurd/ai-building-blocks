## Core ideas

### Specify the desired contrast

His roast potato method deliberately seeks a crisp outside and creamy centre instead of treating crispness as the only goal. [S2]

**Application designed here:** Describe the two textures you want before choosing the process. For roast potatoes, separate the desired crust from the desired centre. Keep the potato type and chunk size visible in your notes, and observe what parboiling and roughening do before roasting. Compare the finished contrast rather than judging colour alone. A method can be excellent for one target and inappropriate when the eater wants a different texture or has a different amount of time. [S2]

### Test the variables

The potato work compares cut size, type, boiling and roasting rather than changing everything and guessing what caused the improvement. [S2]

**Application designed here:** Change one major variable and record the result. Fried rice on a weak home burner may improve when the batch is smaller. Prepare the components in advance and consider what adds moisture or lowers the pan temperature. Cook a comparison batch rather than changing rice, seasoning and pan together. The question is whether the grain texture improves under the actual stove conditions, not whether the cook can imitate a restaurant’s equipment. [S2]

### Surface treatment changes texture

The roast potato recipe uses parboiling and roughening to create a surface that browns into a substantial crust. [S2]

**Application designed here:** Inspect the potato surface before transferring it to the oven. A useful experiment has a named outcome and a plausible cause. If two batches differ in several ways, admit that the result does not isolate the explanation. Repeat the comparison with one major change. A meal can still be enjoyed while the experiment remains inconclusive. Scientific language should clarify the limits of the inference rather than conceal them. [S2]

### Adapt to a home burner

His fried rice teaching addresses batch size and ingredient handling so the pan can fry effectively in an ordinary kitchen. [S3]

**Application designed here:** Reduce the batch when added food cools the cooking surface. For a quick rice bowl, choose a base and a familiar topping whose preparation fits the evening. Keep additions purposeful and leave a manageable final assembly. Record the version the household likes so it can become a standby. The benefit of understanding a technique is not only making a spectacular dish; it is reducing the work of getting a decent ordinary meal ready. [S3]

### Keep the main ingredient legible

The fried rice recipe treats additions as flavor support rather than allowing them to overwhelm the rice. [S3]

**Application designed here:** Choose a small set of mix-ins and cook in stages. When choosing between recipes, compare their texture goals and resources before their claims to be best. A crispness-focused method may require preparation you do not want tonight. Explain the likely tradeoff and offer the simpler path without pretending it is identical. The user should be able to choose an informed compromise rather than being pushed into the most elaborate published version. [S3]

### Explain why as well as how

His stated project is to help people understand cooking so the lesson remains useful beyond a particular printed recipe. [S1]

**Application designed here:** Give the physical reason for the one step that matters most. A tasting comparison needs a fair question. Let eaters describe the difference before telling them which sample is meant to be superior. If preferences split, report that distinction instead of announcing a universal winner. Physical measurements and subjective enjoyment can inform the same decision while remaining different forms of evidence about what happened at the table. [S1]

### A repertoire reduces weekday effort

His Wok Week note describes quick stir-fries and rice bowls as repeatable meals, rather than requiring a new elaborate project every night. [S4]

**Application designed here:** Learn one adaptable rice or noodle dinner for a busy evening. When a trial disappoints, return to the first point where it diverged from the reference. Was the surface still wet, was the pan crowded or was the preparation incomplete when cooking began? Select the most relevant variable for another attempt. Avoid a long list of disconnected fixes that makes the next result impossible to interpret. [S4]

### Experiments should return to pleasure

His official introduction puts joy and empowerment at the centre of the kitchen-science project. [S1]

**Application designed here:** Stop adding steps when the improvement no longer justifies your effort. A team-workflow comparison can borrow the habit of specifying and testing a change. Choose a small task, define the benefit and keep the old process available as a reference. The transfer should not import kitchen terminology just for effect. Its useful output is a decision based on observed work and a clear statement of what the comparison could not establish. [S1]

## Where they were wrong or criticised

Testing one dish does not establish a universal rule for every ingredient or burner. Avoid turning an optimisation exercise into unnecessary labor, and distinguish the cook’s preferences from measured outcomes. These limitations concern applying the public work; they do not imply a consensus of critics or a newly discovered personal fault. [S5]

## Blind spots to watch

A familiar method can make a poor fit look like the user’s fault. Ask whether the problem is really within this person’s field, whether the user controls the relevant conditions, and whether the plan requires time or resources they do not have. Separate an informed practical suggestion from a professional assessment. Keep the first action reversible where possible, and stop when new evidence shows the premise was mistaken. These are editorial safeguards for using this profile; they are not quotations or newly attributed personal beliefs. [S2]
