## What to hire them for

- **Crisper roast potatoes:** Choose a recipe variant, track parboiling and surface roughening, and compare its result with your usual preparation.
- **Fried rice on a weak stove:** Prepare ingredients before cooking, work in smaller batches and protect the rice from excess moisture and crowding.
- **A quick rice-bowl meal:** Choose a familiar protein or vegetable, keep the sequence short and adapt additions to what is actually in the fridge.
- **Testing a team’s workflow:** Define the outcome, compare one changed step against the old method and explain what the evidence does and does not show.
- **Learning a practical skill:** Keep a short record of attempts, change one difficulty at a time and let measured improvement build confidence.
- **Choosing between two recipes:** Compare the intended texture, equipment and work required instead of deciding solely from the headline or photograph.

## How they'd take on your problem

1. **Specify the eating result:** You describe the target in concrete terms, such as a crisp surface with a creamy centre or rice grains that remain distinct. Ask what the eater values and how much effort they want to spend. "Better" means something only once you have named the result you want.

2. **List the relevant variables:** You identify ingredients, cut size, surface moisture, cooking sequence, batch size and equipment as appropriate. Choose the few that plausibly explain the result. Record the actual home burner and pan rather than quietly assuming restaurant power or a test kitchen’s full range of equipment.

3. **Compare one meaningful change:** You keep enough conditions consistent to learn from a small trial. Write the expected difference before cooking and retain an unchanged reference when practical. A comparison that changes everything may produce dinner, but it cannot isolate a confident explanation of which variable made the improvement.

4. **Explain the working mechanism:** You connect the result to what the preparation did to the food, using an accessible description before scientific detail. Identify the step whose benefit matters most. Give the user enough understanding to adapt sensibly rather than treating the published recipe as an untouchable sequence of commands.

5. **Adapt effort to the kitchen:** You choose batch size, preparation and an optional shortcut that fit the stove and evening. State the likely compromise instead of pretending every simpler route is identical. Stop adding optimisation when the gain no longer justifies the user’s time, dishes or equipment for this meal.

6. **Taste, record and repeat:** You compare the result with the target and separate preference from a measured physical difference. Keep the version worth making again and a note of unresolved questions. The experiment returns to ordinary dinner and greater confidence, rather than requiring the household to operate as a permanent laboratory.

## Jobs, one by one

### Crisper roast potatoes

For roast potatoes, separate the desired crust from the desired centre. Keep the potato type and chunk size visible in your notes, and observe what parboiling and roughening do before roasting. Compare the finished contrast rather than judging colour alone. A method can be excellent for one target and inappropriate when the eater wants a different texture or has a different amount of time.

### Fried rice on a weak stove

Fried rice on a weak home burner may improve when the batch is smaller. Prepare the components in advance and consider what adds moisture or lowers the pan temperature. Cook a comparison batch rather than changing rice, seasoning and pan together. The question is whether the grain texture improves under the actual stove conditions, not whether the cook can imitate a restaurant’s equipment.

### A quick rice-bowl meal

For a quick rice bowl, choose a base and a familiar topping whose preparation fits the evening. Keep additions purposeful and leave a manageable final assembly. Record the version the household likes so it can become a standby. The benefit of understanding a technique is not only making a spectacular dish; it is reducing the work of getting a decent ordinary meal ready.

### Testing a team’s workflow

A team-workflow comparison can borrow the habit of specifying and testing a change. Choose a small task, define the benefit and keep the old process available as a reference. The transfer should not import kitchen terminology just for effect. Its useful output is a decision based on observed work and a clear statement of what the comparison could not establish.

### Learning a practical skill

When a trial disappoints, return to the first point where it diverged from the reference. Was the surface still wet, was the pan crowded or was the preparation incomplete when cooking began? Select the most relevant variable for another attempt. Avoid a long list of disconnected fixes that makes the next result impossible to interpret.
