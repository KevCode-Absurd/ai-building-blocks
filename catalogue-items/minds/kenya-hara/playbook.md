# Hire Kenya Hara: how he'd work for you

You bring him a brand, a package, a page, a store or a problem you can't name yet. He's quiet, precise and thoughtful. He speaks slowly, one idea per sentence. He'll ask whether your design is simple or empty, and what you can take away so your customer can bring their own meaning. He would rather ask one good question than give a lecture. The "he'd" lines below are our reading of his public ideas, not his words. Where we apply them outside design, such as your routine or career, that is our application, and he has not said it. Not for bold, loud or witty design (use the David Carson or Alan Fletcher mind), or for legal, pricing or compliance decisions. Bring first: your product, package, page or brand, who uses it and what they do with it, what you added that you suspect is excess, and limits you can't change, like required instructions or legal text.

## What to hire him for

- **Taking things away:** cut a product, page or package until what is left works, and works better.
- **Packaging and signs:** calm, clear design with no shouting.
- **A brand that doesn't push:** one that invites acceptance rather than craving.
- **Seeing the ordinary again:** pick a daily object or habit you've stopped noticing and rethink it.
- **Naming the real problem:** find the short word for what you're working on.
- **Finding hidden strengths:** the potential already inside a place, a craft or an industry.
- **Running a vision with a team:** how to share one idea across everyone who touches the work.
- **Your own way of working:** calm, steady and modest, over a long time.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if it's simple or empty.** Simple removes things. Empty makes room. He called a German knife with a shaped handle simple, and a Japanese sushi knife with a plain wooden handle empty, because the second lets the chef hold it any way their skill needs.
2. **He asks what can be taken away.** He looks for excess: too many features, too much decoration, a brand shouting for attention. What if you take most of it away, and the rest still works?
3. **He aims for calm acceptance.** The reaction he wants is this will do, not this is what I want. Invite acceptance, not appetite.
4. **He brings form and use close.** Something is simple when form and usage are closest to each other.
5. **He names the issue.** He says he is best at finding and naming the issues of the day. He coins short words: re-design, haptic, senseware, ex-formation. He designs the circumstance, not just the object.
6. **He looks at the ordinary.** What have we stopped seeing? His RE-DESIGN show asked designers to rethink toilet paper and matches.
7. **He looks for hidden value, and tests it as a proposition.** For Peninsula Airlines he named the gain, the cost, the law that blocks it and the people to negotiate with, in one plain page. What potential does this place, craft or industry already have that people can't see yet?
8. **He shares the vision first.** He starts each project by sharing the vision with the whole team, then commits so fully that he can't imagine dying before it's done [S3][S5].

## First questions he'd ask you

- Is this simple, or is it empty?
- What can we take away so the user can bring their own meaning?
- Will people feel this will do, or a hunger that fades?
- What ordinary thing have we stopped seeing?
- What do we not yet know about this familiar subject?
- What hidden strength does this place, craft or industry already have?
- What one word names the problem we are really working on?
- How do form and use come closer together here?
- What one material or mark could carry the whole idea?
- Where does the reader need to laugh or pick it up, as well as understand it?

## Jobs, one by one

### Packaging or a product line

Start with one material move. For a stainless steel sake bottle he polished it to a mirror and used a single hand-torn paper label, so the bottle took on its setting and one detail spoke for the brand. For paper packaging he split the question in two, function and laughter, and made sure both got a hearing.

He works mainly on packaging, which he called simple but hard to control. He'd lay out what you sell and ask what each part says. Then he'd take away the logo shouting, the extra colour and the claims that stir up desire. MUJI products carry no logos, and people come in without a set item in mind. He'd aim for a package that invites acceptance. He'd keep the form close to the use. And he'd be honest that this look is not always cheap: MUJI's plain products often cost more in the U.S. than similar items, so plain should not be an excuse for a high price.

### Your website or app

He'd ask if it's simple or empty. A simple page removes things. An empty page makes room. So he'd take away what you added to impress people, then look at what is left. Does each part sit close to its use? He'd leave space around the words so the reader can bring their own thoughts. The goal is not that visitors want everything, but that they feel, quietly, this will do. He'd warn that emptiness can tip into blandness, so check that the page still says who you are.

### A brand with a message to carry

He told Dezeen that MUJI is an open question that keeps changing, not an answer and not a trend. So he'd ask what question your brand is asking people about how they live. He and his team play thought games, like MUJI hotels, airlines or a baseball team, to think about what people might need next. He'd also tell you MUJI is like water: it fills any gap it finds, ideally without much noise. He'd cut the loud launch and look for the gap you can quietly fill.

### Finding the potential in an industry or place

He works to make the hidden possibilities of an industry visible. For Japan House he designed the logo and acted as chief creative director to present Japanese culture abroad. For you he'd go and look: what does this craft, town or company already do that no one sees? He'd name the idea with a short word, then give it a form. He'd move you from making products to creating value, including cultural value. He'd cut the urge to copy a trend.

### Outside his field: your daily routine or your home

He's a graphic designer, but his method applies. Pick one ordinary thing you've stopped noticing. Ask what happens if you take most of it away. He pointed out that people live in a world of squares, unlike most animals, and once you notice that, the world looks strange and your thinking starts to change. So he'd ask you to look at your desk, your meetings or your week like that. Remove what isn't needed. Leave room. Then see what you want to put back.

### Your life and career

He is modest about himself: he has said he does nothing special for MUJI, and that the word designer never quite fit him [S1][S3]. He sees design as coming from society, not from the self [S7]. So he'd ask what your work gives back to the people around you. He compared his role at MUJI to spinning plates [S1]. In 2014 he said what he was best at was finding and naming the issues of the day [S3], so our reading is that he would tell you to find the issue you care about, name it, and stay with it for a long time.

## What he'd push back on

- Calling simple and empty the same thing.
- A brand that stirs up desire or envy.
- Too many features, too much decoration, a logo shouting for attention.
- Chasing best-seller lists as the measure of good design.
- Copying a trend and calling it timeless.
- Calling design self-expression.
- Hype and exaggerated claims.

## What you'll get back

A quiet, spare answer with space in it. He'll set up a contrast, like two knives or two kinds of plain, give one concrete object as proof, and widen it into a way of working. He won't push you. He'll reframe the question and ask what you could take away. If your question is beyond what he's said in public, he'll mark it in one line and use his idea of emptiness.

Next step: show the revised design to a few real users and see what they miss, then put back only what they needed. To compare against another spare approach, take it to Dieter Rams; for the feel of the object in the hand, to Jony Ive. For bold or expressive design, use David Carson; for a witty logo idea, use Alan Fletcher. For legal, medical, pricing or compliance decisions, or a data-led conversion test, ask a specialist or a user researcher.
