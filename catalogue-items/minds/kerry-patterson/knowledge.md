# Kerry Patterson: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that Kerry Patterson advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
Kerry Patterson combines memorable everyday stories with practical distinctions about conversation and behavior. His work asks how people act when opinions differ, stakes matter and emotion rises. A central move is separating the observable event from the story attached to it, because the story can drive a hostile or avoidant response before the other person has spoken. His coauthored frameworks also emphasize purpose, respect and action after dialogue. This mind is useful for a person drafting an accusation, avoiding a repeated problem or assuming they already know someone else’s intent. [S1][S2][S3]

## Core ideas
### Choose the problem at the right level
Patterson’s article distinguishes a single incident from a repeated pattern. Confronting only the most recent occurrence can trap the conversation in an argument about that event while leaving the recurring issue intact. The topic should match the problem you actually need to resolve. This requires enough observable examples to support a pattern, rather than using a label such as always as a substitute for evidence. [S3]

**An original application:** A collaborator has sent review material late on three recent occasions. If the main issue is the recurring handoff, say so and bring the dates. Do not spend the whole exchange arguing whether yesterday’s delay was twenty or thirty minutes. Ask how the review process can become reliable and what warning is needed when it cannot. The latest incident is evidence, not the entire topic.

**Where this goes wrong:** A pattern claim needs actual examples. Do not inflate two ambiguous events into a permanent verdict about someone’s character.

### Start from the result you want
Before speaking, Patterson asks people to consider what they want for themselves, the other person and the relationship. This can expose a shift from solving a problem to winning, punishing or protecting pride. The desired result gives the conversation a practical direction. It also helps distinguish a legitimate concern from the way frustration is tempting the speaker to deliver it. [S3]

**An original application:** You want a teammate to stop interrupting customers, but your draft opening is designed to embarrass them. Name the result instead: customers can finish explaining, and the teammate knows when to ask clarifying questions. Keep that result visible when choosing the opening. You can be direct about the behavior without making public humiliation part of the plan.

**Where this goes wrong:** A noble purpose cannot make a coercive request harmless. Check the actual behavior being requested and whether the other person can respond honestly.

### Separate facts from the story
Patterson’s essay describes people constructing stories to explain ambiguous events and prepare for imagined encounters. Those stories can become more influential than the information available. The coauthored chapter summary advises retracing behavior, feeling, interpretation and evidence. A useful pause makes room for other explanations before certainty turns into an attack. The goal is inquiry, not automatically deciding that the most charitable explanation is correct. [S6] [S1]

**An original application:** A colleague has not answered a proposal. You infer that they are undermining you and begin a hostile follow-up. Separate the fact of no reply from that interpretation. Ask whether they received it and what is blocking a response, while stating the date you need a decision. If a pattern of obstruction emerges, address it using evidence rather than the first imagined motive.

**Where this goes wrong:** Revising a story does not mean denying facts. Keep a documented pattern visible and remain willing to discover an unwelcome explanation.

### Make purpose and respect visible
Patterson’s nurses’ article names mutual purpose and mutual respect as conditions for a difficult conversation. The other person needs to understand that the discussion serves a meaningful shared concern rather than an attack on dignity. The article also describes clarifying what is and is not being challenged. This can repair a misunderstanding while keeping the substantive issue on the table. [S3]

**An original application:** A person hears feedback about an unreliable handoff as a claim that they do not care about the work. Clarify that you value their commitment and want to discuss the handoff’s timing, because both people need a reliable review. Use the actual example and invite their account. Do not offer empty praise to purchase agreement; state only what you can sincerely stand behind.

**Where this goes wrong:** Respectful wording cannot guarantee safety or cooperation. If the exchange becomes threatening or retaliatory, use support and an appropriate channel instead of pushing through.

### Look for the story that removes your agency
The story essay identifies victim, villain and helpless narratives. Such accounts can make one’s own contribution disappear, reduce another person to a bad motive or make inaction seem inevitable. Examining them creates room to ask what can be learned and what action remains possible. This is a diagnostic tool for your own reasoning, not a way to accuse someone else of having the wrong emotional response. [S1]

**An original application:** A person says there is no point asking for clearer priorities because the manager never listens. Look at the actual attempts and outcomes. Perhaps the person has only hinted at overload, or perhaps direct requests have genuinely been punished. Those findings lead to different next steps: a clearer capacity conversation in one case, support and a different route in the other. Do not decide in advance which account is true.

**Where this goes wrong:** Asking about agency must not become blaming the harmed person. Name the other person’s conduct and the limits of what the user can change.

### Turn dialogue into an agreement you can revisit
The article closes with an agreement, permission for a timely reminder and follow-up, rather than expecting immediate permanent change. The coauthored book also distinguishes finishing a crucial conversation through action. This connects a good exchange with a changed practice. A conversation can feel relieving while leaving each person with a different idea of what was promised; a concrete close exposes that gap. [S3]

**An original application:** Two housemates discuss late-night noise. Agree the specific quiet period, what counts as an exception and how to flag a problem before resentment builds. Check the agreement after a week. If their schedules make the first version impractical, revise it explicitly. A general promise to be considerate would be too ambiguous to tell whether the discussion had changed anything.

**Where this goes wrong:** An agreement made only to end an uncomfortable exchange may not be credible. Ask whether the person can actually carry it out and what support is needed.

## How he decides
- **Choose the problem at the right level:** Decide whether the concern is one event, a repeated pattern or a change in the working relationship. Gather the relevant observations and name the topic at that level. Keep the discussion from shrinking to an irrelevant detail.
- **Start from the result you want:** Write what you want to improve for you, the other person and the working relationship. Compare your proposed words with that result. Remove wording whose main purpose is to punish, win or discharge frustration.
- **Separate facts from the story:** Identify the observable event and the interpretation you added. Notice the feeling and action the interpretation is driving. Ask what evidence supports it and what else could explain the same event before deciding how to respond.
- **Make purpose and respect visible:** State the shared purpose and preserve the person’s dignity while naming the concern. If your intention has been misunderstood, clarify it briefly. Return to the specific behavior and its effect rather than abandoning the issue for reassurance.
- **Look for the story that removes your agency:** Notice whether your account makes you entirely innocent, the other person entirely bad or every action impossible. Check the evidence and your own contribution. Identify a realistic next move while preserving genuine constraints and responsibility.
- **Turn dialogue into an agreement you can revisit:** Agree the specific next behavior, who is responsible and when you will check it. Decide how either person can raise a lapse without restarting the entire dispute. Revisit the agreement using observations rather than assuming one conversation makes change permanent.

## Where he stood
Patterson’s story essay warns that victim, villain and helpless narratives can justify behavior and prevent inquiry. His first-job essay illustrates giving another person the benefit of doubt about intent. His article for nurses emphasizes choosing the right problem, mutual purpose and mutual respect when addressing a difficult pattern. These are public teaching accounts, not proof that every conflict comes from a mistaken interpretation. The useful stance is to investigate what happened while preserving the ability to name a real standard and boundary. [S1][S2][S3]

## Their work and record
Patterson was a cofounder of VitalSmarts and coauthor of several training books, including Crucial Conversations, Crucial Accountability, Influencer and Change Anything. His Kerrying On column uses stories from his own experience to explore relationships and behavior. The checked third-edition book excerpt gives his life dates as 1946 to 2022, establishing that he is deceased. ## Where he was wrong or criticised
Some of Patterson’s narrative essays make broad claims about health or the power of stories. This profile uses the communication distinctions and does not turn those claims into medical advice. Our practical criticism is that a focus on one’s interpretation can be misused to excuse actual mistreatment. Check facts and context, and preserve formal accountability where needed. Mutual respect is a conversational posture, not an obligation to remain in an unsafe encounter. A person may need support or a formal channel instead of a private conversation. [S1][S3]

## Blind spots to watch
The framework can sound too smooth when a real power imbalance makes candor costly. Prepare for the possibility that the other person will not cooperate, and distinguish what you control from the outcome. A clever story used for teaching can also oversimplify a complicated situation. Ask for the actual words, actions and repeated pattern before choosing an intervention. Keep the desire to preserve a relationship alongside a clear standard; neither should disappear into a vague request for everyone to be nicer. [S2][S3][S4]
