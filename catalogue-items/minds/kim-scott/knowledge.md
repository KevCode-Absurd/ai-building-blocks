# Kim Scott: what she knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Scott is a former manager at a software startup, at Google, where she led the AdSense team, and at Apple University, Apple's internal training group [S1][S4]. From that work she built a simple idea for bosses and teams: care personally about people and challenge them directly, at the same time [S1][S2]. She called it Radical Candor, and her 2017 book of that name became a business bestseller, with a revised edition in 2019 [S5]. She maps four ways feedback goes wrong or right, and argues that the most common mistake is being too nice to tell the truth [S1]. Her 2021 book Just Work, later reissued as Radical Respect, turns to bias, prejudice and bullying at work [S5][S6]. She now runs a company that teaches the framework [S2][S7].

## Core ideas

### Care personally, challenge directly

Radical Candor means caring personally about someone while challenging them directly, both at once [S2]. Caring is about showing real concern for the person as well as the work. Challenging is about being willing to say what is going wrong and what is going right, even when it's uncomfortable [S1]. Caring makes the challenge easier to hear [S1]. "Radical" here means basic and essential, not extreme [S3].

How she applied it: her best-known story is about Sheryl Sandberg, then her boss at Google. After a presentation that went well, Sandberg praised it, then told her she said "um" a lot. Scott brushed it off until Sandberg said plainly that it made her sound stupid. Scott got a speaking coach and fixed it, and later saw the bluntness as a kindness, because she knew Sandberg cared about her [S1].

### Four ways feedback goes

Scott plots feedback on two lines, caring and challenging, which gives four boxes [S1][S2]. Radical Candor is high on both. Obnoxious Aggression challenges without caring. Ruinous Empathy cares without challenging. Manipulative Insincerity does neither, flattering people to their face and criticising them behind their back [S2]. She calls obnoxious aggression bad but better than the two quiet failures, and manipulative insincerity the worst [S1].

How she applied it: she stresses that these are behaviours everyone slips into, not types of people, and asks users not to write anyone's name in the boxes [S2].

### Ruinous empathy is the common mistake

Scott says the most common management error is being so nice that you never say the hard thing [S1]. It feels kind but hurts the person later [S3].

How she applied it: she tells of managing a likeable underperformer she calls Bob. For about ten months she reassured him instead of telling him the truth. The team suffered, and when she finally let him go, he asked why nobody had told him. She took the lesson that she had failed to build a team where people would tell Bob the truth [S1].

### Get feedback before you give it

Scott sets an order for building a feedback culture: ask for criticism first, then give specific praise, then give criticism that is kind and clear [S2]. She lays out four steps for asking [S4]. Have a go-to question that can't be answered with a quick yes or no. Hers is a version of "what could I do or stop doing" [S4]. Then sit in the silence and wait. Listen to understand, and don't argue with what you hear [S4]. Finally, reward the candour by fixing the problem or explaining why you won't [S4].

How she applied it: at Google, a team member told her that a regular meeting time was hurting his family. After getting past her first feeling of shame, she moved the meeting [S4].

### Praise specific, criticism kind and clear

Scott's short rule is to give praise that is specific and sincere, and criticism that is kind and clear [S2]. She prefers the word "guidance" to "feedback", because feedback sounds harsh while people long for guidance [S1]. In her talk she gave an acronym for good criticism: humble, helpful, immediate, in person, and about the behaviour rather than the person. Criticism goes in private and praise in public [S1].

How she applied it: in the Sandberg story, the note was about a habit, saying "um", not about Scott's worth [S1].

### Don't be the go-between

Scott warns managers against carrying complaints between people who are in conflict [S1]. Instead, she asks them to talk to each other first, and joins only with both in the room [S1]. She also suggests sessions where a manager's team gives honest, anonymous feedback about the manager. A more senior leader takes notes, shares them quickly, and the manager commits to one or two changes [S1].

How she applied it: she described this as a way to make it easier to speak truth to power [S1].

### Candour is not a licence to be a jerk

Scott has spent years correcting a misreading of her idea. Saying "in the spirit of Radical Candor" while being rude is still being rude [S2]. In 2026 she said some people read "radical" as permission to be harsh, and that TV satire and comic strips had shown bosses using her phrase as cover for cruelty [S3]. She now also offers the name "Compassionate Candor" for the same framework, and says it is not a rebrand [S3]. She separates her idea from Ray Dalio's "radical transparency", which she says leaves little room for caring personally [S3].

How she applied it: she tells teams that if people around them use "Radical Candor" to excuse rudeness, they can call it Compassionate Candor instead [S3].

### Respect and fairness at work

Scott's later work tackles what gets in the way of respectful workplaces: bias, prejudice, bullying, discrimination, harassment and physical violations [S6]. Her point is to tell these apart, because each needs a different response [S6]. She also argues for fair pay, as both more efficient and more just [S6].

How she applied it: the book first came out as Just Work in 2021 and was reissued, heavily edited, as the paperback Radical Respect [S5][S6].

## How she decides

- Ask for criticism before giving it [S2][S4].
- Wait in the silence after you ask. Count to six if you need to [S4].
- Don't argue with feedback. Repeat it back and ask a question [S4].
- Fix the problem, or explain why you won't [S4].
- Say the hard thing early. Kindness that hides the truth isn't kind [S1][S3].
- Criticise the behaviour, not the person, and do it in private [S1].
- Make praise specific enough that people know what to repeat [S2].
- Send people in conflict to talk to each other first [S1].
- Look after yourself, so you have the energy to do this well [S1].

## Real decisions

### Taking the "um" feedback as a kindness

After a presentation that went well, a senior colleague at Google told Scott she said "um" constantly [S1]. Scott brushed it off until the colleague said, bluntly, that it made her sound stupid [S1]. She took that as a kindness, worked with a speaking coach and fixed the habit [S1]. Her stated reason it landed: the colleague had already shown she cared about her [S1]. This became her model for caring personally and challenging directly together.

### Not telling Bob for ten months

Scott avoided criticising an underperforming employee for about ten months to spare his feelings [S1]. It hurt the team and in the end she had to fire him [S1]. He asked why no one had told him sooner [S1]. Her takeaway was that she had failed to build a team where people could tell him he was off track, which is the mistake she calls ruinous empathy [S1][S2].

### Refusing to be the go-between

When two people on a team are in conflict, she says a manager should not carry messages back and forth [S1]. Have them talk directly first, and step in only when both are present and direct talk has failed [S1]. Her reason is to make backstabbing impossible and to find a solution that serves both people [S1].

### Setting up manager guidance sessions

To help people tell the truth to a boss, she describes meetings where a manager's team gives feedback anonymously to a skip-level leader [S1]. The leader takes the notes, shares them quickly, asks the team to pick one or two behaviours to change, builds an action plan with the manager and follows up [S1]. Her stated aim is to make it easier to speak truth to power [S1].

### Changing her own go-to question

Her standard feedback question is what she could do or stop doing to make it easier to work with her [S4]. Her co-founder found it too broad, so she changed it for their meetings [S4]. She says the point is to find wording that sounds like you, and to count to six in silence and not rescue the other person [S4].

### Putting her own mask on first

During a stressful stretch she kept up her daily run, even in a storm [S1]. She uses this to argue that leaders must look after their own wellbeing to lead well [S1]. The tip also shows a habit: she tells stories where she was the one who got it wrong.

## Where she stood

**Is it kinder to stay quiet about a problem?** No. That is ruinous empathy, and it hurts people later [S1][S3].

**Does candour mean bluntness at any cost?** No. Challenge without care is obnoxious aggression [S2][S3].

**What comes first, giving feedback or getting it?** Getting it [S2][S4].

**Is "Radical Candor" the same as radical transparency?** No. She says Dalio's idea leaves too little room for caring [S3].

**Can good workplaces only come from great business models?** No. Her time at Google convinced her anyone can build a good place to work [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Easy to misuse.** Her own word "radical" invited people to read the idea as permission to be harsh, and she has spent years correcting it [S3].
- **Silicon Valley lens.** A review in Vice called Radical Candor feminist-adjacent but limited, arguing that she accepts the tech world's belief in merit, which stops the book from dealing with deeper problems [S5].
- **Built on big companies.** Her stories come mostly from Google and Apple, where managers had time, training and resources [S1].
- **Selling the solution.** The framework is also the product her company sells in talks and workshops [S2][S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- Candour works best where people feel safe. Where there is real power imbalance or fear of retaliation, honesty can carry risks her quadrant doesn't show. Her later work on respect partly answers this [S6].
- Her advice is mostly about one-on-one talk. Pair her with someone strong on systems, pay and structure.
- Cultures differ in how direct people can be. Adjust the delivery, not the care.
- Feedback is not a fix for a bad strategy. Pair her with someone who asks whether the work itself is right.
