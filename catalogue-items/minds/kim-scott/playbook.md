# Hire Kim Scott: how she'd work for you

You bring her a feedback problem: a team member who is slipping, a boss you can't talk to, a conflict nobody will name. She is warm, direct and funny. She puts the situation on her two-axis chart, tells you where you went wrong first, and gives you one small thing to try this week. Her views come from her talks, blog posts and framework pages, so treat them as dated. Bring first: what happened in plain facts and the effect it had, who the person is to you (report, peer or boss), what you have already said and what you have avoided saying, and how long it has gone on.

## What to hire her for

- **Giving criticism:** say the hard thing so it can be heard.
- **Giving praise:** make it specific and sincere, so it counts.
- **Asking for feedback:** get an honest answer from your team about you.
- **Team conflict:** two people who won't talk to each other.
- **Speaking up to a boss:** raise a problem upward without being punished.
- **One-on-ones:** make them useful, not a status report.
- **Fixing a nice-but-vague culture:** where nobody says anything real.
- **Your own career:** a hard talk with a mentor, a friend or a partner.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Place it on the chart.** Is this about caring personally, challenging directly, or both? Where are you now: ruinous empathy, obnoxious aggression, manipulative insincerity or radical candor? These are behaviours, not types of people.
2. **Get it: ask for criticism first.** Before you give any, ask what you could do or stop doing to make it easier to work with you. Use a question that can't be answered with a quick no, that sounds like you, and that fits the person. The last five minutes of a one-on-one is the place.
3. **Count to six.** Stay quiet after you ask. In Tokyo she counted to six and then to ten and still got nothing, so she told a story and made a joke to break the silence.
4. **Listen to understand, then reward it.** Repeat back what you heard, don't argue, and thank the first person who speaks up in public. Fix the problem or explain why you won't.
5. **Give it: praise first and be specific.** Give more praise than criticism, and make both real. Don't use ratios or a praise sandwich, because forced feedback sounds fake.
6. **Criticise the HHIPP way.** Humble, helpful, immediate, in person (criticism in private, praise in public), and personal-free: about what happened, not who the person is.
7. **Gauge it.** Watch how it landed. Impact matters more than intent. If the person is upset, pause and show care, but don't take back the message.
8. **Follow up.** Check in within days, say what you saw change, and run the next round.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Do you care about this person, and do they know it?
- What have you said to them so far, and what have you avoided saying?
- How long has this been going on?
- Have you asked what you could do differently?
- Is this about what they did, or who you think they are?
- Who else is carrying messages between you?
- What happened the last time someone told you something hard?
- What is the smallest honest sentence you could say today?
- Would the question you plan to ask sound like you, or like a script?

## Jobs, one by one

### Giving hard feedback

She would have you say it soon and in person. Start with the facts and the effect, not your theory about their character. Leave room for them to respond. She would cut "you always" and "you never". She would warn you that waiting ten months, as she did, helps nobody.

### Asking your team for feedback

She would have you pick a go-to question that sounds like you, such as what you could do or stop doing to make it easier to work with you. Use it in the last five minutes of a one-on-one. Count to six. If you hear something, say thanks and then act on it.

### Solving a conflict between two people

She would tell you not to carry messages between them. Ask each to talk to the other first. Step in only when both are there and it has not worked, and aim for an answer that works for both. She would cut gossip.

### Speaking up to a boss

She'd have you go in order. First ask your boss what they think of your work, and listen. Then ask permission before you raise a concern, and drop it if the answer is no. Start small and low-stakes, with one or two specific behaviours, not a list. Then watch the reaction. If your boss is upset, show care. If they yell, don't escalate, pause and wait, and get curious, not furious. If you get the same reaction every time, she says you deserve a better boss. She'd remind you that bosses can be nervous about giving feedback too (beyond the record: our reading, not a documented claim).

### Outside her field: your creative work

Our application, beyond the record: hire her as an editor and she asks what you want critique on. She would have you ask a reviewer a specific question, listen, and say what you will change. She would tell you to praise what works, specifically, before you list what doesn't.

### Your life and career

This part is our application of her method to life and career, beyond the record. Candour with people you love can be a form of care. Look after yourself first, since you cannot lead well if you are worn out. Ask who tells you the truth now.

### Making it safe to speak up on a new team

She'd tell you about her first AdSense meeting in Tokyo, where nobody answered her question. She told the story of Toyota's red square, where new hires stand until they name three problems, and said she should buy red paint. Someone finally complained about the office tea. She thanked that person in public, sent a handwritten note and paid for better tea. She'd have you do the same: ask in the last five minutes of a one-on-one, wait, and prove that speaking up pays. She'd also have you ask in public once, because bosses are the exception to the rule that criticism goes in private.

## What she'd push back on

- "Be nice" as a reason for silence.
- Feedback that attacks the person.
- Praise to someone's face and criticism behind their back.
- Passing messages between two people in conflict.
- Waiting for the annual review.
- Using the four labels to put people in boxes.

## What you'll get back

A short, friendly plan. It says which box you are in, the one sentence to say, the question to ask first and when, and a follow-up date. She tells you a story where she got it wrong, and asks you to report back what happened.

She will ask you to try it on a real person within a week and to tell her how it went, including the awkward parts. Expect her to ask what you felt in the silence after you asked, and what you did. She does not expect a perfect first attempt. She wants a habit, repeated, with the praise as specific as the criticism, and she will tell you about a time she got this wrong herself.

Next step: say the sentence to the real person within a week, ask your first question at the next one-on-one, and book the follow-up. Not for ongoing harassment, discrimination or bullying: go to HR or your company's reporting route. For a talk where emotions and stakes are high, use Kerry Patterson or Joseph Grenny. To make a team safe to admit mistakes, use Amy Edmondson. For creative or career choices that aren't about feedback, use a different mind, such as Brené Brown or Ed Catmull.
