# Konosuke Matsushita: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Matsushita was born in 1894 in what is now Wakayama Prefecture [S1]. He left home young to work as an apprentice in Osaka, then joined the Osaka Electric Light Company [S1]. In 1918, with his wife Mumeno and her brother Toshio Iue, he founded a small firm making electrical housewares [S6]. It grew into Matsushita Electric, sold goods under the National brand and later became Panasonic [S1][S2]. In 1932 he told his staff the company's true mission: to make goods so plentiful and cheap that poverty would end, through a 250-year plan [S6]. He kept people on in the Great Depression, split the firm into divisions, rebuilt it after the war, and introduced a five-day week in 1965 [S2]. He founded the PHP Institute in 1946 to study "peace and happiness through prosperity," and the Matsushita Institute of Government and Management in 1979 [S2][S5]. In Japan he is often called the god of management [S1].

## Core ideas

### A business needs a mission bigger than profit

In spring 1932, fourteen years after he founded the firm, Matsushita came to see what he called its true mission [S6]. He asked whether industrialists had a higher duty to fulfil [S6]. His answer was that a maker's job is to supply goods in such abundance that poverty disappears and people can be happy [S6][S7]. The PHP Institute describes this as making products as widely available as running water, the idea known as the tap water philosophy [S4].

How he applied it: on May 5, 1932, he gathered about 168 employees in Osaka, told them the mission and read a declaration. Staff came to the stage one by one to pledge themselves, from 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. [S6]. The company still treats May 5 as its true founding day [S6].

### Plan in centuries, act in stages

Matsushita thought a mission that big needed a very long plan. At the 1932 meeting he announced a 250-year plan to fulfil it [S6]. One summary describes it as ten phases of 25 years each [S8].

How he applied it: he told staff that growth for its own sake was not the point. The company should grow to serve its mission [S6]. He also set shorter targets inside the long plan, such as a five-year plan in 1956 and a 1960 promise to bring in a five-day week within five years [S2].

### Material and spiritual prosperity together

Matsushita believed people are born able to live in limitless prosperity, peace and happiness, but often don't know it [S3]. He blamed poverty on human behaviour, such as greed and uncontrolled desire, rather than on fate [S3]. In his view, spiritual growth had to keep pace with material progress, or the imbalance would cause conflict [S4].

How he applied it: he founded the PHP Institute on November 3, 1946, in the hard years after the war, to study and spread these ideas [S2]. His 1979 mission statement for a school of leaders warned that a people's spirit can fall into disorder even with economic success, and called for prosperity that is both material and spiritual [S5].

### Keep your people through hard times

Matsushita treated employees as partners in the mission, not as costs to cut. When the Great Depression hit in 1929, the company cut production and factory hours by half, but paid full salaries and dismissed no one [S2].

How he applied it: the loyalty ran both ways. After the war, when occupation authorities moved to purge him as head of a zaibatsu family, the labour union and distributors petitioned to keep him [S2]. Wikipedia puts the number who signed at 15,000 employees [S1].

### Give each unit its own results

Matsushita split the company into product divisions, each linking making and selling and each answerable for its own results [S1][S2]. This let him grow the firm while keeping each part small enough to manage.

How he applied it: the PHP Institute dates the division system to 1933, with nine divisions by 1935 [S2]. Wikipedia dates the first move to 1929 (dates differ) [S1]. After postwar restrictions were lifted, he brought the division system back in 1950 [S2].

### Collective wisdom

Matsushita believed no one person holds the full truth. The PHP Institute quotes him saying that no human being is almighty, and that none has a monopoly on the truth [S4]. He thought wisdom shared across many people becomes far greater than any one mind [S3].

How he applied it: the PHP Institute was built on pooling the views of specialists and people from many backgrounds, then testing them in practice in a cycle of observe, think and act [S4]. At the 1964 Atami Conference he brought together sales companies and distributors to face falling sales together, then took over as acting sales manager to reform the system himself [S2].

### The untrapped mind

Matsushita taught that people judge things through gain, loss, likes and desires, which bends what they see [S4]. He called the cure a *sunao* mind: open, plain and free of bias, able to see things as they are [S4]. It is linked to generosity and care for others, and it lets a person adapt [S4].

How he applied it: the PHP Institute says it works to build this habit of mind as part of its mission [S4].

### Dam management

In 1965 Matsushita announced an idea he called dam management [S2]. A dam holds back water so the flow stays steady through wet and dry seasons. A business, he argued, should hold reserves in the same way so it can ride out sudden changes [S8].

How he applied it: he set it out at the Kansai Economic Seminar in 1965, the same year the five-day week came in [S2]. One summary describes the reserves as covering funds, stock and capacity [S8].

### Start again from today

Matsushita believed a company must keep renewing itself. In 1951, after near ruin, he told a policy meeting that he wanted to run the firm as if it were starting business again that day [S2].

How he applied it: that year he made his first trip to the U.S. and Europe, and in 1952 he agreed a technical partnership with Philips [S2]. In 1964, at 69, he stepped back in as acting sales manager to fix distribution [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask what the business is for, beyond profit [S6].
- Plan for decades, with shorter stages inside the plan [S2][S6].
- Don't grow just to grow [S6].
- Keep people on when times are hard, and share the pain fairly [S2].
- Hold reserves so a shock doesn't force bad choices [S2][S8].
- Hear many views, then test ideas in practice [S4].
- Look at the facts with an open mind, free of gain and loss [S4].
- When things go wrong, start again as if from day one [S2].

## Where he stood

**What is a business for?** To serve society by making good goods plentiful and affordable, and so to end poverty [S6][S7].

**Is profit the goal?** No, not on its own. The mission comes first, and growth should serve it [S6].

**Why are people poor?** Not by nature. He blamed human behaviour, and believed people could reach prosperity if they changed it [S3].

**Is material wealth enough?** No. Spiritual growth must keep pace, or people become unhappy and fight [S4][S5].

**Who knows best?** No single person. Pool the wisdom of many [S3][S4].

**What should leaders do in a slump?** Protect jobs, cut hours if they must, and keep the mission in view [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **War and the zaibatsu label.** After the war, occupation authorities named his company a "restricted concern" and his family a zaibatsu family, and moved to purge him [S2]. The restrictions were lifted in 1950 [S2].
- **Near collapse.** In 1949 the company announced business difficulties, offered voluntary retirements for the first time, and owed so much tax it was nicknamed the "King of Tax Arrears" [S2]. His promise to protect jobs did not hold in every crisis.
- **A friendly record.** Most English sources on his ideas come from organisations he founded, which present him well [S2][S4][S6]. Independent criticism in English is scarce, so treat his record as one-sided.
- **Mission language.** Summaries of his sayings circulate widely online, and many are hard to trace to a source [S8]. Some quotes credited to him may not be his.

## Blind spots to watch

- His mission talk can drift into large, vague ideals. Ask what it means for this month's plan.
- He assumes staff share the mission. Check that people are paid and treated fairly, not just inspired.
- A 250-year view can hide short-term danger. Pair him with someone who watches cash this quarter.
- His sources are mostly his own institutions. Test his ideas against critics and data [S4][S6].
- His world was mass manufacturing in Japan. Some ideas, such as lifetime loyalty, may not fit every team.
