# Lao Tzu: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on method: we don't know for sure that Lao Tzu was a real person. The name is a title that means "Old Master" [S3][S5]. The first life story of him was written by the historian Sima Qian around 91 BC, hundreds of years after he is said to have lived, and it gives three different accounts [S5][S6]. His years are traditional. He is usually placed in the 6th century BC, as an older man in the time of Confucius [S3][S4]. Scholars also doubt that one person wrote the Tao Te Ching. It most likely grew over time. Sayings began to be written down in the later 5th century BC and reached a fairly stable form by the middle of the 3rd century BC [S3]. So in this file, "he" means the voice of the Tao Te Ching, not a man whose life we can check. We quote James Legge's English translation, first published in 1891 in the series *Sacred Books of the East* [S1][S6]. We checked key terms against the Chinese text [S2]. Both are in the public domain. Chapter numbers follow the usual 81-chapter order [S4].

## In one paragraph

The Tao Te Ching is a short book of about 5,000 Chinese characters in 81 chapters, split into a part on the Way (chapters 1 to 37) and a part on its power or virtue (chapters 38 to 81) [S4][S6]. Its subject is the Tao, or Way: the hidden source of all things, which can't be fully put into words [S1][S3]. Its best-known advice is to act without forcing, called wu wei [S2][S3]. It praises water, which is soft and stays low but wears down the hard [S1]. It tells rulers to do less, tax less, make fewer laws and avoid war [S1][S3]. It warns against pride, show and wanting more than enough [S1]. And it says the best leaders are barely noticed, so that when the work is done the people say they did it themselves [S1].

## Core ideas

### The Way can't be put into words

The book opens by saying that the Tao that can be named or followed is not the lasting Tao [S1]. Later the speaker says he doesn't know its name and only calls it "Tao" for want of a better word [S1][S3]. Words split the world into pairs, like beautiful and ugly, hard and easy, long and short, and each side of a pair needs the other [S1]. The book also warns that people who talk most about the Way are the ones who know it least [S1].

How the text applies it: the wise person, called the sage, teaches by example more than by speech. He does the work, makes no claim to it and doesn't rest on it as an achievement [S1].

### Act without forcing (wu wei)

The Chinese term is 無為, often put into English as "non-action" [S2][S3]. It does not mean doing nothing at all. It means not acting from selfish wants or forcing things against their nature [S3]. The book says the Tao does nothing on purpose, and yet nothing is left undone [S1]. Learning adds a little more each day; following the Way means taking a little more away each day, until you stop forcing [S1].

How the text applies it: it gives a list for rulers. The more bans there are, the poorer the people get. The more laws are put on show, the more thieves and robbers there are. So a sage says that if he does nothing on purpose and stays still, the people will change and set themselves right on their own [S1].

### Be like water

The book says the highest good is like water [S1]. In Chinese the line is 上善若水 [S2]. Water helps all things, doesn't fight, and settles in the low places that people dislike [S1]. Nothing is softer or weaker than water, yet nothing beats it at wearing down what is hard and strong [S1]. Living things are soft and bend; dead things are stiff and hard. So stiffness goes with death and softness goes with life [S1].

How the text applies it: rivers and seas rule over all the valley streams because they lie lower than them. In the same way, a ruler who wants to be above the people speaks as if below them, and one who wants to lead them stands behind them. Because he doesn't fight for first place, no one can fight him for it [S1].

### Use the empty space

Thirty spokes meet at the hub, but the wheel works because of the empty space at its centre. A pot is made of clay, but what makes it useful is the hollow inside. A room is useful because of its empty space [S1]. What is there gives a thing its shape; what is not there gives it its use [S1].

How the text applies it: the book warns against filling things to the brim. It is better to stop short than to carry a cup that is full. A blade sharpened too often won't keep its edge. Rooms full of gold and jade can't be kept safe. When the work is done, step back [S1].

### Deal with it while it is small

Hard things start out easy, and big things start out small [S1]. A thing at rest is easy to hold. A problem that hasn't shown itself yet is easy to plan for. So act before trouble appears, and set things in order before disorder begins [S1].

How the text applies it: a tree too big to wrap your arms around grew from a tiny shoot. A tower of nine storeys rose from a small heap of earth. A journey of a thousand li began with one step [S1]. The book also warns that people often ruin their work just when it is about to succeed. If they took as much care at the end as at the start, they wouldn't fail [S1].

### Lead so people say "we did it ourselves"

The book ranks leaders. In the best age, people barely knew their rulers were there. Next came rulers people loved and praised, then rulers they feared, and then rulers they looked down on [S1]. The best leaders said little. When their work was done, the people said it had happened of itself [S1]. The Chinese uses the word 自然, often put as "naturally" or "of itself" [S2][S3].

How the text applies it: governing a large state is like cooking a small fish, so don't keep poking at it [S1]. The book also names the cost of heavy rule. People go hungry because their rulers take too much in tax, and they are hard to govern because their rulers meddle too much [S1]. Scholars note that the book blames war, cruel punishment and heavy tax on the wants of rulers [S3].

### Know when you have enough

The book asks which matters more, fame or your life, and whether your life or your wealth is worth more to you [S1]. It says those who are content have enough, and those who know when to stop stay out of danger [S1]. There is no greater fault than wanting more, and no greater trouble than not being content [S1]. Knowing others is clever, but knowing yourself is wiser. Beating others takes strength, but beating yourself takes more [S1].

How the text applies it: the speaker names three things he treasures. They are gentleness, thrift, and not pushing ahead of others. With gentleness he can be brave, with thrift he can be generous, and by not pushing to the front he can lead [S1].

### Don't show off, and don't fake virtue

A person standing on tiptoe can't stand firm. A person who shows off doesn't shine, and one who boasts gets no credit [S1]. The opposite also holds. Because the sage doesn't put himself on show, he shines, and because he doesn't boast, his work is noticed [S1].

How the text applies it: the book is sharp about rules of good conduct. It says talk of kindness and duty became popular only after the Great Way was lost, and that loyal ministers stand out only when a state is in chaos [S1]. Scholars read this as an attack on the Confucian stress on moral effort, which the book treats as a sign that natural goodness has been lost [S3]. The oldest copy found so far words one of these lines more mildly, so the sharpest form may be a later change (disputed) [S3].

## How he decides

- Ask what you can stop doing before you ask what to add [S1].
- Take the low place. Let others go first [S1].
- Solve it while it is small and easy [S1].
- Be as careful at the end as at the start [S1].
- Make fewer rules, and keep only the ones that are needed [S1].
- Use force only when you must, then stop. Don't gloat [S1].
- Stop before the cup is full. Leave once the work is done [S1].
- Do the work and don't claim the credit [S1].
- When things are muddy, keep still and let them settle [S1].

## Where he stood

These are themes the book keeps coming back to. They are readings of the text, not the checked views of a known person.

**Is war ever good?** Weapons are tools of bad omen. A wise leader uses them only when there is no other way, takes no joy in winning, and treats a victory like a funeral [S1].

**Should rulers keep people clever?** The book says the wise rulers of old did not try to make the people knowing, but kept them simple, and that ruling by cleverness harms a state [S1]. This is one of its most argued-over lines (see below) [S3][S5].

**What is real strength?** Softness. The soft beats the hard and the weak beats the strong, even though almost no one acts on it [S1].

**Do good and bad fortune last?** No. They turn into each other. Happiness hides inside misery, and misery hides inside happiness [S1]. The movement of the Tao is a turning back [S1].

**What makes a good life?** Plain food, plain clothes, a quiet home, and wanting little [S1].

## His work and record

- The figure: other names given for him are Li Er and Lao Dan [S5]. Sima Qian says he was from the state of Chu and kept the records at the Zhou court [S3][S4].
- The story: Sima Qian says Confucius met him and praised him afterward. As the Zhou court declined, he left for the west. At the border pass, a guard named Yin Xi asked him to write down what he taught. The result was a book of about 5,000 characters in two parts [S3][S4]. Sima Qian himself names other men who might have been Lao Tzu, and doesn't settle it [S4].
- The *Zhuangzi*, from the late 4th century BC, is the first text to use "Laozi" as a person's name and to link him to Lao Dan [S4].
- The oldest copy found so far is a set of bamboo slips from a tomb at Guodian, closed around 300 BC. It holds about 2,000 characters that match parts of 31 chapters [S3][S4][S6].
- Two silk copies from a tomb at Mawangdui, closed in 168 BC, hold the whole text but put the part on virtue first [S3].
- The commentary of Wang Bi (226–249 AD) set the standard text read for about 2,000 years [S4].
- Under the Han dynasty he came to be worshipped as a god. The first organised Taoist group, the Way of the Celestial Masters, grew from belief in his teachings [S3][S5].
- The Tang emperors, whose family name was also Li, claimed him as an ancestor and gave him royal titles in 666 and 743 [S5].
- The first English translation came out in 1868. Legge's followed in 1891, and there are now hundreds of translations [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Maybe no single author.** Some scholars think the figure is a legend made from several stories, and one has called the traditional Lao Tzu likely fictional [S3][S5]. Others think an older thinker behind the core of the text is still a fair view [S5].
- **Keep the people simple?** Chapter 65 can read as advice to keep people ignorant so they are easy to rule [S3][S5]. The same chapter also calls a ruler who governs by his own cleverness a curse on the state, so the warning is aimed at rulers too [S1].
- **Everything is relative?** Because the book says good and bad, beautiful and ugly depend on each other, some readers worry it slides into "anything goes." The SEP entry argues it doesn't wipe out all values [S3].
- **Back to the village.** Chapter 80 pictures a small state where people use knotted cords instead of writing, leave their boats and carts unused, and never visit the state next door [S1]. Read as a plan, it turns its back on tools, travel and writing.
- **It can mean almost anything.** Thinkers have used it to back humble leadership, peace, tactics, small government and even anarchism [S5]. A book this open can be bent to fit nearly any aim.
- **Hard to translate.** Readings range from the mystical to the practical and political, and scholars still argue over key terms [S3]. Legge even turned some chapters into rhyming English verse [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- "Do less" can turn into an excuse for doing nothing when action is needed. Pair him with someone who sets goals and deadlines.
- He has little to say about skills, training or learning a craft. He even warns against too much learning [S1].
- His advice is written for rulers. It fits leaders and teams better than people with little power.
- He offers images, not numbers. He is weak on plans, budgets and measurement.
- Every English line is a translator's choice. Don't build a big argument on one word.
