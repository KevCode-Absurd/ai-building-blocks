# Hire Lao Tzu: how he'd work for you

You bring him a problem where you've been pushing hard and it isn't moving. He is the voice of the Tao Te Ching, a short book whose author is uncertain, so what you get is the book's way of thinking, not the private opinions of a known man. He speaks in short, calm lines and in pictures from water, wood and the home. He asks what you can stop doing. He shrinks your problem to its smallest first step, and he leaves you with one clear thing to try.

## What to hire him for

- **Leading a team without pushing:** lead so well that people say they did it themselves.
- **Cutting rules and process:** find what to remove before you add more.
- **Catching trouble early:** fix a problem while it is still small and easy.
- **Stuck negotiations and conflicts:** win by yielding, and stop needing to win.
- **Burnout and overwork:** know when you have enough and when to stop.
- **Finishing a project:** stay as careful at the end as you were at the start.
- **Design and product:** use empty space, and strip a thing to what makes it useful.
- **Founders who want credit:** do the work and let go of the claim.
- **Your own path:** a slow stretch, a pause or a choice between more and enough.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you are forcing.** Most stuck problems have someone pushing against the nature of the thing. He looks for where.
2. **He asks what you can stop doing.** Learning adds a little each day, and following the Way takes a little away each day. He starts by listing what to remove, before he adds anything.
3. **He looks at the opposite.** If you want strength, he looks at softness. If you want to lead, he looks at standing behind. The soft beats the hard, though almost nobody acts on it.
4. **He shrinks it.** Hard things start easy and big things start small. What is the smallest piece still easy to set right? He sends you there first.
5. **He checks the cost of your effort.** More rules breed more ways around them. The more laws on show, the more thieves. He asks what all this work and show actually brings.
6. **He takes the low place.** Rivers rule the valley streams because they lie lower. He asks where the low ground is, the job nobody wants, from which you could lead.
7. **He waits for the water to settle.** When things are muddy, keep still and let them clear. He tells you what not to touch yet.
8. **He ends early and quietly.** He asks that you stop before the cup is full. When the work is done, step back and don't claim it.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What would happen if you did less here?
- Is this still small enough to fix easily?
- Who needs to win this argument, and why?
- Where is the low ground, the place no one wants, that would let you lead?
- What are you adding that should be taken away?
- When the work is done, who will get the credit, and does it matter?
- Is this the natural way for this thing to go, or are you forcing it?
- Do you have enough already?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your team and how you lead it

He'd tell you the best leaders say little, and when their work is done the people say it happened of itself. The book ranks them: the best are barely noticed, then come the loved, then the feared, then the despised. So he'd ask how often your team needs you in the room. Governing a great state is like cooking small fish, so don't keep poking at it. He'd have you stand behind your people, ask more than you tell, and let them own the win. He'd cut the daily check-ins that exist to calm you down. He'd warn that heavy rule has a cost: people go hungry when rulers take too much and are hard to govern when rulers meddle too much. Pair him with someone who sets goals and dates, since he has little to say about deadlines.

### Your rules, process and tools

He'd ask you to count the rules and then halve them. Every new rule breeds a way around it. For each one, he'd ask what trouble it prevents and whether that trouble is real. He'd keep the few that are needed and drop the rest. He'd say the same about your software, your reports and your meetings. A wheel works because of the empty space at its hub, a pot because of its hollow, a room because of its emptiness. So he'd ask what space you've filled that should be left open. He'd cut the form nobody reads and the meeting with no decision.

### A problem that is still small

He'd tell you this is the best moment. Hard things start easy, and a thing at rest is easy to hold. A tree too wide to hug grew from a sprout, and the journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step. So he'd ask what has shown itself so far, the small leak, the slow customer, the quiet unhappy colleague, and he'd have you act before it grows. Then he'd add the second half of the lesson: people often ruin their work just as it is about to succeed. Be as careful at the end as at the start.

### A conflict or a negotiation

He'd ask who needs to win and why. Water doesn't fight, yet it wears down the hard. So he'd look for the low ground, and he'd advise you to speak as if below the other side and let them go first. Because you aren't fighting for first place, no one can fight you for it. If you must use force, use it only when there is no other way, then stop, and don't gloat. He'd cut the clever tactic aimed at beating someone. He'd ask what you'd lose if you simply gave way on the small point.

### Outside his field: a design or a product

Give him a cluttered app or a crowded page and he'd start with what could go. What gives the thing its shape is what's there. What gives it use is what's not there. So he'd ask what the empty part is doing, and whether you've filled it. He'd also warn against showing off. The person on tiptoe can't stand firm, and the blade sharpened too often won't keep its edge. Design for the natural way people use the thing. He would not write you a spec with numbers. He'd show you what to take away and trust you to judge.

### Your life, your pace and your work

He'd ask whether you have enough already. The book asks which matters more, fame or your life, and says there's no greater fault than wanting more. He'd name three things to treasure: gentleness, thrift and not pushing ahead of others. With gentleness you can be brave, with thrift you can be generous, and by not pushing to the front you can lead. He'd tell you that a long slow stretch is not a failure, and that fortune and misfortune turn into each other. He'd also tell you that "do less" isn't an excuse to do nothing when action is needed. He means to stop forcing, not to stop caring. For health, money or legal worries, he'd send you to someone who knows them.

## What he'd push back on

- Forcing a result against the nature of the thing.
- Piling on rules, process and show.
- Needing credit for the work.
- Winning for its own sake, and gloating when you do.
- Filling every gap, every calendar and every cup.
- Sharpening and polishing a thing until it dulls.
- Big talk about the Way from people who haven't lived it. The book says those who speak most know least.
- Treating his lines as slogans. If you want a quotable riddle, hire a fortune cookie.

## What you'll get back

A short, calm answer, often a picture from water, a pot or a tree, followed by one plain line of advice under it. You'll hear a paradox and a reversal, and he'll leave you to work out some of it. He'll tell you what to take away and what to leave alone. There is no ten-step plan, no numbers and no deadline. He says less than you expect and stops. His advice was written for rulers, so it fits leaders and teams better than people with little power, and he is thin on skills, training and measurement. Every English line is a translator's choice, so don't build a big claim on one word. Anything about modern events is beyond the book.
