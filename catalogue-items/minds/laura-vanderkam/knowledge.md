# Laura Vanderkam: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that Laura Vanderkam advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
Laura Vanderkam approaches the feeling of having no time by asking where a whole week actually goes. Her writing combines time diaries with practical planning and a deliberate account of what someone wants from life. The unit of a week exposes possibilities that a particularly difficult day can hide. Her work also invites readers to plan satisfying personal experiences, rather than assume that leisure will arrange itself after all obligations are complete. The aim is a more deliberate use of available time, grounded in an observed schedule. [S1][S2][S4]

## Core ideas
### Observe a full 168-hour week
A week has 168 hours, and Vanderkam uses that full denominator to examine time. A diary can reveal patterns that disappear when someone estimates from the most stressful part of a day. Her 2021 essay recommends looking at an ordinary week rather than requiring indefinite tracking from everyone. The purpose is awareness: understand where time goes before deciding which changes would help. [S1]

**An original application:** A person remembers working every evening, but the week’s log shows two heavy nights, one social evening and several short periods of casual browsing. Preserve the heavy nights as evidence rather than erasing them with an average. Then ask whether one predictable evening could hold a desired activity. The log gives a more precise problem than the statement that all evenings are lost.

**Where this goes wrong:** A log can become so detailed that keeping it crowds out living. Choose a workable level and use estimates openly where exact recall is unavailable.

### Name what the time is for
Vanderkam recommends considering a list of personal dreams and desired experiences alongside tracking. An observed schedule is useful because it can be compared with a life someone wants. Without that comparison, a diary can turn into an exercise in minimizing low-status activities or maximizing work. Her welcome includes seasonal lists and reading as examples of using time deliberately for enjoyment and personal interests. [S2]

**An original application:** A worker says they want more free time but has no picture of a satisfying evening. They name three possibilities: a walk with a friend, a cooking class and reading a novel. The next step is choosing one that fits the coming week. This turns “less busy” into an experience worth protecting, rather than merely an empty square on a calendar.

**Where this goes wrong:** A long wish list is an inventory of possibilities. Treating every dream as a current obligation can recreate the scarcity it was meant to relieve.

### Look across days for balance
The 168-hour perspective allows different days to carry different emphases. A day dominated by paid work need not prove that the entire week lacks relationships or personal time. Conversely, a pleasant weekend should not excuse an unsustainable pattern across the week. The wider view makes actual tradeoffs visible and helps the reader judge the pattern rather than demand that every day contain an identical mix. [S4]

**An original application:** A person has two unavoidable late shifts. Rather than schedule a family dinner that cannot happen on those nights, identify a breakfast, a free afternoon and the weekend period they can share. Check the total pattern with the people involved. If all the remaining days are already claimed, the arithmetic reveals a capacity problem rather than a creative scheduling opportunity.

**Where this goes wrong:** An attractive weekly average can conceal a punishing daily peak. Keep the stressful days visible and plan support for them rather than averaging them away.

### Plan meaningful priorities before leftover time
Vanderkam’s TED talk frames priorities as things to place into the available time rather than wait to discover after everything else is done. Her broader introductory guidance joins knowing what matters with knowing the present allocation. This gives the reader a practical planning stance: important experiences need a visible chance to happen. The approach does not require pretending that all incoming demands are optional. [S5]

**An original application:** Someone wants to call a distant friend but repeatedly leaves it to Sunday night. Check both people’s availability and choose a midweek slot before filling every evening with errands. If the call cannot be fixed, agree two possible windows. The priority now has a practical plan and an alternative, rather than depending on the week becoming unexpectedly quiet.

**Where this goes wrong:** Scheduling a priority does not create authority over every demand. Identify whose agreement or support is needed for the plan to remain credible.

### Examine the story of busyness
Time tracking lets a person compare a global impression with a more detailed record. Vanderkam’s work asks readers to notice where recollection and allocation differ. This can reveal more room, a different problem, or confirmation of genuine overload. The value comes from the comparison, not from deciding in advance that everyone who feels busy is exaggerating. A diary should be capable of changing the advice in either direction. [S1]

**An original application:** A person believes chores consume the whole weekend. The record shows a long shopping trip, a social obligation, three short chore sessions and several interrupted periods. The useful adjustment might be a planned shopping route or sharing a task, not insisting the entire weekend is free. Distinguish what took time from what felt burdensome. Both are relevant to a better plan.

**Where this goes wrong:** Using the log to prove someone wrong makes honest recording less likely. Treat the person’s experience as information alongside the diary.

### Make ordinary leisure intentional
Vanderkam’s starting guidance includes reading, seasonal enjoyment and specific experiences as practical uses of time. These are everyday activities, not only career ambitions or rare holidays. A small amount of planning can make a desired activity easier to choose than the default available one. The principle supports a fuller ordinary life while leaving room for unplanned rest and the legitimate pleasure of doing little. [S2]

**An original application:** A person wants to draw more but reaches for their phone in every short break. Put a sketchbook where the break happens and choose one approachable subject. A separate evening can remain open for rest. At the end of the week, judge whether drawing was enjoyable and accessible, rather than whether every spare minute became productive. The planned option should add a choice, not another obligation.

**Where this goes wrong:** Treating every gap as a slot to fill can remove the sense of abundance. Intention can include choosing to rest without an improvement target.

## How she decides
- **Observe a full 168-hour week:** Record an ordinary week at a useful level of detail and total the major categories. Note unusual events rather than treating them as normal. Review the pattern with curiosity before deciding which parts you would change.
- **Name what the time is for:** List specific experiences, relationships and kinds of work you want more of. Choose a few with a realistic place in the coming week. Use that desired experience to evaluate possible schedule changes instead of cutting activities by reputation alone.
- **Look across days for balance:** Map fixed commitments across the week, then look for suitable places for important activities. Allow different days to have different shapes. Check both the weekly allocation and the lived difficulty of the busiest days.
- **Plan meaningful priorities before leftover time:** Before the week fills, identify a small number of meaningful priorities and give them plausible windows. Coordinate with anyone involved. Keep a backup for a fragile plan and state which routine activities can move around it.
- **Examine the story of busyness:** Write the current account of the problem, then compare it with the log. Identify the specific mismatch or confirmation. Ask which allocation and which experience should change, since a shorter task can still feel stressful.
- **Make ordinary leisure intentional:** Choose one satisfying activity that fits an ordinary interval and prepare what it needs. Keep some unplanned space as well. After the week, ask whether the experience was worth repeating and what made it easier to begin.

## Where she stood
Vanderkam’s introductory guidance starts with aspirations as well as measurement. She recommends a list of things a person would like to do, have or experience, then uses tracking to examine the present allocation. The combination prevents the log from becoming an exercise in judgment with no desired destination. Her TED framing emphasizes building a life within the time available. These ideas support honest choices about an ordinary week, while the individual’s actual care duties, work control and resources remain important constraints. [S2][S5]

## Their work and record
168 Hours appeared in 2010 and argues for examining time across a whole week. I Know How She Does It, published in 2015, draws on 1,001 days of diaries from women with demanding careers and children. Off the Clock appeared in 2018, and Tranquility by Tuesday in 2022. Her official biography also identifies her weekday Before Breakfast podcast and Best of Both Worlds collaboration with Sarah Hart-Unger. Her 2021 time-tracking essay describes her own recording practice as of that date; this profile does not assume that an old personal report establishes her present routine. [S1][S3]

## Where she was wrong or criticised
Time diaries can replace faulty impressions with useful observations, but they remain records interpreted by people. A selected group of successful professionals cannot represent every income level, disability or care arrangement. Her book pages and biography describe the datasets and audiences behind some of her work; they do not establish that all readers have interchangeable free hours. Our criticism is directed at careless application: telling someone to find time before examining their constraints can make an otherwise useful perspective sound dismissive. [S3][S4]

## Blind spots to watch
Weekly arithmetic can reveal available time without proving that every fragment is usable for every task. Ten minutes between caregiving interruptions differs from ten uninterrupted minutes with the needed materials. Distinguish time, energy, predictability and control. Protect genuinely restorative activity without making the diary a surveillance system. If another person records time, obtain agreement about purpose and privacy. Use the log to understand the week, not to cross-examine someone about every small interval. [S1][S2]
