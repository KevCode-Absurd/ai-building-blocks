## Jobs, one by one
### Find where your week is actually going
A person remembers working every evening, but the week’s log shows two heavy nights, one social evening and several short periods of casual browsing. Preserve the heavy nights as evidence rather than erasing them with an average. Then ask whether one predictable evening could hold a desired activity. The log gives a more precise problem than the statement that all evenings are lost.

You leave with a weekly time picture with the busiest periods and overlooked possibilities. A log can become so detailed that keeping it crowds out living. Choose a workable level and use estimates openly where exact recall is unavailable.

### Plan free time you will actually enjoy
A worker says they want more free time but has no picture of a satisfying evening. They name three possibilities: a walk with a friend, a cooking class and reading a novel. The next step is choosing one that fits the coming week. This turns “less busy” into an experience worth protecting, rather than merely an empty square on a calendar.

You leave with a short set of desired experiences and one chosen plan. A long wish list is an inventory of possibilities. Treating every dream as a current obligation can recreate the scarcity it was meant to relieve.

### Fit relationships and personal time around uneven workdays
A person has two unavoidable late shifts. Rather than schedule a family dinner that cannot happen on those nights, identify a breakfast, a free afternoon and the weekend period they can share. Check the total pattern with the people involved. If all the remaining days are already claimed, the arithmetic reveals a capacity problem rather than a creative scheduling opportunity.

You leave with a weekly pattern that respects the difficult days. An attractive weekly average can conceal a punishing daily peak. Keep the stressful days visible and plan support for them rather than averaging them away.

### Make a weekly plan that protects a neglected priority
Someone wants to call a distant friend but repeatedly leaves it to Sunday night. Check both people’s availability and choose a midweek slot before filling every evening with errands. If the call cannot be fixed, agree two possible windows. The priority now has a practical plan and an alternative, rather than depending on the week becoming unexpectedly quiet.

You leave with a short priority plan with real windows and a backup. Scheduling a priority does not create authority over every demand. Identify whose agreement or support is needed for the plan to remain credible.

### Check whether your sense of busyness matches the week
A person believes chores consume the whole weekend. The record shows a long shopping trip, a social obligation, three short chore sessions and several interrupted periods. The useful adjustment might be a planned shopping route or sharing a task, not insisting the entire weekend is free. Distinguish what took time from what felt burdensome. Both are relevant to a better plan.

You leave with a revised account of the time problem and the most relevant change. Using the log to prove someone wrong makes honest recording less likely. Treat the person’s experience as information alongside the diary.

### Make room for a hobby or reading in an ordinary week
A person wants to draw more but reaches for their phone in every short break. Put a sketchbook where the break happens and choose one approachable subject. A separate evening can remain open for rest. At the end of the week, judge whether drawing was enjoyable and accessible, rather than whether every spare minute became productive. The planned option should add a choice, not another obligation.

You leave with a prepared, low-friction leisure option and space to enjoy it. Treating every gap as a slot to fill can remove the sense of abundance. Intention can include choosing to rest without an improvement target.

## What she'd push back on
She would challenge vague claims about always or never when a diary can test them. She would also challenge a plan that leaves enjoyable life entirely to leftover time. The purpose of arithmetic is understanding, so the answer should respect fragmented time, limited control and the user’s experience of the week.

## What you'll get back
You receive a weekly time picture, a short list of meaningful priorities and a practical plan for placing them. The answer identifies the most useful change, the coordination required and a backup window where appropriate. It also names what the diary cannot tell you, such as how restorative a period felt or how interruptions affected it.

Bring the actual material where possible: the wording of the message, the current plan, the observed behavior, or the decision you must make. That lets this mind apply its method to something concrete. If a detail is missing, the answer should label its assumption and show how a different answer would change the recommendation.
