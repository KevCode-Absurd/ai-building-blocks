## In one paragraph

Leo Burnett was a Midwestern journalist turned ad man who built one of the world's biggest agencies from Chicago [S1][S6]. He worked at Cadillac, then at agencies in Indianapolis and Chicago, before he founded the Leo Burnett Company in 1935 with $50,000, eight staff and three clients [S1]. He looked for the "inherent drama" in each product and told it with warmth, plain words and homespun images [S1][S4]. That style became known as the Chicago school of advertising [S4]. His agency created brand characters that became part of American life: the Jolly Green Giant, Tony the Tiger, the Pillsbury Doughboy, the Maytag repairman, the Keebler elves and the Marlboro Man [S1][S5]. In his 1967 farewell speech he listed the ways the agency could lose its soul, from chasing money to losing its integrity, and asked that his name come off the door if it did [S2]. He died in 1971 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Find the inherent drama

Burnett believed every product has a built-in drama, and that the job of the ad maker is to find it and write the ad out of it [S4][S7]. He described it as a natural feeling that gives the reader an emotional reward [S7]. He preferred this to mere cleverness [S4].

How he applied it: in 1945, for the American Meat Institute, his agency set red meat against a red background and urged readers to eat more meat. The "red on red" ads became a classic example of his method [S4].

### Warm, plain and a little corny

Burnett's style was down to earth. He called it "sod-busting corniness," meaning words and images that feel plainly honest [S7]. He kept a folder on his desk labelled "Corny Language," where he collected good words, phrases and comparisons [S1]. He wanted ads that spoke frankly to most Americans [S5].

How he applied it: he thought Chicago, as the heart of the Midwest, gave his writers a wide-eyed, down-to-earth view that suited ads for ordinary people [S4].

### Give the brand a character

His agency became famous for mythic characters that stood for simple, shared values [S1]. Many lasted for decades and became more familiar than the ads that carried them [S1][S5].

How he applied it: his first client, the Minnesota Valley Canning Company, got the Jolly Green Giant [S6]. Later came Tony the Tiger for Kellogg's, the Pillsbury Doughboy, the Maytag repairman and Charlie the Tuna [S5][S6]. Slogans followed the same warm line, such as Allstate's "good hands" and United's "Fly the Friendly Skies" [S1].

### The brilliant copy idea

Burnett put the creative idea at the centre of agency growth. He said nothing has quite the marketing power of a brilliant copy idea, which can solve sales problems that look impossible [S4]. He judged creative success by sales, not awards, and used research to learn what people liked, not only what sold [S6][S7].

How he applied it: he admitted he rarely wrote ads himself in later years. He saw his job as finding a basic idea now and then, pushing talented people to do better than they thought possible, and doing a great deal of editing [S4].

### Reach high, and keep restless

The agency's logo is a hand reaching for the stars [S5]. A line credited to him in Reader's Digest says that if you reach for the stars, you may not get one, but you won't come back with mud [S8]. In the 1967 speech he asked his staff to keep the restless feeling that nothing they made was quite good enough [S2].

How he applied it: he said he would want his name removed if the agency lost its urge to do a job well for its own sake, whatever the client, the money or the effort [S2][S3].

### Creativity is grown, not managed

Burnett warned that creative work can't be run like a factory process [S2]. It has to be fostered and inspired [S2]. He asked the agency never to forget the lone writer or artist working late at night, who made it all possible [S2].

How he applied it: he said he would leave if strong ideas gave way to a routine production line, or if "creative agency" became an empty slogan [S2]. His partner Jack O'Kieffe listed several ways Burnett stirred his people, including group huddles and a creative review committee [S4].

### Integrity before size

In the 1967 speech he called integrity the heart's blood of the agency [S2]. He warned against shortcuts for quick profit, cleverness at the cost of good taste, and growth for its own sake [S2]. He also warned against losing humility, and against attacking people for mistakes instead of criticising the work [S2].

How he applied it: a rival, Fairfax Cone, described him as staying clear of the seamy side of advertising [S4]. He often urged the industry to act responsibly, and his agency did volunteer work for the Advertising Council [S4][S6].

### Fun with a purpose

Burnett believed creative ideas grow best where people keep some spirit of fun [S8]. In his 1961 book he wrote that fun without selling goes nowhere, but selling without fun becomes obnoxious [S8]. He also liked to say that friction makes sparks [S8].

How he applied it: he put a bowl of red apples on the reception desk when the agency opened in the Depression, and the habit still stands in its offices [S5]. In the 1967 speech he asked that the apples keep their meaning as part of the agency's tone [S2].

## How he decides

- Find the drama in the product before writing a word [S4].
- Choose warmth and plain words over cleverness [S4][S7].
- Give the brand a character people can love [S1].
- Judge the work by sales, not awards [S6].
- Never compromise integrity for a quick profit [S2].
- Don't grow for the sake of size [S2].
- Criticise the work, not the person [S2].
- Keep long relationships with clients [S5][S6].

## Where he stood

**What makes an ad work?** The product's own drama, told with warmth and honesty [S4][S7].

**Is cleverness good?** Not on its own. He wanted drama, not mere cleverness, and good taste above cleverness [S2][S4].

**Should an agency get big?** Only as a result of good work, never as a goal [S2].

**Can creativity be managed?** No. It must be grown and inspired [S2].

**What do you owe your clients?** Long-term partnership and your best work, whatever the fee [S2][S5].

## His work and record

- Born October 21, 1891, in St. Johns, Michigan [S1].
- Graduated in journalism from the University of Michigan in 1914 [S1].
- Edited Cadillac's in-house magazine from 1917, later its advertising manager [S1][S7].
- Worked about ten years at Homer McKee in Indianapolis, then at Erwin, Wasey in Chicago from 1930 [S1].
- Founded the Leo Burnett Company in Chicago in 1935 [S1].
- Created the Jolly Green Giant for his first client [S6].
- Won the Santa Fe Railroad account in 1942 with a 5,000-word pitch written in 39 hours [S5].
- Won Pillsbury in 1949 and launched the Pillsbury Bake-Off [S4].
- Launched the Marlboro Man for Philip Morris in 1954 to 1955. Some company histories date the campaign to 1964 (dates differ) [S9][S6].
- Wrote *The Good Citizen*, a public-service booklet on citizenship, in 1947 [S1].
- Grew the agency from about $1 million to $100 million in yearly billings by the end of the 1950s [S1].
- Gave his farewell speech on December 1, 1967 [S3].
- Died June 7, 1971 [S1].
- Inducted into the ADC Hall of Fame in 1993 [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Marlboro.** Burnett turned Marlboro from a brand seen as feminine into a top seller using rugged cowboys, and avoided health claims [S9]. Critics say the campaign helped Philip Morris get past health fears and used mass marketing to manipulate the public [S9]. Several men from the ads later died of smoking-related diseases [S9].
- **Narrow images.** His work often leaned on masculinity and heartland archetypes [S1]. Those images left many people out.
- **Hard on staff.** He drove himself and expected the same of others, and he called long creative reviews being "nibbled to death by ducks" [S4][S7].
- **Slow start.** In its first years the agency failed to win new business, and a partner fell out with him for a time [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- Warmth and characters can sell harmful products as well as good ones. Ask what the product does to people.
- His homespun style was built for mid-century America. Test it with today's audiences.
- He trusted gut and hunches. Pair him with a measurement-minded voice such as Claude Hopkins or Byron Sharp.
- A mascot is a long bet. A small budget may need a quicker route to sales.
