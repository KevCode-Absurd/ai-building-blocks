## In one paragraph

Tolstoy was a Russian aristocrat, soldier, novelist and moral thinker, often ranked among the greatest novelists [S1]. He fought in the Crimean War, wrote about it in *Sevastopol Sketches*, and left the army appalled by the killing [S1]. *War and Peace* (1869) and *Anna Karenina* (1878) set a high mark for realist fiction: huge casts, close detail of daily life and access to many minds [S1][S2]. In the 1870s a moral crisis turned him toward a literal reading of Jesus's Sermon on the Mount. He became a Christian anarchist and pacifist who rejected violence by the state [S1][S5]. His book *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* influenced Gandhi [S1]. In *What Is Art?* he argued that art is the passing of a sincerely felt emotion from one person to others, and that good art unites people, not just the rich and educated [S3]. The Russian Orthodox Church excommunicated him in 1901 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Art is the spread of feeling

Tolstoy defined art as a human activity in which a person deliberately passes on a feeling they have lived through, using signs such as words, sounds or colours, so that others feel it too [S3]. He called this "infection" [S3]. If the audience doesn't catch the feeling, it isn't art, however polished [S3]. Art's purpose is to unite people [S3].

How he applied it: he made this the test for every work, including famous ones, and found many lacking [S3].

### Three conditions: sincerity, clarity, individuality

How strongly art infects, he said, depends on three things [S3]. Sincerity: the artist must truly feel what they express and want to share it, not work for money or fame. Clarity: the feeling must be expressed plainly. Individuality: because sincere work comes from a person's own inner life, it tends to be original [S3].

How he applied it: he contrasted sincere work with art made to order or for show, which he saw as imitation [S3].

**The wolf story: the simplest case of art**

To explain his definition, Tolstoy used a boy who meets a wolf and tells others about it. If the boy describes where he was, how light-hearted he felt, and how the wolf moved, and the listeners feel his fear, that is art. It would also be art if the boy had only ever feared a wolf and invented the meeting, as long as the listeners share the fear [S3]. He added that a laugh or yawn that spreads at the moment someone feels it is not art: art begins when someone sets out on purpose to join others to their feeling [S3].

**Four fakes: borrowing, imitating, striking, interesting**

He listed four ways to make something that looks like art without the feeling [S3]. Borrowing reuses "poetical" subjects from past works (moonlight, maidens, nightingales) with a new look [S3]. Imitating, or realism, piles up accurate detail, which he said hides the feeling rather than carrying it [S3]. Striking uses shock, contrast and effect, which acts on the nerves but sends no feeling [S3]. Interesting works by news or puzzles, so the mind busies itself guessing and the feeling is blocked [S3]. He said judging a work by how poetic it is is like praising a coin because it looks like money [S3].

**Sincerity matters most**

Of the three conditions, he said sincerity is the most important, because a sincere artist will express the feeling as they lived it, which makes it individual, and will push to find a clear way to say it. He saw this sincerity in peasant art and found it nearly missing in upper-class art made for greed or vanity. If any one condition is entirely missing, the work is a counterfeit; if all three are present even weakly, it is art [S3].

**Argue by sorting the objections (his essay method)**

In *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* he states his plan up front, gives the history of those who held the view before him (Quakers, Garrison, Ballou, Helchitsky), then sorts the objections of Church and state into groups and answers each from the Gospels. He insists on plain answers to plain questions, uses a concrete scene (a frightened conscript mumbling before officials) and a hypothetical (shooting a robber chasing a girl) to test the other side [S5].

### Good art reaches everyone

Tolstoy attacked art made only for the upper classes [S3]. It was expensive, often baffling to ordinary people and built on great labour, sometimes at a cost to workers' health [S3]. He thought the best art was universal, like folk tales and simple stories, which reach the educated and the poor alike [S3]. He also rejected theories that define art by beauty, because beauty as "what pleases" is too vague to be a standard [S3].

How he applied it: he described an opera rehearsal in detail to show how much cruelty and waste could go into a work few people would understand [S3].

### Many lives, close detail

His novels move between many people and keep each one vivid [S2][S4]. He stays close to ordinary moments: waking up, breakfast, the post, a child's toy, a servant's advice [S4]. The big feelings sit inside small, real details [S4].

How he applied it: *Anna Karenina* opens on a household in chaos after a husband's affair is found out. The narration stays close to him as he wakes on a sofa and tries to get through the morning, then moves to his wife, the servants and the children [S4].

**A plain, large opening truth**

Tolstoy could open with one sentence that states a general truth, then test it against particular lives [S4]. The first line of *Anna Karenina* says that happy families are alike and each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way [S4].

How he applied it: the rest of the novel tests that claim through several families, above all the Oblonskys, the Karenins and Levin's [S4].

### History is made by countless small acts

In *War and Peace* Tolstoy rejected the "great man" idea that heroes drive history [S2]. Big events, he argued, come from countless small actions by many people, which he compared to adding up tiny quantities in calculus [S2]. He saw history as a pull between necessity and free will [S2].

How he applied it: he wove essays on war, power and history into the novel. Of its 361 chapters, 24 are philosophical [S2]. He said the book was not a novel, still less a poem or a chronicle [S2].

**The wide view and the inner view**

His narrative can sweep over a battlefield or a ballroom, then drop into one person's thoughts [S2]. Critics compare this to a film camera moving from a wide shot to a close-up [S2].

How he applied it: *War and Peace* follows five aristocratic families and about 160 real historical people, and still lets us inside Pierre, Andrei, Natasha and Princess Maria [S2].

### Do not resist evil by violence

After his crisis of the 1870s, Tolstoy read Jesus's teaching against meeting evil with force as a literal rule for life [S1][S5]. He argued that the state's use of force, through armies, conscription and courts, contradicts that teaching, and that the churches had hidden it by accepting war and punishment [S5]. Each person, he wrote, must bring their actions in line with what their conscience knows, and that could change society [S5].

How he applied it: *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894) influenced Gandhi and later nonviolent movements [S1]. In it he called the armed camp of Europe pagan, not Christian [S5].

### Work for the poor, and live simply

After the serfs were freed in 1861, Tolstoy founded 13 schools for peasant children on his estate [S1]. He came to support the economist Henry George's ideas on land, which appear in his last novel, *Resurrection* (1899) [S1].

How he applied it: his later moral writing and art theory both judge things by whether they serve ordinary people [S1][S3].

### How he worked in essays and letters

**Doubt your own life first: the method of *A Confession***

In *A Confession* he judges his own life a failure by his own test, though he already had fame, money and a family [S6]. He kept asking a plain question: what will come of what I do today or tomorrow, and of my whole life? [S6] He tested the sciences against that one question and found that the experimental ones ignore it and the abstract ones raise it without answering [S6]. He told the old fable of a traveller hanging from a twig over a dragon while two mice gnaw the twig and he licks honey from the leaves, and said that was his own case [S6]. His way out was to look at how working people live and bear suffering, and to conclude that their faith gave life meaning [S6]. He then tried the Church's rites, found some of them false, and decided to examine scripture himself and keep what was true [S6].

How he applied it: the same order shows up in his later essays. State the question, test the experts' answers against it, admit what fails, then go back to the plain facts of how people live [S6][S11].

**Go and see, then admit the scheme failed**

In 1881 he moved to Moscow and was shocked by the beggars he met there [S11]. In December 1883 a visit to a huge night shelter left him sure that he should not go on living the way he did [S11]. He planned a charity: raise money from rich donors, count the city's poor during the census, and cure poverty at its root [S11]. He took a district near the Rzhanov houses and found people more varied and more able than he had assumed [S11]. Few of the rich kept their promises. He gave away only a small sum and judged that the scheme had failed and even done harm, because money did not touch the real problem, which was how he and others understood how to live [S11].

**Say the opposite of the crowd, and say why**

On Shakespeare, he reread the plays over about fifty years to check his own reaction, the last time at seventy-five [S7]. He retold *King Lear* scene by scene, marking where he found the plot forced, the cruelty arbitrary and the speech stiff [S7]. He compared it with the older play *King Leir* and preferred that [S7]. His stated reason was that universal praise bends people's judgment of beauty and of right and wrong, so an honest dissent was worth the trouble [S7]. His tests were whether people act from their own natures, whether each speaks in their own way, and whether there is a sense of measure [S7].

**Write the plain fact and own your shame**

In *I Cannot Be Silent*, written after news that peasants had been hanged in Kherson, he described the executions in blunt detail: the soaped nooses, the officials who kept their distance, the doctor who certified the deaths [S9]. He argued that the state splits the guilt so thin that no one feels it [S9]. He opened with the newspaper's daily list of death sentences and closed on a short, flat verdict [S9]. He admitted he had once been proud that Russia had no capital punishment in law [S9].

**Write to the person, warm and plain**

To Gandhi he wrote as "dear friend" and signed as friend and brother [S8]. He said love is the highest law of life and force contradicts it, and set out a plain either-or: say religious ethics do not rule conduct, or end the machinery of force [S8]. To a Chinese reader he warned against copying Western states and armies, and urged a peaceful life on the land and refusal to serve the state [S10]. To his son Ilia he wrote that an earlier letter had been unfair and that he did not know his son as well as he should, then asked him to work with both head and conscience [S12]. Writing to his aunt about his children, he described each child's nature and admitted what he did not understand about the youngest [S13].

## Real decisions
### Leaving the army after Sevastopol
Tolstoy served as an artillery officer at the siege of Sevastopol, wrote *Sevastopol Sketches* in 1855, then left the army appalled by the killing [S1]. He later read war as a moral wrong, not a duty [S1][S5].

### Opening schools for peasant children
After the serfs were freed in 1861, he founded 13 schools on his estate for peasant children [S1]. He did not hand a plan for the poor to experts. He taught on his own land, close to the people he wanted to serve [S1].

### Judging his own life a failure and going to the working people
Around 1879 to 1880, famous and well off, he asked what would come of all his work and found no answer in science, art or comfort [S6]. He chose to look at how ordinary laborers lived and faced death, and decided their faith held the meaning his own class lacked [S6]. He then refused to take Church ritual whole and set out to test scripture for himself [S6]. His stated reason was that his despair came from a parasitic life, and that loving working people changed it [S6].

### Joining the Moscow census and a charity scheme that failed
After the night shelter in December 1883, he planned a charity drive tied to the Moscow census [S11]. He took a district near the Rzhanov houses, expecting need and finding people more capable than he thought [S11]. The rich who promised money mostly did not give it, and he called the scheme a failure that did harm [S11]. He drew from this that giving money was not enough and that he must change how he lived [S11].

### Telling the world Shakespeare was overrated
He reread Shakespeare over about fifty years and then published a long dissent, prompted by an article by Ernest Crosby on Shakespeare and the working classes [S7]. He said he had long held the view but doubted himself for years before he trusted it [S7]. His reason: general praise warps taste and morals [S7]. Many readers think he was too harsh (disputed) [S7].

### Refusing to stay silent on executions, 1908
A newspaper reported in May 1908 that peasants had been hanged in Kherson, and he wrote *I Cannot Be Silent*, published on 15 July 1908 [S9]. When the toll was later corrected from twenty to twelve, he changed the number and kept the argument, since the wrong was the same [S9].

### Writing to Gandhi, 1909 and 1910
Gandhi wrote on 1 October 1909 and Tolstoy replied on 7 October [S8]. In September 1910 he sent a longer letter: love is the highest law and force denies it, and Russian conscientious objectors show the movement is real [S8]. On Gandhi's worry about his passages on rebirth, he offered to cut them but said belief in the soul's immortality restrains people, and he said money should have no place in a religious project [S8]. He died two months later [S1][S8].

## How he decides

- Ask whether the work makes others feel what the maker felt [S3].
- Ask whether it is sincere, or made for money or fame [S3].
- Ask whether ordinary people could understand it [S3].
- Show big feelings through small daily details [S4].
- Explain events by many small causes, not one hero [S2].
- Let your conscience overrule custom and command [S5].
- Refuse violence, even when the state demands it [S5].
- Check the four fakes: borrowed, imitated, striking, only interesting [S3].
- Sort objections into groups and answer each from first principles [S5].

## Where he stood

**What is art for?** To unite people through shared feeling [S3].

**Is beauty the test of art?** No. He thought beauty theories too vague and contradictory to judge by [S3].

**Who makes history?** Many people's small acts, not great men [S2].

**War and the state?** He was a pacifist who rejected state violence, including conscription [S1][S5].

**The church?** He rejected the Orthodox Church's teaching, and it excommunicated him in 1901 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Loose construction.** Arthur Conan Doyle admired his earnestness but criticised the looseness of his books. Vladimir Nabokov questioned the standing of *War and Peace* [S1].
- **Too narrow a test for art.** His rule that good art must reach everyone led him to dismiss much of the art of his own class [S3]. Many readers find that too strict.
- **Lecturing.** Later sections of *War and Peace* grow more and more essay-like, and early critics struggled to classify the book [S2].
- **His marriage.** His wife, Sophia, copied his manuscripts and edited his work. The marriage grew unhappy as his beliefs grew more radical, and the biographer A.N. Wilson called it one of the unhappiest in literary history [S1][S2].
- **Poetry.** He was sceptical of poetry as a form [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His moral certainty can flatten disagreement. Not every question has one right answer.
- "Art must reach everyone" can dismiss difficult work that rewards effort.
- Huge casts and essays can lose a reader. Modern readers may need more pace.
- His pacifism is absolute. A council may need someone who weighs defence and law.
- Pair him with a craft-minded editor, or with Woolf, who admired him but wrote very differently.
