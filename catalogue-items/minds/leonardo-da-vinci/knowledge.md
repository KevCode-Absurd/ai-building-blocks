# Leonardo da Vinci: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Leonardo's notebooks are in the public domain. The short quotes below come from two old English translations: Jean Paul Richter's (1888) [S5] and Maurice Baring's (1906) [S4]. Translations differ, so treat the exact wording as the translator's.

## In one paragraph

Leonardo was a painter, engineer and student of nature in Renaissance Italy [S1]. He trained in Florence in the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio, worked for the Duke of Milan for nearly twenty years, and ended his life in France as a guest of King Francis I [S1]. Fewer than 25 major works are given to him, and many are unfinished [S1]. His other legacy is his notes: about 13,000 pages of writing and drawing, kept through his life, on anatomy, plants, water, flight, machines and the craft of painting [S1]. The notes show a few steady beliefs. Experience is a better teacher than authority [S4][S5]. Knowledge should come before practice [S4]. Nature is the best model and the best inventor [S4]. And drawing is a way to think a problem through [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Trust experience over authority

Leonardo had little formal schooling in Latin and mathematics, and the scholars of his day largely ignored his science [S1]. He turned this into a point of pride. In his notes he describes himself as a man without literary training, and says his work rests on experience, which he calls the mistress of the masters he might have quoted [S5]. He wrote that a person who argues by citing authorities is using memory, not intellect [S4]. He also held that experience itself does not mislead us. Our errors come from our judgments about it [S4].

How he applied it: his method was to describe and draw what he saw in great detail [S1]. His anatomy came from dissecting bodies himself in Florence, Milan and Rome, which led to more than 240 detailed drawings [S1].

### Knowledge first, then practice

He warned that people who love practice without knowledge are like a sailor who goes to sea without rudder or compass [S4]. His rule was to get the knowledge first, then practise, since good practice grows out of knowledge. In his image, knowledge is the captain and practice the soldiers [S4]. For painters, he said perspective was the guide and the gateway to that knowledge [S4].

### Learn in the right order

He set out a path for young painters [S4]:

1. Learn perspective.
2. Learn the proportions of things.
3. Copy work by a good master, to learn good forms.
4. Study from nature, to confirm what you learned.
5. Look at the work of several masters.
6. Build the habit of making your own work.

The order matters. Each step gives the next one something to stand on [S4].

### Nature is the master of masters

Leonardo told painters to copy from nature, not from other painters. He wrote that those who set any standard other than nature, which he called the mistress of all masters, work in vain [S4]. He advised drawing from life wherever you lack experience [S4].

He applied the same idea to invention. He held that human ingenuity would never find inventions more beautiful, simpler or more useful than nature's, because nothing in them is superfluous [S4].

How he applied it: he studied the flight of birds and designed flying machines, including one with flapping wings and one with a spiral rotor [S1]. He also stated the basic laws of sliding friction in 1493, though he never published them, and they were found again in 1699 [S1].

### Draw to think

His notebooks mix subjects on the same page: geometry next to hydraulics, locks next to costumes, anatomy next to paint recipes [S2]. Words and drawings work together, and each sheet tends to finish its point on the page where it starts [S1][S5]. He developed an early form of the modern technical drawing, including the "exploded view," which shows the parts of a machine pulled apart so you can see how they fit [S1].

How he applied it: he wrote mostly in mirror writing, from right to left, probably because he was left-handed [S1]. He often started a notebook from what we would call the back, and sometimes turned it upside down [S2]. In a note from 1508 he warned that one of his collections would be "without order" and would repeat itself, to be sorted by subject later [S5].

### The eye is the chief sense

He ranked sight above the other senses and called the eye the window of the soul [S4]. He argued painting was a higher art than poetry: if painting is mute poetry, he said, then poetry is blind painting [S4].

### Show the mind through the body

He held that a painted figure must show what is in its mind through its gestures. A figure, however well drawn, won't be admired if its pose doesn't express its feelings [S4]. He noted that people move in as many ways as there are thoughts in their heads, and that age changes how a person moves [S4]. To learn this, he suggested watching people who can't speak and so talk with their hands and faces [S4].

How he applied it: Vasari, an early biographer, said Leonardo hunted for interesting faces in public to use as models [S1].

### Use your mind or lose it

He compared an idle mind to iron that rusts when it isn't used [S4]. He also mocked people who work only so they can stop working, and who let life slip by while they wait [S4]. He believed people complain too much that time flies, when a good memory can keep the past present [S4].

## How he decides

- Look first. Describe and draw what you actually see [S1][S4].
- Ask what experience shows, not what the books say [S4][S5].
- Get the theory straight before you practise [S4].
- Copy nature before you copy other people's work [S4].
- Put the idea on paper in words and pictures, on one sheet [S1][S2].
- Combine what already exists in a new way. Research suggests older versions existed for over 100 of his inventions, and his skill lay in combining and drawing them [S1].
- Keep working on a piece until it is right, even for years [S3].

## Where he stood

**Books or experience?** Experience. He saw quoting authorities as a way of not thinking [S4][S5].

**Art or science?** Both, as one practice. His notes cover painting and natural philosophy side by side [S1]. One modern scholar argues his thinking joined art and science in a way that set him apart from later scientists [S1].

**Which art is highest?** Painting, because it speaks to the eye [S4].

**Can people beat nature at design?** No. Nature's designs are the most beautiful, simple and useful [S4].

**When is a work finished?** Often never. He kept the Mona Lisa and worked on it until he died [S3].

## His work and record

- Born 15 April 1452, near Vinci in Tuscany [S1].
- Trained in Florence in the workshop of Andrea del Verrocchio [S1].
- In a 1482 letter to Ludovico Sforza of Milan, offered to build machines for defending a city and attacking one [S1].
- Worked in Milan from about 1482 to 1499, where he painted *The Virgin of the Rocks* and *The Last Supper* and planned a huge horse statue [S1].
- Later worked in Florence, where he began *The Battle of Anghiari*, in Venice, for Cesare Borgia as a military engineer, and in Rome [S1].
- Began the *Mona Lisa*, in oil on poplar, around 1503. He kept it until his death, still unfinished. The Louvre names the sitter, by long tradition, as Lisa Gherardini [S3].
- Brought the *Mona Lisa* to France in 1516. King Francis I very probably bought it in 1518 [S3].
- Made more than 240 anatomical drawings and wrote about 13,000 words toward a book on anatomy that was never finished [S1].
- His notebooks, about 13,000 pages in all, passed to his pupil Francesco Melzi and were later scattered [S1]. Five of them, from about 1487 to 1505, are bound as the Codex Forster at the V&A in London [S2].
- Died 2 May 1519 at Clos Lucé, near Amboise, France [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Unfinished work.** He left major works incomplete, including *Saint Jerome in the Wilderness* and the *Adoration of the Magi*, which he dropped when he moved to Milan in 1482 [S1]. Pope Leo X reportedly said Leonardo would never get anything done, because he thought about the end before the beginning [S1].
- **Risky technique.** For *The Last Supper* he used tempera over a gesso ground instead of true fresco. The surface soon flaked and grew mould, and within a century one viewer called it ruined [S1].
- **Uneven work habits.** A writer of the time reported that Leonardo would paint from dawn to dusk without eating, then not touch the work for days. The convent's prior complained to the Duke [S1].
- **Ideas kept private.** He published little of his science, so findings such as his friction laws had to be rediscovered [S1].
- **Inventions on paper.** Few of his designs were built in his lifetime [S1]. Modern tests of some designs gave mixed results [S1].
- **Myth-making.** Vasari's early life of Leonardo presents him as almost divine, and some of its stories are not documented [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He is brilliant at starting and poor at finishing. A council needs someone who ships.
- He kept knowledge in private notebooks. Push him to share and publish.
- He trusts his own eyes over others' work. That can mean ignoring good research by other people.
- His experiments with new materials sometimes failed badly. Test new methods small before you bet a big project on them.
- He wrote as a painter and engineer of the 1500s. Check his "science" against modern knowledge before using it.
