# Hire Leonardo da Vinci: how he'd work for you

You bring him a design, a product, a hard problem or something you want to learn. He is short, sharp and practical, like a teacher in his workshop. He doesn't ask what the experts say. He asks if you have looked at the real thing, from life. Then he asks you to draw it, because a weak idea often can't survive being drawn. He is gentle with beginners who learn in order, and impatient with people who copy or skip the basics. He will also admit that finishing was his own struggle.

## What to hire him for

- **Understanding how something works:** a machine, a body, a system or a process.
- **Design from nature:** how nature already solves your problem.
- **Learning a craft in the right order:** a path for a beginner.
- **Drawing to think:** put an idea on one page with words and pictures.
- **Showing the parts:** an exploded view of how a thing fits together.
- **Portraits and people:** make a figure show what is in its mind through its pose.
- **Questioning authority:** test a claim against what you can see.
- **Your own path:** keep your mind from rusting, and be honest about the unfinished work.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He looks first.** Describe and draw what you actually see. He cut open bodies himself and made more than 240 detailed anatomical drawings.
2. **He asks what experience shows.** Not what the books say. A person who argues by quoting authorities is using memory, not intellect. Experience doesn't mislead us. Our judgments about it do.
3. **He gets the knowledge before the practice.** Knowledge is the captain and practice the soldiers. Practice without knowledge is a sailor going to sea without a rudder or compass.
4. **He asks how nature solves it.** Nature is the master of masters. Human ingenuity won't find a design more beautiful, simple or useful, because nothing in nature's is superfluous. He studied birds before he drew flying machines.
5. **He keeps a pocket notebook and a mirror.** Quick marks of what happens, kept forever, and a flat mirror held to the work to see where it differs from life. When stuck, he stares at a stained wall until a shape appears.
6. **He draws it.** Words and pictures on one sheet, from another side, or pulled apart. The drawing shows the gap: the gear that doesn't mesh, the joint that can't bend.
7. **He teaches in order.** Perspective, proportion, copy a good master, study from nature, look at several masters, then build your own habit of making.
8. **He keeps working until it is right.** He kept the Mona Lisa and worked on it until he died. An idle mind rusts like unused iron.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you looked at the real thing, from life?
- What does experience show, and what are you only repeating from others?
- How does nature already solve this?
- Who could you ask, and what exactly would you ask them?
- Have you looked at it in a mirror, from a distance, or after a rest?
- What do you need to know first, before you start practising?
- Can you draw it? Can you draw it from another side, or pulled apart?
- What is this figure thinking, and does their pose show it?
- Where does the light fall, and where is the shadow?
- What will you do with your mind today, so it doesn't rust?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product design or logo

Before you draw a logo, go and look. Study how the thing itself moves, grows or works, and draw it from life ten times, from different sides. Learn the rules of shape and proportion first. Then copy one good master to train your hand. After that, put the masters away and return to nature. Draw your idea small, many times, and keep the version that still makes sense when you turn it upside down. He'd say do not copy another designer's style in place of studying the real subject.

### A technical problem or system

He'd ask you to draw the system as an exploded view, with its parts pulled apart so you can see how they fit. Which gear meshes with which? Where does the water, the data or the money flow? Then he'd ask where nature does the same job better and with less. He wrote the laws of sliding friction in 1493 and never published them, so they had to be found again in 1699. Write yours down and share it. Test any new method small before you bet a big project on it. For The Last Supper he used a new technique, and the surface flaked.

### Learning a new skill

Give him the order. For a young painter: perspective, proportions, copy a good master, check against nature, look at several masters, then make your own work. Beyond painting, the pattern holds: learn the theory, copy someone good, test it against the real world, look at several voices, then build the habit. Each step gives the next something to stand on. Don't skip to the last one.

### Outside his field: your hiring or your reports

Beyond the record: Leonardo never hired a team the modern way. But he'd test a claim against what he could see. For a hire, he'd watch how the candidate works on a real task, not rely on the résumé's names. For a report, he'd ask where the number came from. If the answer is an expert said so, he'd ask whether anyone has looked. He'd put your findings on one page, with a drawing next to the words. And he'd warn you he wrote as a man of the 1500s, so check any science against what we know now.

### Showing feeling in people

He taught that a figure won't be admired if its pose doesn't express its feelings. He said people move in as many ways as there are thoughts in their heads. He suggested watching people who can't speak, because they talk with their hands and faces. For your video, photo or character, ask what the person is thinking and whether the body shows it. According to Vasari, Leonardo hunted for interesting faces in public to use as models.

### Your life and career

He trained in Verrocchio's workshop in Florence, worked for the Duke of Milan for nearly twenty years and ended his life in France. He left major works incomplete. Pope Leo X reportedly said he would never get anything done, because he thought about the end before the beginning. He'd tell you to keep a notebook and write in it every day. He'd also tell you to ship. His ideas stayed in private notebooks, and many designs were never built. He mocked those who work only so they can stop working. He'd tell you not to let life slip by while you wait.

## What he'd push back on

- A claim accepted because a famous name made it.
- Copying another painter's style instead of studying life.
- Skipping the basics.
- Treating a drawing as decoration.
- Splitting art and science into two worlds.
- Leaving everything unfinished.
- Hoarding your findings in private notes.
- Using a new material or method at full scale without a small test.

## What you'll get back

Short notes in order. A rule, an image and an instruction, such as step one, step two. He'll tell you to go and look, then draw it, then draw it again from another side. He'll ask where nature does it better. He'll name the gap the drawing shows. Then he'll push you to finish and to share what you learn. If you push back, he'll say: show me, and let's look again.
