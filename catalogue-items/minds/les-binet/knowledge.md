# Les Binet: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Les Binet is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from a 2022 interview he gave, an article he wrote in 2025, reports of his talks in 2016 and 2020, and biographies [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]. His books with Peter Field are summarised here only through those reports, since we didn't open the books themselves. His birth year isn't in our sources. Treat each view as "as of" the date of its source.

## In one paragraph

Binet is a UK-based researcher who spent nearly 40 years measuring what advertising does. He read physics at Oxford and took a master's in artificial intelligence at Edinburgh in 1985 [S5]. He joined the London agency BMP as an econometrician in 1987, led its econometrics work for 16 years, founded the DDB Matrix unit, and later became group head of effectiveness at adam&eveDDB, leaving in February 2025 [S5]. He has won more IPA Advertising Effectiveness Awards than anyone else [S6]. With Peter Field he wrote six books on effectiveness, including *Marketing in the Era of Accountability* (2007) and *The Long and the Short of It* [S5][S6]. His core findings are that brands need both long-term brand building and short-term sales activation, roughly 60:40 but varying by case; that emotion builds brands while rational messages trigger sales; that growth needs share of voice above share of market; and that measurement must look past clicks [S1][S2][S4].

## Core ideas

### The long and the short

Binet separates two jobs. Sales activation drives short-term sales. Brand building produces long-term growth and makes buyers less sensitive to price [S4]. In a 2016 talk he urged marketers to combine both [S4]. In 2022 he linked the split to how people decide. Most thinking is long term and much of it is unconscious, and only a smaller share is the active, short-term thinking that activation can sway [S1].

How he applied it: he cited the retailer John Lewis, which built emotional affinity with its Christmas ads and also ran ads for launches and sales [S4].

### Roughly 60:40, but not a law

He suggested splitting spend about 60% on brand building and 40% on activation for best efficiency [S4]. In 2022 he said the rule still holds but isn't fixed. It varies with brand size, price and category, and might be 65:35 or 50:50 [S1].

How he applied it: he treats the ratio as a starting point, then adjusts it for the brand's size, price and category [S1].

### Emotion builds brands, reason triggers sales

According to the 2016 report of his talk, a 2013 study he did with Field found emotional priming had the biggest effect on brands but the smallest direct effect on sales [S4]. People remember how brands make them feel, so build the brand on emotion [S4]. Rational messages work best for driving a purchase, so each message should prompt a response [S4].

How he applied it: in 2022 he suggested that in an inflation squeeze, a key job may be making people pay less attention to price, and that only emotional branding can do that [S1].

### Reach widely, aim for fame

He argued that heavy segmentation, targeting and retargeting limit a brand, and that reaching new and existing customers broadly works better [S4]. Build mental availability, the ease with which a brand comes to mind, in stages: awareness, then salience, then fame [S4]. Fame means people share and talk about the brand [S4].

How he applied it: he told marketers to join popular culture as a way to reach the mass market [S4].

### Spend enough to be heard

He says brands tend to grow when their share of voice, their share of category advertising, is above their share of market [S4]. In 2022 he named underspending as one of the biggest mistakes. Great creative with too little money fails because the brand isn't heard [S1]. He would pick a good ad with a big budget over a great ad with a small one [S1].

How he applied it: he warned against a common error, cutting the budget once sales level off after a period of growth [S1].

### Creativity buys efficiency

In 2016 he argued that efficiency comes from emotional brand building, not from product-focused messages or cuts [S4]. The report says his research with Field found that creatively awarded work was much more efficient with its budget [S4].

How he applied it: he warned that creative should not be reused unchanged across every platform. Each medium needs its own version [S4].

### Share of search

In 2020 Binet presented a new metric: a brand's share of organic Google searches in its category, taken free from Google Trends [S3]. He had tested it for six years in cars, energy and mobile phones [S3]. It tracked market share in all three and led it, by up to a year for cars [S3]. Advertising had a large short-term effect on search that faded fast and a smaller long-term effect that built up. With steady advertising, about 60% of searches came from the long-term effect [S3]. He called it "by no means a silver bullet," because price and other factors affect conversion [S3].

How he applied it: he suggested a brand's base level of search without advertising could serve as a new measure of brand strength [S3].

### Measure with more than clicks

In a 2025 article he argued that clicks and last-touch data help tune single campaigns but can mislead, because not every click is a real, extra sale [S2]. He compared early online click tracking to coupon replies in print a century ago [S2]. Modelling the whole marketing mix shows overall effects but lacks detail; experiments show cause and effect but cost time and money; attribution is easy to get but misses most of the journey [S2]. He wants all three combined, covering short and long effects [S2].

How he applied it: he described an insurance project where his team combined attribution, experiments and modelling to predict call centre volumes within 1%, month after month [S2].

### Focus on what doesn't change

In 2022 he said marketers should ignore short-term noise, fashion and news, and focus on the stable parts of human psychology and demand [S1]. He cited Bill Bernbach's idea of the "unchanging man" [S1]. He said he avoids newspapers and heavy TV news for this reason [S1]. In 2025 he made a related point about data: prefer long data that shows trends over ever more short-term data [S2].

How he applied it: he pointed to Google Trends search data, which goes back to 2004, as an example of long data [S2][S3].

## How he decides

- Is this job brand building, sales activation, or both? [S1][S4]
- Start near 60:40 and adjust for brand, price and category [S1][S4].
- Is our share of voice above our share of market? [S4]
- Use emotion for the brand and reason for the response [S4].
- Reach widely before you target narrowly [S4].
- Don't cut spend just because sales stopped growing [S1].
- Check that a measured effect is real and incremental [S2].
- Watch long-run trends more than this week's numbers [S1][S2].

## Where he stood

**Brand or performance?** Both, in a balance that leans toward brand on average [S1][S4].

**Big idea or big budget?** He'd take a good idea with a big budget over a great idea with little money [S1].

**Targeting or reach?** Broad reach, for growth [S4].

**Does creativity matter to the bottom line?** Yes. Emotional, creative work lasts longer and makes budgets work harder [S4].

**What happens when budgets get squeezed?** In 2022, with bills rising, he said buyers may grow more price-sensitive. He wasn't sure which way the balance between emotional and price messages would move, but he said a key task may be getting people to think less about price [S1].

**Is one measure enough?** No. Each method sees part of the picture, so he combines modelling, experiments and attribution, and checks forecasts against what really happened [S2].

**Can one ad do everything across every channel?** No. He said the same creative shouldn't be pasted unchanged onto every platform [S4].

**Can AI help measurement?** As of 2025, he pictured models updated often and checked against outcomes in near real time [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Rules taken too literally.** The 60:40 split is often quoted as a law. He has said himself that it varies and can be 50:50 [S1]. Users who copy the number without the caveat misuse his work.
- **Hard for small budgets.** Long-term brand building and share of voice above market share assume money to spend [S1][S4]. A small or cash-poor business may not survive long enough to see the long-term payoff.
- **Award-winning creative.** The finding that awarded creative is more efficient [S4] can be read as backing award culture. Skeptics of creative awards would want to see how cases were selected.
- **New metrics have limits.** He conceded share of search is imperfect and needs careful reading [S3].
- **Targeting debate.** His case against heavy targeting [S4] sits uneasily with marketers who see precise targeting as core to strategy.

## Blind spots to watch

- His evidence comes mostly from big advertisers with long data. A start-up may need to adapt it.
- He says little about the craft of making an ad, only that creativity pays. Pair him with a creative.
- Broad reach can waste money for a niche product sold to a tiny market.
- His long view can underplay a cash crisis this quarter.
