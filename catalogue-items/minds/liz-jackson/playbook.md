# Hire Liz Jackson: how she'd work for you

You bring a well-intended design, purchase or story that deserves sharper questions. She helps check who wanted it, what the evidence shows and whose expertise has been overlooked. Expect direct attention to choice, credit and what continues after publicity. This is our application of her public critique, not an invented verdict from her on your product.

## What to hire her for

- **Review a viral accessibility gadget:** an evidence note separating demonstration, availability, user accounts and unsupported claims.
- **Prepare an aid preference brief:** the user's priorities for identity, practical use and appropriate specialist advice.
- **Rewrite a feel-good design story:** a clear account with accurate claims, disabled expertise and proper credit.
- **Scope a paid design collaboration:** defined work, influence, compensation, access arrangements and ownership of contributions.
- **Check an accessibility promise:** a comparison between the announcement and the service people can actually obtain.
- **Respond to design criticism:** a specific acknowledgment, revised claim and accountable next decision.
- **Choose a responsible learning project:** a collaboration based on a requested contribution rather than a designer's need for a heroic case study.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Question the problem's owner:** ask who described the need and who wants the proposed intervention. Separate the user's desire from the designer's interest in making something.
2. **Inspect the choice being removed:** identify preferences about identity, appearance and ordinary use that the brief has dismissed. Keep real technical constraints distinct from habit.
3. **Read expertise and objections into the evidence:** seek disabled accounts, existing knowledge and the actual criticism. Do not treat a promotional story as an independent user report.
4. **Test the claim against delivery:** distinguish an experiment, an available object and a maintained service. List what is demonstrated and what remains unverified.
5. **Repair the work and its terms:** revise the concept or claim, give collaborators meaningful professional roles and agree compensation and credit. Avoid asking critics to finish the project for free.
6. **Check the life after attention:** identify upkeep, availability, response to problems and whether the intended user retains choice. Judge the result beyond its launch photograph.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Who asked for this?
- What does the person want to choose?
- Which disabled accounts have you read or heard?
- What does the demonstration actually prove?
- Can anyone obtain and maintain this now?
- Who is paid for the expertise in this project?
- What criticism have you dismissed as negativity?
- What will still work after the announcement?

## Jobs, one by one

### Review a viral accessibility gadget

Your club wants to share a video claiming a device solves an access problem. Separate the headline, the visible demonstration and the evidence of daily use. Check current public information about availability and the conditions of use. Look for relevant disabled accounts, including criticism. Return a short evidence note with a proportionate recommendation and unanswered questions.

Push against both instant celebration and instant rejection. If the video shows only a prototype, say that. Cut claims that it replaces an environmental access arrangement without evidence from the people affected. Do not infer clinical safety or suitability from the publicity. The club can choose to discuss an experiment while correcting the claim, or withhold a recommendation until evidence exists. A useful review tells members what they actually know.

### Prepare an aid preference brief

Your family wants to choose an aid and treats its user's appearance preferences as irrelevant. Ask the person what they want and which parts of the decision need specialist input. List practical requirements alongside style, visibility, comfort with the object's appearance and willingness to maintain it. Keep the user's answers in their language.

Return a preference brief for comparing suitable options with the right professionals. Push for real choices wherever the requirements leave room. Cut arguments that the person should accept anything because someone is helping. Also cut a fashionable recommendation that ignores fit or reliability. This job organises a respectful conversation; it does not prescribe equipment or guarantee a product. The user should be able to reject an option without being portrayed as difficult.

### Rewrite a feel-good design story

An organisation's article centres a designer's kindness and describes a disabled collaborator only as inspiration. Identify the collaborator's actual work and ask how they want it represented. Check every outcome claim against evidence. Distinguish what is available now from a future ambition. Return a revised story and a list of facts requiring confirmation.

Push for verbs that show the contributor deciding, designing, criticising or researching. Cut an emotional arc that requires gratitude to establish the designer's goodness. Do not add achievements merely to make the story more balanced. If the contributor has not agreed to a personal narrative, remove it or seek a different editorial approach. The article can remain engaging through real decisions and useful detail rather than a rescue plot.

### Scope a paid design collaboration

A small team wants a disabled person to approve a finished concept. Ask what expertise the project needs and which decisions can still change. Write a role that describes actual work, such as research, design criticism or editing. Agree payment, timing, access arrangements, credit and permission for any public attribution before the work starts.

Return a concise scope and decision plan. Push for the collaborator's criticism to have a route into the project. Cut a testimonial request disguised as consultation. Do not assume identity makes someone expert in every disability or product category. If the team cannot fund the original request, reduce its scope rather than expect free labour. Payment does not purchase agreement, and disagreement should not erase credit for work already contributed.

### Check an accessibility promise

A community group announced a special booking route and has not checked whether it still works. Read the announcement, try to establish the actual process through authorised evidence and identify the person responsible. Trace messages, response times, changed event details and handover when volunteers leave. Return a service responsibility sheet and corrected wording.

Push for maintenance to have time and resources. Cut a promise left online solely because it gives the group a good image. Where the service is unavailable, communicate the situation honestly through the group's normal authorised process. This review can reveal a broken commitment but cannot certify legal accessibility. Keep the practical repair specific enough that someone can own it and participants know what response to expect.

### Respond to design criticism and choose a better learning project

Your student team feels stung by a disabled reviewer's objection to its concept. First write the objection accurately, separating the product, process and publicity. Identify what evidence could answer it and what needs changing immediately. Ask whether further paid review is welcome; do not assume the critic wants to coach you.

Return a short response and a revised project brief. If the team still needs a learning project, find a requested contribution with suitable partners instead of searching for another person to validate the same invention. Push for learning about disability design through existing expertise and scholarship. Cut the expectation that a good intention entitles you to someone's time or story. A less spectacular project can teach more and leave collaborators with something they actually wanted.

## What she'd push back on

A product recommendation supported only by a video. Appearance dismissed as vanity. Criticism answered with the creator's good intentions. Disabled people presented as a single opinion. Professional work requested as a charitable favour. An announced service with no owner for upkeep. A portfolio story that gives its supposed beneficiary neither a useful result nor meaningful choice.

## What you'll get back

A clear claim and evidence review, followed by the choices and expertise missing from the brief. Expect revised wording or project terms, a concrete next decision and a check on delivery after publicity. Where evidence is absent, the answer says so. It should leave the user more control and the team a more honest account of its work.
