# Hire Liz Wiseman: how she'd work for you

You bring her a team that waits for rescue, a delegation that never sticks or effort that does not create useful impact. She helps inspect the effect of your behavior and redesign the handover. Expect an emphasis on real capability, clear standards and ownership that survives the first difficulty.

## What to hire her for
- **Find the habit that makes your team dependent:** you get one helping habit named, what it does to people, and a replacement to try.
- **Assign work around people’s strengths:** you get a short match of each person’s proven strength to a real task.
- **Raise the quality bar without fear:** you get a written standard and a review format where people can ask questions and admit mistakes.
- **Run a decision meeting with real debate:** you get a one-page brief with the tradeoffs, who debates and who makes the call.
- **Delegate so the other person owns it:** you get an agreed result, limits on what they decide alone and a check-in rhythm.
- **Make your work more useful to colleagues:** you get a short recommendation that fits what the team needs now.

## What to bring first
- The habit, handover, meeting or decision, in your own words.
- The real material if you have it: the message, the plan, or the decision to make.
- Who is involved and what each person is good at.
- What you want to hold in your hands when you finish.

If something is missing, she labels her assumption and shows how a different answer would change the advice.

## How she'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem. It is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Notice the effect of your help.** List the three things people most often ask you to fix or finish. Next to each, write who else could do it. Pick one and ask the people involved how they respond when you step in. Output: one named habit and the thinking it may be replacing. Judge the pattern and its effect, not your character.

2. **Find capability beyond the job title.** For each person, write one thing you have seen them do well and where it would matter on current work. Give one of them a real assignment with a clear result. Output: a strength-to-task match. A strength is information, not a label that boxes someone in.

3. **Create intensity without intimidation.** Write the standard in one or two sentences. Say what people may question and where they may ask for help. Remove one behavior that makes people hide mistakes. Output: a written standard and a review format.

4. **Debate before the decision.** Write the decision, who has the final say and what would make you reconsider. Ask each relevant person for evidence on the tradeoffs before the meeting. Say up front whether input can change the choice or only how it is carried out. Output: a one-page decision brief.

5. **Hand over ownership and stay available.** Agree the result, the budget or time, the limits and the conditions that need a check. Name the decisions you release. Set a check-in where they bring risks and requests for help. Output: an ownership agreement. Letting go without resources is abandonment.

6. **Aim effort at the work that is needed.** Ask the team what contribution would help most now. Cut your deliverable to that need and make it easy to act on. Step back where someone else should lead, and close the handoff. Output: a short recommendation. Agree priorities so you do not absorb every loose task.

## First questions she'd ask you
- What do people stop doing because they know you will do it for them?
- Whose capability is already here but not being used where it matters?
- Is the pressure helping people think harder or making them protect themselves?
- What needs to be debated before the decision, and who makes the call afterward?
- Have you actually let go of the baton, or are you still steering every choice?
- What does the team need from you now, and how could you make that easier to use?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one
### Find the management habit making your team dependent
Invented example. A manager rewrites every report before sharing it. The team learns to send rough work and wait for the manager’s version. Ask the authors what happens when they know the rewrite is coming. Try returning the next draft with a clear standard and two questions, leaving revision with its owner. Compare whether the next submission shows more judgment rather than merely fewer manager edits.

You leave with one habit that makes people smaller, what it does to the team, and a replacement behavior to try this week. Judge the pattern and its effect, not the manager's character.

### Assign work around people’s demonstrated strengths
Invented example. A quiet operations colleague is unusually good at spotting missing steps in a plan. Invite them to review the onboarding handoff before launch, with authority to identify gaps and propose repairs. Do not make them sit through every unrelated brainstorm. The assignment uses a specific capability where it helps the team, and their contribution becomes visible through the improved handoff.

You leave with a short match of each person's proven strength to a real task. Treat a strength as information, not a label that boxes them in.

### Raise the quality bar without making review sessions frightening
Invented example. A team must improve a difficult presentation. State the audience need and quality standard, then let each owner revise their section. Invite concerns about what is not yet understood. During review, discuss the material rather than humiliating the presenter. The pressure stays on making the presentation useful, and people can admit a weak argument before it reaches the audience.

You leave with a written quality standard and a review format where people can ask questions and admit mistakes. Check that they actually can.

### Run a decision meeting with real debate and a clear finish
Invented example. A small team is choosing between two suppliers. Ask each relevant person to examine cost, reliability and switching effort before the meeting. Debate the tradeoffs for a fixed period, then have the owner decide and explain the reasoning. Someone whose preference loses can still see that their evidence shaped the choice or the risk plan. Avoid reopening the whole issue at every later handoff.

You leave with a one-page decision brief, the tradeoffs to debate and who makes the call. Say up front whether input can change the choice or only how it is carried out.

### Delegate a project so the other person truly owns it
Invented example. You ask a colleague to run a community event. Agree the budget, purpose, date and conditions requiring consultation. Let them choose the working plan and suppliers within those limits. Set a check-in where they bring risks and requests for help. If you quietly rebook everything yourself, you have restored the old dependency. Offer support that leaves the event’s decisions with its owner.

You leave with an agreed result, the limits of what they can decide alone, and a check-in rhythm. Give them the authority, information and access to help that ownership needs.

### Make your work more useful to colleagues and your career
Invented example. A person prepares a long update while the team urgently needs a clear decision on one blocked task. Ask what would be useful now, deliver a short recommendation and make the next handoff easy. Keep the larger analysis if it serves a later purpose. The change is not simply doing more outside the job description; it is aligning effort with a need the team recognizes.

You leave with a short recommendation that fits what the team needs now, and an easier handoff. Agree priorities so you do not become the person who absorbs every loose task.

## What she'd push back on
She would challenge the leader who answers every question before others can think, and the manager who calls a handoff complete while still holding every decision. She would also challenge vague empowerment without standards or resources. Use a specific behavior and its effect rather than turning the framework into a way to judge people’s character.

## What you'll get back
You receive a diagnosis of one limiting leadership habit, a replacement behavior and an ownership agreement for a real task. For a decision meeting, the answer specifies the debate and the decision rights. For career use, it identifies the contribution the team needs and how to deliver it with less coordination burden.

Next step: try the replacement behaviour on one real task this week, then compare the next result. Take your focus and time limits to Cal Newport, or your priorities to Peter Drucker. For hard feedback to one person, use Kim Scott; to build safety first, use Amy Edmondson.
