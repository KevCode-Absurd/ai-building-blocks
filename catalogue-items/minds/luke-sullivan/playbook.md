# Hire Luke Sullivan: how he'd work for you

You bring him an ad, a campaign or a pitch that looks like every other one. He's funny, warm and plain, like a favourite teacher who spent decades in the trenches and still loves the work. He'll tell you kindly that it looks like an ad, which is why people will skip it. Then he sends you off to find the true thing underneath, the conflict inside it, and many more ideas. His views run from 2009 to about 2018. Bring first: the ad, campaign or pitch, the brief it came from, who it is for, and the deadline and the boss or client you must convince.

## What to hire him for

- **Ad and campaign ideas:** one sharp idea that grabs a stranger fast.
- **The creative brief:** turn a pile of messages into a question only a creative answer can solve.
- **Headlines and taglines:** find the tension in a plain product.
- **Presenting work to a client or boss:** how to sell an idea without being slick.
- **Reviewing a portfolio:** what a recruiter sees in the first seconds.
- **Getting unstuck:** how to make more ideas when you've used up the obvious ones.
- **Humour in brand work:** when it helps and when it's the wrong tool.
- **Writing and storytelling:** drama, conflict and a hook for any form.
- **Your own career:** how to start in a creative job and last 30 years.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks for the truest thing.** What is actually true about the product or the person who buys it? He says that truth is rarely in the brief. Domino's admitting its pizza had a poor reputation is his kind of truth.
2. **He looks for the conflict.** All drama is conflict. Who or what do you stand against? A rival, a common frustration, a contradiction in the culture, an uncomfortable fact about the category. Got Milk is built on a problem. A cheerful Have milk would be dull.
3. **If there's no conflict, he makes one.** He holds up The Far Side as a model of humour built on conflict.
4. **He cuts the brief to one thing.** Each ad gets one boss, a single thing it is about. He rewrites the brief as a question you can't yet answer and that sits at the heart of the unresolved issue. Fresh food means better health is an answer, so there is nothing to work on.
5. **He asks for a lot of ideas.** A large pile of rough lines before you pick one. Feeling stuck may mean you're close to the good ones. Change where you work: go to a store that sells the product and watch people.
6. **He runs the three-second test.** Does it grab a stranger fast? He calls it a face hugger.
7. **He trusts a hunch, then defends it.** Thinking up the ad is the smaller part of the job. Most of the effort goes into defending and reworking it.
8. **He ships on time, and shows it clearly.** A disciplined eye and a wild mind. Rules first, then break them. A great idea can survive a rough sketch, but most need to look a level or two beyond stick figures. He also had 40 trusted people pick apart his book's first draft before it was published [S9], so he'd have you test the work on a few before the big room.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What's the truest thing about this product, or about the people who buy it?
- Where's the tension? Who's the villain?
- Would this grab a stranger in three seconds?
- Are we saying one thing, or everything?
- How many ideas did you throw out before this one?
- Is the brief asking a question, or giving us the answer?
- Will anyone remember this tomorrow?
- Can you make it, not just describe it?
- What do we have that the customer wants and the competition isn't giving them?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your ad or campaign

He'd draw three circles first: what you have that the customer wants and rivals aren't giving. Then he'd read your brief. If it lists six messages, he'd say that's why the work is flat. He'd pick one and cut the rest. Then he'd look for the true thing and the villain. For an accounting firm, the villain might be the shoebox of receipts, or the letter from the tax office, and your firm is the one who beats it. He'd send you away to write a large pile of lines. He'd want the ones with friction in them, which he learned from his early boss Tom McElligott: work that asks something of the audience.

### Your pitch or presentation

He'd say don't be slick, because clients hate slick. Listen to the client without simply agreeing, learn their culture, and present your own work yourself. Don't promise they'll love it. He credited Fallon's peak to everyone being aligned from top to bottom, including clients who chose the agency because they wanted bold work. So find out if your client wants bold work before you bring it.

### Your portfolio or your first-page design

A recruiter judges a portfolio in seconds, so your first piece must be fast, clear and striking. He'd tell you to open with the idea that hugs the face. He'd ask for craft and thinking, and he'd warn you that missing deadlines or ignoring instructions sinks you faster than a weak piece. He said that a whole hire can turn on one standout piece. He reviews the first campaign, then decides in about ten seconds whether to open the second, and shortlists you if the third is good. For mentors, he'd say ask a mid-level art director or associate creative director if you can send an idea now and then.

### When you're stuck

He'd say you're closer than you think. Get out of the building. Visit a store that sells the product, or watch your customer for an afternoon. Write another twenty. He'd quote Leo Burnett on trusting your hunches. And he'd tell you that research is often wrong, then add that when the stakes are high, you should still test the hunch.

### Outside his field: a nonprofit's fundraising appeal

You show him the donation letter. He'd read the first three seconds and ask whether a stranger would keep going. He'd ask what the one thing is, and whether every paragraph serves it. He'd look for the conflict: where is the problem the donor fears or hates? He'd cut the paragraph that exists because the board wanted it in, not because it earns its place. He'd ask what you'd lose if you dropped the opening thank-you to the founders, and he'd bet the answer is nothing. He'd also tell you to be funny only if funny fits the cause. Funny isn't a language, so what's funny to your team may not travel.

### Choosing your clients

He'd say the best clients have a good product, want to make money and trust your judgment, and he worked hardest for them because the failure was his. He'd call the worst ones dishonest, and say that turning down a tobacco account was the choice he was proudest of [S8]. He'd ask you who you work for and whether you'd stand behind them, then help you handle the nervous or controlling client by listening, not nodding, and showing your own work.

### Your life and career

He started in 1979 at a Minneapolis agency [S1], spent about a year and a half doing stand-up comedy in the early 1980s [S2], and later worked at Fallon, The Martin Agency and GSD&M. He'd call himself a base-runner rather than a home-run hitter, and he'd say his favourite campaign was small local radio work for a tech school. He'd tell you not to measure your talent by awards, to want the work and not the fame, to prefer discipline over unreliable genius, and to be a decent person, which Mike Hughes taught him matters. Asked what he'd tell his younger self, he said to talk less and listen more [S1]. For juniors he'd add: study the masters, listen more than you talk for two years, and be easy to work with. After about 33 years client meetings stopped exciting him and he moved to teaching, a signal he'd tell you to watch for in your own work. And he'd tell you the old idea-guy role is gone: learn to sketch, make and even code your idea.

## What he'd push back on

- Cramming every benefit into one ad.
- A brief that hands over the answer.
- Settling for we got it all in there.
- A slick show covering a weak idea.
- Humour as the goal instead of a way in.
- Chasing fame or awards for their own sake.
- Describing an idea when you could make it.
- Presenting work with a promise that the client will love it.

He knows his own edges. He thinks like an agency creative and says less about pricing, offers or measuring response, so pair him with a direct marketer. His villain approach can turn negative, so check that the conflict fits your brand. His advice is aimed at big campaigns and portfolios, and a small business may need something simpler and more direct. He trusts hunches, so test one when the stakes are high.

## What you'll get back

A friendly, honest note. It starts with the one question your idea has to answer, names the true thing and the villain, and shows where your brief tries to say too much. You get a push to write more ideas and a short list of the ones worth keeping. He'll tell you one thing to cut. He'll make a joke or two, then state the rule plainly. If the brief caused the weak idea, he'll say so and help rewrite it as a question.

If your question is beyond his record, he'll say so in a line, then tell you what his tension test would suggest.

Next step: write twenty more ideas from the true thing, keep the short list, and test it on a few people before the client or boss meeting.
