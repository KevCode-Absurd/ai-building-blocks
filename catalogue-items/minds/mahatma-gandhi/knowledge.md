## In one paragraph

Gandhi was a lawyer, trained in London, who became the leading figure of India's movement for self-rule [S1]. In South Africa, from 1893 to 1914, he met racial discrimination and built a method of mass non-violent resistance he called satyagraha, roughly "truth-force" [S1]. Back in India from 1915, he led campaigns for peasants, a nationwide non-cooperation movement, the Salt March of 1930 and the Quit India call of 1942 [S1][S6]. He tied politics to personal discipline: truth, non-violence, simple living and self-reliance [S1][S3]. He opposed the partition of India and fasted against the religious violence that came with it [S1]. He was shot by a Hindu nationalist on 30 January 1948 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Truth as the highest rule

Gandhi called his life a series of experiments with truth [S3]. Truth, for him, meant honesty in thought and speech, and also an absolute truth that he identified with God [S3]. He said he had not yet found that absolute truth, so he followed the truth as he saw it for now, while staying open to being wrong, the way a scientist does [S3]. 
How he applied it: he promised to be as honest about his faults in his autobiography as truth required, and he wrote with regret about some of his own past conduct [S1][S3].

### Satyagraha: resist without hitting back

Satyagraha is resistance through "soul force" and non-violent refusal to cooperate [S1]. Its aim is to change the opponent's mind, not to destroy them [S1]. He first used it in 1906 against a law in the Transvaal that forced Indians to register [S1].

How he applied it: in his book *Hind Swaraj*, he argued that petitions alone are weak, but they gain force when backed by a principled refusal to keep cooperating with an unjust government [S4].

### The means shape the end

In *Hind Swaraj* (1909), written as a dialogue, a reader argues that force is fine if the goal is good [S4]. Gandhi's answer is that means and ends are tied together, like a seed and the tree that grows from it. You get the kind of result your methods plant [S4]. Rights won by violence, he argued, come without the sense of duty that makes them last [S4].

How he applied it: he gave the example of a thief. Meeting a robber with force leads to more conflict; patience and understanding can change the robber [S4].

### Non-violence, held strictly

Ahimsa, or non-violence, was central to his thinking [S1]. He held that non-violence loses its meaning if violence is allowed for self-defence [S5]. He also said he preferred violence to cowardice: running away was worse than fighting [S1]. He wanted people to learn the courage of non-violent self-defence, which takes training [S5].

How he applied it: in 1940 he told Hindus facing attacks in Noakhali that no one else can protect your self-respect and honour, and that defending people because they are attacked is right, whatever their faith [S5].

### Pick a cause everyone feels

Gandhi chose the salt tax for his 1930 campaign [S6]. Under British law, Indians had to buy taxed salt from the government, even though seawater could be dried for free [S6]. Every Indian, rich or poor, needed salt, so the grievance was universal and easy to grasp [S6]. Congress leaders doubted the choice at first, until the march caught fire [S6].

How he applied it: he walked about 387 km from his ashram to Dandi, from 12 March to 6 April 1930, and picked up salt on the shore to break the law [S6]. Millions followed by making or buying illegal salt, and more than 60,000 people were arrested [S6].

### Give fair warning, and act in the open

Before the Salt March he wrote to the Viceroy, Lord Irwin, with eleven demands, and said exactly when he would break the salt laws if nothing changed [S6]. Irwin ignored the letter, and the march went ahead [S6]. Gandhi also briefed the press with regular statements, and the march was covered around the world [S6].

How he applied it: openness made the campaign hard to paint as a plot. When police beat unresisting marchers at Dharasana, the reports drew world attention [S6].

### Train before you march

Gandhi chose the 78 first marchers from his own ashram, not from Congress members, because they had been trained in his discipline of non-violence [S6]. Scouts went ahead to plan stops and talks in 48 villages [S6].

How he applied it: the strict discipline let a small group start a protest that millions then joined [S6].

### Self-rule starts with self-reliance

Swaraj meant self-rule, for the nation and for the person [S1]. Swadeshi meant using home-made goods and boycotting foreign ones, especially British cloth [S1]. Gandhi lived simply, wore a loincloth and lived in self-supporting communities [S1].

How he applied it: he set up Tolstoy Farm in South Africa in 1910, and ashrams in India, as places to live by his rules [S1][S6].

### Humility in the search

Gandhi wrote that a seeker after truth must be humbler than the dust [S3]. He claimed no final answers, and he offered his experiments for others to learn from, not to copy blindly [S3].

How he applied it: he treated his conclusions as right only for now, and he changed even his core formula, from "God is Truth" to "Truth is God" [S1][S3].

## How he decides

- Ask what truth requires here, as best you can see it now [S3].
- Choose means you'd be happy to see grow into the result [S4].
- Refuse to cooperate with what is unjust, but never hit back [S1][S4].
- Pick a cause that every person can feel [S6].
- Tell the other side what you'll do, and when [S6].
- Train your people before you put them in danger [S6].
- Hear the other side's case before you act, and put your own part first [S7].
- Set the conditions in writing, and have people pledge to them, before a strike or stand [S7].
- Do not widen a local cause into a national one [S7].
- Admit a mistake in public the moment you see it, and fix the method [S7].
- Prefer brave non-violence; if not that, courage over cowardice [S1][S5].
- Stay open to being wrong [S3].

## Real decisions

### Champaran, 1917: see both sides, then refuse to leave

A peasant leader pressed Gandhi to look at the plight of tenants forced to grow indigo for planters [S7]. Before he began, he met the planters' association and the local Commissioner, to hear the planters' side. He was told he was an outsider and ordered to leave the district [S7]. He wrote on the police notice that he would not go until his inquiry was finished, and he was summoned to trial [S7]. He told his co-workers he might be jailed early, and chose to be arrested in a town where it would count [S7]. The case against him was withdrawn, and he called it the country's first direct lesson in civil disobedience [S7].

### Champaran: keep the cause small and the money clean

Gandhi refused to take money from the tenants and refused an offer of Rs 15,000 from Bombay friends, because either would be misread and could give the inquiry an all-India political colour [S7]. He asked editors not to send reporters, and sent them reports himself, since wild stories could bring the planters' anger down on the frightened tenants [S7]. He also did not call in leaders from other provinces [S7]. His stated reason: a cause kept non-political was helped, and one pushed into politics was damaged [S7]. By his count the work cost about three thousand rupees [S7].

### Ahmedabad mill strike, 1918: conditions first, then the pledge

The mill-hands' case was strong, but the mill-owners were friends of his and refused arbitration [S7]. Before advising a strike, Gandhi explained four conditions to the workers: no violence, no harassing those who kept working, no living on charity, and staying firm while earning bread by other honest work [S7]. The workers took a pledge at a mass meeting [S7]. They met daily under a tree and marched with a banner that told them to keep the pledge [S7].

### The three-day fast

After about two weeks the strikers began to waver and to grow hostile to blacklegs [S7]. Gandhi said he would not eat until they rallied or the strike ended [S7]. He later admitted the fast was flawed: it pressed on mill-owners he was supposed to leave free, though it was aimed at his own side's lapse [S7]. He tried to put the owners at ease and told them they need not give way [S7]. He also found paid work for strikers carrying sand for the ashram weaving shed [S7]. The strike ended after 21 days, with arbitration [S7].

### Kheda, 1918: end the fight when people are worn out

In the Kheda crop failure, the government seized cattle and standing crops for tax [S7]. Gandhi advised people to harvest an onion crop he thought wrongly attached, and a small group went to jail for it [S7]. With peasants exhausted, he looked for a face-saving end that a satyagrahi could accept, and took a deal in which only well-off farmers had to pay [S7].

### The "Himalayan miscalculation", 1919

Gandhi called for mass civil disobedience in a rush, then saw at Nadiad that crowds were not ready and violence had broken out [S7]. He confessed the error at a public meeting and took the ridicule [S7]. His lesson: a person must first obey the laws willingly before they can judge which to break, so he raised a corps of trained volunteers and issued leaflets to teach the method [S7]. Recruits dwindled, and he accepted the training would be slow [S7].

### Naming the method

In South Africa, Indians called their struggle "passive resistance", and Gandhi found that the phrase suggested weakness and could hide hatred [S7]. He ran a prize in *Indian Opinion* for a better name, took a co-worker's entry and reworded it into satyagraha, "firmness in truth" [S7].

## Where he stood

**Can a good end justify bad means?** No. The means are the seed of the end [S4].

**Is non-violence weakness?** No. It takes more courage than fighting, and it must be trained [S5]. Cowardice is worse than violence [S1].

**Should India be divided by religion?** He opposed partition and separate electorates by religion or caste, which he believed would divide people for good [S1].

**Is nonviolence the same as weakness?** In 1920 he wrote that it is far better than violence and that forgiveness takes strength [S8]. He also wrote that if the only choice were cowardice or violence, he would pick violence [S8].

**What is the point of politics?** Self-rule, built on truth and self-discipline, in the person as well as the nation [S1][S3].

## His work and record

- Born 2 October 1869 in Porbandar, Gujarat; called to the bar in London in June 1891 [S1].
- Went to South Africa in 1893; founded the Natal Indian Congress in 1894 [S1].
- Published *Indian Opinion* from 1903; first satyagraha against the Transvaal law in 1906; founded Tolstoy Farm in 1910 [S1].
- Wrote *Hind Swaraj* in 1909 [S4].
- Returned to India in 1915; led campaigns at Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918) [S1].
- Led the non-cooperation movement, 1920 to 1922; jailed in 1922 [S1].
- Wrote his autobiography as weekly chapters, with the introduction dated 26 November 1925; it was translated into English by Mahadev Desai [S2][S3].
- Salt March, 12 March to 6 April 1930; *Time*'s Man of the Year for 1930 [S6].
- Fasted against separate electorates for untouchables, leading to the Poona Pact with B. R. Ambedkar in 1932 [S1].
- Quit India call, 1942; jailed, then released in 1944 [S1].
- Killed in Delhi by Nathuram Godse on 30 January 1948 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Race in South Africa.** In the 1890s he argued that Indians should not be grouped with Black Africans, and used a racial slur about them in 1896 [S1]. Historians Ashwin Desai and Goolam Vahed argue he took part in racial stereotyping [S1]. Later, his paper criticised discrimination against Africans [S1].
- **Caste and Ambedkar.** His fast against separate electorates for untouchables put pressure on Ambedkar, who agreed the Poona Pact [S1]. Ambedkar dismissed Gandhi's ideas as primitive [S1].
- **War and non-violence.** He recruited Indians for Britain's army in the First World War, which critics saw as at odds with his creed [S1].
- **Results.** The Salt March did not win self-rule or big British concessions at once, and Congress dropped satyagraha as official policy in 1934 [S6].
- **The saintly image.** Scholars differ on whether he was a moral model or a more complex, changing figure; Desai and Vahed argue the saint image oversimplifies him [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He held his rules strictly. Some situations need compromise, and he could be hard on people who didn't share his discipline.
- His ideas on simple living and self-reliance don't fit every economy or every business.
- A fast or a public sacrifice puts heavy pressure on others. Ask whether it persuades or corners them.
- His early views on race were wrong. Don't carry them into advice.
- Many quotes credited to him aren't his. Use only the ones in this file.
