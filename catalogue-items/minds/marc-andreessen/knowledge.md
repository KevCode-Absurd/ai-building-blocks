# Marc Andreessen: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Marc Andreessen is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from four essays he wrote: a 2007 blog post on product-market fit [S1], "Why Software Is Eating the World" from 2011 [S2], "It's Time to Build" from 2020 [S3] and "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto" from 2023 [S4]. It adds study notes on his 2018 Tim Ferriss interview [S7] and public reference pages [S5][S6]. For this pass only Wikipedia and short study notes were on disk in full; the essays are as read earlier. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its essay. He is a venture investor, so this mind explains his frameworks only. It never gives buy or sell advice, and it does not speak for him on politics or new events.

## In one paragraph

Andreessen helped build Mosaic, an early web browser, as a student in 1993, then co-founded Netscape, which went public in 1995 and put him on the cover of Time [S5]. In 2009 he and Ben Horowitz founded the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz, often called a16z [S6]. His essays have shaped how founders talk. In 2007 he argued that the only thing that matters for a young company is product-market fit, and that the market matters more than the team or the product [S1]. In 2011 he argued that software was eating the world: software firms would take over large parts of old industries [S2]. In 2020 he wrote that the West had chosen not to build, and called for more housing, factories, schools and energy [S3]. In 2023 he set out a strong case that technology and markets drive human progress [S4].

## Core ideas

### Market matters most

In his 2007 essay he split a startup into three parts: team, product and market [S1]. Of the three, he argued, market matters most. A great market pulls a product out of a startup, even a weak one, and a team can be upgraded along the way. A bad market beats even a great team with a great product [S1]. He credited this view to the investor Andy Rachleff [S1].

How he applied it: he told founders to judge a market by the number of possible customers and how fast that number is growing [S1].

### The only thing that matters is product-market fit

He defined product-market fit as being in a good market with a product that can satisfy it [S1]. You can feel when it's missing. Customers don't get much value, word doesn't spread, sales take forever and deals stall [S1]. You can also feel when it's there. Customers buy as fast as you can make the product, money piles up, and you can't hire fast enough [S1].

How he applied it: his advice was to do whatever it takes to get there. Change people, rewrite the product, move to a new market, or raise more money [S1]. He warned that founders who succeed often credit the wrong cause, when the real reason was fit [S1].

### Software is eating the world

In 2011 he argued that software firms were about to take over large parts of the economy, and would usually win [S2]. His examples included Amazon over Borders, Netflix over Blockbuster, and Pixar, a software firm, which became the top animation studio and was bought by Disney [S2]. He said the same shift was coming to farming, oil and gas, defence, logistics and finance [S2].

How he applied it: he explained why the time was right. Broadband users had grown from about 50 million to over two billion in a decade, and running a basic web service had fallen from about $150,000 a month in 2000 to about $1,500 on Amazon's cloud [S2].

### Bubble talk misses the value

In the same essay he rejected the claim that tech was in a bubble [S2]. He noted that big tech firms such as Apple traded at about the same price-to-earnings ratio as the wider market [S2]. He argued that the debate looked too hard at prices and not enough at the real value these firms created [S2].

How he applied it: he named the real risks instead, such as a shortage of people with software skills, and workers in older industries being left behind [S2].

### It's time to build

In April 2020 he argued that the West was not ready for COVID-19 because it had stopped building [S3]. He pointed to shortages of tests, masks and ventilators, and to slow aid payments [S3]. The same failure showed up in housing, schools, factories and transport [S3]. He said the cause was not money or skill. The cause was a lack of desire, plus inertia and incumbents who shape the rules to block rivals [S3].

How he applied it: he called on both the political right and left to back building, with goals such as many new nuclear reactors and wider access to top universities [S3]. He ended by asking readers what they were building [S3].

### Technology and markets drive progress

His 2023 manifesto argued that growth drives progress and that technology is its main lasting source [S4]. Gains in productivity lower prices, raise wages and create new jobs [S4]. He described free markets as the best way to organise a technical economy, and argued for speeding up technical progress on purpose [S4].

How he applied it: he made strong claims for AI as a universal problem solver and for nuclear power as clean, near-unlimited energy [S4].

### Aim for abundance

The manifesto's goal is abundance: prices falling across the economy until physical goods become very cheap, with room for a much larger population [S4]. He framed it as not a perfect world, but close to one [S4]. He argued that faster technical progress, not slowing down, is what protects the environment [S4].

How he applied it: in 2020 he had already pointed at specific gaps, such as one-to-one tutoring, a method that works but has never been scaled, and automated factories that could bring better-paid jobs back home [S3].

### Name the ideas you oppose

The manifesto lists "enemies," which he framed as bad ideas rather than bad people [S4]. They include stagnation, central planning, bureaucracy and regulatory capture, the precautionary principle, and degrowth [S4]. He also named some sustainability, tech ethics and "trust and safety" ideas, as he framed them [S4].

How he applied it: the essay closes with a long list of "patron saints," thinkers and builders he admires [S4].

### Help founders with a full-service firm

When he and Horowitz started a16z in 2009 with a $300 million fund, they set it up to help founders as well as fund them [S6]. Partners worked for all the firm's companies, a model borrowed from the Hollywood talent agency CAA, and the firm kept a network of designers, coders and executives to fill startup roles [S6].

How he applied it: early bets included Skype in 2009, Airbnb in 2011, GitHub in 2012 and Coinbase in 2013 [S6].

## How he decides

- Judge the market first: how many customers, and how fast it grows [S1].
- Before fit, ignore almost everything except getting to fit [S1].
- After fit, scale hard [S1].
- Ask which old industry software will take over next [S2].
- Look at the real value a firm creates, as well as its price [S2].
- When something isn't being built, ask who or what is blocking it [S3].
- Default to optimism about technology, and argue it plainly [S4].

## Real decisions

### Build a browser, then a company around it (1993 to 1995)
As a student he helped write Mosaic at the NCSA in 1993, then co-founded Netscape in 1994. It went public in 1995 [S5]. AOL bought it in 1999 for $4.3 billion [S5].

### Switch from one business to another when the market moved (1999 to 2007)
He co-founded Loudcloud, which became Opsware, and it was sold to HP for $1.6 billion [S5]. His 2007 advice on fit shows the habit: if the market is not pulling, change the people, rewrite the product, move to a new market or raise more money [S1].

### Start a firm that gives founders a full service (2009)
He and Ben Horowitz founded Andreessen Horowitz [S6]. Early bets included Skype in 2009, Airbnb in 2011, GitHub in 2012 and Coinbase in 2013 [S6].

### Write the case before the crowd does (2011)
In "Why Software Is Eating the World" he argued that software firms would take over big parts of old industries, and backed it with numbers on broadband users and cloud costs [S2]. He also said that bubble talk missed the value and named the real risks [S2].

### Make a deal survive an attack (as he describes the firm's process, 2018)
In his Ferriss interview he says any partner can push a deal through, but must first survive a deliberate attack from colleagues, sometimes a formal red team [S7]. He says he wants conviction to make a bet and expects to change his mind when facts change [S7].

### Call for building, not only criticism (2020 and 2023)
"It's Time to Build" pointed at specific gaps: one-to-one tutoring at scale, and automated factories that could bring better-paid work home [S3]. The 2023 manifesto went further, with strong claims on AI and nuclear power [S4]. In 2023 a16z also launched an American Dynamism fund for space, defence and manufacturing [S6].

## Where he stood

**What matters most in a startup?** The market, then product-market fit [S1].

**Is tech a bubble?** Not in 2011, by his reading of the numbers [S2].

**Why doesn't the West build more?** A lack of desire, inertia and incumbents who capture the rules [S3].

**Is technology good for people?** Yes, strongly, as the main source of growth and abundance [S4].

**Should we slow AI down?** No, as of 2023. He saw AI as a way to save lives and expand what people can do [S4].

## His work and record

- 1993: graduated in computer science from the University of Illinois. Co-wrote Mosaic at the NCSA [S5].
- 1994: co-founded Netscape [S5].
- 1995: Netscape went public [S5].
- 1999: AOL bought Netscape for $4.3 billion [S5].
- 1999 to 2007: co-founded Loudcloud, later Opsware, sold to HP for $1.6 billion [S5].
- 2007: wrote the Pmarca Guide to Startups [S1].
- 2009: co-founded Andreessen Horowitz [S6].
- 2011: wrote "Why Software Is Eating the World" [S2][S5].
- 2013: shared the first Queen Elizabeth Prize for Engineering [S5].
- 2020: wrote "It's Time to Build" [S3].
- 2023: wrote "The Techno-Optimist Manifesto." a16z launched an American Dynamism fund for space, defence and manufacturing [S4][S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **India tweet.** In 2016, after India blocked Facebook's Free Basics, he posted a tweet suggesting anti-colonialism had hurt India. He deleted it after a backlash [S5].
- **Housing.** In 2022 he and his wife opposed a 131-home project in Atherton, California. Critics saw this as at odds with his call to build more housing [S3][S5].
- **Board conduct.** A Facebook shareholder lawsuit alleged he coached Mark Zuckerberg on a share change while serving as an independent director [S5].
- **Crypto and big bets.** Some a16z-backed crypto projects drew criticism or regulatory scrutiny, and the firm reportedly lost $288 million on its Twitter stake by 2024 [S6].
- **Politics.** Since 2024 he has been active in U.S. politics, and commentators describe him as a controversial political figure [S5][S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- His optimism can skip over who gets hurt by a new technology. Pair him with someone who asks about harms [S4].
- "Do whatever it takes to get to fit" can justify burning a team out [S1].
- His enemy list treats some safety work as an obstacle. Not every rule is capture [S4].
- Market-first thinking is a venture investor's lens. A small, steady business can thrive in a modest market.
