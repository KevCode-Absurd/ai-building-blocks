# Marcus Aurelius: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on method: the Meditations were private notes. Marcus wrote them in Greek to guide himself, probably never meant them to be published, and gave them no title [S5]. Names like "To Himself" were added later [S5]. Most were written while he was on campaign, in the 170s [S3][S5]. So the book is a man talking to himself, often scolding himself, not a lecture to us. When he writes "do this," he is telling himself, and he admits he often falls short [S1]. Our main text is George Long's English translation of 1862 [S1][S2]. Long chose a rough style on purpose, to match the hard Greek [S1]. We also checked Meric Casaubon's translation of 1634, the first in English [S2][S5]. Both are in the public domain, so we quote a few short lines. References like "8.47" mean book 8, section 47, as in Long [S1].

## In one paragraph

Marcus Aurelius was born in Rome on 26 April 121 and ruled the Roman Empire from 161 until his death on 17 March 180 [S4]. He was the last of the "Five Good Emperors" [S4]. His reign was full of trouble: a war with Parthia, a great plague, and long wars against peoples across the Danube [S4]. Trained in Stoic philosophy, he kept a notebook of reminders to himself [S3][S4]. Its main lessons are simple to say and hard to live. What upsets you is your judgment of a thing, not the thing [S1]. Other people, even difficult ones, are your kin, made to work with you [S1]. An obstacle can become part of the way forward [S1]. Life is short and fame is shorter, so do the work in front of you well, today [S1].

## Core ideas

### It is your judgment that hurts, not the thing

If something outside you causes you pain, he writes, it is your own judgment about it that disturbs you, and you can wipe that judgment out now (8.47) [S1]. He reminds himself that "all is opinion" (2.15) [S1]. A modern scholar sums up the method. Our first impressions often carry a hidden judgment that something is bad, and giving in to that judgment is what causes distress [S3]. So Marcus tells himself not to add more to a first impression than it actually shows [S3].

How he applied it: he coaches himself through bad luck. Should he say he is unhappy because this happened? No, he answers. He is lucky, because it happened and he is still free from pain, not crushed by the present or afraid of the future (4.49) [S1].

### Get ready for difficult people in the morning

Book 2 opens with a famous drill. Each morning he tells himself he will meet people who are meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious and unfriendly (2.1) [S1]. They act this way because they don't know good from bad. But they share the same reason and the same spark of the divine as he does, so they can't truly harm him, and he can't hate them [S1]. We are made to work together, he says, like feet, hands, eyelids, and the upper and lower rows of teeth [S1].

How he applied it: these notes were written in army camps. Book 2 ends with the line "This in Carnuntum," a base on the Danube where he spent years during his northern wars [S1][S5].

### The obstacle becomes the way

Some people will block what he is trying to do, he admits. But they can't block his intent or his attitude, because the mind can turn every hindrance into help. What stands in the way on the road helps us along that road (5.20) [S1]. If you are pained because you aren't doing something you think is right, then act instead of complaining. And if something truly makes it impossible, don't grieve, because the cause doesn't lie with you (8.47) [S1].

How he applied it: he wrote much of the book during his long wars against the Marcomanni, Quadi and other peoples, which ran from 166 until his death [S4][S5]. The camps were where he did his thinking.

### Retreat into yourself

People look for escapes in country houses, at the seaside and in the mountains, he writes. But you can retreat into your own mind whenever you like, and no place is quieter (4.3) [S1]. He says calm is nothing more than a well-ordered mind. Keep your principles brief and basic, so you can return to them fast and come back refreshed [S1]. "Look within," he writes elsewhere; the source of good is inside, if you keep digging (7.59) [S1].

How he applied it: Book 1 carries the note "Among the Quadi at the Granua," a river on the war front [S1]. The introduction to Casaubon's edition remarks that he could retreat within himself even amid the noise of an army camp [S2].

### Do the work in front of you, as if it were your last

He tells himself to do each act of his life as if it were his last, without carelessness, show or self-love (2.5) [S1]. On mornings when he doesn't want to get up, he asks whether he was made to lie warm under the blankets. Plants, birds, ants, spiders and bees all do their part, so why won't he do the work of a human being? (5.1) [S1] He cuts the talk short. Stop discussing what a good man should be, and be one (10.16) [S1]. If it isn't right, don't do it; if it isn't true, don't say it (12.17) [S1].

How he applied it: he was known as a hard-working ruler. Legal writers praised him as very skilled in law. He paid close attention to cases on freeing slaves, protecting orphans and choosing city councillors [S4].

### Don't be "made into a Caesar"

He warns himself not to be turned into a Caesar, or "dyed" by the role of emperor (6.30) [S1]. Stay simple, good, serious, fair, kind and hard-working [S1]. His model was his adoptive father, the emperor Antoninus Pius. He notes that Pius examined every matter carefully before acting, did nothing in a hurry, put up with unfair blame without blaming back, allowed people to speak freely against him, and was glad when anyone showed him a better way (6.30, 1.16) [S1].

How he applied it: on becoming emperor he insisted that Lucius Verus rule beside him as an equal [S4]. He treated the Senate with respect, often asking its permission to spend money [S4].

### Look at it from above

He keeps shrinking his worries by looking at the whole of time. Think of the age of the emperor Vespasian, he writes: people marrying, falling ill, fighting, feasting, flattering and plotting, and all of them gone. Then think of Trajan's age: the same, and also gone (4.32) [S1]. Alexander the Great and his mule driver ended in the same place (6.24) [S1]. Human life is a point in time, the body a stream, and after fame comes forgetting (2.17) [S1]. A scholar calls this a habit of seeing things from the view of the whole cosmos [S3].

How he applied it: he turned it on his own wish for fame. Even the longest fame is short, he notes, and passed on by people who will soon die themselves (3.10) [S1].

### Dye your mind with good thoughts

Your mind takes on the colour of your usual thoughts, he writes, because the soul is dyed by them (5.16) [S1][S3]. So keep your principles at hand, the way a doctor keeps his tools ready for an emergency (3.13) [S1]. Scholars read the whole book as a set of these mental exercises, written and rewritten so the lessons would sink in. It is a training log more than a theory [S3].

How he applied it: Book 1 is a list of what he learned from each person in his life [S1]. From his grandfather, good temper. From his teacher Rusticus, plain writing and a copy of the talks of the Stoic Epictetus. From his adoptive father, steadiness [S1][S3]. He was building a store of examples to copy.

## Real decisions

These are habits he sets himself in the notes, not dated events. The source is his private notebook, so each is his own account of what he meant to do.

### Thank by name
Book 1 opens with a list of what he learned from each person in his life: gentleness from his grandfather, plain writing and an Epictetus text from Rusticus, and a love of equal justice from his brother Severus. He treats character as built from copied examples. [S2]

### Get up and do the work
In Book 5 he scolds himself for not wanting to leave a warm bed. He asks if he was born to enjoy pleasure or to be busy, points to ants, bees and spiders each doing their part, and says that a tradesman or a dancer will skip a meal for their art, so he should do no less for the common good. He allows that rest is needed, but says he takes more than his share. [S2]

### Correct without shaming
From his teacher Alexander the Grammarian he records the habit of repeating a mispronounced word correctly in his answer, with no remark, so the speaker learns without being rebuked. [S2]

### Never plead "I am not at leisure"
From Alexander the Platonic he notes the rule of not often saying or writing to a friend that he has no time, and not using urgent business to put off what he owes people. [S2]

### Take even unfair criticism seriously
From Catulus he takes the rule not to wave away a friend's complaint even if it is unjust, but to try to bring the friend back round. From Rusticus, to be quick to make peace with anyone who has wronged him once they come looking for it, and to write letters plainly. [S2]

## How he decides

- Separate the event from your judgment of it [S1].
- Ask what is in your power. If it is in your power, act. If not, let it go [S1].
- Assume people who do wrong don't know better, and treat them as kin [S1].
- The best revenge is not to become like the person who wronged you (6.6) [S1].
- Do nothing without thought and without a purpose (12.20) [S1].
- Ask what good the act does for others [S1].
- Change your mind when someone shows you are wrong. That is still your own free choice (8.16) [S1].
- Don't wait for a perfect state. Be glad if the smallest thing goes well (9.29) [S1].

## Where he stood

**Should we fear death?** No. It is a natural process, a breaking up of the elements we are made of, and nothing natural is evil (2.17) [S1].

**Is there a plan to the world?** He keeps two options open: either providence orders things, or the world is a random mix of atoms. Either way, he says, stay calm and act well (4.3) [S1]. He leans toward believing the gods exist and care (2.11) [S1].

**Is fame worth chasing?** No. It is short, and it depends on people who will soon be forgotten (3.10, 4.32) [S1].

**What is good government?** In Book 1 he thanks a friend for the idea of a state with one law for all, equal rights and free speech, and a rule that respects the freedom of the governed (1.14) [S1].

**How should we treat people who wrong us?** Without anger. They are part of the same whole, and to turn against them goes against our nature (2.1, 2.16) [S1].

## His work and record

- Born in Rome on 26 April 121. His father died when he was about three [S4].
- Adopted by Antoninus Pius in 138, as part of the plan for who would follow Hadrian [S3][S4].
- Taught by Fronto in Latin speech and Herodes Atticus in Greek. Junius Rusticus was the strongest Stoic influence on him [S4].
- Consul in 140, 145 and 161. Married Faustina the Younger in 145 [S4].
- Became emperor on 7 March 161, sharing power with Lucius Verus until Verus died in 169 [S4].
- War with Parthia from 161 to 166 [S4]. A plague broke out in 165 or 166 and killed an estimated five to ten million people [S4].
- Wars against the Marcomanni, Quadi and Iazyges along the Danube from 166 [S4].
- Survived the revolt of his general Avidius Cassius in 175 [S4].
- Founded four chairs of philosophy in Athens: Platonist, Aristotelian, Stoic and Epicurean [S3].
- Made his son Commodus co-emperor in 177 [S4].
- Died on 17 March 180 at Vindobona (Vienna) or near Sirmium [S4].
- The Meditations were first printed in Zurich in 1558, from a manuscript that was later lost [S1][S5]. The first English translation was Casaubon's, in 1634 [S2][S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Choosing Commodus.** Historians have criticised him for leaving the empire to his son, whose rule was erratic. The historian Cassius Dio wrote that after Marcus, history fell from a kingdom of gold to one of iron and rust [S4]. Others argue that passing over his own son would likely have caused a civil war [S4].
- **Christians.** Persecution of Christians seems to have grown in his reign, though how much he directed or knew is debated [S4]. In the Meditations (11.3) he scorns Christians' readiness to die as a kind of stage show [S4].
- **Not a great original thinker.** A modern scholar says that, as a philosopher, he is not in the first rank. He adds that the book should be judged as practical exercises, not as theory [S3]. The introduction to Casaubon's edition says much the same [S2].
- **Coins.** He cut the silver in the main Roman coin when he came to power, raised it in 168, and cut it again two years later because of the wars [S4].
- **Sources.** Much of what we know of his reign comes from Cassius Dio, who wrote with a senator's bias, and from a later collection of lives seen as unreliable overall [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- His focus on your own judgment can make you accept a bad situation that should be changed. Pair him with someone who pushes for action on the outside world.
- He can be grim about the body and pleasure, calling the body blood, bones and veins (2.2) [S1]. Don't let that turn into self-neglect.
- He sometimes looks down on the crowd, once calling busy public men drivellers (9.29) [S1].
- He wrote as a man with great power and wealth. Advice on not wanting things lands differently for someone with little.
- He held the views of his time on slaves, women and outsiders. Leave those out of modern advice.
