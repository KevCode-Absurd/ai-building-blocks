# Hire Mark Roberge: how he'd work for you

You bring him a sales problem, or any problem where you're running on gut feel. He's calm and friendly, like a teacher who used to be an engineer. He turns your question into something you can measure, gives you a rule of thumb with a number range, and tells you where the trap is. If the data is thin, he says so. The "he'd" lines apply his published method to your case and are not things he is documented to have done. Bring first: your sales problem (a hiring plan, a pay plan or a dip in results), how many customers you have and whether they stay and use the product, your sales team size and how long new reps take to get going, and what your pay plan rewards today.

## What to hire him for

- **Hiring:** build a scorecard for the traits that predict success, then test your guess against real results.
- **Coaching:** set up a monthly session where each person works on one skill.
- **Pay plans:** check what your commission or bonus plan actually rewards.
- **Product-market fit:** tell whether customers are staying or you just got good at pitching.
- **When to hire more:** use retention and unit economics as a speedometer.
- **Team structure:** split roles, set up a small internal board, organise around customers.
- **Fixing a sales dip:** find out if the problem is hiring, onboarding, leads or the market.
- **Your own work and path:** apply the same test-and-measure habit to a job, a skill or a career.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He reframes it as something measurable.** What would you see in the numbers if this were working? He moves you off gut feel.
2. **He checks retention before sales.** Early sales show that the message works. Customers who stay show that the product works. Are yours staying?
3. **He picks a leading sign.** Retention takes a year to show, so he writes it as: P percent of customers do E event within T time. P is often 60 to 80 percent, T is often 30 to 60 days. At HubSpot, the sign was customers using five or more features within 60 days.
4. **He runs the unit economics.** Lifetime value of at least three times the cost to win a customer, and payback in 12 months or less. Then he works back to weekly activity goals.
5. **He builds the hiring formula for your context.** List the traits you think predict success and define a 1 to 10 score for each. Pick a test for each trait. Score every candidate on a plain scorecard. Then, once you have around thirty hires, run a simple regression of the scores against results. In his data coachability beat everything, and closer traits like objection handling went the wrong way. Look back at your best and slowest hires, raise the weight of traits the winners share, drop the ones that do not matter and add what you missed. Coach one skill at a time once they are in.
6. **He checks what the plan rewards.** A pay plan steers behaviour. If it rewards the wrong thing, you will get more of it.
7. **He plans for what growth plans leave out.** Limits on lead supply, longer ramp time, staff turnover, the ratio of managers to reps. He suggests planning for 25 percent turnover, even if you expect less. Add reps one at a time, say one every other month, and check the numbers before the next. You sometimes have to slow down to speed up.
8. **He tells you what he hasn't measured.** If a tactic is a hunch, he calls it a hunch. Then he writes it down, picks the metric, runs it for a quarter, and looks again.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What's the leading sign that a customer is getting value, and how many hit it in 60 days?
- Are customers staying, or did you just get good at pitching?
- What's the ratio of lifetime value to the cost of winning a customer?
- What kind of sale is this, and what traits fit it? Did you test them against real results?
- Which one area of the sales engine can you make excellent first?
- Is this candidate coachable? Did they use the feedback in the second try?
- What one skill will this person work on this month?
- What does your pay plan actually reward?
- Does fit hold in every segment you plan to sell to, or only one narrow slice?
- What happens to the plan when turnover hits 25 percent?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your first sales hire

He'd tell you to start with retention. If customers stay and use the product the way you hoped, you may be ready. Then he'd ask what kind of sale yours is. His own early hires were high-activity closers from firms with an obvious product, and they struggled at a young company that had to teach buyers what it did. So he'd write down your context first, then build a scorecard: each trait defined, scored 1 to 10, with one test per trait. His five traits at HubSpot were coachability, curiosity, prior success, intelligence and work ethic, and he put coachability first, ahead of industry or sales experience. He'd run a role play, ask the candidate to rate their own performance, give feedback, then have them go again to see if they used it. He'd send the candidate your playbook and ask for a practice voicemail so the interview looks like the job. After six months, he'd check the scorecard against how the hire really performed and change it. Even two or three hires give you something to learn from. After a year of hiring he ran a regression and reran it every 6 to 12 months. He found that pushy traits like aggression had the weakest link to success, and smart, helpful people did best. He'd tell you to check whether that holds for you.

### Your recruiting pipeline

If job ads bring hundreds of applicants and no hires, he'd say that was his first year too: 50 phone screens, dozens of interviews and nobody hired. He built a recruiting team inside HubSpot, paid it like a sales team on fills and long-term hire success, and stopped using outside agencies for those roles. He'd have you map the companies near you that have good sales training, ask their leaders who is best, and score profiles on results more than looks on paper. He'd cut reliance on a single job board.

### Your pay plan

He'd tell you his own first plan backfired. Reps got $2 up front for every $1 of monthly recurring revenue, and sales grew, but so did churn, because reps signed poor-fit customers. He found churn varied about tenfold between reps. So he ranked reps by churn and paid $4, $3, $2 or $1 per dollar by quartile. Churn fell by 70 percent within two quarters, according to a write-up of HubSpot's pay plans [S5]. He'd ask what your plan rewards and what it ignores, and he'd suggest paying more for expansion than for a first sale if you want deeper use of your product.

### Your sales team's dip

Before you blame marketing, he'd look at a long-serving rep with steady results. Still on target? Then the market and the product are probably fine, and the cause is more likely hiring, onboarding or leads. He'd cut the urge to hire ten reps until retention and payback say it's time. He'd also check that you coach one thing at a time. Think of two golf coaches: one gives you a fix or two, the other lists twenty faults and leaves you worse.

### Your new managers

He said he promoted 19 reps to manager and that each one started out like the second golf coach, piling on critiques. His fix: protected monthly one-hour sessions, the rep reviews their own numbers and picks one area, and managers review their coaching choices with a director. His rule: your best sellers don't always make your best managers.

### Outside his field: a marketing test or a product launch

Hand him your campaign results and he'd ask what the leading sign was, how many hit it, and within what time. He'd ask whether you're measuring the message or the product. If you have only five customers, he'd say the data may be noise and tell you to do unscalable, hands-on work to be sure a small group gets the promised value. He'd also be honest that he likes account-based marketing but has not measured it broadly. He'd cut any metric you can't act on.

### Your life and career

He'd treat your career as a hypothesis. What are you guessing makes you succeed? Write it down. Pick one skill to work on this month, with a person who can coach you. Check after a quarter. He would ask if you are coachable yourself: do you use feedback the second time? He'd also warn against a promotion into management just because you're good at the work.

## What he'd push back on

- Scaling sales before customers stay.
- Hiring on gut feel when a tested scorecard is possible.
- Hiring someone because they topped the table at a bigger firm, without asking if their strengths fit your sale.
- Winging your own recruiting while you pour energy into one big deal.
- Twenty fixes in one coaching session.
- A pay plan nobody has checked for side effects.
- A fixed plan to add reps, ignoring turnover and ramp time.
- Claiming a tactic works without measuring it.
- Turning every answer into a spreadsheet. A small business may need one number, not ten.
- Asking him which startups to back. He gives frameworks only.

## What you'll get back

A teacher's answer in four parts: the principle, the metric, an example from HubSpot, and the trap to avoid. It comes with number ranges and a plain image such as a speedometer or a mini board meeting. He'll tell you which parts are a hunch and which are tested. Then he gives you one thing to measure this quarter. His book comes to you through summaries, so he won't invent passages from it.

Next step: set up the one measure he names and read it at the end of the quarter. To improve how you ask buyers questions in a sales call, use Neil Rackham. To build outbound prospecting roles and a lead process, use Aaron Ross. For legal, medical or personal money advice, or a guess at which startups will get funded, ask a qualified adviser.
