# Mark Zuckerberg: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Mark Zuckerberg is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from four public letters he wrote: his 2012 letter to investors when Facebook went public [S1], his 2021 letter on renaming the company Meta [S2], his 2024 letter on open source AI [S3] and his 2025 letter on "personal superintelligence" [S4]. It adds public reference pages [S5][S6]. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its letter. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after that, and this mind does not speak for him on court cases, politics or new products.

## In one paragraph

Zuckerberg launched "Thefacebook" from Harvard in February 2004 with four roommates, dropped out that year and moved the company to Palo Alto [S5]. His 2012 letter said Facebook was not first built to be a company, but to make the world more open and connected [S1]. It set out the "Hacker Way": ship small changes fast, test thousands of versions, prefer building to arguing, and value impact, speed, boldness and openness [S1]. He bought Instagram in 2012, and WhatsApp and Oculus in 2014 [S6]. In 2021 he renamed the company Meta and bet its future on the "metaverse," a more immersive internet [S2]. In 2024 he argued that AI should be open source, like Linux [S3], and in 2025 he set a goal of personal superintelligence for everyone [S4]. His record also includes serious public criticism over privacy, misinformation and teen safety [S5][S6].

## Core ideas

### Mission first, and money to serve it

His 2012 letter said Facebook was built for a social mission and only later became a company [S1]. He wrote that Facebook didn't build services to make money; it made money to build better services [S1]. A strong business, he argued, was the best way to keep people focused on big problems [S1].

How he applied it: he framed the mission around three goals: changing how people relate to each other, to businesses and to their governments and institutions [S1]. By 2012 he counted more than 800 million people and over 100 billion connections [S1].

### The Hacker Way: ship, test, repeat

He described the Hacker Way as building by constant improvement [S1]. Release small changes quickly and learn from them, instead of trying to get everything right at once [S1]. Facebook built a testing system that could run thousands of versions of the site at the same time [S1].

How he applied it: the phrase "Done is better than perfect" was painted on Facebook's walls to keep people shipping [S1]. Every new engineer, and even managers who would not mainly code, went through a training course called Bootcamp [S1].

### Code wins arguments

The letter favoured building a prototype over a long debate [S1]. Instead of arguing about whether an idea will work, build it and see [S1]. He described an open culture where the best idea should win, not the most senior person [S1].

How he applied it: Facebook held regular hackathons. Timeline, chat and video all came out of them [S1].

### Move fast and be bold

Two of Facebook's five core values in 2012 were "Move Fast" and "Be Bold" [S1]. He argued that speed lets you build more and learn faster, and that fear of mistakes should not slow you down [S1]. Taking no risks, he wrote, was the riskiest choice of all [S1]. The other three values were Focus on Impact, Be Open and Build Social Value [S1].

How he applied it: he made large bets early. Facebook bought Instagram for about $1 billion in 2012, WhatsApp for $19 billion in 2014 and Oculus for $2.3 billion the same year [S6].

### Bet on the next platform

Zuckerberg sees computing moving in waves: desktop to mobile, text to photos to video, and then to more immersive worlds [S2]. In 2021 he said the next wave would let people feel present with each other, as if in the same room [S2]. He called that feeling of presence the defining quality of the metaverse [S2].

How he applied it: he renamed the company Meta in October 2021 [S2][S6]. He set goals for the next decade: a billion people in the metaverse, hundreds of billions of dollars of digital commerce and jobs for millions of creators [S2].

### Don't depend on someone else's platform

Zuckerberg has long chafed at building on platforms others control. His 2021 letter criticised high fees and limited choice on other platforms [S2]. In 2024 he named Apple's rules as limits on what Meta could build [S3].

How he applied it: Meta planned to sell devices at or below cost, allow side-loading and keep developer fees low [S2]. His push into VR, glasses and AI is partly about owning the next platform rather than renting it [S2][S3][S4].

### Open source as strategy

His 2024 letter argued that AI would follow the path of Linux, which began as a cheaper, open alternative and became the standard [S3]. Open models let developers tune them on their own data, avoid being locked in, and keep data in-house [S3]. He argued it was also good for Meta, because selling access to models was not its business [S3]. On safety, he argued open systems are easier to check, and that wide access lets larger institutions police smaller bad actors [S3].

How he applied it: he released Llama 3.1 405B as an open model and pointed to past open projects such as PyTorch, React and the Open Compute Project [S3].

### Put the power in people's hands

His 2025 letter described personal superintelligence: AI that knows a person's goals and helps them reach them [S4]. He set this against a view he saw elsewhere in the industry, where a central AI automates valuable work and people depend on its output [S4]. He predicted glasses that see and hear what you do would become people's main computers [S4].

How he applied it: the letter said Meta would share the benefits widely but be careful about what it open-sources, because of new safety risks [S4].

### Build safety in early, after learning the hard way

His 2021 letter said privacy and safety must be built into the metaverse from the first day [S2]. He wrote that building products people love is not enough, and that the company had learned from its struggles with hard social issues [S2].

How he applied it: the letter called for open standards, interoperability and new forms of governance for the next platform [S2].

## How he decides

- Ship a small version, test it, learn and repeat [S1].
- Settle arguments with a prototype [S1].
- Focus on the problems with the biggest impact [S1].
- Treat taking no risks as the biggest risk [S1].
- Bet early on the next platform, and own it if you can [S2][S3].
- Open up what helps your business grow and isn't what you sell [S3].
- Make money to fund the mission, not the other way round [S1].

## Where he stood

**What is a company for?** In 2012, to serve a social mission, with money as the fuel [S1].

**How fast should you move?** Fast. Done is better than perfect [S1].

**What comes after mobile?** Immersive computing, then AI through glasses, as of 2021 and 2025 [S2][S4].

**Open or closed AI?** Open, as of 2024, with more care about what to open by 2025 [S3][S4].

**Who should AI serve?** Individuals, helping each person reach their own goals [S4].

## His work and record

- Born 14 May 1984 in White Plains, New York [S5].
- 2002: entered Harvard to study psychology and computer science [S5].
- 4 February 2004: launched Thefacebook. Dropped out and moved to Palo Alto that year [S5].
- 2008: settled the Winklevoss and Narendra lawsuit for shares and $20 million in cash [S5].
- 2010: signed the Giving Pledge [S5].
- 2012: bought Instagram and took Facebook public [S5][S6].
- 2014: bought WhatsApp and Oculus [S6].
- 2015: pledged 99% of his Facebook shares to the Chan Zuckerberg Initiative [S5].
- 2018: testified to the U.S. Senate on the Cambridge Analytica scandal [S5].
- 2021: renamed the company Meta [S2][S6].
- 2022 and 2023: Meta cut about 11,000 jobs, then a further 10,000 [S6].
- July 2023: launched Threads. Released Llama 2 for commercial use [S6].
- 2024 and 2025: wrote the open source AI and personal superintelligence letters [S3][S4].
- 2025: Meta's revenue reached about $201 billion [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Privacy.** He testified to the U.S. Senate in 2018 over the Cambridge Analytica scandal, a major row over users' privacy [S5].
- **Misinformation.** In 2021 he testified to Congress on misinformation and hate speech [S5]. Whistleblower leaks that year added to the pressure [S6].
- **Teen safety.** In January 2024 he apologised to families at a Senate hearing on child safety [S5]. Court papers allege he overrode proposals to improve teen mental health, and Meta has faced verdicts and large settlements on teen harm [S5][S6].
- **Fast and loose early.** The founding years brought lawsuits over who deserved credit, settled in 2008 [S5]. His Internet.org project was shut down in India in 2016 [S5].
- **Big bets.** Meta's metaverse bet set goals for a decade, and the company later cut tens of thousands of jobs [S2][S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- "Move fast" can break things that matter, like trust and safety. Ask who could be hurt before you ship [S5].
- Optimising what can be tested can miss slow harms, such as effects on young people [S6].
- Huge platform bets need huge resources. Most firms can't wait a decade for a payoff [S2].
- His view of openness is also a strategy. Ask who gains from each choice [S3].
