# Marty Cagan: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Marty Cagan is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from articles he has published on the Silicon Valley Product Group (SVPG) website between 2007 and 2026, an SVPG overview of the product operating model, a 2024 interview write-up, and his public bio. Treat each view as "as of" the article it comes from. His birth year is not in our sources, so we show the years he has been active. We leave out his private life, and nothing here speaks for him on later events.

## In one paragraph

Cagan is a software product leader turned writer and coach [S1]. He spent about ten years as a developer at Hewlett-Packard Laboratories, then worked at Netscape for co-founder Marc Andreessen, and later ran product and design at eBay as a senior vice president [S1]. He founded SVPG to help others build products through writing, speaking, advising and coaching [S1]. His books include INSPIRED, EMPOWERED (with Chris Jones) and TRANSFORMED [S9]. His core message is steady across two decades. Most product ideas fail, so teams should learn fast and cheaply before they build [S2]. They should face four big risks early: value, usability, feasibility and business viability [S3]. And companies should give teams problems to solve and judge them on results, not hand them lists of features to ship [S5]. As of 2026, he argues this matters even more with AI [S8].

## Core ideas

### Product discovery comes before building

In a 2007 article he set out the idea of product discovery. Before you build, answer two questions. Is there a real market that wants this? And can you find a solution that is useful, usable and possible to build [S2]? He chose the word "discovery" over "requirements" because it admits how uncertain the work is [S2].

How he applied it: he described teams that start with an assessment of the opportunity, talk to real users, build a prototype with a designer, test it and change course based on what they learn [S2]. He said prototyping is pointless if the results don't change the plan [S2].

### Most ideas won't work, so test them cheaply

Cagan wrote that most software releases fail to meet their goals [S2]. His fix is speed and low cost in testing. He criticised companies that use a whole engineering team over several releases to build what is really a very costly prototype, and that test on live customers without telling them [S2]. Instead, one person making prototypes for a few weeks can often learn more [S2].

How he applied it: he suggested engineers join discovery early, so they can prepare the groundwork while the team searches for a solution that works, and hiring can wait [S2].

### Face the four big risks early

In 2017 he named four risks every product idea carries [S3]. Value risk asks whether customers will buy it or users will choose it. Usability risk asks whether people can figure out how to use it. Feasibility risk asks whether engineers can build it with the time, skills and technology at hand. Business viability risk asks whether it works for the rest of the business, such as sales, legal, costs and brand [S3].

How he applied it: he gave each risk an owner. The product manager owns value and viability, the designer owns usability, and the lead engineer owns feasibility [S3]. Strong teams deal with these risks before they build, not after [S3].

### Problems to solve, not features to ship

In 2019 he split teams into three kinds [S5]. Delivery teams mostly work through a backlog, which he called waterfall in new packaging. Feature teams get a ranked list of features with dates, known as a roadmap, and are judged on what they ship [S5]. Empowered product teams get problems to solve and are judged on outcomes. Their job is to solve problems in ways customers love and the business can use [S5].

How he applied it: he argued that with feature teams, the executive who asked for a feature carries the risk of whether it works. With empowered teams, the team carries it, and the product manager owns value and viability [S5].

### Outcomes over output, and over predictability

Shipping on time is not the same as succeeding. SVPG's account of the product model puts measurable results ahead of shipping work [S6]. In an October 2026 article, Cagan wrote that he learned early that just building and shipping was no longer enough [S8]. In a talk the same year, he said that promising a predictable roadmap can get in the way of learning, and that clear thinking and outcomes matter even more now that AI is here [S8].

How he applied it: the model asks teams to release small changes often, ideally at least every two weeks, so they can learn and prove value quickly [S6].

### Missionaries, not mercenaries

In 2015 he wrote that strong teams act like missionaries. They care about customers, know the business and feel ownership [S4]. Weak teams act like mercenaries, carrying out orders with no real sense of power or responsibility [S4]. He blamed three causes: leaders who dictate solutions and dodge the results, staffing built on outsourced or disengaged people, and heavy processes that reward compliance [S4].

How he applied it: his cure was to educate leaders, build lasting cross-functional teams, raise the bar on staff, and set a clear product vision with business outcomes [S4].

### The product operating model

SVPG describes the product operating model as a set of first principles that the best product companies follow [S6]. It covers how to build (small, frequent releases), how to solve problems (teams decide how, inside limits), and how to decide which problems to solve (leaders set a vision and a strategy) [S6]. Strategy is the plan to reach the vision, built from data and insight and set roughly each quarter [S6].

How he applied it: the model gives each role a clear job. Product managers own value and viability, designers own the experience, tech leads own delivery, and product leaders coach and give context [S6]. His book TRANSFORMED is about how a company moves to this model [S9][S10].

### Product management theater

In January 2024 he wrote that many people with product titles are really coordinators or backlog administrators [S7]. He called this product management theater: doing project management while calling yourself a product manager [S7]. He expected generative AI to speed up a reckoning for these roles [S7].

How he applied it: he advised people at risk to assess their skills honestly and build knowledge of the business, the data and the customer [S7]. He said certificates show training in a process, not the product skills that matter [S7].

## How he decides

- Assume most ideas will fail, and test before you build [S2].
- Check value, usability, feasibility and viability early [S3].
- Use the cheapest test that will teach you something [S2].
- Give teams problems and outcomes, not feature lists [S5].
- Judge results, not output or dates [S5][S6].
- Ship small changes often [S6].
- Let product, design and engineering share the work from the start [S2][S6].
- Earn trust from executives by delivering outcomes [S10].

## Where he stood

**Are roadmaps of features useful?** He sees them as the mark of feature teams. He prefers problems and outcomes, and warned in 2026 that chasing predictability can block learning [S5][S8].

**Who decides what to build?** Leaders choose which problems matter. Teams find the solutions [S6].

**Is the product manager a project manager?** No. As of 2024, he said that version of the job is theater [S7].

**Is his model too idealistic?** He hears that often. His answer, as of 2024, is that it comes from first-hand work with strong teams, and that many critics have never seen product done well [S10][S11].

**What does AI change?** As of 2024 to 2026, he says it raises the stakes on real product skill and on outcomes [S7][S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Too idealistic.** The most common criticism is that his ideas don't fit most companies. One writer suggests treating empowered teams as an almost impossible goal that still shows a direction [S11].
- **Aspirational.** An interviewer called the model aspirational, noting most firms are far from it [S10].
- **Hard on critics.** He has said many critics never saw strong product work, which some may read as brushing off real limits [S10].
- **Harsh on job titles.** His "theater" article was blunt about people in product roles, and even said some layoffs are unavoidable [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- His examples come from tech companies with strong engineers. A small business without them may need simpler steps.
- Empowered teams need trusting leaders. Where leaders won't let go, his advice can leave a team stuck [S10].
- He says less about sales-led or service businesses, or about marketing. Pair him with a marketer and a seller.
- Testing everything takes time. Some choices need a fast call, not a study.
- Pair him with someone who knows the founder's view, such as Jeff Bezos, or with a lean start-up thinker.
