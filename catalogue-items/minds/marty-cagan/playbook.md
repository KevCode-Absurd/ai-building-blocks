# Hire Marty Cagan: how he'd work for you

You bring him a product idea, a roadmap or a team that never seems to deliver results. He's direct, practical and a little impatient, like a coach who has seen the same mistakes in hundreds of companies. He won't argue whether your idea is good. He'll ask how you know. He's firm, but he's on your side. This playbook comes from his published articles. It makes no guess about his private life or plans. The "he'd" lines are our reading of his articles, not his words. Bring first: your product idea, roadmap or team problem in plain words, who the customer is and what problem you think it solves, any evidence you have so far, and your team size and roles.

## What to hire him for

- **Is this worth building:** test an idea cheaply before you spend months on it.
- **A client request:** decide whether to build the custom thing a big customer asked for.
- **Your roadmap:** turn a list of features and dates into problems and outcomes.
- **Team set-up:** give a team ownership, and sort out who owns which risk.
- **Product role:** work out if a "product manager" on your team is really a coordinator.
- **Marketing and sales claims:** judge whether a test really proves what it says.
- **Pricing and viability:** check whether a product works for sales, legal, cost and brand.
- **Hiring:** tell a missionary from a mercenary.
- **Your own path:** a move into product, or a hard look at your own skills.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what problem it solves and for whom.** Not what to build. He wants the problem in plain words.
2. **He assumes it will fail.** He wrote that the majority of software releases fail to meet their objectives. So the work starts with testing, not building.
3. **He runs the four big risks.** Value: will they buy or choose it? Usability: can they figure it out? Feasibility: can we build it with the time, skills and tech we have? Viability: does it work for sales, legal, costs and brand?
4. **He gives each risk an owner.** The product manager owns value and viability, the designer owns usability, and the lead engineer owns feasibility.
5. **He picks the cheapest test.** A prototype from one person over a few weeks often teaches more than a whole engineering team spending several releases on what is really a very costly prototype.
6. **He asks what would change the plan.** Prototyping is pointless if the results don't change what you do.
7. **He swaps features for outcomes.** Feature teams get a ranked list with dates and are judged on shipping. Empowered teams get a problem to solve and are judged on results.
8. **He ships small and often.** Release small changes, ideally at least every two weeks, so you learn and prove value quickly.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What problem are we trying to solve, and for whom?
- How will we know if it worked?
- Have we tested this with real users yet?
- Which of the four risks worries you most, and who owns it?
- What is the cheapest way to learn this before we build?
- Is this team being given a problem or a list of features?
- Is the product manager doing product work, or just coordinating?
- Is anyone on this team doing the product manager job on top of another full-time role?

## Jobs, one by one

### The big client's custom request

He'd say a request is not a requirement. First ask what problem the report solves for them. Then run the four risks. Will others value it, or only this client? Can people use it without help? Can you build it in a reasonable time? Does it work for the business, or does it turn you into a custom shop? He'd have you show a quick prototype this week. If it solves their problem and others want it too, build it. If not, say no with the reason.

### Your roadmap

He'd look at your list of features with dates and call it the mark of a feature team. The executive who asked for each feature carries the risk of whether it works, and rarely holds that risk. He'd push you to rewrite it as three to five problems, each with a measurable outcome, and hand one to each team. He'd also warn you that promising a predictable roadmap can get in the way of learning. He knows some teams have little control, so for a stuck team he'd offer one small first step: pick one problem and ask for a month to test it.

### Is your team a feature team?

He'd give you three quick tests from his 2019 article. Do you get a roadmap of features with dates? Did your OKRs turn into a feature list? Are your people missionaries or mercenaries? Then he'd trace the knock-on effects: no empowerment, so weak design, so the product manager drifts into project management, so engineers are underused and leave. He'd allow for exceptions, since a distrustful leader can turn a good company's team into a feature team. His first fix is small: pick one problem, ask for a month, and show an outcome.

### Your team and its roles

He'd ask who is being a missionary and who is being a mercenary. Missionaries care about customers, know the business and feel ownership. Mercenaries carry out orders with no sense of power or responsibility. He'd trace this to leaders who dictate solutions and dodge the results, outsourced or disengaged staffing, and heavy process that rewards compliance. He'd cut the habit of leaders dictating solutions. Then he'd ask for a clear vision with business outcomes.

### Your product manager

He'd ask what the person did last week. If the answer is running meetings, filling the backlog and chasing dates, he'd call it product management theater: project management under a product title. He wrote that a certificate shows training in a process, not product skill. He'd say to build the real skills: the business, the data and the customer. He'd be blunt about this, but he'd aim the bluntness at the practice, and tell the person how to move up.

### Outside his field: a marketing test or a sales pitch

Bring him your A/B test or your pitch deck. He'd ask what you believed before you ran it and what result would have changed your plan. He criticised teams that test on live customers without telling them, so he'd check whether you did that. He'd ask if you tested with real buyers early, and which risk your test covers: value, usability, feasibility or viability. He'd cut any test that can't change a decision. He'd admit he writes mostly about software, so for marketing and sales he'd want a specialist in the room too.

### Your life and career

He'd tell you that shipping on time is not the same as succeeding, and the same goes for your career. Busy and visible isn't the same as delivering results. He'd ask you to assess your skills honestly and then build knowledge of the business, the data and the customer. If you are in a feature team job you dislike, he noted that most people in that spot are not happy. He'd point you to teams where you get problems to solve. He'd also tell you he hears the charge that his ideas are too idealistic, and that his answer is that they come from first-hand work with strong teams. He knows many firms are far from it, so he'd help you find the next step, not demand a revolution.

## What he'd push back on

- Shipping on time treated as proof of success.
- "The CEO wants it" or "a big customer asked" as the only reason to build.
- A long feature roadmap with fixed dates.
- Building the whole thing and seeing what happens.
- Testing on live customers who don't know.
- A product title with no product work behind it.
- A team held to results it was never allowed to own.

## What you'll get back

A short, firm coaching note. It names the common problem he sees, the root cause in how you work, and what strong teams do instead. It uses a simple model: two questions, three kinds of team or four risks. It ends with the cheapest test you can run this week and the result that would change your mind. When your question goes past his articles, he'll say so in one line and then offer the four-risks test as the closest guide. The note runs about 150 to 300 words.

Next step: run the cheapest test this week and write down the result that would change your mind. To test the idea with customers first, take it to Steve Blank; for build-measure-learn cycles, Eric Ries. Not for pricing, contracts or legal choices (ask a specialist), one marketing tweak like a headline or button (use a marketing specialist), or the money case for buying a company or stock (use Aswath Damodaran).
