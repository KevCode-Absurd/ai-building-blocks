# Marty Neumeier: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Marty Neumeier is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his 2003 Brand Gap slide deck, a 2007 interview, essays and book pages on his own website, one designer's write-up of his "onliness" exercise, and a reference page. Most of the essays on his site carry no date. We give the year where the source shows one, and otherwise treat each view as "as of" the mid-2010s, when most of the books and essays appeared. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after that.

## In one paragraph

Neumeier is a designer and writer who started in advertising and package design, moved to Silicon Valley in 1984, and helped firms such as Apple, Adobe and HP with their brands [S12][S7]. He then turned to short, picture-heavy books that teach branding without the heavy reading [S1][S7]. *The Brand Gap* (2003) defines a brand as a person's gut feeling about a product, service or organization, and says great brands come from joining business logic with creative magic [S1]. *Zag* (2006) argues that being a bit different is no longer enough, and that brands must be radically different to matter [S2][S8]. Later books carried the same habits into design thinking, innovation, the skills people need as machines take over routine work, and the power customers now hold over brands [S5][S9][S10].

## Core ideas

### A brand is a gut feeling, and the customer owns it

In *The Brand Gap* slides, Neumeier says a brand is not a logo, an identity system or a product [S1]. It is a feeling inside one person's head about a product, service or company. Because each person forms their own feeling, brands are defined by individuals, not by companies or markets [S1]. His home page puts it more bluntly: your brand is what customers say it is, not what you say it is [S7].

How he applied it: he used this idea to argue that every business with customers already has a brand, whether it manages it or not, and that a badly managed one can turn against the company [S6].

### Close the gap between logic and magic

The "gap" in the book's title is the space between strategic, analytical thinkers and creative, intuitive ones [S1]. Strategy without design is dull. Design without strategy drifts. When both sides work together, he says, the result can be a charismatic brand [S1].

How he applied it: in *The Designful Company* (2008) he went further and argued that design thinking should be a main tool for running a business, offering 16 "levers of change" to build a culture of nonstop innovation [S9].

### Be radically different

*Zag* argues that keeping up with rivals no longer wins [S8]. Crowded markets and fast copying mean a brand that looks like the others doesn't matter to anyone [S2]. In a 2007 interview he said most people underestimate how different they must be, because insiders think they are more distinct than outsiders see [S2]. The aim is to be different and good at the same time, which opens new space that lasts until others copy it [S2].

How he applied it: he pointed to the Prius, which looked odd but saved fuel, and the Aeron chair, which seemed strange at launch but at one point brought in about 30 percent of its maker's revenue [S2]. Each one puzzled people at first and still felt valuable.

To find a zag, he told the interviewer to list rivals the way customers see them, put them on a grid against the factors that matter, and rate each one [S2]. The gaps show where nobody is strong [S2]. Then ask what rivals don't do, do badly or haven't thought of, and whether doing the opposite could start a new category [S2]. Combine the gaps with what you love and how much risk you can take, and stop fighting where the rivals are strongest [S2].

How he applied it: he advised testing a risky idea small and cheap first, even under a separate name, so the main brand is safe if the test fails [S2].

### Say what only you do, and keep focus

Neumeier's "onliness" exercise asks a brand to finish one sentence about what it is the only one of. Designer Dan Mall described the template's six prompts: what, how, who, where, why and when [S11]. To test it, swap in a rival's name. If the sentence still fits, it isn't distinct yet [S11]. *The Brand Gap* gives a simpler focus test with three questions: who are you, what do you do, and why does it matter [S1]. People notice only what is different, and the brain filters out what is familiar, so brand extensions that blur the focus do harm [S1].

How he applied it: Mall's worked example, built on Neumeier's format, describes Harley-Davidson as the only maker of big, loud motorbikes for a certain kind of rider [S11].

### Trust comes from reliability plus delight

The Brand Gap deck gives trust as a short formula: reliability plus delight [S1]. You earn trust by meeting, then beating, what customers expect [S1]. A charismatic brand is one for which people believe there is no substitute, and he argued any brand can become one [S1].

How he applied it: he described marketing as building tribes. People join different tribes for different parts of their lives, and a brand can be the one they pick for driving or for reading [S1].

### Customers now hold the power

*The Brand Flip* (2015) argues that social media has moved control of a company's future toward its customers [S10]. Instead of selling products to people, a brand can become a small movement that customers help build and keep alive [S10]. The book offers two tools, a Brand Commitment Matrix and a Brand Commitment Scale [S10].

How he applied it: he framed it as a reversal of the old order and wrote the book for strategists, designers, founders and marketing heads [S10].

### Small can beat big

In "Scissors Paper Rock" he compares companies to the children's game [S3]. Small, focused "scissors" firms take slices of business from giants too slow to respond. Mid-sized "rock" firms win on momentum. Huge "paper" firms win with networks and resources [S3]. Companies tend to grow from scissors to rock to paper, but compete the other way round, so each size has a rival it can beat [S3].

How he applied it: he used eBay as the scissors example, a focused company that thrived for most of a decade, and General Electric and Mitsubishi as paper firms pushing the limits of size [S3].

### Sell a new idea with a story and a picture

His essay on buy-in borrows from the geneticist J. B. S. Haldane four stages of reaction to new ideas (worthless nonsense, interesting but perverse, true but unimportant, and "I always said so"): this is his scale for how colleagues take a game-changing idea [S4]. He read strong early resistance as a sign of a strong idea [S4]. To speed people through, tell a simple story, show a vision instead of a plan, and protect the idea with rough metrics and a senior sponsor [S4].

How he applied it: he contrasted a money target, like a revenue goal in five years, with Microsoft's early picture of a computer on every desk and in every home, which people could see in their heads [S4].

### Build the skills machines can't copy

"The Robot Curve" describes work sliding from creative, to skilled, to rote, to automated as it gets standard [S5]. The safest jobs stay near the creative top. He named five "metaskills" to get there: feeling, seeing, dreaming, making and learning [S5]. He argued that schools built on tests and memory don't teach these, and called for hands-on, project-based learning [S5].

How he applied it: he made this the subject of his 2012 book *Metaskills* [S12][S5].

## How he decides

- Start with how the customer feels, not what the company says [S1][S7].
- Ask the three focus questions before anything else [S1].
- Map the rivals and look for open space [S2].
- Aim for different and good, not just different [S2].
- Test risky ideas small, cheap and early [S2].
- Treat strong pushback on an idea as a possible good sign [S4].
- Show a picture of the future, not a list of facts [S4].
- Keep books and decks short [S7].

## Real decisions

### Putting a radio station's zag in a separate brand (2007)

Asked how a station should differentiate, he advised building a separate online brand that could grow or be dropped without harming the main station. [S2] His stated reason: the strongest signal is a prototype people find unusual yet valuable, and small, cheap tests keep the risk low. [S2]

### Teaching "onliness" with a swap test

His statement uses six prompts (what, how, who, where, why, when). [S11] Its test is to put a rival's name in the sentence: if it still fits, the statement is not distinct, and if it turns awkward or false, it is strong. [S11] In a group, people fill in worksheets alone, then merge in pairs, fours and eights until two or three statements are left. [S11]

### Writing short, picture-heavy books

He chose books of about 150 pages built like slide decks, with a bold line and a small picture on each spread, over long dense ones. [S7][S1] His reason was that busy leaders would read and use them. [S7]

### Founding Level C (c. 2019)

He had predicted in *The Brand Gap* that branding would matter as much to business as the spreadsheet, and by about 2019 felt it had. [S6] Because books and talks could not reach enough executives, and online courses could not build a real community, he and Andy Starr started in-person, two-day masterclasses with five certification levels. [S6]

### Answering automation with metaskills

Watching routine work slide down the Robot Curve toward machines, he decided to teach people the skills that stay at the top: feeling, seeing, dreaming, making and learning. [S5] He also argued for project-based schooling over test prep. [S5]

### Naming the failure of Critique

He starts from the plain fact that his magazine *Critique* ran about five years and failed, and says so. [S7][S12]

## Where he stood

**What is a brand?** A gut feeling in the customer's head. The logo is only one part [S1].

**How different should a brand be?** Radically. Most firms are less distinct than they think [S2].

**Who controls a brand?** More and more, the customers, through what they say to each other [S10][S7].

**Can a small firm beat a big one?** Yes, with sharp focus, because giants are slow to respond to small attacks [S3].

**Is branding a real profession?** Yes. By about 2019 he described it as a core business function, and co-founded a five-level training program, Level C, to certify brand people [S6].

## His work and record

- Born October 10, 1947 [S12].
- Studied at Art Center College of Design from 1967 to 1969 [S12].
- Moved to Silicon Valley in 1984. Clients included Apple, Adobe, HP, Microsoft and Symantec [S12][S7].
- Founded *Critique*, a magazine about design thinking, in 1996. He says it ran five years before it failed [S7][S12].
- Started Neutron, a branding consultancy, in 2003. It merged with Liquid Agency in 2009 [S12].
- Books: *The Brand Gap* (2003), *Zag* (2006), *The Designful Company* (2008), *Metaskills* (2012), *The 46 Rules of Genius* (2014), *The Brand Flip* (2015), *Brand A-Z* (2017), and later *Scramble* [S12][S7].
- *Zag* is listed among *The 100 Best Business Books of All Time* [S12][S8].
- Served on the AIGA board and led its Center for Brand Experience [S12].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Little formal criticism on record.** The reference page we checked has no criticism section, and its cited reviews are mostly positive [S12]. Treat the points below as limits rather than settled faults.
- **Dated examples.** *The Brand Gap* came out in 2003, and its tribe examples and brand cases are from that era [S1]. The principles may hold up better than the examples.
- **Short by design.** He says he keeps books to about 150 pages rather than 500 [S7]. That makes them fast to read, but it leaves out detail, research and counter-cases.
- **A failed venture.** He has been open that *Critique* magazine failed after five years [S7].
- **Easy to misread.** "The brand is what they say it is" can be heard as giving up control. His own work answers that with focus, trust and design, but users should keep both halves [S1][S10].

## Blind spots to watch

- He writes for brand teams and leaders. A one-person shop may not need matrices, scales and certification levels [S6][S10].
- Radical difference is risky. His own advice to test small is easy to skip in the excitement [S2].
- He says little about price, sales or operations. Pair him with a sales or numbers voice.
- "Different and good" is hard to judge from the inside. Get outside eyes, as he warns [S2].
- His examples lean toward big, famous brands. Check that a lesson fits your size.
