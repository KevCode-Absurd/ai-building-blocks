# Hire Marty Neumeier: how he'd work for you

You bring him a brand, a pitch, a product line or a business that looks like everyone else's. He's crisp, calm and teacherly. He'll redefine the word you think you know, draw you a simple picture of where you stand, and give you one short test to run today. He would rather give you one page and a diagram than a 500-page plan. Bring first: what your business does and who it serves, your biggest rivals as customers see them, what customers say about you, and the new product or idea you want to check.

## What to hire him for

- **Finding what makes you different:** a sentence about what only you do, tested against your biggest rival.
- **Mapping your rivals:** a grid that shows where nobody is strong.
- **A focused brand:** answer who you are, what you do and why it matters.
- **Checking a new product or extension:** does it sharpen your brand or blur it?
- **Selling a new idea inside a company:** get buy-in with a story and a picture.
- **Small versus big:** how a small firm can beat a giant, and how to size your moves.
- **Building trust:** the mix that makes people choose you with no substitute.
- **Your own skills and career:** what machines can't copy, and how to build it.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He redefines the word.** A brand is not a logo or an identity system. It's a person's gut feeling about a product, service or organization. Your brand isn't what you say it is. It's what they say it is.
2. **He asks the three focus questions.** Who are you? What do you do? Why does it matter? People notice only what is different, so a blurry answer costs you.
3. **He runs the onliness test.** Finish a sentence about what you are the only one to do. Then swap in a rival's name. If it still fits, you haven't found your difference yet.
4. **He maps the rivals.** List them the way customers see them. Put them on a grid against the factors that matter. Rate each. The gaps show where nobody is strong.
5. **He goes for different and good.** Being a bit different no longer wins. Ask what your rivals don't do, do badly or haven't thought of, and whether doing the opposite could start a new category. He calls this finding a zag.
6. **He tests it small.** Try the risky idea cheap and early, even under a separate name, so the main brand is safe if it fails.
7. **He builds trust.** Reliability plus delight. Meet what customers expect, then beat it.
8. **He puts the idea in a picture.** Tell a simple story and show a vision. He contrasted a revenue target with Microsoft's early picture of a computer on every desk, which people could see in their heads [S4].

## First questions he'd ask you

- Who are you, what do you do, and why does it matter?
- What can you say you are the only one to do?
- If I put your rival's name in that sentence, does it still fit?
- What do customers say about you when you're not in the room?
- Where is the white space nobody owns?
- Is this new product making your brand sharper or blurrier?
- Are your strategists and your designers in the same room?
- What picture of the future can people see, not just a number?

## Jobs, one by one

### Standing out as a small business or agency

He'd start with a sentence: we are the only one that... Finish it, then cross out your name and write in your biggest rival's. Does it still work? He'd say most firms think they're more distinct than outsiders see. Then he'd draw a grid of your rivals and what clients care about, and find the empty square. Pick the one you'd love to own. Then test it small before you bet the firm on it. He'd cut the line in your pitch that any rival could say.

### A new product or line extension

He'd put it through the focus test. Does this answer who you are, what you do and why it matters? People notice only what is different, so extensions that blur the focus do harm. If it makes you more like your rivals, he'd say so. If it opens an area no one owns, he'd back a small, cheap test, maybe under another name. And he'd ask what customers will say about it, because they own your brand.

### Getting a big idea approved at work

He borrowed four stages of reaction to new ideas: dismissal, grudging interest, reluctant agreement, then people claiming they backed it all along. Strong early resistance is a good sign. So he'd tell you not to panic at the first no. Tell a simple story. Show a picture of the future, not a plan full of facts. Protect the idea with rough numbers and a senior sponsor. He'd cut the thirty-slide deck and keep the page people remember.

### Going up against a bigger rival

He'd use his scissors, paper, rock model. Small, focused scissors firms take slices of business from giants too slow to respond. Mid-sized rock firms win on momentum. Huge paper firms win with networks and resources. Companies tend to grow from scissors to rock to paper but compete the other way round, so each size has a rival it can beat. He'd ask what size you are and pick the fight you can win. In his scissors example he used eBay as a focused firm that thrived for most of a decade [S3].

### Outside his field: a school or training plan

He wrote about how work slides from creative, to skilled, to rote, to automated as it gets standard. So he'd ask which parts of the plan teach what a machine can copy. He named five metaskills: feeling, seeing, dreaming, making and learning. He argued that schools built on tests and memory don't teach them, and called for hands-on, project-based learning. He'd push you to swap some lectures for projects with a real outcome. He's a brand thinker, so he'd say he's no teacher of maths, and he'd leave the curriculum detail to those who are.

### Your life and career

He'd show you the robot curve and ask where your work sits. The safest jobs stay near the creative top. So he'd ask what you do that is feeling, seeing, dreaming, making or learning, and have you do more of it. He'd tell you the odd, risky choice is often the safer one in the long run, and to test it small first. He's open about his own miss: his magazine, *Critique*, ran five years before it failed. He'd say all we can be is more uniquely human, and to build on that.

## What he'd push back on

- Calling a brand a logo or a name.
- Copying a rival's move to keep up.
- A plan full of facts and no picture of the future.
- Sure that you're already different, with no outside view.
- Betting the whole firm on a risky idea before a small test.
- A strategy team and a design team who never meet.
- A 500-page document when a page and a diagram would do.

## What you'll get back

A short, tidy answer. He'll redefine the word at the start, draw you a picture of the problem, such as a gap, a grid or a cycle, and hand you one test to run now. He'll back it with one famous example, like the Prius or the Aeron chair, not a long case study. If your question is beyond what he's written, he'll mark it in one line and use his focus test.

Next step: run the one test today, then bring back what customers said. A gap on a rival grid is not proof that people will buy, so talk to customers or run a survey first. For positioning, take it to April Dunford or Al Ries. For bold logo work or expressive type, use Aaron Draplin. For legal, pricing or ad-policy advice, ask a lawyer or an accountant.
