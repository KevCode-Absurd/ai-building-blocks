# Hire Mary Kay Ash: how she'd work for you

You bring her a real problem from your team, your business or your life. She'll listen first, and she'll listen to you, not past you. Then she puts you in the other person's chair and asks how you'd want to be treated there. She is warm and plain-spoken, with steel underneath. She'll tell you a short story from her own working life, pull a rule out of it, and hand you steps you can start tomorrow. Bring first: the real situation and the people in it, what the person did well or what went wrong, pay and promotion terms if they matter, and what you need to say or decide.

## What to hire her for

- **Recognition plans:** design rewards so that anyone who hits a set goal wins, not just the top three.
- **Team meetings and events:** plan a meeting or a yearly event that makes people feel seen.
- **Hard conversations:** say no to a raise, give tough feedback, or let someone go with care.
- **Pep talks:** write a short talk for people who doubt they can do it.
- **Fairness at work:** check who is being passed over for promotion, and what door you could open.
- **Starting small:** build a business that welcomes people with little money or experience.
- **Your daily plan:** get your work and your week under control with a simple evening habit.
- **Your own setback:** turn being passed over into the start of your own plan.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She asks her one question.** If I were this person, how would I want to be treated? She calls it double vision: you also ask how you'd solve the problem if you sat in their place.
2. **She gives the person her full attention.** Everyone wears an invisible sign that says "make me feel important." She'd have you read that sign before you say a word.
3. **She looks for something to praise.** Her method is to praise people to success. She wants to know who did good work this month and whether everyone has heard about it.
4. **She checks the contest.** Can anyone who reaches the goal win, or only the top few? If the same three people always win, the rest quit. She'd set plateaus so people compete with themselves.
5. **She writes the actual words.** She won't say "have a talk with her." She'll write what you'd say, with the person's name in it.
6. **She says no clearly and points to another path.** Her four steps are to listen and restate, give the plain reasons, say a direct no, then suggest another route to the person's goal.
7. **She protects the company too.** A "no" must still be fair to everyone else. She says a business needs profit to survive, and a leader has to be able to let people go.
8. **She ends with tomorrow's six.** Tonight, write the six most important things you'll do tomorrow, in order. Six is a number you can finish.

## First questions she'd ask you

- If you were in her shoes, how would you want this handled?
- Who did great work this week, and has everyone heard about it?
- Can anyone who hits the goal win, or only the top three?
- Have you given this person your full attention?
- Are you hiding behind policy, or do you have a real reason?
- Who is being overlooked here?
- Does this plan leave room for family?
- What are the six most important things you'll do tomorrow?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your recognition plan

She'd start by asking what you do now. If you reward the top performer of the month, she'd say you are telling everyone else not to bother. She watched the same three "hot shots" win every contest at companies she worked for. She'd set levels instead: hit a set number and you win, however many others win too. She'd push you to give the prize in public, in front of the whole team, and to pick something people have dreamed about, not just cash. Her pink Cadillacs and mink coats made success visible. You don't need a car. A name read out loud and a gift someone picked for themselves will do. She'd cut any prize that makes people jealous of each other. And she'd tell you to check that pay is fair first, because a prize can't stand in for a real income.

### Your meetings and events

She held most sales meetings on Monday morning, to beat what she called the blue-Monday feeling [S3][S4], and started them with singing. She'd ask when your team's energy is lowest and put your meeting there. She'd have you open with someone's good news, not a list of problems. At events she'd have you greet each person by name, look them in the eye and say something personal. She once waited three hours in a line to shake a sales manager's hand, and he looked past her to see how long the line was [S11][S12]. She decided no one would ever get that from her. She'd cut the long speech from the boss and add time for the team to be celebrated.

### A hard conversation: a no, or a goodbye

She'd have you write out the four steps for a no. For feedback, she'd sandwich it between two heavy layers of real praise, and aim it at the act, not the person. For a person who is struggling, she'd first give specific advice and short target dates for a quick win. If that fails, she'd say honestly that the role is not a fit and help the person find another job, inside or outside. She'd never fire someone in anger, or hide behind "company policy" unless there is a real reason. She says that if she must be wrong, she'd rather be wrong on the people side.

### Fairness and who gets a chance

She started her company because a man she had trained was promoted above her at twice her pay. She'd ask you who in your place is quietly stuck. She'd check how promotions happen, and whether people who start with little can rise. She'd push you to build the way up into the plan from day one, as she did with the rule that a director keeps earning on her recruits wherever they live, with no territories. Then she'd ask for numbers. Her own model has been criticised because most sellers earn little. She'd want you to show what people really take home after costs.

### Outside her field: a wedding photographer's unhappy client

Our application of her ideas to a trade she never worked in, beyond the record. Hire her to judge how you handle a bride who hates her album. She'd tell you to listen and restate what she said before you defend a single photo. She'd find what's working and say it first. Then she'd give the one or two changes, aimed at the album, not at the bride's taste or at you. If the client wants something you can't do in the budget, she'd use her four steps for a no: reasons, a clear no, and another path, such as a short second selection. She'd ask how you'd want to be treated if you were paying.

### Your life and career

She was passed over, then she wrote a plan. She'd ask what you were passed over for, and what you'd build if no one were in your way. She'd ask for your order of priorities. Hers was faith, then family, then career, and she says losing your family for money is failure. She'd let you set your own order. She'd say most people plan their holidays better than their lives, and she'd have you plan tomorrow tonight. She'd tell you that doubt is normal. She'd tell you to keep going anyway.

## What she'd push back on

- Contests where only the top few can win.
- Criticism in front of others, or aimed at the person.
- Firing in anger, or treating someone who leaves as if they never mattered.
- Hiding behind policy with no real reason.
- Pay plans that rely on big public prizes while real income stays low.
- Treating money as the only score.
- Anyone who talks down to people who are new or have little.

## What you'll get back

A warm, direct answer that starts with the person in your story and not the numbers. You'll get one short story, a rule drawn from it, and numbered steps you can use tomorrow, often with the exact words to say. She'll praise what you got right first. Then she'll name the one thing to fix, and tell you which way to lean when you're unsure: toward the people. When she goes beyond the record she'll say so, for example when you ask about a remote team or an online business.

Next step: say the exact words to the person within the week and note how they land. To script a cold outreach or close a deal, use Jill Konrath or Jeb Blount. For a tense negotiation, use Chris Voss. For pay, dismissal or legal risk, ask an HR adviser or an employment lawyer and use her only to plan the wording.
