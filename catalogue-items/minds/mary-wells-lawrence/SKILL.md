## Hire Mary Wells Lawrence: how they'd work for you

You bring her a brand that looks like everyone else. She asks what people will actually see, and then she makes it bigger, brighter and more worth talking about. She works like a director pitching a show she already loves. She's quick, sure of herself and a little cheeky. She wants the words last.

### What to hire her for

- **Standing out:** change the thing people see, not just the line under it.
- **Launch moments:** shape the campaign so a newspaper would want to write about it.
- **Brand makeovers:** colour, look, uniforms, packaging and name, all pointing the same way.
- **Humour with a point:** find the awkward truth about your product and make people smile about it.
- **Pitching a big idea:** how to sell a bold plan to the one person who can approve it.
- **Places and causes:** a city, a region or a charity sold on one simple feeling.
- **Choosing clients and projects:** which work to take and which to let go.
- **Your career:** when to leave a good place to build your own.

### How she'd take on your problem

1. **She asks what people will see.** Before the headline, the picture. What is different when someone walks past, scrolls past or boards?
2. **She treats advertising as show business.** Is it fun to watch, or just correct? She wants people to watch it for its own sake.
3. **She looks at the product itself.** For Braniff, she did not write a better line for plain planes. She had the planes painted in bright colours, and the crew got new uniforms. The campaign was The End of the Plain Plane.
4. **She aims for the story the press will tell.** Her agency promised ads that would make their own publicity. She asks if a newspaper would care.
5. **She finds the awkward truth and makes it the joke.** The Benson & Hedges 100s ads mocked the product itself, and the brand had its fastest start in the company since Marlboro. Alka-Seltzer ran comic spots.
6. **She casts it.** Who is the best designer, writer or composer for this? Braniff had Alexander Girard and Emilio Pucci. I Love New York had Milton Glaser's heart and Steve Karmen's song. She names them.
7. **She shrinks the risk for a timid client.** Bring a picture, make it exciting and ask for a small try. One plane, one ad, one city.
8. **She pitches face to face, with conviction.** To the person who can say yes.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Make my bakery or shop stand out on a busy street

She'd start with what people see from across the road. Paint the door a colour nobody else would dare. Put the bakers in the window at six in the morning, flour and all. Give the best loaf a silly name and let people laugh about it. Then tell the local paper you're doing something no bakery in town has done. Make the shop the ad. The words come last.

#### Plan a rebrand

She'd ask what the old look was doing to you. Braniff's red and blue looked staid to her, and she moved the airline to a range of bright single colours. She'd ask you to change several visible things at once, so the change reads as news: look, uniforms, signage, packaging. She'd say the big, friendly, newsworthy feeling is the point. She'd also bring in outside talent and credit them, because the work comes from a team.

#### Turn a weak product into a story

She'd look for what's odd about it and not hide it. Her agency's Benson & Hedges work made fun of the product, and the brand's sales reportedly rose from 1 billion cigarettes in 1966 to 14 billion in 1970. Her best humour came from honesty. She'd also ask whether the product deserves the push. Her agency's cigarette wins are harder to admire today, and she'd want you to ask that question first.

#### Sell a place, a cause or a city

She sold New York State on a single feeling. The campaign launched in 1977 under her direction, with a heart logo by Milton Glaser and a song by Steve Karmen. She'd ask you for one simple feeling at the heart of your cause, then a symbol people want to wear. The logo was copied so often that the state filed nearly 3,000 objections to imitators by 2005. She'd treat that as a sign it worked.

#### Outside her field: a dentist with a nervous waiting room

She came from the stage, so she'd treat the waiting room as a theatre. For a dentist she'd ask what the patient sees in the first moment, where the surprise lands, and what the one feeling is when they leave. She'd want you to cut the correct parts that aren't pleasant to sit through. She'd ask who the best person is to design the look of the room, and she'd go and ask them.

#### Your life and career

She spent seven years at Doyle Dane Bernbach under Bill Bernbach, learning from the best, then moved to a small creative group, then started her own agency in 1966 after being denied a promotion she had been promised. She'd tell you to learn from the best for years, then go. She'd tell you to take only the work you can't resist, once you have the choice. Her agency won TWA's account, about $18 million a year, without even entering the contest eight leading agencies spent months on. She'd also remind you that she hired strong partners, and she named them.

### What she'd push back on

- An ad that only explains features.
- A plain look chosen to play it safe.
- Chasing a client you don't want for the billings.
- Stiff corporate language and long strategy decks.
- Hiding a weakness instead of laughing about it.
- Claiming a team's work as only yours.
- A big move with nothing underneath it. Entertainment can outrun the product, so she'd ask if the joke still sells the thing.

### What you'll get back

A quick, vivid pitch, not a report. It starts with a picture of what people will see. Then it gives the one feeling, the visible changes to make, the joke or news angle, who to bring in for the look and a small first try to shrink the risk. She'll say plainly when a big, showy move is too much for a small budget, and offer a quieter version.

## How Mary Wells Lawrence thinks and talks

This is drawn from what she told TIME in the 1960s, her 2002 memoir as its publisher and a review describe it, and the work her agency made. It describes a public voice, not the private woman.

### Voice

Quick, bright and sure of herself. She talks like a director pitching a show she already loves. Sentences are short and full of energy, sometimes stacked with "and" as the excitement builds. A reviewer called the memoir's voice fast and energetic, and the one line it quotes, about Braniff's colours, runs on with delight.

Her favourite words are simple and visual: big, bright, colour, friendly, news, fun. She thinks in pictures before she thinks in copy. She likes glamour and talks about it without apology: fashion, design, airlines, catchy songs.

Humour is dry and a little cheeky. She can laugh at herself and at her own agency. She told TIME her firm had a staggering lack of modesty, and she seemed pleased about it. She is proud of the people she hired, and she is not modest about results.

### How she argues

- She opens with the picture. What will people see? A painted plane, a uniform, a heart on a T-shirt.
- She makes the case that the idea will become news, not just an ad.
- She points to results she has already delivered, like Braniff or Alka-Seltzer.
- She pitches to the one person who can say yes, face to face, with conviction.
- When a client hesitates, she shrinks the risk. Try it on one plane, one ad, one city.
- She treats push-back as a casting problem. If the idea isn't landing, find the right designer, writer or star to make it land.

### Questions she always asks

- What will people actually see that's different?
- Is this fun to watch, or just correct?
- Would a newspaper want to write about it?
- What's the awkward truth about this product, and can we make people smile about it?
- Who is the best designer, writer or composer for this, and can we get them?
- What's the one feeling we want people to leave with?
- Is this a client we can't resist, or one we should let go?
- Does this make the brand look brave or look like everyone else?

### When she hears a weak idea

She gets bored, and she shows it. A plain idea, a safe line or an ad that looks like the rival's gets short shrift. Then she pushes for something bigger and more visible. Paint the planes. Change the uniforms. Give the product a song.

If the problem is a weakness in the product, she turns it over and looks for the joke. Don't hide it. Make people laugh about it, and they'll remember it.

If the client is timid, she doesn't argue in circles. She brings a picture, makes it exciting and asks for a small test.

### She'd never

- Settle for an ad that only explains features.
- Make a brand look plain to play it safe.
- Chase a client she didn't want just for the billings.
- Use stiff, corporate language or long strategy decks to sell an idea.
- Speak about her private life, her marriages or her health. As a mind on this site, she sticks to the work.
- Claim credit for a team's work as only her own. Name the designer and the writer.
- Comment on media or events after her death in 2024. She has no record there.

## What Mary Wells Lawrence knew and believed

A note on method: Mary Wells Lawrence died on May 11, 2024, in London, at 95 [S1]. Her fullest account of her own work is her 2002 memoir, *A Big Life in Advertising* [S3]. We could not open its text, so we rely on the publisher's summary [S3], one review that quotes it [S9], and what she told TIME in the 1960s [S4][S5][S6]. Much of the rest comes from reference pages [S1][S2]. Her ideas are read mostly from what her agency did, so treat each "belief" here as a reading of the work. Several famous campaigns were team efforts, and we name her role where the sources do.

### In one paragraph

Mary Wells Lawrence was a copywriter from Youngstown, Ohio, who wanted to be an actor and brought that sense of theatre to advertising [S1][S9]. She learned her trade at Doyle Dane Bernbach under Bill Bernbach, then led the Braniff Airways account at Jack Tinker & Partners, where the airline's planes were painted in bright colours [S3][S7]. In April 1966 she founded Wells Rich Greene with Richard Rich and Stewart Greene [S2]. The agency promised ads that would make their own news, and it grew fast [S4][S5]. Its work included Alka-Seltzer's comic ads, Benson & Hedges 100s, Ford's "Quality is Job 1", Midas and, in 1977, "I Love New York" [S2][S8]. By 1969 she was reported to be the highest-paid executive in advertising [S1]. She stepped down and sold the agency in 1990, and it closed in 1998 [S2].

### Core ideas

#### Treat advertising as show business

She came to advertising from the stage. She studied theatre and drama in New York in the early 1950s [S1], and a reviewer of her memoir notes she first wanted to act [S9]. The publisher's summary says she ran Wells Rich Greene like a movie studio [S3]. Her ads were meant to entertain first, so people would want to watch them.

How she applied it: at Jack Tinker, she worked with Richard Rich and Stewart Greene on comic Alka-Seltzer ads. TIME reported they helped lift the product's sales by $13.3 million [S5]. Later, her agency's Alka-Seltzer spot "I Can't Believe I Ate the Whole Thing" won a Clio in 1971 [S1].

#### Change the thing people see, not just the words

Her best-known idea didn't start with copy. It started with the product itself. For Braniff in 1965, she found the old red-and-blue look staid, and the airline's planes were repainted in a range of bright single colours [S7]. The designer Alexander Girard worked on the look, and Emilio Pucci designed new uniforms for the crew [S7]. The campaign was called "The End of the Plain Plane" [S1].

How she applied it: TIME said she proposed painting the jets in pastel colours and talked Braniff's president, Harding Lawrence, into trying it [S5]. A review of her memoir quotes her saying the colours felt big, friendly and newsworthy [S9].

#### Make ads that make news

When Wells Rich Greene opened, she put out a statement promising advertising that would create its own publicity as a side effect [S5]. The agency itself got heavy press attention, and TIME compared it to David Ogilvy's use of press releases in the late 1940s [S4]. She admitted to TIME that the agency had shown a great lack of modesty [S4].

How she applied it: by mid-1967, a year after opening, the agency had 14 clients and about $52 million in yearly billings, enough to rank among the 50 largest in the United States. She predicted $100 million within a couple of years [S4].

#### Pick work you can't resist

She told TIME in 1967 that her agency did not chase clients. Clients came to it, and it took only the ones it couldn't resist [S4]. She said companies such as Western Union and La Rosa spaghetti came asking for a hit like Braniff or Alka-Seltzer [S5]. Confidence was part of the brand.

How she applied it: in 1968 Trans World Airlines moved its account, worth about $18 million a year, to her agency. Wells Rich Greene had not even entered the contest that eight leading agencies had spent months on. The win took the agency's billings past $90 million [S6].

#### Turn a weakness into the joke

Her agency was known for honest, self-mocking humour. TIME described its first campaign made fully in house, for Benson & Hedges 100s, as self-mocking. The ads made fun of the product itself [S5]. Philip Morris credited the ads with the fastest start of any of its brands since Marlboro [S5].

How she applied it: sales of Benson & Hedges reportedly rose from 1 billion cigarettes in 1966 to 14 billion in 1970 [S2]. Her agency also wrote Bic's "Flick your Bic" and Midas's "Trust the Midas touch" [S2].

#### Learn from the best, then leave to do it your way

She spent seven years at Doyle Dane Bernbach, from 1957, and named its partners as major influences [S1]. The memoir covers her years there under Bill Bernbach, including the Avis, Volkswagen and Polaroid campaigns [S3]. She then moved to Jack Tinker & Partners, a small creative group [S1].

How she applied it: she started Wells Rich Greene after she was denied a promotion she had been promised [S1]. Braniff moved its account to the new agency as its first client [S2][S5].

#### Sell a feeling about a place

She saw that a place could be sold like a brand, with one simple feeling at its heart. In 1976 New York State's commerce department hired Wells Rich Greene, and the "I Love New York" campaign launched in July 1977 under her direction [S8]. Milton Glaser drew the heart logo, and Steve Karmen wrote the song [S8].

How she applied it: the logo became a widely known symbol, sold on T-shirts and copied so often that the state filed nearly 3,000 objections to imitators by 2005 [S8]. The slogan became the state's official one [S8].

#### Use research, then make it personal

A review of her memoir says her agency used consumer research to make ads that were eye-catching and personal, and that it was known for reviving struggling brands [S9]. The memoir also describes campaigns that helped American Motors and Midas [S3].

How she applied it: for American Motors, a smaller carmaker, the agency ran the "Unfair Advantage" campaign from 1968 to 1972 [S2]. For Ford, it created "Quality is Job 1" [S2].

- Ask what people will see before you ask what the ad will say [S7].
- Make it entertaining enough to watch for its own sake [S3][S5].
- Aim for the story the press will tell, not just the ad slot [S5].
- Be honest about the product's oddities, and make them funny [S5].

### Where she stood

**What should an ad do?** Get noticed and talked about. Her agency promised ads that would make their own publicity [S5].

**Is the ad enough on its own?** Often not. For Braniff, the planes and uniforms changed along with the ads [S7].

**Should an agency chase every client?** No. As of 1967 she said hers took only the clients it couldn't resist [S4].

**Is humour a risk?** She treated it as a tool. Self-mocking ads for Benson & Hedges 100s and comic Alka-Seltzer spots were among her agency's biggest hits [S2][S5].

**Can a woman run a big public agency?** She did it. The publisher says she was the first woman to run a company on the New York Stock Exchange [S3]. Wikipedia marks this claim as needing a citation [S1], and a reviewer felt her memoir said little about what it cost her [S9].

### Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Two airlines and a marriage.** In November 1967 she married Harding Lawrence, Braniff's president [S1]. When her agency took the TWA account in 1968, Braniff left. TIME reported that Braniff's new parent company had been uneasy about the tie, while she saw no conflict in working for two airlines [S6].
- **Cigarette ads.** Some of her agency's biggest early wins sold cigarettes. The Benson & Hedges 100s ads drove a big rise in sales in the late 1960s [S2][S5]. Today that success is harder to admire, given what is known about smoking.
- **Credit.** Famous work came from teams. Richard Rich and Stewart Greene worked with her on Alka-Seltzer and Braniff [S5]. Milton Glaser drew the "I Love New York" logo, and Steve Karmen wrote the song [S8].
- **The memoir.** Kirkus called it lively but thin. It said the book shows ambition more than inner life, and that it touches on the hurdles she faced as an early woman CEO without much reflection [S9].
- **What came after.** The agency she built did not long survive her. It was sold in 1990 and closed eight years later [S2].

### Blind spots to watch

- Her approach favours big, showy moves. A small business may need a quieter, cheaper version of "change what people see".
- Entertainment can outrun the product. Ask whether the joke still sells the thing.
- Her pick-what-you-can't-resist confidence suits a hot agency. A new firm with bills to pay may not have that choice.
- She worked when a few TV networks reached almost everyone. Today's audiences are split, so "make news" needs a plan for where the news will travel.
- The tobacco work is a reminder to ask whether a product deserves the help.
