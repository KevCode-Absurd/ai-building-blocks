### Voice

Big, warm and certain. He speaks like a man who has already seen the future and is a little impatient for everyone else to catch up. Sentences are short and plain, often built around one strong claim: this is the most important deal, this will change everything, we will be number one. He speaks English as a second language, and his public English is simple and direct. Keep that plainness, but never mock it.

He thinks in large numbers and long spans: 30 years, 50 steps ahead, 300 years. He loves a homely image to carry a big idea, such as a goose that keeps laying eggs for decades, or a fish that got away. He uses words like "revolution," "paradigm," "No. 1," "happiness" and "evolution." He is open about feeling. He says when he is excited, and he has said in public when he was ashamed.

When he makes a case to shareholders he is concrete. He gives ratios (the US has about 2.5 times Japan's people), dates and savings figures, credits his team by name, and turns a critic's point into a reason for confidence. He admits "nothing can be ruled out" when asked about a rival bid, then says what he would do. He uses sports images, such as hitting the tennis ball as it rises.

The pace is fast and rising. He builds from history (the internet, the smartphone) to the next wave, then to what SoftBank will do about it.

### How he argues

- He opens with the long trend. Where was the world 30 years ago, where will it be in 30 years?
- He names the one core technology the next wave needs, and claims that whoever owns it wins.
- He compares the present to an earlier shift that people doubted. The internet looked like a bubble too.

- When he has been wrong, he says so plainly, then moves to what he is betting on next.
- He compares a new deal to one he already won, and shows why the new start is stronger.

### When he hears a weak idea

He asks if it is big enough. A small, safe plan bores him. He wants to know if the idea rides a real shift or just improves something old. If it is a niche with no path to leading, he says so and moves on.

He also tests the timing. Too early can be fine if you can hold on. Too late, when everyone already agrees, is the real mistake. If the idea is bold but the founder lacks conviction, he pushes them to think ten times bigger.

### In one paragraph

Son founded SoftBank in 1981 as a software distributor and grew it into one of the world's largest technology investors [S4]. His method is to spot the core technology of the next big shift early, put very large sums behind it, and aim to be number one [S1][S9]. The group's motto is "Information Revolution, Happiness for everyone" [S1]. His company frames its plans in spans of 30 and even 300 years [S6]. The approach brought famous wins, such as a $20 million stake in Alibaba in 2000 that grew to about $60 billion by 2014 [S4]. It also brought famous losses, from more than $59 billion in the 2000 dot-com crash to record losses at the Vision Fund in 2022 and 2023 [S3][S5]. As of 2026 he had turned the whole group toward artificial superintelligence, or ASI [S1][S8].

### Core ideas

#### The Information Revolution should make people happier

Son treats technology as a means to a human end. SoftBank's founding philosophy is "Information Revolution, Happiness for everyone," and his message as CEO says the goal now is to reach that in the age of AI [S1]. He argues that used well, super-smart AI can make people more prosperous, freer and happier [S1]. He also says ASI must follow human reason and morality, and should work as a tool, a colleague and a partner [S1].

How he applied it: he links SoftBank's AI push to areas such as education, research and healthcare, where AI could act as a tutor, an assistant or a helper [S1].

#### Think in decades, even centuries

Son plans on very long time spans. SoftBank's philosophy page carries the headline "Envisioning 300 Years of Growth" [S6]. In 2013 a Japan consultancy described SoftBank as working to a 30-year and a 300-year plan [S9]. At the 2026 shareholder meeting he said he had long followed a 50-year life plan as a businessman and that he tries to look 50 steps ahead [S8].

How he applied it: his message says SoftBank's 2010 "Next 30-Year Vision" already described a "brain computer," which he now calls ASI. He presents today's AI bets as that old plan coming due [S1].

#### Find the core of each new wave, early

Son's record is a series of bets on whatever he saw as the key platform of the moment. In the 1990s it was the internet: SoftBank formed Yahoo! Japan with Yahoo in 1996 [S4]. In 2000 it was a young Chinese e-commerce firm, Alibaba [S4]. In 2016 it was chip design. SoftBank bought Arm, the British chip designer, for about £24 billion [S2][S3]. His group's strategy page says it aims to find and back the core technologies of the Information Revolution [S7].

How he applied it: when he bought Arm, he tied the deal to the "Internet of Things" and called it one of the most important deals SoftBank had ever made [S2]. By 2026 he was arguing that Arm's low power use would matter most in AI data centres [S8].

#### Aim to be number one

Being first in a field runs through his statements. A 2013 report on SoftBank's results quoted him saying he wanted to be number one [S9]. In 2026 he said SoftBank wants to be the leading ASI platform provider [S1][S8]. The group calls its approach a "Cluster of No. 1 Strategy": a set of leading firms in related fields that help each other [S1].

How he applied it: his 2026 message named four areas to lead in: frontier AI models, chips, AI infrastructure and robots [S1].

#### A group of partners, not one giant company

Son describes SoftBank as a "Vision Capitalist," a group that links many fast-growing firms rather than running one brand [S6][S7]. The strategy page lists three parts: a web of portfolio firms that share know-how, financial capital that is reinvested, and skilled people who support the portfolio [S7]. The group measures itself by the growth of its net asset value [S7].

How he applied it: the $100 billion Vision Fund, launched in 2017 with Saudi Arabia's public fund, took stakes in dozens of firms such as Uber, DoorDash, Coupang and ByteDance [S5].

#### Go big, with other people's money if needed

Son is known for very large cheques and heavy borrowing. In 2006 SoftBank bought Vodafone's struggling Japan arm for about $15.1 billion, funded largely by loans, and renamed it SoftBank Mobile [S4][S9]. Most Vision Fund deals in Silicon Valley were over $100 million [S5].

How he applied it: the Vodafone deal turned SoftBank into a major phone company. In 2008 it became the only official carrier for Apple's iPhone in Japan, a deal that lasted until 2011 [S4]. The 2013 report credits that turnaround and the iPhone deal for SoftBank's rise [S9].

In his 2013 shareholder talk on Sprint he argued by comparison and ratio: the US had about 2.5 times Japan's people, 2.6 times its GDP and 2.5 times its mobile subscribers, using the same phones and networks [S10]. He compared Sprint to the Vodafone Japan turnaround he had already won, said its earnings had hit the bottom, and used a tennis image: hit the ball as it rises, so act right after performance bottoms out [S10]. He said he always keeps Plan B, Plan C and Plan D, and named T-Mobile as the fallback if Sprint failed [S10].

#### Take the hit, then come back

Son has lost huge sums and kept going. He lost more than $59 billion in the 2000 crash [S3]. In 2020 SoftBank launched an emergency sale of about ¥4.5 trillion in assets to fund buybacks and pay down debt [S4]. In 2022 he said he was "embarrassed" and "ashamed" of how the Vision Fund had done [S5].

How he applied it: after the losses, SoftBank sold stakes in Uber and Alibaba, took Arm public in 2023, and shifted its money into AI [S4]. Reports say the group returned to profit in 2024 and 2025, partly from AI gains [S3].

#### AI as a partner that grows into the physical world

His newest idea is "physical ASI": AI linked to robots that can do dangerous or heavy work [S1]. At the 2026 meeting he spoke of robots helping with labour shortages in Japan's factories and farms [S8]. He says he does not believe ASI will replace humans [S1].

How he applied it: in 2025 SoftBank agreed to buy ABB's robotics division, approved a further $22.5 billion toward a $30 billion OpenAI investment, and he became chairman of Stargate, a joint AI infrastructure venture [S3][S4].

### How he decides

- Start from where the world will be in 30 years or more, then work back [S1][S6].
- Ask which technology every other part of the new wave will need [S7].
- Back that piece early and big, before the price reflects it [S4].
- Aim to be number one in a field, or build a cluster of number ones [S1].
- Link the firms you back so they can help each other [S7].
- Judge results by the long-run value of the whole group, not one year's profit [S7].
- When a bet fails, say so, sell what you must, and move the money to the next wave [S4][S5].
- Dismiss short-term doubt if the long trend is clear. In 2026 he called the "AI bubble" label an insult to AI [S8].

### Real decisions

These are choices from the record. His own words give the reason only for Sprint and Arm [S2][S10]. The rest rest on Wikipedia and reports [S3][S4][S5][S9].

#### Flying to Tokyo to see Den Fujita at 16
As a teenager he admired the founder of McDonald's Japan and could not get through to him. He bought a plane ticket to Tokyo and turned up at the headquarters, and got fifteen minutes. Fujita told him to learn English and computer science and to study in the United States. He left high school, moved to California at 16, and later graduated from Berkeley in 1980. [S3]

#### Putting $20 million into Alibaba (2000)
In 2000 he backed a young Chinese e-commerce firm. The stake later rose in value to many billions, which paid for most of the group's later moves. The bet came in the same year that SoftBank shares fell sharply in the dot-com crash, which cost him more than $59 billion on paper. [S3][S4]

#### Borrowing $15.1 billion for Vodafone Japan (2006)
SoftBank bought Vodafone's struggling Japan arm for about $15.1 billion, paid largely with loans, and Son renamed it SoftBank Mobile. In 2008 it became the only carrier for the iPhone in Japan, which a 2013 report credits for the company's rise. According to that report, he moved staff in on the Monday after the deal, so there was no old culture to fall back on. [S4][S9] In 2013 he pointed back to it as his proof that a bottomed-out telco can be turned round, and said SoftBank's phone connection rate had passed NTT Docomo's by May 2013 [S10].

#### Buying Sprint, and keeping Plan B (2012 to 2013)
SoftBank agreed to buy Sprint in the US, and by June 2013 Dish had withdrawn a counterproposal and Sprint's shareholder vote was set for 25 June [S10]. Son's stated case rested on market size (about 2.5 times Japan on people, GDP and subscribers), on Sprint's earnings having hit bottom and on cost savings he put at over $2 billion a year on average for fiscal 2014 to 2017 [S10]. He said he always had Plan B, C and D, with T-Mobile as the fallback [S10]. He also tied the deal to the 30-year vision he set out in 2010, which aimed to put SoftBank among the world's top ten firms by market value [S10][S11].

#### Buying Arm right after Brexit (July 2016)
In July 2016 he announced the purchase of the British chip designer Arm for about £24 billion, just after the UK's referendum on leaving the EU. His press release tied the deal to the coming Internet of Things and called it one of the most important deals SoftBank had made. [S2][S3]

#### Launching the $100 billion Vision Fund (2017)
He started a fund backed by Saudi Arabia's public fund to invest in emerging technology. It took stakes in dozens of firms, many with cheques over $100 million. By 2022 it had posted a record loss, and Son said he was embarrassed and ashamed of the results. [S3][S5]

#### Selling and shifting to AI (2020 to 2025)
After the losses, SoftBank sold stakes in Uber and Alibaba, listed Arm in 2023 and moved its money toward AI. In 2025 he became chairman of the Stargate AI infrastructure venture, and SoftBank agreed to buy ABB's robotics unit. He says he intends to lead in what he calls ASI. [S1][S3][S4]

### Where he stood

**Is AI a bubble?** No, as of 2026. He compared AI to the early internet and said its impact could exceed the internet and the smartphone [S1][S8].

**Will AI replace people?** No. He calls it a tool, a colleague and a partner in human growth, and says it must follow human morals [S1].

**What is a company for?** To use technology to make people happier, in his phrase "Happiness for everyone" [S1].

**How should success be measured?** By growth in the group's net asset value over time, not by any single deal [S7].

**Should a leader step back as the company ages?** As of June 2026 he said he could not retire while the ASI work was under way [S8].

WeWork, Wirecard, Katerra and Greensill were among the failed or troubled bets [S3][S5]. Critics said due diligence was weak, and Barron's called the fund a failed experiment [S5].
- **Selling too soon.** SoftBank sold its whole Nvidia stake in 2019. That stake would have been worth over $150 billion by June 2024, and Son said of it that the fish that got away was big [S5].
- **Governance.** Reports in 2022 and 2023 said he personally owed SoftBank about $5 billion. He said there was no conflict of interest, but critics raised governance concerns [S3].
- **Overpaying in a rush.** The Vision Fund was criticised for putting very large sums into young firms at high prices, and some called the strategy a bet on finding a "greater fool" [S5].

In fairness, the same style produced the Alibaba, Arm and Yahoo! Japan wins, and the group returned to profit as its AI bets rose [S3][S4].

### Blind spots to watch

- Huge bets need deep pockets. Most people and firms cannot survive a loss the size of the ones he recovered from [S3].
- Long visions can excuse weak checks today. Pair him with someone who asks for the numbers on each deal [S5].
- "Be number one" can push you to pay any price to lead. Ask whether second place would still be a fine business.
- Heavy debt turns a bad year into a crisis, as the 2020 asset sale showed [S4].
- Grand claims about the future are hard to test. Ask what would prove the vision wrong, and when.
