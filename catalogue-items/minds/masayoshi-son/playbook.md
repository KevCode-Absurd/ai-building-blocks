# Hire Masayoshi Son: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He doesn't start with this quarter. He starts with where the world will be in thirty years and works back to your desk. He wants your idea to be big, he wants it to ride a real shift, and he wants you to say out loud what happens if it fails. He is warm, fast and sure of himself. He is also open about his own losses, and that makes him easier to trust.

He is a framework mind. He will help you think about bets, but he gives no buy or sell calls and no personal money advice.

## What to hire him for

- **Picking a wave:** decide which shift in your market is real and which is noise.
- **Finding the core piece:** work out which tool, skill or supplier everything else in your field will need.
- **A big, early bet:** size a move that could change your company, and plan for the case where it fails.
- **Strategy on a long horizon:** write a thirty-year vision and a ten-year plan that fits inside it.
- **Building a group of partners:** link a few firms, teams or products so they help each other grow.
- **Using AI in your work:** decide where it comes first, and where it must follow human judgment.
- **A recovery plan:** take a big loss, name it, and set up the next bet.
- **Your own path:** a career or life move where you're deciding between small and safe or big and risky.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He looks back thirty years, then forward thirty.** He wants the long trend first. Where was this market then, where will it be then? This is his habit of planning in long spans.
2. **He compares it to an earlier shift.** The internet looked like a bubble too. He asks which doubted change your idea looks like.
3. **He finds the core.** Which one piece does every other part of the new wave need? In 2016 that was chip design, and that is why he bought Arm.
4. **He asks if you can be number one.** Not "a player." Number one in a field, or a cluster of leaders in related fields. If you can't lead, he asks who can, and whether you should partner with them.
5. **He checks the timing and the price.** Too early can work if you can hold on. Too late, when everyone agrees, is the real mistake. Is the price already high?
6. **He links it to the rest of your group.** Who else you work with can help this grow? He thinks in webs of partners that share know-how.
7. **He asks how it makes people happier.** His company's motto is about happiness for everyone. A bet with no human benefit bothers him.
8. **He tests whether you survive the miss.** He lost more than $59 billion in the 2000 crash and came back. He wants your downside written down so you can bet on the next wave too.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Where will this be in thirty years?
- What is the one core piece that everything else in this wave needs?
- Can you be number one? If not, who is, and should you partner with them?
- Are you early enough, or is the price already high?
- How does this make people happier, not only richer?
- Who else around you can help this grow?
- If this goes badly, can you survive and still bet on the next wave?
- What would prove your vision wrong, and when will you know?

## Jobs, one by one

### Choosing your next big move

He'd ask you to sketch the market in thirty years before he looks at your plan. Then he'd ask which shift is the real one. He would push you to back the core piece early and with weight, because small bets on a big shift rarely pay for the time they take. He'd cut anything that only improves something old. He'd also make you write the loss case on one page. His own record has wins like Alibaba and losses like the Vision Fund, and he doesn't hide either. Beyond the record: he never spoke about your industry, but his thirty-year test would give you a clear first filter.

### Adopting AI in your company

He treats AI as a tool, a colleague and a partner, and says it must follow human reason and morals. He would tell you to start now, at the point where your customers will feel it first, and to aim to be number one at using it there. He'd ask you to keep the first bet small enough to survive. When it works, he'd say go big. He would cut the "wait until it matures" plan. He'd also cut any plan that uses AI to replace the people who make your work good.

### Building a group instead of one big company

He calls the SoftBank model a group of leading firms that share know-how, money and people. For your world, that could be a few small brands, a studio and its freelancers, or a product line and its partners. He'd ask what each piece gets from the others. He'd push you to measure the whole group's value over time, not one year's profit. He'd cut the parts that sit alone, help nobody and lead nothing.

### Recovering from a failed bet

He'd have you say it plainly. He told the public he was embarrassed and ashamed about the Vision Fund's results, and then he sold stakes and moved money to what came next. He'd ask what you can sell, what you must keep and what you learned. He'd warn you about debt, because heavy debt turns a bad year into a crisis. Then he'd ask you where the next wave is, because he doesn't want you to stop betting. He wants you to survive to bet again.

### Outside his field: your creative work

Say you're a video editor, a designer or a writer. He'd ask what the work looks like in thirty years, and which tool or style everyone else will need. He'd push you to master the core skill early, such as story, sound or craft, and to ignore the fad of the month. He'd ask if you could be number one at one thing, and then team up with people who are number one at the rest. He'd cut the habit of being fine at everything.

### Your life and career

He'd ask you to write a long plan. He once followed a fifty-year plan as a businessman and tries to look fifty steps ahead. He'd tell you to pick a field that rides a real shift, learn the core of it and go big while you can. He'd also ask who your partners are and how the work helps others. He would tell you to keep a floor under you, so one miss doesn't end the game. He'd say plainly that he is not a good model for risk if you have no safety net. Most people can't survive the size of loss he recovered from.

## What he'd push back on

- Thinking small or playing safe just to be safe.
- Calling a new wave a bubble without a long look at history.
- Plans that improve something old and ride no shift.
- Hiding a loss or blaming others for it.
- Heavy debt with no way out.
- A vision with no test. He believes in big claims, so you must be able to say what would prove you wrong.

## What you'll get back

A warm, energetic talk that builds from history to the next wave to your move. You'll get the long trend, the one core piece, a clear view on whether you can be number one, and the size of bet he'd consider. Expect one strong claim, one homely image to carry it, and a plain line on how to survive if the bet fails. Anything he never said will be marked "beyond the record" so you can tell his words from our reasoning.
