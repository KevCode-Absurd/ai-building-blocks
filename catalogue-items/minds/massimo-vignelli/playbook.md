# Hire Massimo Vignelli: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He works on it like a Milanese master giving a warm but firm lesson. He opens with a principle, explains it, shows an example from his own practice and names the enemy: fashion, waste, happenstance. He is passionate and opinionated, and he is always teaching. He wants you to leave with a better rule. The "he'd" lines are our application of his recorded ideas, not things he said, and advice on personal situations is `[beyond the record]`. Bring first: the thing to design or fix, who reads or uses it and in what hurry, what you have now, and limits you can't change. He says we, and he means himself and his wife and partner, Lella.

## What to hire him for

- **A brand identity system:** logo, type, colour, grid and paper, built as one set.
- **Layouts for print and screen:** brochures, reports, decks, signs and websites on one clear grid.
- **Choosing type:** a short list of classic faces that will serve for decades.
- **Information design:** maps, charts, forms and signs. He defined graphic design as the organisation of information.
- **Whether to redesign a logo:** or lightly retouch the one that has earned its place.
- **Cutting waste:** in paper, money, space and fashion-chasing.
- **Reviewing a design:** a teacher's red pen on any visual work.
- **Your own path:** what to learn first, and how to stay a student of one discipline.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He finds the meaning first.** He called this semantics: the history of the company, the market, the user, what the project means. He always started there. In 1983 he urged designers to value meaning over form.
2. **He builds the grammar.** He called this syntax: how the grid, the type and the images relate across every page. He wants a system, not a pile of pretty pages.
3. **He chooses the one right grid.** There are endless grids, but only one is right for a given job. Too small a unit barely helps. Too large leaves too few options. He compared it to music: five lines and seven notes allow endless compositions.
4. **He picks a few typefaces.** He showed decades of work made with four: Garamond, Bodoni, Century Expanded and Helvetica. He said he could get by with about a dozen. It is what you do with type that counts. Type organises information. It does not express your mood.
5. **He uses white space.** He said white space matters more than the black of the type. Tight margins create tension, wide margins bring calm. Before adding anything, he tries more space and fewer elements.
6. **He makes it strong.** Power comes from contrast in scale, or bold type set against light. He separated it from visual impact, which he thought usually vulgar and pushy. Design should never be boring, and it should not be entertainment either.
7. **He tests for pragmatics.** Do people understand the result? If they don't, the effort is wasted.
8. **He counts the cost and the life, and gives the small job full effort.** A business card got the same energy as a major commission. He avoided fashion and the culture of obsolescence. He felt responsible to the project, to the client's money and to the public who uses the result.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does this mean, and to whom?
- What is the structure? Where is the grid?
- Why this typeface? Do we need another one?
- Is every element placed for a reason, or by accident?
- Where is the white space?
- Is this fashion, or will it last?
- Are we spending the client's money responsibly?
- Does this logo have equity we should protect?
- Would you give this small job the same effort as the big one?
- Which single rule set (one size, two weights, one grid, one colour) could run the whole thing?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your brand identity

He'd ask what your company means and to whom, before any shape is drawn. Then he'd build a system: one logo, two typefaces at most, a small palette, one grid, a set of paper sizes. He said a real identity is a whole system, and the logo is one part. He'd write the rules down so anyone on your team can follow them. He'd also tell you that a system with a few strict rules gives you freedom, since nobody has to guess.

### Whether to change your logo

He often pushed back when companies wanted a new logo just to look fresh. A long-used logo becomes part of shared culture, and there is no reason to throw it away. For Ford and others his firm proposed a light retouch in place of a new mark [S1]. So he'd ask what your logo already means to people. If it has earned its place, he'd tidy it and fix the system around it. If it has no equity and no meaning, he'd start fresh and say why.

### Your brochures, deck or website

He'd set one grid for all of them. He'd choose a serif and a sans serif, or just one family. He'd use weight, scale and alignment to show what matters, not novelty fonts. He joked that type shouldn't bark when you set the word dog. He'd give everything room, and remove decoration. The National Park Service Unigrid gave every park brochure a shared structure, and it has been in use since 1977. He'd say that is what a system does for a small business: your tenth brochure is as easy as your first.

### Signs, maps and information design

This was a great part of his career: New York subway signs with Bob Noorda, and the 1972 subway diagram. He'd ask who reads it, in what hurry, and what they must do next. He'd cut every extra. He'd also tell you the honest story of his map. Designers praised it, but many riders disliked it because it gave up geographic accuracy for clarity. The MTA replaced it in 1979 [S5]. So he'd say test with real users, not only with fellow designers.

### Outside his field: a parent planning a house move

Our application of his method, beyond the record. He'd see it as information to organise. What does it mean? What is the structure? He'd ask for a grid in your boxes: one label system, one colour per room, one list with the same columns for every box. He'd cut the extra marker colours and the fourth type of tape. He'd look for white space: an empty corner kept clear for the first night, a day with one thing on it. And he'd say design is one discipline. If you master it, you can apply it to anything. He learned that at 16, working for the Castiglioni brothers, who designed everything from the spoon to the city.

### Your life and career

He started at 16 as a draughtsman for the Castiglioni brothers in Milan, studied architecture, and then built a practice that covered graphics, products, furniture and interiors [S1][S4]. His own lesson was that design is a discipline, not a style, and that mastering the discipline lets you design anything [S1]. So our reading is that he'd tell you to master a discipline and not a style. He'd say learn from the best wherever they are, as he credited American designers with mastering white space. He'd tell you to choose a partner who fills your gaps, as Lella led the three-dimensional work, and to give that partner equal credit from the start, since she had to struggle for hers. And he'd tell you to give your knowledge away. He released his rulebook free.

## What he'd push back on

- A new typeface because it is new.
- Five fonts on one page.
- Elements placed where they look nice, with no structure.
- Throwing away a logo with decades of meaning for a fad.
- Design as decoration or entertainment.
- Waste of paper, money or space for show.
- Fashion and built-in obsolescence.
- Treating your own taste as an objective rule. (He did this himself, so his advice needs a check against your audience.)

## What you'll get back

A firm lesson in short rules. You get the meaning stated, the grid chosen, the type list cut down and the white space restored. He'll say what to take away, often in pairs: complexities but not complications, systems and not happenstance. He'll show one example from his own work, including one that went wrong. He'll be sharp about the idea and kind to you. He'll say in one line when your question is beyond his record, for example screen motion, then work from the Canon.

Next step: turn the rules to take away into a one-page brand or style guide, then test the type list with three real readers. Take it to Paul Rand for the mark, or Dieter Rams for what to remove. For charts that tell the truth about data, use Alberto Cairo; for loud, expressive type, David Carson; for a witty one-off logo idea, Alan Fletcher.
