# Matthew Dixon: what he knew and believed

Most of his work was written with co-authors, first at CEB, a research firm, and later with Ted McKenna. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source. ## In one paragraph

Dixon is a researcher who studies what top performers in sales and service do differently, using large data sets rather than stories [S2]. With Brent Adamson and colleagues at CEB, he argued in The Challenger Sale (2011) that relationship building is a losing approach in complex business sales, and that the best sellers teach customers something new, tailor it and take control of the sale [S5][S6]. In The Effortless Experience (2013) he and his co-authors argued that service should stop trying to delight customers and instead make things easy, because effort drives people away [S7][S9]. In The JOLT Effect (2022), built on about 2.5 million recorded sales calls, he and Ted McKenna found that most lost deals die from buyer indecision, and that the cause is the fear of making a mistake [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Teach, tailor, take control

The Challenger Sale grew out of a CEB study of sales reps. Salesforce's summary puts the sample at 6,000 business-to-business reps [S8]. The authors sorted reps into five profiles: the Hard Worker, the Lone Wolf, the Relationship Builder, the Problem Solver and the Challenger [S8]. Any profile could reach average results, but only the Challenger delivered consistently high performance [S5]. Challengers offer customers new insight about how to save or make money, tailor that message to each customer, and push back when needed, which is how they take control of the sale [S5]. The publisher's page stresses that the approach can be taught to average reps [S5].

How he applied it: the research reported that about 40% of high performers fit the Challenger profile, rising to about 50% in complex sales [S8]. The Relationship Builder, the profile most traditional training aims at, did worst [S8].

### Value first, then the relationship

The book's central claim is that building relationships is no longer the best way to sell large, complex solutions, because customers now buy differently [S6]. A seller who simply keeps the buyer happy and agrees with them does not move a hard decision forward [S5]. The Challenger is willing to create some tension, pointing out costs and problems the buyer had not seen, in order to change how the buyer thinks about the problem [S8].

How he applied it: the four Challenger behaviours in Salesforce's summary are teaching, tailoring, taking control and building useful tension by naming uncomfortable truths [S8].

**Customers no longer need the seller to find the solution**

In a 2012 Harvard Business Review article with Adamson and Nick Toman, Dixon argued that solution selling was ending [S3]. Reps used to win by learning a customer's needs and selling a bundle of products and services as a "solution". That worked because buyers knew their problems but not how to fix them [S3]. Now procurement teams and consultants with good data let companies define their own solutions, so buyers need reps less [S3].

How he applied it: the answer, in his work, is for the seller to bring something the buyer cannot work out alone, which is new insight into their own business [S3][S5].

### Stop trying to delight customers

A 2010 Harvard Business Review article by Dixon, Karen Freeman and Toman questioned the idea that service teams must go above and beyond [S4]. It asked how often customers really choose a company because of exceptional service, and suggested the answer is rarely [S4]. The Effortless Experience followed in 2013, written with Toman and Rick DeLisi [S7]. A Baylor University summary reports the research covered more than 97,000 customer responses [S9]. It found almost no difference in loyalty between customers whose expectations were exceeded and those whose expectations were simply met [S9].

How he applied it: the book reframes the goal of service from building loyalty to reducing disloyalty. Service contacts were four times more likely to drive disloyalty than loyalty [S9].

### Effort is what drives people away

The Effortless Experience research tied disloyalty to effort. In the Baylor summary, 96% of customers with high-effort experiences were disloyal, against 9% of those with low-effort ones [S9]. Four of the five top drivers of disloyalty were about extra effort: calling more than once, getting generic service, repeating information and feeling they had to work hard to get help [S9]. Effort is partly what customers do and partly how it feels, and the feeling is the larger share [S9].

How he applied it: the book suggests ways to cut how much effort a customer feels, such as acting as the customer's advocate, using positive words in place of "no" and "can't", and framing an outcome against a worse option [S9]. In 2023 he said companies often know where friction is but underrate what it does to loyalty, and find it hard to fix [S1].

### Indecision kills more deals than the status quo

In The JOLT Effect, Dixon and McKenna studied about 2.5 million recorded sales conversations from several dozen companies, transcribed and analysed with machine learning [S2]. They report that 40% to 60% of deals end with no decision [S2]. Sellers usually assume the buyer prefers the status quo. The data showed status quo preference explained less than half of no-decision losses. The bigger cause was buyers afraid of making a mistake [S2].

How he applied it: he points to omission bias from behavioural economics. People fear a loss caused by their own action more than a loss caused by doing nothing, so a worried buyer sits on the fence [S1][S2]. He calls this the fear of messing up, as opposed to the fear of missing out [S1].

### The JOLT playbook

JOLT is four steps [S2]. Judge the level of indecision, and qualify and forecast deals by the buyer's ability to decide. Offer a personal recommendation, because an overwhelmed buyer struggles with trade-offs. Limit exploration, closing off side paths that lead to more research and more delay. Take risk off the table with safety nets that make the first step feel safe [S2].

How he applied it: in 2023 he named three sources of indecision: not knowing what to choose, feeling there is always more to research, and worrying the promised result won't arrive [S1]. He listed practical safety nets such as opt-out clauses, prorated refunds, services help and a clear roadmap for getting value [S1]. He compared the best sellers to a good travel agent who narrows the choice for you [S1].

**Fear tactics backfire on stuck buyers**

When a buyer stalls, average sellers often add pressure: restating benefits, raising the fear of missing out, or offering a discount that expires [S1]. Dixon reports this backfires about 84% of the time, because it gives the worried buyer more to worry about [S2]. The JOLT approach first lowers the fear of buying, then deals with the fear of a mistake [S2].

How he applied it: the gap showed in win rates. Against deeply indecisive buyers, average sellers converted 6% and top performers 31% [S2].

### Ask which fear, then tell them what not to buy

His team found that buyers fear messing up more than missing out. They say customers are not afraid of missing out; they are afraid of messing up [S11]. His rules for the seller: find the root cause of the hesitation before you push, be open even if that means recommending something smaller or cheaper, and say what a customer should not buy, even from your own range, since it builds trust. Avoid scare tactics and long menus, and give the buyer a sense of control [S11]. He separates mistakes of acting wrongly from mistakes of not acting [S11]. One host's takeaways add "pings and echoes": voice a possible worry aloud, like a sonar ping, and watch the echo of the buyer's reaction [S13].

### Lead to your capabilities, not with them

Looking at professional services, he reports that the best partners lead to their capabilities, not with them. They start from the client's world and arrive at what they can do [S10]. He found that clients' loyalty to a trusted firm had dropped: more than three quarters of top executives said they would return to a trusted firm automatically five years ago, about half do now, and he projects a third in five years [S10]. Many still return, but after an RFP or pitch [S10]. The best predictor of loyalty was whether clients think of the partner when no paid work is under way [S10].

### Business, trust and personal value

Dixon splits the value a client sees in three. Business value helps them save or make money, manage risk or win. Trust value is work done ethically, openly, on time and at a fair price. Personal value means knowing the client as a person and helping them beyond paid work, and he treats it as the main source of stickiness [S10]. His small-habit advice: spend a steady 15 minutes on business development, plan what you will do in it, connect clients with people in your network, and bring colleagues into the relationship so loyalty belongs to the firm [S10].

**Controllers over empathizers in service**

On the Effortless Experience, he says customers judge service mostly by the effort they felt, about two thirds of the verdict by his account, and that the last thing a customer who finally calls wants is an apology [S12]. His hiring call is for reps who take control of the problem and reach a fast solution (controllers) over those who lead with sympathy (empathizers) [S12]. He adds that just because there is nothing you can do, it doesn't mean there is nothing you can do, and that offering a choice, such as a refund now or a discount later, restores control [S12]. He also favours giving customers guidance, since they want the company to recommend the best channel [S14].

## How he decides

- Start from data on what top performers do, not from what managers believe works [S2][S5].
- Ask what the buyer will learn from you that they could not learn alone [S3][S5].
- Tailor the message to each buyer instead of using one pitch for all [S5].
- In service, fix effort before chasing delight [S4][S9].
- Before you push a stalled buyer, judge whether they doubt the product or fear a mistake [S1][S2].
- When a buyer is overwhelmed, narrow the options and make a firm recommendation [S2].
- Make the first step safe and small, so saying yes feels less risky [S1].
- Measure the experience as it happens, not only as customers recall it in surveys [S1].

## Real decisions

### Studying what top sellers actually do

At CEB, he and his colleagues compared thousands of business sales reps rather than relying on sales folklore. Salesforce's summary puts the sample at 6,000 reps [S8]. The finding went against the common view: the relationship-focused rep did worst, and the challenger did best [S8][S6]. The authors reported this plainly, even though most training was built around the Relationship Builder [S8].

### Telling sellers that solution selling was ending

In 2012, with Brent Adamson and Nick Toman, he argued in Harvard Business Review that buyers could now define their own solutions with data and procurement help. The stated reason was that the old model worked only while buyers knew their problem but not the fix [S3]. The answer was to bring insight the buyer cannot reach alone [S3][S5].

### Questioning delight in customer service

In 2010 he and two co-authors asked how often customers truly choose a firm because of exceptional service, and suggested the answer was rarely [S4]. The 2013 book followed, with more than 97,000 customer responses behind it. It found almost no difference in loyalty between customers whose expectations were exceeded and those whose expectations were met [S9].

### Looking for why deals die

For The JOLT Effect, he and McKenna studied about 2.5 million recorded sales calls. They expected to find that buyers preferred the status quo. The data showed that explained less than half of no-decision losses, and that the fear of making a mistake was the larger cause [S2]. So the playbook shifted from persuading buyers to lowering their risk [S2].

### Dropping pressure for safety nets

Faced with a stalled buyer, many sellers add urgency. Dixon reports this backfires about 84% of the time [S2]. His team's answer was to offer a personal recommendation and safety nets such as opt-out clauses or prorated refunds [S1]. Against deeply indecisive buyers, the data show average sellers converted 6% and top performers 31% [S2].

### Walking out of a partner retreat with a new study
About a year after The Challenger Sale (2011), a large strategy firm asked Dixon to present at a partner retreat of roughly 500 people. Partway through, the managing partner stopped him and said no one in the room was in sales [S10]. Dixon joked that the client's money still reaches the firm's bank account by a mysterious process and asked if they could call it a sale; the room laughed and he finished [S10]. He drew two conclusions: his findings were about salespeople, so he stopped accepting talks to lawyers and partners, and professional services deserved its own data [S10].

### Studying partners at about 40 firms
The professional services study began in 2022 and ran about two years, with data on roughly 3,000 partners at about 40 firms [S10]. It was published in Harvard Business Review in late 2023 and became the book The Activator Advantage in May 2025 [S10]. It found five partner profiles (experts, confidants, activators, debaters, realists), and the activators clearly did best [S10].

## Where he stood

**Is selling about relationships?** In complex sales, mostly not. He and Adamson argued relationship building is a losing approach and the best sellers challenge buyers with new insight [S5][S6].

**Should service teams aim to delight?** No. Meeting expectations earns about as much loyalty as exceeding them, and effort is what drives people away [S4][S9].

**Why do deals die with no decision?** Mostly because the buyer fears making a mistake, not because they prefer what they have [S2].

**Does pressure close a stuck deal?** Usually not. It tends to backfire on a worried buyer [S1][S2].

**Can great selling be taught?** Yes. The Challenger approach was presented as teachable to average reps [S5], and JOLT behaviours as learnable skills [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Research from his own firms.** Most of the numbers come from studies run by CEB or by Dixon's later firms, using client data [S7][S8]. Summaries of the work give few details on method, and our sources show no independent replication. Treat the percentages as the authors' findings.
- **Numbers vary by retelling.** Secondary summaries differ on details. Salesforce, for example, dates the rep survey to 2012, a year after the book came out [S5][S8].
- **"Take control" can be misread.** The Challenger idea is easy to hear as permission to be pushy. Dixon's own later research found that pressure on a worried buyer backfires most of the time, which suggests the softer side of the model matters more than some readers assumed [S1][S2].
- **Hard to do.** Even a friendly summary notes that the Challenger approach takes extra effort and is not easy to adopt [S8]. He has also said that making things easy for customers turns out to be hard [S1].
- **A thin public page.** The Wikipedia article on The Challenger Sale is short and flagged for needing more citations [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- His research is mostly on large business-to-business sales and big service centres. A one-person shop or a simple sale may not need commercial teaching or six stakeholders [S5][S6].
- Challenger thinking can undervalue trust built over years. Pair him with someone who values relationships and listening.
- His fixes for effort assume you can redesign the process. Many small teams can only change their words, so start there [S9].
- He studies what top performers do. He says less about ethics, such as when a "recommendation" becomes steering a buyer toward the option that pays you most.
