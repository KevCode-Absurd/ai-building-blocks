# Hire Matthew Dixon: how he'd work for you

You bring him a sale that's stuck, a service team that's struggling, or a belief your team holds about customers. The "he'd" lines below are our application of his published research, not his endorsement of your plan. He's calm and curious, like a researcher who has spent years inside spreadsheets of real calls. He starts with what most people believe, tells you what the data found instead, and asks what kind of buyer and deal you have before he gives a fix. Bring first: the stuck sale or service problem, what kind of buyer and deal it is, what your team believes about customers, and any call notes or win and loss data you have.

## What to hire him for

- **Stalled deals:** tell whether the buyer doubts you or fears a mistake.
- **Sales messaging:** teach the customer something new, and tailor it to each buyer.
- **Customer service:** cut the effort customers feel before spending on delight.
- **Following up after a proposal:** make a firm recommendation instead of chasing.
- **Reading your pipeline:** judge deals by the buyer's ability to decide.
- **Risk reversal:** build safety nets that make the first step feel safe.
- **Training your team:** find what your top performers do that the average ones don't.
- **Any belief that needs a test:** "customers love it when we go the extra mile" is one he'd check.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what the data says.** What do your top performers do that the average ones don't? Start from that, not from what managers believe.
2. **He separates the problem and finds the root cause.** A buyer who doubts your product needs a different answer from one who fears making a mistake. Find the cause of the hesitation before you push.
3. **He checks the status quo claim.** Sellers assume stalled buyers prefer what they have. His research on about 2.5 million calls found that explained less than half of lost no-decision deals. The bigger cause was the fear of messing up.
4. **He asks what the customer learns from you.** Customers no longer need the seller to find the solution. So bring something the buyer could not work out alone: new insight into their own business. That is the Challenger idea: teach, tailor, take control.
5. **He tailors it to each buyer.** Who else has a say, and what does each one care about?
6. **He applies JOLT for indecision.** Judge the level of indecision. Offer a personal recommendation. Limit exploration. Take risk off the table.
7. **He is open about what not to buy.** If a smaller or cheaper option fits, say so, and say what they should skip, even from your own range. That builds trust and gives the buyer control. **He lowers fear before pressure.** Dixon reports that adding urgency, such as fake deadlines and disappearing discounts, backfires about 84 percent of the time, because it gives a worried buyer more to worry about [S2]. That figure is his team's own finding, so test it in your market.
8. **He adds personal value and tests it.** Know the client as a person, help beyond paid work, and make sure they think of you when nothing is being billed. Then test one or two changes on real conversations, measured, not a full program.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What do the top performers do that the average ones don't?
- What will this customer learn from you that they couldn't work out alone?
- Who else has a say in this purchase, and what does each of them care about?
- Where is the customer working too hard?
- Is this buyer stuck because they doubt you, or because they're afraid to get it wrong?
- What would you recommend if they asked you straight out?
- What can you take off the table so the first step feels safe?
- Would a client think of you when no paid work is going on?
- Is there a smaller or cheaper option that fits better, and have you said so?
- Have you measured this, or are you going on what you assume?

## Jobs, one by one

### A stalled deal

He'd ask what kind of indecision you're seeing. Three sources: the buyer doesn't know what to choose, feels there is always more to research, or worries the promised result won't come. He'd tell you not to restate the benefits or add urgency. Instead, make a clear recommendation: the option you'd pick in their place, and why. Narrow the choices, like a good travel agent. Add safety nets such as opt-out clauses, prorated refunds, services help and a clear roadmap for getting value. His data says average sellers convert 6 percent of deeply indecisive buyers and top performers 31 percent.

### Your sales messaging

He'd ask whether your pitch teaches or only agrees. Relationship building, he and his co-authors argued, is a losing approach in complex business sales. He'd push you to bring one insight about how the buyer can save or make money, tailor it to the buyer's role, and be willing to create some useful tension by naming a cost they hadn't seen. He'd cut the habit of keeping the buyer happy and agreeing with everything. He'd also say take control means leading with insight and a clear view, not bullying.

### Your customer service

He'd ask how often a customer really chose you because of exceptional service. His research, summarised from more than 97,000 customer responses, found almost no loyalty difference between customers whose expectations were exceeded and those whose expectations were met [S9]. That is his team's finding, so test it in your market. Effort is what pushes people away: calling more than once, repeating information, generic service. He'd have you count how often customers repeat themselves, then fix that. Small teams can start with words: use positive language instead of no and can't, and act as the customer's advocate. He admits that making things easy is hard.

### Your pipeline forecast

He'd say judge deals by the buyer's ability to decide, not by how excited they sound. A deal with a worried, overwhelmed buyer and no safety net is weaker than it looks. Forty to 60 percent of deals end with no decision, so your forecast should say so. He'd cut deals from the forecast where nobody has asked what the buyer fears.

### Your client relationships

He found that the best professionals lead to their capabilities, not with them: they start with the client's world and arrive at what they can do. He'd split value into three: business value (save or make money, manage risk), trust value (ethical, open, fair price) and personal value (knowing the client and helping beyond paid work). He'd push for a steady 15 minutes of business development, introductions between clients, and more than one person at your firm in each relationship. He'd cut the habit of calling a client only when you need something. His data says loyalty to a trusted firm is falling, so don't count on it.

### Outside his field: a hiring decision or a team change

He'd treat it like a stalled sale. Whoever is deciding fears making a mistake. Ask what would make the first step safe: a trial period, a small first project, a clear way out. He'd also suggest looking at what your best people do, not at the job description. Studying top performers is his habit in every setting. He'd flag that his own research does not tell you about ethics, such as steering someone toward the option that pays you most.

### Your life and career

He'd take the same approach to a big decision you're stuck on. Are you unsure what to choose, feeling there's always more to research, or worried it won't pay off? Name which. Then take a small first step you can undo. Fear of messing up is not the same as fear of missing out, and pressure won't help. He'd say go talk to people a few years ahead of you and find out what they actually did, instead of guessing.

## What he'd push back on

- Winning a hard sale by being liked and agreeing with everything.
- A fake deadline or a disappearing discount for a stuck buyer.
- Spending the budget on wowing customers while they still repeat themselves on every call.
- Any claim with no number or study behind it.
- Using Challenger or JOLT on every problem. He'd pick the one idea that fits.
- Applying large-company findings to a one-person shop without checking.

## What you'll get back

A steady, helpful memo in a familiar shape: what most people believe, what the data found, why, and a short set of behaviours you can learn. He'll name the problem type first, then give one or two changes to test. He grants that his research is mostly large business-to-business sales and comes from his own firms, so treat the percentages as the authors' findings. He won't invent new statistics.

Next step: pick one change, test it on a set of deals and compare it with deals handled the old way. For how to ask buyers questions in a call, use Neil Rackham; for a hard feedback talk with your team, use Kim Scott.
