## In one paragraph

Maya Angelou was born Marguerite Annie Johnson in St. Louis in 1928 and died in North Carolina in 2014 [S1]. She grew up partly in Stamps, Arkansas, with her grandmother [S1]. Before she was a writer she was, among other things, San Francisco's first Black woman streetcar conductor, a nightclub singer and dancer, a touring performer in *Porgy and Bess*, a civil rights organiser and a journalist in Egypt and Ghana [S1]. Her first memoir, *I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings* (1969), made her famous and began a series of seven [S1][S3]. She also wrote poetry built to be spoken, and in 1993 she read her poem "On the Pulse of Morning" at a US presidential inauguration [S4]. She taught at Wake Forest University for decades without ever earning a bachelor's degree [S1].

## Core ideas

### Courage comes first

In her 1997 interview, Angelou called courage the most important virtue, because without it you can't practise any other virtue for long [S2]. Her own life story is built on acts of nerve: taking a job no Black woman had held, speaking again after years of silence, standing on stage before the nation [S1][S2].

The lesson: before a speech, a story or a hard talk, decide what you are afraid of, and do it anyway.

### Silence taught her to listen

After a childhood trauma, which she describes in *Caged Bird*, she stopped speaking for almost five years [S1]. In her interview she said she had a voice but refused to use it [S2]. Biographers argue that this long silence sharpened her memory, her love of books and her skill at listening and observing [S1]. A teacher, Beulah Flowers, helped her speak again through books and reading aloud [S1][S3].

The lesson: listening is part of writing. Notice how people really talk before you put words in their mouths.

### Catch the music, keep the content

Angelou said she tries to catch the music of the African American preacher's voice [S2]. If she gets the music and the content together, she said, she has the public's attention [S2]. She told young people to close the door and read aloud, to hear how their voices carry, and to read speeches by Thomas Paine, Martin Luther King Jr. and Barbara Jordan aloud [S2]. She also argued that a writer should change style as easily as changing a jacket, preaching, joking or singing the blues as the moment needs [S2].

How it shows in the work: "Still I Rise" is built in short, rhymed lines with a steady beat for reciting, and a refrain that keeps coming back stronger [S6]. It teases the reader with questions, then answers each with a bold image of strength [S6].

### Tell the hard truth with dignity and humour

*Caged Bird* deals with racism, trauma and a young girl's search for identity [S3]. Critics praise its voice as rich, funny and intense, and its mix of Southern speech and literary language [S3]. The book moves from a girl who feels inferior toward one with self-respect [S3]. It does this without self-pity. Humour lets the reader stay with the pain.

How she wrote it: an editor, Robert Loomis, told her that writing autobiography as literature was almost impossible, and she took that as a challenge [S3]. James Baldwin also encouraged her [S3]. She used the tools of fiction, such as dialogue, character and themes, and arranged the book by theme in short episodes rather than strictly by date [S3].

### Turn pain into strength by going through it

Angelou told students to learn painful history rather than avoid it, because the only way out of something is all the way through it [S2]. Wikipedia reports her saying that she dealt with pain by admitting it and letting it come [S1]. Her memoirs return to hard memories on purpose, and her poems turn hurt into defiance. In the Moyers programmes she says evil has often been built into history in the name of good, that trauma can breed cynicism, which she calls a tragedy, and that suffering can be turned into strength [S8]. She also warned that guilt immobilises [S2].

### Speak to everyone in the room

"On the Pulse of Morning" gives voices to a rock, a river and a tree, who speak in turn to every kind of person: every nation, faith and job [S5]. It moves from deep time to the present, faces the costs of the nation's past, and ends not with a grand claim but by asking people to greet their neighbours with a simple good morning [S4][S5]. She was the first African American woman to read at an inauguration, and the recording won a Grammy [S4].

How she wrote it: she followed her usual routine, wrote down everything she could about the theme, America, and then shaped the notes into verse [S4].

### Read ceaselessly, and spend what you learn

She urged young people to read without stopping and to work through library reading lists [S2]. She called knowledge a currency you can spend [S2]. She also told young poets to start at home, reading at church and school, and let word spread outward [S2].

### Write as daily work

Angelou wrote in rented hotel rooms with the pictures taken down, lying on the bed with legal pads, a thesaurus, a Bible, a deck of cards and a bottle of sherry [S1]. She wrote from early morning into the afternoon [S4]. She averaged 10 to 12 pages a day and cut them down to three or four [S1]. She also said plainly that she wrote for money, as Balzac had [S1].

### Words are things, and courtesy is a rule

In the Oprah conversation she said words are things: they shape people and places, so she would not let racist or cruel jokes be told in her home [S11]. She taught the same rule to young women: accept nothing less than courtesy and give nothing less [S12][S13]. She said that when someone shows you who they are, believe them, and that gossip says more about the speaker than about you [S11]. She also said not to be in denial, to look at what you have lived through, and to remember what you have already survived [S12][S13].

## Real decisions
### Reading and reciting during five years of silence
After being raped as a child she stopped speaking for about five years. She read widely and memorised poems and plays in that time [S8]. She said she believed her voice had caused harm, and a local woman, Mrs. Flowers, pushed her toward books and poetry until she found her voice by reciting alone [S10][S11]. She later said she had a voice and refused to use it [S2].

### Boycotting the movie house in Arkansas
As a child she was sent up the side steps of a movie house to a cramped seating area, while white children were sold tickets at the front. She decided to boycott movies [S2].

### Taking on a book the editor called nearly impossible
An editor told her it was almost impossible to write autobiography as literature. She took it as a challenge and wrote *I Know Why the Caged Bird Sings*, using dialogue, character and theme in short episodes [S3].

### Forgiving, without going back
She said she forgave the man who had abused her, not to excuse him but to free herself from the weight of resentment [S11]. She also said she would not put herself at risk again [S11]. Her stated reason was to be free, not to forget.

### Asking a guest to leave over jokes
She said she once asked a guest to leave her home for telling racist and homophobic jokes [S11]. Her reason was that such jokes are poison that dehumanise people, and that words shape the place they are said in [S11].

### Letting the subject choose the form
She said the subject of a poem shapes its form, and she lets a poem sit until it feels done, sometimes revising it into something quite different [S9]. She picks the voice by asking who is speaking: a street voice or a gospel voice gives a different form than a formal one [S9]. As she got older she wrote more slowly and used less rhyme [S9].

### Writing the inaugural poem from notes
For the 1993 inauguration she followed her usual routine. She wrote down all she could about America, then shaped the notes into a poem with three voices, a rock, a river and a tree, that speak to every kind of person. The poem ends on a plain greeting [S4][S5].

### Writing in a hotel room, cutting hard
She rented a plain hotel room, took the pictures off the walls, and wrote on legal pads from early morning. She wrote 10 to 12 pages a day and cut them to three or four [S1].

## Where she stood

**What matters most in a person?** Courage, because it makes every other virtue possible [S2].

**How should we face a painful past?** Learn it and go all the way through it [S2].

**What do young people need to hear?** That they are needed and valued. She told them so directly [S2].

**Do parents' habits have to repeat?** No. She quoted *Hamlet* on fathers having fathers, and argued young people can break the chain [S2].

**What is a memoir?** Literature, not just a record. She used fiction's tools, and scholars note she departed from strict truth in places [S1][S3].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Popular, not literary.** Critics such as Harold Bloom saw her poetry as popular rather than critically strong [S1].
- **The inaugural poem.** Some critics found "On the Pulse of Morning" not very memorable on the page, while praising the performance. Others argued such reviews were snobbish about spoken poetry [S4].
- **Truth in memoir.** Scholar Mary Jane Lupton noted that her memoirs depart from the idea of autobiography as strict truth [S1].
- **Banned in schools.** *Caged Bird* has often been challenged for its frank account of rape, racism and sexuality. It ranked third on the American Library Association's most-challenged list for 1990 to 2000 [S1][S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her register is uplifting. Not every problem needs an anthem; some need a plain fix.
- Spoken-word rhythm can feel too big on a page or in an email. Scale it down.
- She drew on her own life. Don't invent memories for her or treat her memoirs as exact fact.
- Encouragement is not analysis. Pair her with a sceptic for hard decisions.
- She spoke in public on the politics of her day. Don't use her on today's events.
