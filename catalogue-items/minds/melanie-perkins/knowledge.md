## In one paragraph

Perkins grew up in Perth, Western Australia, and while studying at university she taught students how to use design software [S1][S4]. She watched them take a long time to learn the basics and decided design tools would become simple, online and shared [S3]. With Cliff Obrecht she first built Fusion Books, a yearbook design tool for schools, to test the idea in one small market [S1][S3]. In 2012 they pitched investors in San Francisco and were turned down more than 100 times [S1][S3]. With Cameron Adams as a third co-founder, they launched Canva in 2013 [S1][S2]. Canva grew into a design platform used by hundreds of millions of people, valued at US$42 billion in a 2025 share sale [S2]. She describes a two-step plan: build one of the world's most valuable companies, then do as much good as possible [S4]. In 2021 she and Obrecht joined the Giving Pledge [S1].

## Core ideas

### Make the hard thing simple

Her founding idea came from teaching. Students spent so long learning a program's interface that they barely got to design [S3][S4]. She argued people shouldn't need a whole term of study just to learn a tool, and should be able to start creating at once [S4]. As of 2017, she wanted Canva to be usable by anyone, including children [S3].

How she applied it: Canva was built on a drag-and-drop editor with templates, so people could make designs without training [S1][S2]. Its stated aim, in her words, was to empower anyone to design [S4].

### Solve a real problem that many people share

Her first piece of advice to founders in 2017 was to solve a real problem that many people have [S3]. She learned this from pitching. Investors had to be convinced the problem existed and affected a lot of people before they would listen [S3]. She argued that visual communication matters in almost every job, which made the market very large [S3].

How she applied it: in 2018 she listed solving a real problem as her first lesson on growth [S5].

### Go niche before you go wide

She tested her idea in a narrow market first [S3]. Fusion Books let high school students design yearbooks online. It grew in Australia and later ran in New Zealand and France [S1]. Only then did she expand into design for everyone [S3].

How she applied it: Fusion Books proved the drag-and-drop approach and gave her hands-on business experience before Canva [S1][S4].

### Treat rejection as feedback

Perkins counts more than 100 rejections from investors [S1][S3]. She wrote in 2017 that rejection always hurts, but she used each one to improve the pitch [S3]. She said she never seriously considered quitting [S3]. Investors cited the founders' base in Australia, their relationship and their lack of technical backgrounds [S1].

How she applied it: to reach one Silicon Valley investor who ran a kitesurfing-themed event, she learned to kitesurf, though she said it scared her. She saw the discomfort as a small price to pay [S5]. Finding a technical co-founder, Cameron Adams, took more than a year of meetings [S3].

### Set crazy big goals

She says having a large goal is central to who she is [S4]. One of Canva's values tells staff to set crazy big goals and make them happen [S4]. She told an interviewer in 2017 that the company was only just getting started [S3].

How she applied it: Canva expanded into many languages from 2016 [S1], into workplace tools in 2022 meant to compete with Adobe, Google and Microsoft [S1], and into AI design tools from 2022 onward [S2].

### Keep letting go

Her first business was bootstrapped, so she did everything: customer service, testing, product and marketing [S4]. As the company grew, she described her job as a constant process of letting go, handing each task to someone better placed [S4]. Growth also needed new skills, from building an office to leading a large team [S4].

How she applied it: Canva grew to over 1,500 staff by 2022 [S4]. An early investor advised the founders to name one leader, and Obrecht nominated Perkins as CEO [S1].

### A free tier that gives real value

In 2018 she credited Canva's growth in part to a free version that gives users real value [S5]. Canva runs on a freemium model, with paid plans on top [S2].

How she applied it: Canva reached profitability in 2017 with 294,000 paying customers [S2], and reported positive free cash flow each year from 2017 [S1]. It launched Canva for Education in 2019 [S2].

### Build value, then do good

The two-step plan is her long-term frame. Step one is to build one of the world's most valuable companies. Step two is to do as much good as possible [S4].

How she applied it: in 2021 she and Obrecht joined the Giving Pledge and committed most of their wealth through the Canva Foundation [S1]. The foundation backs GiveDirectly's cash transfers to people in extreme poverty in Malawi, with US$150 million committed by October 2025 [S1][S2].

### Look after yourself, and keep perspective

In 2018 she shared a letter she had written to herself. Its point was that everyone is a first-timer, nobody has all the answers, and challenges and mistakes can be a source of drive [S5]. Her routines were simple: writing, sleep, holidays and quiet time [S5].

How she applied it: she said the gap between where Canva was and where it could be was what inspired the team [S5].

## How she decides

- Start from a real problem many people share [S3].
- Ask how fast a beginner gets value, then cut the steps [S4].
- Test in a small market first, then go wide [S3].
- Use each rejection to sharpen the pitch [S3].
- Set a very big goal and break it into next steps [S4].
- Hire the best people and focus on high-impact work [S3].
- Organise the whole company around its goals, and get comfortable with data [S5].
- Hand off tasks as the team grows [S4].
- Build the free version so it gives real value on its own [S5].
- Treat mistakes as fuel, and keep perspective by remembering everyone is a first-timer [S5].

## Real decisions

### Test the idea on school yearbooks first
In 2007 she and Cliff Obrecht started Fusion Books, an online drag-and-drop yearbook maker for schools, rather than a tool for all of design [S1][S6]. Yearbooks were one narrow market with a clear buyer. It grew in Australia and later ran in New Zealand and France [S1]. It proved the drag-and-drop idea before Canva [S3].

### Ask investors for advice, not money
In San Francisco in 2012 she opened meetings by asking for advice [S6]. She revised the deck after each meeting, and many early slides survived the many rewrites [S6]. More than 100 investors said no, citing Australia, the couple's relationship and no technical background [S1]. She kept going and sought the missing skill instead of arguing [S1][S3].

### Watch real users, then add play
Videos of people trying the product showed they were scared to click [S6]. The team added playful first steps, such as putting a hat on a monkey, so a beginner got a quick win [S6]. The choice came from watching users, not from a design theory [S6].

### Name one leader
An investor told the founders to pick a single leader. Obrecht put Perkins forward as CEO [S1]. She tells the story as a team call, and credits co-founders before herself [S6].

### Move to Sydney to find builders
After a technical co-founder, Cameron Adams, joined, the founders moved from Perth to Sydney to hire engineers [S1]. She said Sydney beat Silicon Valley partly because key co-founders were there [S6]. Location was a choice, not a default [S6].

### One priority per year
She used annual company priorities in sequence [S1]. In 2016 it was going international, with six languages added. In 2017 it was rewriting the editor code, which later made video editing possible [S1]. Canva reported positive free cash flow every year from 2017 [S1].

### Free core, paid upgrades
She kept the core product free and charged for extras such as premium images and printing [S6]. She credited the free version, which gives real value, for part of early growth [S5].

## Where she stood

**Do design tools need to be hard?** No. As of 2017 she argued they would become simple, online and shared [S3].

**What should a founder do after many rejections?** As of 2017, keep going and refine the pitch each time [S3].

**Start broad or narrow?** Narrow. As of 2017 her advice was to go niche before you go wide [S3].

**What is a company for?** As of 2022, first to create great value, then to do as much good as possible [S4].

**How should a leader grow?** As of 2022, by letting go of tasks one after another [S4].

**Who should design be for?** As of 2017, anyone, including children. She argued visual communication matters in nearly every job [S3].

**What keeps a founder going?** As of 2018, a big goal, simple routines such as sleep and quiet time, and the gap between where you are and where you could be [S5].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Data security.** In May 2019 a breach exposed data of about 139 million Canva users. In January 2020 about 4 million decrypted passwords were shared online, and Canva reset them [S2].
- **Valuation swings.** Canva's valuation peaked at US$40 billion in 2021, then levelled at US$26 billion in 2022, before a 2025 share sale at US$42 billion [S2].
- **AI costs.** In August 2026 Canva cut its expected yearly revenue growth from 30% to 20%, citing the high cost of running AI features [S2].
- **Early doubts.** Investors doubted the founders because they were in Perth, a couple and not technical. The doubters were wrong about the outcome, but the gap in technical skill was real and took a year to fill [S1][S3].

These are fair points from the record. Her own words give her reasoning, not her critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- Her persistence story is a survivor's story. Many founders face 100 rejections because the idea is weak. Check the problem as well as the grit [S3].
- "Crazy big goals" can stretch a small team too far. Pair her with someone who watches cash and focus.
- Simple tools for beginners can frustrate experts. Ask who might be left behind.
- Her record here is short articles and interviews. Mark inferences beyond them.
- She reports her own pitch story. We have no investor's account of those meetings [S6].
- Fast growth raises the stakes on security and trust, as the 2019 breach showed [S2].
