# Michael Bierut: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Bierut is an American graphic designer, writer and teacher, born in Cleveland in 1957 [S5]. He trained at the University of Cincinnati, spent ten years with Massimo and Lella Vignelli, and became a partner at Pentagram in New York in 1990 [S5][S6]. His clients have included Mastercard, Saks Fifth Avenue, Verizon, Slack, the MIT Media Lab and the Brooklyn Academy of Music [S6]. He designed the "H" logo for Hillary Clinton's 2016 campaign as a volunteer [S1][S6]. He co-founded Design Observer in 2003, teaches at Yale, and has written several books of essays [S5][S6]. His working rules are short: the problem contains the solution, don't avoid the obvious, and indulge your obsessions [S4]. He wants design to take part in the real world, while staying modest about how much power designers have [S2].

## Core ideas

### The problem contains the solution

In a 2009 talk built on drawings from his 86 notebooks, Bierut gave five lessons on design work. The first was that the problem contains the solution [S4]. Read the brief closely and the answer is often already in it.

How he applied it: for the Clinton campaign the problem was that the candidate was already known to everyone. The team's job was to make a familiar figure look fresh, and the brief also asked for a mark that worked on phones, tablets and watches [S1]. Those two needs shaped a small, simple square "H" [S1].

### Don't avoid the obvious

Another of his lessons was not to avoid the obvious [S4]. Designers often fear an idea because it seems too simple. Bierut treats an obvious answer as a strength if it works.

How he applied it: he wanted the Clinton logo to be simple enough for a child to make, and said it was meant to be not clever or artful [S1]. The team looked at dozens of symbols before settling on the H with a forward arrow, set with a clean, bold typeface in upper and lower case [S1].

### Build a system around the mark

Bierut saw the square H as a window that others could fill [S1]. That made it a system, not a fixed picture. The campaign's own design team, led by Jennifer Kinon, used it across websites, videos, GIFs and the #ImWithHer hashtag, and supporters made their own versions in seashells, pumpkins and crochet [S1].

How he applied it: the logo was redrawn in rainbow colours on April 28, 2015, for a Supreme Court case on same-sex marriage, the first of many changes for different occasions [S1]. The Mastercard identity Pentagram describes also works as a system: refined circles, a new type family, a warm grey base and shapes that create patterns [S7].

### Make the familiar fresh

Much identity work starts with something people already know. The challenge is to update it without losing what they recognise [S1][S7].

How he applied it: Pentagram's Mastercard project kept the two overlapping circles used since 1968 but made them crisper and better for screens, with a brighter orange where they overlap. It looked back to a 1979 logo for ideas [S7]. The firm reports that 81% of consumers recognised the new symbol without the name [S7]. A lowercase "c" signalled that the brand was more than a card [S7].

### Indulge your obsessions

The third lesson he named in 2009 was to indulge your obsessions [S4]. He drew and wrote in notebooks for decades, and used them to show his thinking [S4].

How he applied it: for his Yale class he created the "100 Days Project," in which people repeat one creative act every day. Pentagram says it is now done around the world [S6].

### Take part in the real world

In a 2004 discussion Bierut warned against designers who see their job as making other people's content look nice [S2]. Because graphic design has few functional rules, he said, it can shrink into developing a style [S2]. He rejected a choice between selling out to mass commerce and retreating to small, marginal work. He wanted a middle path, with thoughtful designers taking part in bigger issues [S2]. He said he entered design because he wasn't a gifted artist, and liked responding to someone else's idea [S2].

How he applied it: by 2004 more than half his work was for non-profits, which he said can be as demanding as corporate clients [S2]. He argued that after the 2001 attacks, posters and T-shirts were not enough, and designers should engage with root causes [S2].

### Stay modest about design's power

Bierut is wary of designers who think they can steer public opinion. In 2004 he said that feeling is empowering, but in daily work he rarely felt it [S2]. After the 2016 election, he asked whether his expertise had failed, since a campaign with weaker design won [S1]. He concluded that design's power depends on people beyond professionals. The Women's March showed him that individual creativity can unite a movement without a style guide [S1].

How he applied it: when the Clinton logo was mocked at launch, he kept a no-comment policy and let the system prove itself in use [S1].

### Say it plainly, in your own voice

Bierut's first published essay was formal and bland, and he felt it didn't sound like him [S3]. He switched to writing the way he spoke [S3]. His advice to writers is to make sure you have something to say [S3]. Writing, he said, helps designers slow down, test their assumptions and notice what they'd otherwise skip [S3].

How he applied it: his first book, *Seventy-Nine Short Essays on Design* (2007), had 272 pages and no pictures, and set each essay in a different typeface [S3]. He made it a book because designers make things, and the final form matters [S3].

### Learn from a strong mentor

Bierut worked for Massimo Vignelli from 1980 to 1990, rising to vice president [S5][S6]. He has said he learned a great deal from Vignelli about being a good designer [S5]. He noted that Vignelli was a committed modernist, while designers today tend to work through smaller, scattered efforts rather than big movements [S2].

How he applied it: his work often uses Helvetica [S5], and he appeared in the 2007 documentary *Helvetica* [S6].

## How he decides

- Read the brief closely. The answer is often in it [S4].
- Take the obvious answer seriously [S4].
- Make it simple enough for anyone to reproduce [S1].
- Design a flexible system that others can use [S1][S7].
- Keep what people already recognise [S7].
- Ride out early criticism and let use prove the design [S1].
- Write it down plainly, in your own voice [S3].

## Where he stood

**What is graphic design for?** As of 2004, taking part in the real world, beyond styling other people's content [S2].

**Commerce or the margins?** Neither. He wanted a middle path [S2].

**Can design win an election?** In 2017 he doubted it. People, not designers, give design its power [S1].

**Should a logo be clever?** Not necessarily. The Clinton mark was meant to be simple and usable [S1].

**Why write?** To slow down and think, and because the final form matters [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The Clinton logo.** At launch it drew heavy mockery. Critics compared it to a hospital sign and the Cuban flag, and called it a PowerPoint design [S1][S5]. Opinion later improved as the system was used [S1].
- **Polish versus authenticity.** Bierut himself noted that one critic found the homemade look of the Sanders campaign more authentic than the polished Clinton style [S1].
- **Design's limits.** He openly asked whether his expertise had failed after the 2016 result [S1].
- **Thin outside critique.** Our sources are mostly his own writing and his firm's pages [S1][S6][S7]. A fuller critical review was not in what we could open.

## Blind spots to watch

- His work is for large brands and institutions with budgets and design teams. A small business may need a cheaper, faster route.
- A simple mark can look bland at launch. Not every client can wait out a backlash.
- His cultural examples are often American. Check them against your own audience.
- Pair him with a strategist or a brand thinker when the question is positioning, not form.
