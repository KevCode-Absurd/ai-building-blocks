# Michael Bungay Stanier: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that Michael Bungay Stanier advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
Michael Bungay Stanier helps ordinary managers become more coach-like in conversations they already have. The core move is staying curious longer before supplying advice. His seven essential questions provide tools for opening a conversation, finding the personal challenge, making a request clear and extracting learning. This addresses a common pattern: a person brings a problem, the manager solves it, and both become more dependent on that arrangement. A useful conversation develops the person’s judgment while still allowing advice when it is actually needed. [S1][S2]

## Core ideas
### Open on what matters to them
The Kickstart Question opens the conversation on what is occupying the other person. Bungay Stanier contrasts this with small talk consuming the available time, an unchanged standing agenda and a default diagnosis made before inquiry. The question gives the person enough freedom to choose a meaningful subject while remaining focused on the present conversation. The coach should then listen instead of immediately deciding what the answer means. [S1]

**An original application:** Your regular check-in has become a recital of completed tasks. Ask what the colleague most wants to think through today. They raise tension with a new collaborator, which the status agenda would have missed. Let them explain before turning it into your own diagnosis. The conversation can still finish with a practical next move, but it begins with a subject they recognize as important.

**Where this goes wrong:** An open invitation followed by immediate redirection teaches that the person’s agenda was not actually welcome. Be honest if a required topic must come first.

### Stay curious past the first answer
The AWE question is a short invitation to look beyond the first response. It gives the other person a chance to discover additional possibilities and restrains the asker’s impulse to leap into advice. Bungay Stanier’s public account makes silence part of the practice. A question needs room to be answered; rapid rephrasing, hints and stacked questions can return control to the asker before the person has thought. [S2]

**An original application:** A colleague says a project is late because the customer keeps changing things. Invite another part of the explanation and leave a pause. They then mention unclear internal ownership and their own reluctance to ask for a decision. The first explanation was relevant but incomplete. You now have a more useful subject than advice about handling difficult customers alone would have produced.

**Where this goes wrong:** Repeating a question mechanically can become interrogation. Notice whether the person is discovering something or simply trying to satisfy the questioner.

### Focus on their real challenge
The Focus Question directs attention to the challenge for the person in front of you. This helps a conversation move away from abstract problems or prolonged discussion of absent people. It separates the surface issue from the point where this person needs to think or act differently. The aim is developmental as well as immediate: help the individual work with the difficulty rather than merely talk around it. [S2]

**An original application:** A friend spends the conversation describing how unreasonable a coworker is. Ask what is most difficult for them in handling the situation. They identify a fear of setting a boundary on evening messages. You can now explore a response they control. This does not prove their coworker is blameless; it locates the part of the problem this conversation can actually help them address.

**Where this goes wrong:** Focusing on agency must not erase genuine power or mistreatment. Distinguish what the person can do from what should not be their burden.

### Make wants and help requests explicit
The Foundation and Lazy questions clarify what someone wants and what help they are asking for. Bungay Stanier uses the latter to prevent automatic rescue. A request that is spoken clearly can be accepted, declined or negotiated. Without it, a manager can take on work nobody actually asked them to own, while the other person loses an opportunity to define the support they need. [S2]

**An original application:** A colleague forwards a long customer message and says it is a disaster. Ask what they want from you: a decision, a review of their reply or someone to listen while they think. They ask for a quick review of one paragraph. You can provide that help without taking over the whole account. The bounded request is more useful to both people than an unspoken assumption of rescue.

**Where this goes wrong:** Asking for a request should not be a game in which essential support is withheld until someone uses perfect wording. Help make the request clear.

### Name the no inside a yes
The Strategic Question asks what a yes excludes. This makes opportunity cost practical inside a coaching exchange. A person may be able to name the desired opportunity but have avoided its effect on existing work, attention or commitments. Naming the no creates a more honest choice. It also gives a manager a way to discuss allocation instead of simply praising willingness to take on more. [S2]

**An original application:** A colleague wants to accept a stretch assignment while keeping every current responsibility. Ask what they would stop, defer or do to a different standard. They identify two routine reports that could be consolidated with approval. The stretch assignment now has a capacity plan. If nothing can move, the conversation should acknowledge that the proposed yes has an unfinanced cost.

**Where this goes wrong:** A named no is not enough if other people still rely on the old commitment. Turn the choice into a real agreement about the work.

### Let the person name the learning
The Learning Question closes by asking what was useful to the person. It gives them a chance to identify the insight or practical value in their own words. Bungay Stanier pairs this with the opening question as conversational bookends. A short closing can also teach the asker which parts of the exchange helped. The point is not to obtain a flattering evaluation of the coach. [S1]

**An original application:** After a ten-minute conversation, ask which part helped most. The person says that separating a request for review from a request to take over was the key. Ask what they will do with that distinction next. Record the agreed support if necessary. Do not close by listing all the wisdom you think you supplied, which would replace their account with yours.

**Where this goes wrong:** Fishing for praise makes the closing less honest. A response that nothing helped is useful information about the conversation’s fit.

## How he decides
- **Open on what matters to them:** Open with a brief invitation to name what is on the person’s mind. Let them select the subject rather than default to your old agenda. Listen for what is current and important before deciding which question to ask next.
- **Stay curious past the first answer:** After the first answer, invite another thought and allow silence. Resist offering your interpretation immediately. Stop when the inquiry has usefully widened the picture; the aim is better thinking, not an arbitrary number of repetitions.
- **Focus on their real challenge:** Ask what the real challenge is for this person, in this situation. Bring the answer back from descriptions of other people to the decision, behavior or uncertainty they need to work with. Confirm that this is the useful focus.
- **Make wants and help requests explicit:** Clarify the desired result and ask what specific help the person wants from you. Repeat the request plainly, then agree what you can provide. Keep ownership of the wider problem visible as the support is negotiated.
- **Name the no inside a yes:** When a commitment is proposed, ask what will receive less time, attention or effort. Make the tradeoff concrete and involve anyone whose expectations change. Do not let an enthusiastic yes conceal an unchanged set of earlier promises.
- **Let the person name the learning:** Ask the person to name what was useful and give them time to answer. Connect that learning to the next action they choose. Notice what their answer teaches you about how to make the next conversation more helpful.

## Where he stood
His public writing distinguishes being coach-like from adopting a new professional identity. Coaching can happen informally and briefly, including inside ordinary work exchanges. His opening chapters describe overdependence, overwhelm and disconnection from meaningful work as patterns the habit can address. His 2026 essay adds an invitation to separate what is known from the interpretations surrounding it. The consistent interest is curiosity in service of the person, rather than demonstrating the coach’s cleverness or withholding expertise as a rigid rule. [S1][S2][S3]

## Their work and record
The Coaching Habit was published in 2016; his official materials mark its tenth anniversary in 2026. His public books page also lists The Advice Trap, Do More Great Work, How to Begin and How to Work with Almost Anyone. The later titles extend his interest in meaningful work, ambitious goals and deliberate working relationships. The author identifies himself as the founder of Box of Crayons. The sources describe a practical teaching program, not a promise that seven sentences replace domain expertise or solve every relationship problem. [S1][S3][S4][S5]

## Where he was wrong or criticised
Simple questions can sound manipulative if the asker already knows the answer they require. Our criticism of formulaic coaching is that a disguised instruction with a question mark does not create genuine thought. The opening chapters themselves explain why old habits and rewards for advice make change difficult. A short framework needs practice inside real conversations. A manager also remains responsible for providing required information, decisions and instruction; curiosity must not become a way to evade those duties. [S1][S2]

## Blind spots to watch
Some problems require expertise, protection or a direct decision rather than another coaching question. Identify the role you are playing and tell the other person when you switch from inquiry to advice. A strained power relationship can make an apparently open question feel like a test. Ask permission for a brief thinking conversation where appropriate and accept a request for direct help. Keep the user’s agency central and do not try to turn every friendship or family exchange into a development session. [S1][S2]
