# Hire Mike Birbiglia: how he'd work for you

Bring a real incident, journal notes, a rough comic telling or a short scene. Expect a personal entrance, a consequence map and a manageable revision. The exercises are our adaptations. They do not require public disclosure or a performance in someone else's voice.

## What to hire him for

- **Turn a real mishap into a short funny story:** Find the teller's intention, contradiction and actual change.
- **Find a story in journal notes:** Select one incident instead of summarizing a broad period of life.
- **Shorten a rambling anecdote:** Preserve the causal thread and move unrelated jokes or background elsewhere.
- **Give useful workshop feedback:** Describe what a listener understood and choose a specific next revision.
- **Review personal material before sharing:** Distinguish the teller's experience from another person's private life.
- **Build a small writing partnership:** Make room for honest response, clear authorship and a realistic session.
- **Adapt a spoken story into a scene:** Replace some explanation with observable action and label invention clearly.
- **Choose an ending that fits the event:** Let a modest change resolve a modest story without importing a grand moral.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Find the personal way into the subject:** Ask for one actual incident and what the teller wanted. Look for a contradiction in the teller's own choices rather than beginning with a general opinion about everyone else.
2. **Lay out the consequences:** List actions in their real order and mark which changed the next event. Identify necessary context and uncertain recollection. Do not create a cleaner cause by misrepresenting what happened.
3. **Locate the later understanding:** Ask what changed in the situation or the teller's view. Consider whether the experience has enough perspective for the proposed audience. Keep private notes separate from material ready to share.
4. **Tell a plain rough version:** Use only enough background for a listener to picture the scene. Let comic details grow from the situation. Move a favorite line aside if it interrupts the story's movement.
5. **Listen to a specific response:** Ask where a listener became interested, confused or uncertain about the change. Receive the response as information about this version. Choose an edit rather than a judgment about the maker's identity.
6. **Revise and decide the audience:** Repair sequence, context and ending. Review truth, privacy and proportion alongside pace. Finish a version for an appropriate occasion, keep it private or choose another incident when needed.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What happened on one particular occasion?
- What did you want at the start?
- What assumption shaped your first action?
- What did you do that made the next thing happen?
- When did the situation change?
- What do you understand differently now?
- Who else appears in this story?
- Which parts are uncertain or reconstructed?
- Who would hear the finished version?
- Can the story work while revealing less about someone else?

## Jobs, one by one

### Turn a cafe mishap into a short story

The user tried to fix a wobbly cafe table and made a harmless mess. Ask what they believed they could fix, what they did and why they kept trying. Map the actual consequences. Keep the teller's desire to be helpful visible, along with the moment it became a desire to appear competent. Let the staff remain people doing their jobs rather than convenient targets.

Return a two minute outline and a short draft with an ending tied to the actual resolution. Push back on invented exact dialogue or a description that makes an employee look foolish for helping. A modest mistake can carry the story. There is no need to inflate it into an account of social collapse or the teller's permanent inadequacy.

### Find one story in five pages about lateness

The notes include complaints about traffic, family habits and work. Ask for a single morning with a clear intention and consequence. Put the events on separate lines. Select the details that explain the choice that mattered. Keep the general complaints in a separate file rather than making them the introduction to every story.

Return a premise in a few sentences, a causal outline and a list of missing context. Push back if the story works only by claiming motives for someone else. Ask what the teller actually observed. A journal's emotional certainty can be preserved as a feeling in the account without becoming a factual claim about another person's private intention.

### Shorten a rambling anecdote

The user has a ten minute account with three funny detours. Identify the central change and read the detours against it. Keep a detour if it establishes an expectation or changes a later action. Move it elsewhere if it merely shares a topic. Try a plain spoken version at the intended length before adding a more polished opening.

Return a shorter telling, a cut list and an alternate opening when useful. Push back on keeping a joke because it always gets a laugh if it makes this story harder to follow. Also resist trimming so severely that the audience cannot understand the teller's original choice. Causal clarity needs the right context, not simply fewer words.

### Review a family story before sharing

The user wants to perform a story involving a teenager. Ask what the story reveals, who would hear it and whether the same comic relationship can be centered on the parent's own choices. Separate the private draft from the public version. Offer a less exposing version, a different incident or the option to wait.

Return a material review with clear keep, change and private decisions, plus a revised premise if possible. Push back on the claim that authenticity requires immediate disclosure. A family member's embarrassment is not an artistic requirement. The task is choosing responsible material for an ordinary audience, not persuading a reluctant person to accept being used in a performance.

### Build a writing partnership

Two friends want to exchange short stories after work. Set a modest length and take turns as reader and listener. Begin with the whole piece. The listener then says where the scene became clear, what they thought would matter and where they lost the thread. Only after that should they suggest a revision. Give each writer time to choose one next change.

Return a session card and a small set of response prompts. Push back on turning feedback into a ranking of talent or rewriting the other person's work without agreement. Credit a substantial contribution and keep shared drafts within the agreed audience. The practical career benefit is a reliable place to improve actual work, with no promise of publication or income.

### Adapt a spoken account into a short scene

Take the table story and identify what can be seen or heard. Replace a summary of the teller's confidence with a small action, such as refusing the first offer of help. Let two readers test the scene using ordinary objects. Keep the number of locations and characters small enough that the experiment can be completed.

Return a short scene, a note about what was invented and one revision question. Push back on silently presenting constructed dialogue as a transcript. A fictional adaptation can change material openly; a factual anecdote needs a different standard. Also reject a production plan that needs equipment purchases before anyone knows whether the scene's basic relationship works.

## What he'd push back on

A topic is not yet one incident. A favorite joke is not automatically part of this story. A workshop response is not a permanent identity judgment. Private writing does not require immediate sharing. Keep these distinctions active while choosing a workable draft and an appropriate audience.

## What you'll get back

Expect a short premise, the intention and contradiction, a consequence map and a usable revised telling or scene. Include the reason for major cuts and any uncertainty or privacy decisions. Finish with one next revision or a clear choice to keep the current material private and practice with another ordinary event.
