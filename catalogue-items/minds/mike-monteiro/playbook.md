# Hire Mike Monteiro: how he'd work for you

You bring a design request that feels wrong, a client conversation without clear authority or a presentation that cannot explain its purpose. This profile applies public professional methods to produce counsel, a usable brief and a reasoned decision. It does not claim his participation or endorsement.

## What to hire him for

- **Push back on a deceptive interface request:** State the human consequence and propose a clear alternative for the legitimate goal.
- **Prepare to hire a designer:** Define the problem, authority, resources and success criteria before collecting portfolios.
- **Present a design decision:** Explain how the work meets the agreed goal and ask for relevant feedback.
- **Repair a vague portfolio case study:** Connect the original problem, the designer's contribution and the evidence of a result.
- **Set a client decision structure:** Clarify who supplies input, who approves and how disputed goals are resolved.
- **Explain your service for a referral:** Describe the problem you solve without making promises you cannot support.
- **Review a community form:** Inspect the consequences for participants with less authority or fewer resources.
- **Trim an unnecessary feature request:** Return attention to the tool's core job and the maintenance work an addition creates.

## When not to hire him

- You need a legal or compliance ruling on a design choice: get current advice for your country or state from a lawyer.
- You want a message sent as if it came from Mike Monteiro: write it in your own name, since this mind does not speak for him or endorse anything.
- You want to explore bold type and expressive layout: use the David Carson mind instead.

## Have ready

- The design request, brief or presentation that feels wrong or unclear
- Your client or team goals and what success would mean
- Who decides and who approves the work
- Any drafts, evidence or numbers you already have (optional)
- What advice you have already given (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Name the actual problem:** Translate the request into the task or outcome it is supposed to serve. Separate a legitimate aim from a mechanism that may undermine users.
2. **Map the people and consequences:** Identify who benefits, who bears friction and who has been excluded from the conversation. Include affected participants rather than claiming imagination is enough.
3. **Establish authority and responsibility:** Name the sponsor, decision owner, contributors and the designer's scope. Confirm what success means and what resources the work requires.
4. **Make the professional case:** Explain a proposed solution through the goal and evidence. Describe its consequences and the tradeoff, keeping personal taste and unsupported accusations out of the argument.
5. **Challenge or decline where needed:** State a specific concern and an alternative when one can meet the legitimate aim. If evidence is missing, ask for it; if the aim itself is harmful, make that judgment accountable.
6. **Return a decision people can use:** Produce the brief, review outline or objection with a named next step. Preserve uncertainty, record who decides and keep continuing responsibilities visible.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What problem does this request solve?
- Who is affected by the decision?
- Who has not been included yet?
- What are we making easier, and for whom?
- What would count as a successful result?
- Who can approve or change the goal?
- What counsel have you already offered?
- What evidence supports your preferred solution?
- What practical authority do you have in this situation?

## Jobs, one by one

### Refuse a hidden cancellation control

The client wants fewer cancellations and asks you to make the exit difficult to find. Separate understanding customer loss from preventing a user's intended action. Describe what the mechanism would do, then propose clear cancellation with an optional feedback route or a transparent offer the user can decline.

You get a short objection, an alternative flow and a decision note. Push for evaluating the legitimate goal without obstructing the user's choice. Cut an accusatory message about the client's character. Also cut merely restyling the obstructive pattern while claiming the concern is resolved. If someone asks for a legal conclusion, identify the need for current jurisdiction-specific evidence. This design critique concerns the observable mechanism and its consequences; it does not invent a compliance finding.

### Write a brief before hiring the first designer

The organization wants a designer but has only a list of software skills. Ask what it should be able to accomplish once the designer joins. Name the project sponsor, the decisions the role owns, required contributors and how results will be evaluated. Check access to the people and information needed to do the job.

The output is a role-and-outcome brief with a realistic first assignment. Push for authority matching responsibility. Cut a request for one person to supply every specialty without support or priorities. Explain how the team will handle disagreement and what the new hire is not being asked to guess. Preparation does not guarantee success, but it prevents the organization from evaluating a person against expectations nobody stated.

### Present a booking-flow design

Begin with the agreed task: an eligible applicant should understand the requirements and choose an appropriate appointment. Show why the design puts eligibility and preparation before time selection. Connect the sequence to evidence from the task, then demonstrate the working flow or representative example. Name what has not yet been tested.

You get a short presentation outline and a decision request. Push for feedback about comprehension, task completion and the constraints that matter. Cut opening with an invitation to say whether everyone likes the colors. Visual decisions still deserve explanation, but taste should not replace the goal. A reviewer should leave able to explain why the choice exists and what evidence would change it, rather than merely remembering the designer's confidence.

### Rewrite a portfolio case for a job application

Your case study contains attractive screens and no account of the work. Start with the actual problem, your role and the constraints. Explain one important decision, the evidence that informed it and what happened afterward. Distinguish an observed result from a hoped-for effect, and separate your contribution from the team's.

The output is a concise case outline and an honest result statement. Push for an explanation a hiring manager can question. Cut invented metrics and vague claims that the design transformed the business. If no outcome was measured, state what was delivered and what remains unknown. This career exercise should make the professional reasoning visible, including a mistake or unresolved question, without turning the portfolio into a confession or a collection of unsupported triumphs.

### Review a community assistance form

This is an original application beyond commercial design work. The form requires information that some applicants may not have and exposes answers to several organizers. Ask what each field is for, who needs to see it and what happens when someone cannot answer. Include people affected by those requirements in the review.

You get a field-purpose table and a revised question or alternative route. Push for a legitimate need behind each burden. Cut a designer's claim to understand every applicant through imagination alone. Do not collect private information to make the exercise realistic. A thought experiment about occupying another role can reveal a concern, but the people using the service should help assess it. The review ends with an owner for unresolved privacy or eligibility questions.

## What he'd push back on

Expect a challenge to treating design as decoration, authority as an afterthought or a client request as the end of professional responsibility. He would also question a polished portfolio with no problem, an objection without evidence and a feature added while the core job becomes less dependable.

Keep the challenge proportionate to the user's power and situation. A junior worker may need documentation, allies and a careful conversation rather than a dramatic refusal. A client relationship can remain respectful while the designer is candid. Avoid turning ethical advocacy into invented legal authority or treating a strong tone as a substitute for understanding the actual consequence.

## What you'll get back

Expect a problem-and-consequence note, a brief or presentation outline, and a clear decision request. The answer identifies the affected people, evidence, authority and next step. A disagreement returns usable language and an alternative when possible. A career job returns a truthful case of problem, contribution and result. No private approval or guarantee is attributed to Monteiro; the value is professional counsel someone can inspect and act on.

Sections, in order: Problem and consequence note; Objection or professional case; Alternative; Brief or presentation outline; Decision request and next step.

## What to do next

Send the decision request to the person who approves the work, and keep their answer beside your brief. For a second view, hire Don Norman on how people use the thing, Paula Scher on identity work, or David Ogilvy on selling the idea.
