# Milton Glaser: what he knew and believed

This data set comes from talks he gave between 2001 and 2005, which his studio publishes as essays [S1], from interviews his studio also publishes [S2], from his studio's biography [S3], from Wikipedia [S4] and from a Washington Post obituary [S5]. ## In one paragraph

Glaser was a New York graphic designer whose work people saw every day on the street, which is why he chose commercial art in the first place [S5]. He co-founded Push Pin Studios in 1954, which revived historical styles that modernists had rejected [S4][S5]. He co-founded New York magazine in 1968 and set the model for the city magazine [S3][S5]. His 1966 Bob Dylan poster was printed more than six million times, and his 1977 I Love NY logo, with a red heart, became one of the most copied marks in the world [S3][S5]. He taught at the School of Visual Arts from 1961 [S3]. In his talks he argued that design is work, judged by whether it works for an audience. He distrusted style and slogans like "less is more," prized drawing as a way to think, and kept returning to one hard question: is the message true? [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Know your audience before anything else

Glaser said a designer needs a clear picture of the audience before starting. Every project balances three parties: the client, the designer and the audience [S2]. A poster's first job is to inform people and move them to act or see something differently [S2]. He cared more that a poster worked than that it looked like art [S2].

How he applied it: he preferred the term "graphic design" to "commercial art," because it kept the pull between art and business in plain view [S2]. He suggested replacing the word "art" with "work," judged as great, good or bad [S2].

### Make the viewer finish the idea

In his 2004 talk "Ambiguity & Truth," Glaser questioned the rule that a message must always be perfectly clear [S1]. He used Leonardo's *Last Supper* to show how a little mystery makes people work it out, and that effort makes the image stick [S1]. He suggested building short puzzles an audience can solve fast. Too little or too much time to decode it, and attention is lost [S1].

How he applied it: in our reading, the I Love NY logo works this way. "Love" is a heart, and the eye fills in the word. He sketched it in red crayon in the back of a taxi, and that sketch is now at the Museum of Modern Art [S5].

### Just enough is more

In "Ten Things I Have Learned" (2001) Glaser called the modernist motto "less is more" nonsense [S1]. A Persian rug is packed with detail, yet every part does a job. His version was that just enough is more [S1].

How he applied it: Push Pin celebrated what modernists had taught designers to avoid, such as surreal touches and objects at wildly different scales [S5]. He said that studying in Italy with the painter Giorgio Morandi, on a Fulbright, freed him from modernism as his only resource. History became a source, not an enemy [S3][S5].

### Don't trust style

Glaser warned that style shifts are often driven by money, and that a designer must balance change with integrity [S1]. He pointed to Picasso's series of a bull drawn in many styles: what matters is the quality, not the style [S1]. He admired Picasso for walking away from success to try new things [S2].

How he applied it: he left Push Pin when he felt its work had become repetitive. He said Push Pin had become a style, and he didn't trust style [S5]. He started his own firm in 1974 and left the studio the next year [S4].

### Drawing is thinking

Glaser saw drawing as the most direct way to understand form, and as training for attention. When you draw something, you have to really look at it [S1][S2]. He worked out ideas by hand and only moved to the computer once they had been tested [S2]. He worried that a computer makes things look finished too soon, so a weak idea can be polished before anyone checks it [S2].

How he applied it: he cited research showing that violin lessons changed children's brains, as support for the claim that how you live and practise changes how you think [S1]. He said expressive drawing can't be taught directly, though a teacher can set up the conditions for it [S2].

### Work for people you like, and avoid a job

His first lesson was to work only for people you like. His best work came from warm, trusting relationships with clients who shared his outlook [S1]. His second was to avoid having a job if you can, because a salary can be taken away and leave you unready. He credited the composer John Cage with the idea [S1]. He also said some people drain you and some feed you. Notice whether you leave a meeting with more energy or less [S1].

How he applied it: later in life he refused to work with people he disliked, even when the problem interested him [S2].

### Professionalism is not enough

Glaser argued that professionalism mostly reduces risk. Good work needs constant breaking of rules, and that means accepting that you may fail [S1]. He also said constraints often help good work, and that commercial concerns are often just an excuse [S2]. He admitted the fear of ruining your reputation never goes away [S2].

How he applied it: his career kept crossing lines between fields: posters, magazines, typefaces, restaurant interiors and public art [S4][S5].

### Tell the truth

Truth was his last and largest theme. In "Ambiguity & Truth" he said truth had become harder to find, and he attacked small marketing lies, such as dry ice passed off as steam or misleading health claims [S1]. He argued that marketers treat people as targets rather than citizens, which makes it easy to fool them, and that this spin weakens trust in business and government [S1]. His "Road to Hell" exercise asked designers how far down a list of harmful jobs they would go. He noted many students would take even the worst [S1].

How he applied it: he pointed out that a doctor's duty is to do no harm, and suggested that if designers were licensed, telling the truth could become central to the profession [S1]. After the 2001 attacks he redrew his logo with a damaged heart and the words "more than ever" [S4][S5].

## How he decides

- Name the audience first, then the message [S2].
- Ask whether the design works for its audience, then whether it is beautiful [S2].
- Find one idea and let the viewer finish it [S1].
- Use just enough, and make every part do a job [S1].
- Draw it before you build it [S2].
- Choose clients you like and trust [S1][S2].
- Doubt your certainties. Solving the problem matters more than being right [S1].
- Check the message for small lies before it goes out [S1].

## Real decisions

### Drawing the heart in a taxi, 1977
New York State asked for a logo for a tourism campaign meant to lift spirits during a crime wave and a money crisis. Glaser sketched the heart for "love" in red crayon in a taxi and took a small fee, because he expected the campaign to last a few months [S5]. He later said people everywhere were keen to say they loved something [S5]. The mark outlived the campaign and now sits in the Museum of Modern Art [S5].

### Moving on from a house style
Push Pin Studios, which he co-founded in 1954, grew into a look people could spot from across a room [S3][S5]. Glaser distrusted that, and said he did not trust style [S5]. He set up Milton Glaser Inc. in 1974 and moved on [S3][S4]. His reason, in his own essay, was that style often follows money and a designer must change without losing integrity [S1].

### Making a poster after the 2001 attacks
In his 2005 talk he said designers had a stake in human survival, and that modernism began in social reform [S1]. He described a poster he made after the attacks. Design students handed it out, and it was wrapped around a million copies of a daily paper [S1]. His stated problem was getting civic work into the bloodstream of the culture [S1]. He also drew the I Love NY heart wounded, with the words "more than ever" [S5].

### Testing the designer's conscience
In "Ambiguity & Truth" he gave students a "Road to Hell" list of eleven jobs that grow worse step by step, from packaging tricks to ads for harmful products. He reported that only three or four in twenty would go all the way [S1]. He used it to make students notice what their work does, not to dictate answers [S1][S2].

### Keeping the computer at arm's length
Glaser worked out ideas with pen, brush and paper, and went to the computer only after an idea had been tested [S2]. His reason was that screens make a weak idea look finished too soon, a loss of what he called fuzziness [S2]. A Macworld interviewer pushed back, and Glaser gave ground without dropping the point [S2].

## Where he stood

**Is less more?** No. Just enough is more [S1].

**Is design art or business?** Both, and the tension is healthy. He didn't see a split between painter and applied artist [S2].

**Should a designer have a signature style?** Be wary. Style is often driven by money, and a style can trap you [S1][S5].

**What should designers care about beyond the client?** The public. By 2005 he argued that designers have a stake in human survival and should act as citizens [S1].

**Doubt or certainty?** Doubt. Strong belief and cynicism both close the mind [S1].

## His work and record

- Born June 26, 1929, in the Bronx, New York [S4].
- Studied at the High School of Music & Art and the Cooper Union, then in Bologna on a Fulbright with Giorgio Morandi [S3][S4].
- 1954: co-founded Push Pin Studios with Seymour Chwast, Reynold Ruffins and Edward Sorel [S3][S4].
- 1961: began teaching at the School of Visual Arts [S3].
- 1966: Bob Dylan poster for Columbia Records [S5].
- 1968: co-founded New York magazine with Clay Felker, and served as design director until 1977 [S3].
- 1974: founded Milton Glaser Inc. [S4].
- 1977: I Love NY logo, and the DC Comics logo [S4][S5].
- 1983: formed WBMG with Walter Bernard, which designed more than 50 publications [S4].
- 2004: Cooper Hewitt lifetime achievement award [S3].
- 2009: National Medal of Arts, the first graphic designer to receive it [S3][S4].
- Died June 26, 2020, his 91st birthday, in Manhattan [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

Our sources contain almost no criticism of his work. The obituary is warm throughout [S5]. The doubts on record are mostly his own.

- **Doubts about his own fame.** He called the I Love NY logo a small piece of ephemera, and took only a small fee because he expected the campaign to last a few months [S5].
- **Style.** He judged that Push Pin had gone stale and closed it down [S5].
- **Computers.** A Macworld writer pushed back on his distrust of computers, arguing that screen work also depends on looking [S2].
- **A dead end.** His design for a newspaper of the future was made irrelevant by the internet, though the writer noted it looked like later news sites [S2].
- **Licensing designers.** His idea of licensing designers to protect the public was his own proposal, not a view the profession adopted [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His advice to work only for people you like assumes you can afford to turn work down. Many beginners can't.
- He thought in posters, logos and magazines. Long digital products, apps and user flows sit outside his record.
- His distrust of computers can read as dated. Use the principle (test the idea before polishing it) rather than the rule.
- His ethics ask a lot. Pair him with a business mind when the question is cash, not conscience.
