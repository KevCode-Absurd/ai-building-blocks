# Naval Ravikant: what he knew and believed

This data set rests on the full text of *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant* (2020), the free book in which Eric Jorgenson collected his tweets, podcast talks and essays [S6], and on his 2018 "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)" thread [S1]. ## In one paragraph

Ravikant moved from New Delhi to New York as a child, studied computer science and economics at Dartmouth, and co-founded the review site Epinions in 1999 [S5]. His 2007 blog on startup funding terms grew into AngelList, which he co-founded in 2010, and he made early investments in companies such as Twitter and Uber [S5]. His best-known ideas are about building wealth without relying on luck: own equity instead of renting out your time; build "specific knowledge" that can't be taught in a classroom; take public accountability; use leverage, especially code and media, which need nobody's permission; and play long-term games with long-term people [S1]. He also argues that judgment matters more than effort once you have leverage [S3], and that happiness is a skill you can learn [S2].

## Core ideas

### Make luck more likely

Ravikant splits luck into four kinds [S1]. Blind luck just happens. Luck from hustle comes from trying many things and bumping into chances. Luck from preparation comes from being ready to spot a chance others miss. The last kind comes from building a character and reputation so distinct that opportunities seek you out [S1].

How he applied it: his thread aims to make getting rich less about the first kind and more about the last [S1]. The way to get there is the rest of his list: specific knowledge, accountability and leverage [S1].

### Wealth, money and status are different things

Ravikant defines wealth as assets that keep earning while you sleep: businesses, products, intellectual property [S1]. Money is how we move wealth around, a record of value you've created for others [S1]. Status is your rank in a social pecking order, and it is zero-sum: for one person to go up, another must go down [S1]. He argues that chasing status leads people to attack those who create wealth [S1].

How he applied it: he frames getting rich as becoming the kind of person who makes money, not as one lucky win [S1].

### Own equity, don't rent out your time

Ravikant argues that you can't earn in a non-linear way when you're paid by the hour [S1]. To get rich you need to own a piece of a business, a product or your own work, so the returns can grow without your hours growing with them [S1].

How he applied it: his own path went from founding Epinions to investing early in startups and building AngelList to connect startups with investors [S5].

### Build specific knowledge

Specific knowledge is know-how that is specific to you and hard to train [S1]. In the book he adds that it cannot be taught but can be learned [S6]. To find it, look at what you did almost without effort as a child or teenager, something you didn't count as a skill but people around you noticed [S6]. His list of examples runs from sales skill and an ear for any instrument to an obsessive memory, a love of science fiction, and even gossip, which could make a good journalist [S6]. It is usually found by following your genuine curiosity, not by picking whatever is hot [S1][S6]. Because it can't easily be taught, it can't easily be copied, and that is what makes it valuable [S1].

How he applied it: he wanted to be a scientist, but his mother told him in his teens he would go into business, and he later saw that his real edge was selling, working out how to make money, and absorbing data fast [S6]. He says venture investing suits him because he learns quickly and gets bored fast [S6]. He also warns that you need foundations first (simple arithmetic, plain English, persuasion) and depth in one or two things [S6].


Ravikant argues that competing on what everyone else is doing drags you into small games [S1]. The way out is to offer what only you can offer. Nobody can compete with you on being you [S1].

How he applied it: he tells people to productise themselves, turning their own skills, name and judgment into the product [S1].

### Use leverage, especially the kind that needs no permission

In the book he names three classes of leverage: labour, capital, and products with no marginal cost of replication, meaning code and media [S6]. He calls labour the oldest form and the worst in the modern world, because managing people is messy and takes strong leadership [S6]. Capital scales well, but someone has to hand it to you [S6]. Code and media are permissionless: you need no one's say-so to write software or publish [S6]. He puts it as leveraged versus un-leveraged, a split he says now matters more than rich versus poor [S6].

How he applied it: he says a leveraged worker can out-produce an un-leveraged one by a thousand times or more, so for such a worker judgment counts for more than hours [S6]. He wants to be paid for his judgment and have a robot, capital or computer do the work [S6].

He pairs leverage with accountability. Ravikant argues that you should take on risk under your own name [S1]. Being answerable for results builds trust and reputation, and reputation brings you chances that others don't get [S1].

### Judgment beats effort

In his view, hard work is overrated in a world of leverage, though you still need to put in time [S3]. Once you have leverage, your judgment decides whether all that force goes the right way [S3]. He defines wisdom as knowing the long-term results of what you do, and judgment as wisdom applied to real problems [S3]. With leverage, one correct choice can win everything [S3].

How he applied it: he advises picking the right direction to walk in, then starting to walk, because direction matters more than speed [S3]. Judgment grows from many quick tries, emotional restraint and wide reading, including philosophy [S1].

### Judgment and clear thinking
He prefers the compliment "clear thinker" to "smart" and says that if you cannot explain something to a child you do not know it [S6]. He wants you to rebuild ideas from the basics, not memorise advanced terms [S6]. He pairs this with a stress on seeing reality: desire and ego cloud it, and suffering is the moment you can no longer deny it [S6]. For big decisions he says to drop memory and identity and focus on the problem [S6]. He values decision quality above effort: someone right 80 percent of the time instead of 70 is worth many times more once leveraged [S6].

How he applied it: he collects mental models from evolution, game theory, Charlie Munger and Nassim Taleb, and uses inversion, trying to rule out what will not work instead of predicting what will [S6]. He calls the principal-agent problem the most basic one in microeconomics [S6].

He says the means of learning are abundant and the desire to learn is scarce [S3][S6]. His advice is to read what you love until you love to read, and to re-read great books more than you chase new ones [S6]. He admits he skims and cannot recall passages, but trusts that books weave into how he thinks [S6].

### Play long-term games with long-term people

Ravikant holds that the big returns in life, in wealth, relationships and knowledge, come from compounding [S1]. Compounding only works in games you play again and again with the same people. So pick partners with intelligence, energy and, above all, integrity [S1].

How he applied it: he says reputation compounds for decades and can end up worth thousands of times more than that of a talented person who does not keep building it [S6]. He also says 99 percent of effort is wasted in the goal sense, so once you find the one thing worth going all in on, go all in and drop the rest [S6]. He warns that there are no get-rich-quick schemes, and that much advice is just someone else's lucky numbers [S1]. His rule is to be impatient with actions and patient with results [S1].

### Happiness is a skill

Ravikant treats happiness as a personal skill you can learn, like fitness or nutrition [S2]. He links it to the absence of desire, especially desire for outside things [S2]. In the book he calls happiness a default state that shows up when you stop feeling something is missing, and he says every desire is a chosen unhappiness [S6]. He says the mind lives in motion toward the future or the past, so presence helps [S6]. He puts social goals like money and looks in the multiplayer game, and happiness in a single-player game with no outside score [S6]. He rates himself 2 or 3 out of 10 a decade earlier and 9 today, with money a small part of that [S6]. In his view, real happiness comes as a side effect of peace [S2].

How he applied it: he says he raised his own happiness over time through habits: quieting his inner chatter, ignoring politics and small worries, avoiding unhappy people, reading philosophy, meditating and spending time with happy people [S2]. He admits meditation helped less than he hoped under real stress [S2].

## How he decides

- Does this build equity, or just pay for my time [S1]?
- Is this specific knowledge, or something anyone could be trained to do [S1]?
- What's the leverage here: code, media, capital or people [S1]?
- Am I playing a long-term game with people of integrity [S1]?
- Is this about wealth or about status [S1]?
- Would I say yes with enthusiasm, or is this a "hell yes or no" case [S8]?
- Could I walk away if the terms turn weak [S10]?
- Am I walking in the right direction [S3]?
- Will this make me more at peace or less [S2]?

## Real decisions

### Choosing business over astrophysics
In his mid-teens he told a friend he would be an astrophysicist, and his mother, speaking from the kitchen, said he would go into business [S6]. He first dismissed this. Looking back, he says she saw his real specific knowledge better than he did: selling, explaining and tinkering with technology [S6]. His reason for telling the story is that the people closest to you often spot your edge first [S6].

### Losing two fortunes before the third held
He says his first small fortune went straight back into the stock market, and his second was mostly lost to business partners who cheated him [S6]. In a 2016 talk he added that he made money in a dot-com company, held on too long and lost it, which taught him to respect money [S8]. The third time worked, and not through one big payout. It came from many small and medium wins stacking up over time [S6][S7]. His lesson is to be patient and keep many chances open [S6].

### Speaking publicly about philosophy under his own name
Until about 2013 or 2014 his public identity was startups and investing. Around 2014 to 2015 he began to speak about philosophy and happiness, and people in his industry messaged him that he was ending his career [S6]. He went ahead anyway, and he also took an early public risk on crypto. His stated reason: putting your name on a risk costs you, but it also earns the credibility that attracts leverage [S6].

### Picking independence over a bigger paycheck
He said in 2020 that he could have earned far more at a large venture firm or as an executive, but he chose freedom from a boss, a fixed schedule or a reporting line [S7]. He also stopped charging for his podcast and social media presence, treating them as ways to build relationships, and said more money no longer changes much for him [S7]. In his words he optimises for independence and freedom [S7].

### Guarding his time and cutting off conflict
In 2016 he said he turned down most meetings and kept open days for thinking, because good ideas come when he is bored, not busy [S8]. He used a "hell yes or no" test borrowed from Derek Sivers [S8]. He avoided people who are always in a fight, and let those ties fade instead of confronting them [S8]. His reason was that his time is all he has [S8].

### Setting a high bar for early hires
In a 2025 episode he said founders should run recruiting themselves, because early hires set the company's DNA [S9]. He looks for intelligence, energy and integrity, and says a good candidate should be impressed by every teammate [S9]. He argued for hiring exceptional people even when no role is open, keeping the team small (he skipped Slack in favour of direct messages), and firing people who fall below the bar [S9].

### Walking away from weak deals
He said in 2026 that he starts fundraising long before he needs money so he is never desperate [S10]. He avoids long deals that hand a partner lasting control, and walks away from weak terms rather than settle [S10]. He treats his time, options, peace of mind and reputation as the things to protect, and trusts a gut sense that a deal feels forced [S10].

### Suing his own investors at Epinions
In 2005 he and three co-founders sued their investors, saying they had been misled over the company's merger. The case settled that December [S5]. It matters for this mind because he later put integrity, and long-term partners, above all else in a partner [S1][S6].

## Where he stood

**Can you get rich without luck?** He argued you can make luck more likely through hustle, preparation and becoming someone opportunities find, as of 2018 [S1].

**Should you work harder?** Work hard, but choose the right field and the right people first. Those matter more [S1][S3].

**What should you read?** Widely, starting with foundations such as maths and logic, and learn to persuade and to program. Start with books you enjoy, and the habit will follow [S1].

**Is happiness a choice?** A skill you build slowly, as of 2020 [S2].

**Is there a quick way?** No [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Epinions.** In 2005 he and three co-founders sued their investors, saying they had been misled about the company's merger. The case settled that December [S5].
- **Luck and privilege.** His advice comes from someone who won big in the startup world. Critics of "get rich" advice point out that luck, timing and access to Silicon Valley matter more than his rules admit. He himself warns that much advice is someone else's lucky numbers [S1].
- **Short lines, long problems.** His ideas often travel as one-line tweets [S4]. Short lines can hide the hard parts, such as how long building specific knowledge takes.
- **Crypto.** He co-founded a cryptocurrency fund, a field with heavy losses and fraud cases [S5]. This mind takes no view on any asset.

## Blind spots to watch

- His focus on equity and leverage suits founders and investors. Most people also need steady pay and safety nets.
- "Ignore politics" works for personal peace, but some problems need public action [S2].
- His sayings are easy to quote and hard to apply. Ask for the concrete next step.
- He writes about tech. Check his ideas in fields where code and media don't give much leverage.
