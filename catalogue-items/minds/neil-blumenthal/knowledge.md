# Neil Blumenthal: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-11-15. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Neil Blumenthal is alive, and views can change. He has written no book. Most big Warby Parker choices were made with his co-founders, above all Dave Gilboa, his co-CEO, and we say so where it matters. This data set rests on reference pages about him and the company [S1][S2], the company's 2021 listing papers [S6], and his own telling in three recorded conversations: First Round in 2012 [S4], Mixergy in 2017 [S3] and Fortune's Leadership Next in 2024 with Gilboa [S5]. Treat everything as "as of" the dates given. Nothing here covers his family or private life.

## In one paragraph

Blumenthal was born in 1980 in New York City [S1]. After a history and international relations degree at Tufts, he spent about five years at VisionSpring, a nonprofit that trains local people, often women, to sell low-cost glasses and eye exams in poorer countries [S1][S3]. He went to Wharton for an MBA, where he and three classmates, Dave Gilboa, Andrew Hunt and Jeffrey Raider, asked why nobody sold glasses online [S1][S3]. They launched Warby Parker from their apartments in February 2010, with glasses that usually cost around $500 sold for about $95, a free try-on at home and a pair given for each pair sold [S2][S3][S4]. GQ and Vogue covered the launch, and the company hit its first-year sales goal in three weeks [S2][S4]. It opened its first store in 2013, listed on the New York Stock Exchange in September 2021, and ended 2025 with 323 stores [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Start from the frustration

The idea began with the founders' own annoyance at losing glasses and paying too much [S3]. Before building, they surveyed about 800 classmates on how often they bought glasses, what they paid and how they felt about it [S3]. His advice to would-be founders is to write down five frustrations every day [S3].

How he applied it: the survey showed price was a quiet frustration that people rarely said out loud, and that they did not want to buy glasses without trying them on [S3]. Both became the offer.

### Find who keeps the price high, and go around them

The founders studied the industry and found one company that owned major brands, store chains and a vision insurer, which gave it reasons to keep prices high [S3]. By designing their own frames and selling direct, they could charge a fraction of the store price [S3][S4][S6]. The listing papers say the direct model lets the company charge less than many rivals [S6].

How he applied it: designer-quality glasses with prescription lenses from $95, a price the company kept as its starting point [S3][S6].

### Take away the reason not to buy

Research said nobody wanted to shop for glasses online, because they wanted to try them first [S3]. That led to a period of doubt [S3]. The answer was Home Try-On: pick five frames, get them free, try them for five days [S2][S3].

How he applied it: the programme also cut costly returns, since glasses sent back with custom lenses usually had to be thrown away [S3]. It ran for about fifteen years and ended at the end of 2025 [S2].

### Own the customer, never wholesale

He took a lesson from Scojo, a well-designed reading-glasses brand sold through drugstores and department stores: depending on wholesale means you don't own the customer [S3]. He has said he never wants to be in the wholesale business [S3].

How he applied it: Warby Parker sells through its own site, app and stores [S6]. The listing papers report a Net Promoter Score that averaged above 80 across its history [S6].

### Mission inside the business

Buy a Pair, Give a Pair funds a pair of glasses for each pair sold [S2]. He explains that giving glasses away for free can hurt local sellers, so Warby Parker works with VisionSpring to sell low-cost glasses through local entrepreneurs [S3]. He says the mission keeps the team motivated and is not just a marketing choice, and that founders should not wall off the mission from the business [S3].

How he applied it: the company reported over 200,000 pairs in its first two years and more than 25 million by 2026 [S2][S4]. It certified as a B Corp in 2010 and became a public benefit corporation [S2].

### Constraints breed creativity, so test cheaply

He says creativity comes from constraints [S3]. The founders started with about $120,000 of their own money, drew every page of the site as slides, and tested them with friends before hiring developers [S3].

How he applied it: early showrooms were in his apartment and then the office, before any store [S3][S5]. A converted yellow school bus toured more than 15 cities, and its sales data helped pick store sites [S3].

### Stores to grow online, not replace it

He describes the path as office showroom, then pop-up, then a permanent store, then a few stores in key cities, which kept doing well [S5]. As of 2024 he said stores were there to raise awareness, build the brand and support online sales [S5].

How he applied it: the first store opened in New York in April 2013 [S2]. By mid-2021 there were 145 stores, about 250 by 2024, and 323 at the end of 2025 [S2][S5][S6].

### Calculated risks, and great or not at all

As of 2024 he said that with dearer money, experiments should be more controlled and risks calculated, and that what reaches customers has to be great [S5]. He also says vendors charge more when there is uncertainty, so define the job clearly first [S3].

How he applied it: the company moved into eye exams, contact lenses and a phone-based vision test, which customers had asked for because booking a visit just to renew a prescription was a hassle [S5][S1].

## How he decides

- What frustrates buyers most, even if they don't say it [S3]?
- Who in the chain keeps the price high, and can we go around them [S3]?
- What stops people buying online, and can we remove it [S3]?
- Does this keep the customer ours, or hand them to a wholesaler [S3]?
- Does the mission help the people it is meant for [S3]?
- Can we test it cheaply first: a showroom, a bus, a slide deck [S3]?
- Is this risk calculated, and will the result be great [S5]?

## Real decisions

### Surveying before building
At Wharton the founders surveyed about 800 classmates on buying glasses [S3]. The results showed price frustration and a strong wish to try frames on, which nearly put them off selling online [S3]. Instead they built the try-on offer around that fear [S3].

### Setting the price
They aimed to sell glasses that usually cost around $500 for about $95 by cutting out the middle layer [S3]. The listing papers in 2021 still gave $95 as the starting price with prescription lenses [S6].

### Picking the cheapest developer, and paying for it
Of four web shops, they chose the cheapest [S3]. The code was poor, it had to be replaced within six months, and it delayed the launch by a few months [S3]. He learned to write down exactly what you need, because vendors charge more for uncertainty [S3].

### Selling out in the first month
After GQ and Vogue covered the launch in February 2010, orders came faster than planned [S2][S4]. The site had no sold-out feature and too few glasses, and the founders stayed up all night answering emails [S4]. They hit the first-year sales target in three weeks, sold out of the top 15 styles in four weeks and built a waitlist of about 20,000 people [S4]. Home Try-On was paused within 48 hours because frames ran out [S3].

### Giving through local sellers
Rather than hand out free glasses, they partnered with VisionSpring so that local entrepreneurs sell low-cost glasses [S3]. His reason was that free goods can harm local economies [S3].

### Opening stores step by step
The first retail was a showroom in his apartment, then the office [S3][S5]. The school bus tour tested cities, and its data helped choose a store in Georgetown, Washington, D.C. [S3]. The first permanent store opened in April 2013 [S2].

### Going public by direct listing
In September 2021 Warby Parker listed on the NYSE through a direct listing, the first public benefit corporation to do so [S2]. Blumenthal and Gilboa kept special shares with about 48% of the voting power [S6].

## Where he stood

**Can you sell anything online?** Only if you remove the reason people won't buy it there. Try-on at home did that for glasses [S3].

**Wholesale?** No. He wants to own the customer [S3].

**Is the mission marketing?** No. It keeps the team going, and it should be built into the business [S3].

**Stores or online?** Both. Stores build the brand and help online sales [S5].

**Two CEOs?** It works with clear areas, constant updates and shared ownership of results, as Gilboa explained in 2024 [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Losses.** The company has often run at a loss; for 2025 Wikipedia lists a small operating loss and net loss [S2].
- **B Corp lapse.** It let its B Corp certification lapse before certifying again in 2021 [S2].
- **Early cost-cutting.** By his own account, the cheapest developer cost them months [S3].
- **Running out.** Launch demand outran stock and systems [S4].
- **Clashes with incumbents.** He has argued that limits on telehealth in some states protect incumbents [S5]. That is his view; others in eye care disagree. A trademark fight with 1-800 Contacts went Warby Parker's way in 2023 [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- A $95 price works with high volume and lean costs. Check your own margin before cutting price.
- Free try-on and free returns cost money. Count the cost per order.
- Warby Parker raised a lot of outside money [S2]. Most stores can't open hundreds of shops.
- A health product has rules. Pair this mind with expert checks.
- A mission must truly help. Check with the people it serves.
