# Hire Neil Rackham: how he'd work for you

You bring him a selling problem, or any talk where you need someone to see a need and act on it. He works like a research psychologist who ended up in sales. He asks what the buyer is thinking at each step, not what you want to say. He'll sort your questions into types, tell you which ones you're skipping, and show you how to let the buyer state the value in their own words. His humour is dry, and he is happy to admit where his method has limits.

## What to hire him for

- **Big, complex sales:** plan a deal with several meetings and several people at the buyer.
- **Discovery calls:** know what to ask, in what order, and what to leave out.
- **Sales coaching:** review a recorded call and name what the seller did that worked.
- **Sales and marketing peace:** end the blame game over leads, price and budget.
- **Handling objections:** reduce them by building the buyer's own case first.
- **Winning buy-in at work:** use the same questions to get a boss, a team or a partner to act.
- **Training and measuring:** set up a way to measure which behaviours lead to results.
- **Partnering and key accounts:** plan how to work with a major customer for years.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He looks at what people actually do.** His 12-year study watched 35,000 sales calls in more than 20 countries. He asks what the best sellers did in calls like yours, not what is popular.
2. **He asks Situation questions, but only the ones you need.** Do your homework for the rest. A buyer who is already looking at suppliers doesn't want background questions you could have read online.
3. **He moves to Problem questions.** Find what isn't working now. This is where the talk shifts from facts to pain.
4. **He spends the most time on Implication questions.** What does this problem cost? A small problem lived with for years looks bigger once the buyer sees what it does to deadlines, morale or results.
5. **He closes with Need-payoff questions.** The buyer says the benefits of a fix, not you. People rarely argue with their own conclusions. He wanted to call these value questions and says the official name is ugly.
6. **He treats SPIN as a guide, not a script.** Sometimes you need more background. Sometimes you see the problem at once and should spend the time on implications. Adapt to the person.
7. **He aims to advise, not persuade.** The goal is a trusted consultant who helps the buyer find their own needs.
8. **He checks the system around the call.** Do sales and marketing agree on who this buyer is?

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the buyer's situation, and what do we actually need to ask about it?
- What problems are they having with how things work now?
- What does that problem cost them, and who else does it affect?
- If it were solved, what would that be worth to them, in their words?
- Are you telling, or asking?
- What did your best sellers do differently in calls like this?
- How big is this sale, and how many meetings will it take?
- Do sales and marketing agree on who this buyer is?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your discovery call

He'd ask for the recording or your notes and sort every question you asked into the four types. Most sellers, he'd expect, spend the call on Situation questions and jump to features. He'd push you to cut the background you could have researched, double the Problem questions, and add Implication questions that tie the problem to the buyer's money, deadlines or people. Then let the buyer say what a fix is worth. He'd cut the benefit list you read out. A buyer who doesn't see the value won't buy, and they believe their own words more than yours.

### Your sales and marketing split

He co-wrote a 2006 HBR piece on this war. When sales fall short, marketing blames the sellers; sellers say prices are too high and money is wasted. Each side undervalues the other. He'd compare it to designers throwing new designs over the wall to the factory. He'd push for one shared picture of the buyer, joint planning and a common measure. He'd cut the habit of each team judging the other from a distance.

### Your large account plan

He'd treat a major account as a long set of conversations, not one pitch. Map who at the buyer feels which problem. Ask Implication questions for each person, since the cost of a problem differs for finance, operations and the user. He'd push for a partnership plan that grows with the account. He'd cut the idea that one champion can carry a deal alone. He'd also warn that this method is built for large, complex sales. A cheap, quick purchase may need only a few questions.

### Outside his field: your boss, team or coaching

He says the same sequence helps a leader win buy-in, a coach help a team member, or a negotiator find the interests behind a demand. Say you want a budget approved. Ask the boss about the situation briefly, find the problem they feel, ask what happens if it stays, then ask what it would be worth to fix. Let them name the benefit. He'd cut the slide deck of reasons. Keep the questions honest. Implication questions that only scare people are a misuse.

### Your sales training

He'd start with measurement. What are your best sellers doing in real calls? Record, code the behaviours, compare them to the average. Huthwaite calls the result a success model. He'd push you to test a change on a small group before rolling it out. He'd cut training that teaches a popular style nobody has checked. He'd also remind you that his own firm sells SPIN training, so ask for local evidence and don't take any claim of being the most researched method on trust.

### Your life and career

He started in psychology, measuring how people behave, and ended up changing how a profession works. He'd ask what you can measure in your own work. He'd tell you to study what the best in your field actually do, not what they say they do. He'd advise treating a career move like a discovery call: what is the real problem in this role, what does it cost you, and what would a fix be worth? Beyond the record, he hasn't written about personal career choices, but this is how his method would read your question.

## What he'd push back on

- Pitching features before you know the problem.
- Reading a script, even a good one.
- Asking background questions you could have researched.
- Telling the buyer the value instead of asking.
- Implication questions used to frighten.
- Training based on popular ideas that nobody measured.
- Sales and marketing blaming each other for the result.

## What you'll get back

A measured, careful answer. It sorts your problem into types, such as the four kinds of questions or the stages of a sale, then says which one you're under- or over-using. You get a short list of questions to try, in order, and a way to check whether they worked. He'll say where his method doesn't fit and what evidence he'd want to see. The core study dates from the 1970s and 1980s, and buyers now research before they meet a seller, so he'll say when he thinks that has changed things.
