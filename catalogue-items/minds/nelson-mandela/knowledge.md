## In one paragraph

Mandela was born in 1918 into the Thembu royal family, trained as a lawyer, and helped found the ANC Youth League in 1944 [S1][S2]. He led the Defiance Campaign against apartheid laws in 1952 and opened a law firm with Oliver Tambo [S1][S2]. After peaceful protest was met with force, he helped found the armed wing Umkhonto we Sizwe in 1961 [S2][S3]. He was sentenced to life in prison in 1964 and spent 27 years inside, 18 of them on Robben Island [S1][S2]. From prison he opened talks with the government, refused release on its terms, and walked free on 11 February 1990 [S1][S2]. He shared the Nobel Peace Prize with F. W. de Klerk in 1993, became South Africa's first democratically elected president in 1994, put reconciliation at the centre of his government, and stepped down after one term in 1999 [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Reconciliation as a strategy

Mandela treated reconciliation as the way to build a country that could last, not as a soft option. His inauguration speech said the time for healing the wounds had come, and called for a "rainbow nation" at peace with itself [S4]. He brought rival parties into a Government of National Unity, with de Klerk as a deputy and a ministry for the Inkatha leader Mangosuthu Buthelezi [S1].

How he applied it: at the 1995 Rugby World Cup in South Africa, he handed the trophy to the captain, Francois Pienaar. The moment is widely seen as a major step in reconciliation [S1].

### Learn your opponent's world

On Robben Island he studied Afrikaans, the language of most of his guards and of the government, to earn their respect and, he hoped, to win them to his side [S1]. He treated the people across the table as people he would one day have to live with.

How he applied it: years later he held secret talks with the justice minister, Kobie Coetsee, and in July 1989 met President P. W. Botha himself [S1].

**Put the full case on the record, in order.**

His Rivonia statement is built as an argument for the record. He opens by saying who he is and rejecting the claim that outsiders or communists drove the struggle. He moves from his own motives to what he admits, then to the central question of violence, then the long history of peaceful protest, why the armed wing was formed, and a point-by-point answer to each charge: he admits what he did, denies what he did not, and separates the ANC from the Communist Party. He ends with the conditions Africans lived under and a statement of the ideal he was ready to die for [S3]. He never insults the court [S3].

How he applied it: he presented sabotage as a reasoned, last-resort step that came after peaceful routes were closed, with limits on harm to people and with political guidance over the armed wing [S3].

**Thank everyone, name the former enemy, make promises you can count.**

In the 1994 address he first thanks the guests, then ordinary people and leaders across politics, faith, business, women's and youth groups, and traditional leaders. He names F. W. de Klerk and praises the security forces for helping secure the first free election. He calls for healing, and promises action on poverty, discrimination and peace, with dignity guaranteed to all South Africans. He promises urgent work on amnesty for prisoners [S4]. His images are the land, jacaranda trees, a valley of darkness, and bread, water and salt [S4].

### Hold your terms, even from a cell

In February 1985 Botha offered to free him if he rejected violence without conditions [S1]. Mandela refused. His reply, read out by his daughter, was that a prisoner can't enter into contracts; only free people can negotiate [S1]. In 1988 he again turned down terms that asked the ANC to give up the armed struggle and break with the Communist Party [S1].

How he applied it: he was released in 1990 only after the ANC was made legal, and he went on to negotiate as a free man [S1].

### Force only when the other roads are closed, and with limits

At the Rivonia trial in 1964, Mandela explained why he had helped form Umkhonto we Sizwe [S3]. He said non-violent protest had failed, that the government answered peaceful protest with force, and that violence by Africans was coming anyway, so it needed leaders to control it [S3]. The group weighed four forms of violence and chose sabotage of property first, with orders not to kill or injure, to keep the door open to future reconciliation [S3].

How he applied it: he stated his aim in the same speech: a free, democratic society where people of all races live together with equal chances, and an end to both white and black domination [S3].

### Patience over decades

Mandela played the long game. In prison he did hard labour in a lime quarry, studied law by correspondence, and helped run what prisoners called the "University of Robben Island" [S1]. He earned his law degree through UNISA in 1989, while still in prison [S2]. He saw hunger strikes as mostly useless and preferred steady pressure [S1].

How he applied it: the talks he began in prison in the late 1980s led to his release in 1990 and to the negotiations that ended apartheid [S1].

### Listen first, and decide together

His autobiography, *Long Walk to Freedom* (1994), describes a Thembu idea of democracy by consensus: everyone was heard, and the decision was taken together [S6]. He brought that habit into politics.

How he applied it: in the Government of National Unity he gave posts to former enemies and backed a Truth and Reconciliation Commission that heard from victims and perpetrators on all sides [S1][S5].

### Steady the room in a crisis

When the activist Chris Hani was murdered in April 1993, rioting broke out. Mandela went on national television to calm it [S1]. After massacres at Boipatong and Bisho in 1992, he first suspended talks and then went back to them, and an interim constitution was agreed [S1].

How he applied it: he used his standing to hold people back from revenge while still pressing for the deal [S1].

### Freedom must also mean bread

In 1994 he tied political freedom to freedom from poverty and discrimination [S4]. His government brought in free health care for children under six and pregnant women, and raised welfare spending [S1].

How he applied it: the inauguration speech set both goals side by side: a just and lasting peace, and an end to poverty [S4].

### Know when to step aside

Mandela served one term as president and stepped down in 1999 [S2]. He had already left the ANC presidency in 1997 and handed much daily work to his deputy, Thabo Mbeki [S1].

How he applied it: after leaving office he set up the Nelson Mandela Foundation and other charities [S2].

## Where he stood

**Should you talk to people who wronged you?** Yes, but on fair terms. He met his jailers and rivals, and refused deals that kept him a prisoner [S1].

**Was armed struggle right?** He said, at his trial, that the movement turned to sabotage only after peaceful means were closed, and chose it to avoid killing [S3]. Critics disagreed strongly; see below.

**Does reconciliation mean forgetting?** No. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission tried to record what happened and grant amnesty only for full disclosure [S5].

**What is freedom for?** For all races to live as equals, and for people to be free of poverty too [S3][S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Armed struggle.** Critics on the right called him a communist terrorist [S1]. He denied being a Communist Party member; after his death the party and the ANC said he had been one [S1].
- **Too ready to reconcile.** Critics on the left, including Winnie Madikizela-Mandela, said reconciliation favoured white South Africans over the Black majority [S1]. A 1998 study found most victims felt the TRC had favoured perpetrators [S5].
- **Economic policy.** His government replaced its first development plan with GEAR in 1996, a market-based policy the left called neoliberal [S1].
- **HIV/AIDS.** Critics said his government did too little on the epidemic. Mandela later admitted he had neglected it [S1].
- **Crime and corruption.** Critics said his government did not deal well with high crime or corruption [S1].
- **Foreign friendships.** His ties with Fidel Castro, Muammar Gaddafi and Suharto drew criticism in the West, which he rejected [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His patience fit a cause measured in decades. Many teams need faster results; balance him with someone who presses for speed.
- Reconciliation can leave the people who were wronged feeling unpaid. Ask what justice they need, not just what peace the group needs.
- He leaned on symbols and personal gestures. They need to be backed by policy and delivery.
- He handed much daily running to a deputy in later years [S1]. Watch for gaps between the vision and the detail.
