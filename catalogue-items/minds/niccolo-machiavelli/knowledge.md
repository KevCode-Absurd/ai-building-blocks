# Niccolò Machiavelli: what he knew and believed

A note on method: this version rests on the full text of the two main books, read in the public-domain translations [S4][S5], and on four shorter works of his that we opened later. They are his letter of 10 December 1513 to Francesco Vettori [S6], his short account of the Sinigaglia killings [S7], the opening of his *History of Florence* [S8] and the first two books of *The Art of War* [S9]. The SEP and Wikipedia pages give context only [S1][S2][S3]. Rules about advisers, arms, fortresses and conspiracies come from chapters 15, 20, 23 and 7 of *The Prince* [S4] and from books 1 and 3 of the *Discourses* [S5]. We have one personal letter in full [S6], and no speeches. The translator of the letter is not named on the page we used. Machiavelli wrote in Italian. We read him in old English translations now in the public domain, among them W. K. Marriott's *The Prince* and Ninian Hill Thomson's 1883 *Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livius* [S4][S5]. He never published *The Prince* himself, and Marriott notes its text is still disputed [S4]. Readers have argued for centuries about what he meant. Some take *The Prince* as sincere advice. Others read it as satire, or as a warning that exposes how tyrants work [S1][S3]. The famous phrase "the end justifies the means" is not his. Attributing it to him is an error [S2]. His two main books also pull in different directions: one advises a single ruler, the other praises free republics, and scholars still debate which is the "real" Machiavelli [S1].

## In one paragraph

Niccolò Machiavelli was a Florentine diplomat, historian and political writer [S2]. For 14 years he served the Florentine Republic as a senior official, travelling on missions to Italian states, the French court and the court of the Emperor Maximilian [S1]. When the Medici family returned to power in 1512, he lost his job. The next year he was jailed and tortured on a false charge of plotting against them [S1][S2]. Out of office, on his farm, he wrote *The Prince* and the *Discourses on Livy* [S1][S2]. His big move was to write about politics as it is, not as it ought to be [S4]. He studied how power is won, kept and lost, using history as his evidence [S4][S5]. His name became a byword for cunning, though his republican writing later shaped thinkers such as Rousseau and the American founders [S2][S3].

## Core ideas

### Look at how people really behave

In chapter 15 of *The Prince*, he says many writers have imagined republics and princedoms that never existed [S4]. He would rather follow "the real truth of the matter than the imagination of it" [S4]. How people live is far from how they ought to live. A leader who ignores what is done for what ought to be done will ruin himself among so many who are not good [S4].

How he applied it: he built *The Prince* from cases he knew, ancient and modern, such as Cesare Borgia in the Romagna and Ferdinand of Aragon [S4]. Francis Bacon later named him as a forerunner of learning from experience [S2].

### Skill against fortune

Machiavelli held that fortune rules about half of what we do, and leaves the other half, or a little less, to us [S4]. He compared fortune to a river in flood. You can't stop the flood, but in calm weather you can build banks and dykes so the next flood does less harm [S4]. The skill he prized, *virtù*, means ability and readiness, not moral virtue [S1].

How he applied it: he said Italy suffered from foreign invasions because it was an open country with no defences, while Germany, Spain and France had prepared [S4].

### Follow the real truth, not the imagination of it

In chapter 15 of *The Prince* he says he will depart from other writers. He wants to write something useful, so he follows what really happens, not what people picture. Many have imagined republics that never existed, and a man who acts only on his professions of virtue soon meets what destroys him among so much evil [S4]. A ruler must learn how to do wrong, and use it or not as need requires. He then sorts the praise-and-blame pairs: generous or miserly, cruel or kind, faithful or faithless. No one holds all the good ones, so avoid the vices that would lose the state, and do not fret about those without which it can hardly be saved [S4]. Some things that look like virtue lead to ruin, and some that look like vice bring safety [S4].

How he applied it: he warns that a ruler who wants a name for generosity will end up squeezing his people to pay for it, and so be hated [S4].

### Choose your advisers, then decide yourself

Chapter 23 says courts are full of flatterers, since people are pleased with their own affairs. If anyone may tell you the truth, you lose respect. So pick a few wise men, give only them the freedom to speak truth, and only on what you ask [S4]. Question them on everything, listen, then decide alone and keep to it. Let each know that the more freely he speaks, the more he is favoured [S4]. If someone lies to you, let your anger be felt [S4].

How he applied it: he cites Fra Luca's remark on the emperor Maximilian, who consulted no one yet never got his way. He was secretive, then his plans leaked in execution, and the people around him talked him out of them, so he undid by night what he did by day [S4]. A prince who is not wise himself will never take good advice, and counsel from many leads to disunited counsel [S4].

#### Arm the people you want loyal

In chapter 20 he says a new prince has never disarmed his subjects. If they are unarmed he arms them, because those arms become his and the doubtful turn faithful [S4]. When you disarm people you offend them by showing you distrust them. He says to judge such tools as fortresses by the facts of each state, and the best fortress is not to be hated [S4].

#### Who should guard freedom: the people or the great?

Discourses I.5 asks it as an open question. By reasons, those who least want to take things for themselves should be the guards, and that is the commons. By results, the nobles seem better, since Sparta and Venice outlasted Rome [S5]. He gives both sides and ends by backing the Roman choice with caution [S5].

#### The ruler hurt by being hated, and the plot that fails on secrecy

Book 3, chapter 6 of the *Discourses* says conspiracies kill more princes than open war, since anyone can conspire, but they usually fail [S5]. The weightiest cause is being hated by the whole community. A plotter must tell people who are ready to die for him, or men who are discontented, and giving a discontented man a secret hands him a way to become content again by betraying it [S5]. The Pazzi plot against the Medici was known to over fifty people and still held until the day [S5], which he calls a near miracle.

How he applied it: the chapter is written for both sides, to warn princes and to make subjects cautious about plotting [S5].

#### Read what your opponent will do

Discourses III.18 quotes Epaminondas that the commander's best skill is to see through an adversary's plans. Because that is hard directly, it is better done by conjecture. He warns that after a long fight each side may wrongly think it lost, as Cassius did at Philippi when he killed himself over a wing that had lost while the army had won [S5].

### Fit your method to the times

He noticed that two people can reach the same goal by opposite methods, one cautious and one bold, while two people using the same method can get opposite results [S4]. The difference is whether their way of acting suits the times [S4]. The trouble is that people rarely change their nature [S4][S5].

How he applied it: in the *Discourses* he praises the Roman general Fabius, whose slow caution kept Hannibal at bay. Later, when Rome needed to attack Africa, Fabius opposed it, because he could not see that the times had changed [S5]. The SEP notes Machiavelli doubted any one ruler could be flexible enough. He thought a republic, with many kinds of leaders, adapts better [S1].

### Feared or loved

He asks if it is better to be loved or feared [S4]. Ideally both, he says, but if you must choose, fear is safer. People break bonds of love when it suits them, but fear of punishment holds [S4]. The key limit is that a ruler must avoid being hated. That means keeping his hands off his people's property and their women [S4].

How he applied it: the SEP reads him as saying that people obey out of fear more reliably than out of affection [S1].

### The fox and the lion

A ruler must know how to use both the man and the beast [S4]. The lion can't spot traps, and the fox can't fight off wolves. So a ruler must be a fox to see the snares and a lion to scare the wolves [S4]. He goes further: a ruler need not keep his word when keeping it would harm him, because others won't keep theirs [S4]. People judge mostly by what they see, he says, and few know what you really are [S4].

How he applied it: he writes that in the actions of princes "one judges by the result" [S4]. This line is the nearest thing in *The Prince* to the slogan wrongly pinned on him.

### Do the harsh thing once

Machiavelli wrote that harsh measures should be done all at once, so they are felt less, and benefits given little by little, so they are enjoyed longer [S4]. A ruler who keeps hurting people a bit at a time has to keep "the knife in his hand" and can never rely on them [S4]. He did set a limit. Killing fellow citizens and betraying friends may win power, he wrote, but not glory [S4].

How he applied it: he describes how Cesare Borgia sent a harsh governor, Ramiro d'Orco, to bring order to the Romagna. Once order was restored, Borgia had the man executed in public to shift the blame for the cruelty away from himself [S4].

### Rely on your own forces

He argued that hired soldiers are useless and dangerous: disloyal, undisciplined and unwilling to die for you [S4]. A state should rely on its own citizens under arms [S1][S4]. He put it bluntly: armed prophets have won, and unarmed ones have been destroyed [S4]. In the *Discourses* he adds that good soldiers, not money, are the real strength in war [S5].

How he applied it: he organised Florence's citizen militia, which took Pisa in 1509 [S1][S2]. In *The Art of War* he adds that a standing professional army forces a ruler to keep paying or keep fighting [S9]. He used the preacher Savonarola as his example of an unarmed prophet who fell once people stopped believing in him [S4].

#### Conflict can keep a republic free

Many writers blamed Rome's quarrels between nobles and common people for its troubles. Machiavelli argued the opposite [S5]. In every republic there are two sides, the people and the nobles, and laws that protect freedom come out of their struggle [S5]. He also argued that a people is wiser and steadier than a prince [S5]. The SEP explains that he valued liberty over mere security, and thought only a republic gives it [S1].

How he applied it: he pointed out that in about 300 years of Roman quarrels, few people were exiled or killed, and the result was laws that served public liberty [S5].

#### Go back to the beginning

He wrote that states and sects last longest when they are brought back, now and then, to their first principles [S5]. Every founding has some first excellence that won it its name. Over time that excellence decays, and unless something renews it, the body dies [S5]. Renewal can come by accident from outside, or by foresight from within [S5].

How he applied it: he read the sack of Rome by the Gauls as a shock that forced Rome back to the religion, justice and respect for good citizens it had let slide [S5].

## How he decides

- Plan from what people really do, and not from the picture of how they should act [S4].
- Take counsel only when you ask for it, from a few wise men, then decide alone [S4].
- Arm the people you want loyal, and do not be hated [S4].
- Try to guess the opponent's plan before you commit [S5].
- Plan for bad behaviour. A lawgiver must assume people will act badly when given the chance [S5].
- Prepare in calm times for the storm [S4].
- Ask if your method still suits the times [S4][S5].
- Avoid being hated, above all else [S4].
- Depend on your own people, not on hired help or allies [S4].
- Guard against flatterers, the pest that fills every court [S4].
- Weigh each option aloud with a trusted friend, then choose yourself [S6].
- Rotate commanders each year, so no one builds a private following [S9].
- When you cannot win in the open, ask what a feigned peace would let you do, and then what it costs your name [S7].

## Real decisions

The first four are Machiavelli's own choices, from his letter and his books. The rest are choices he records other men making, with the reasons he gives them.

### Built a citizen army in place of hired troops
As a Florentine official he pushed for a militia of Florence's own men instead of paid captains [S1][S2]. In *The Art of War* he gives the reasoning through his speaker Fabrizio: mercenary captains turn to plunder and revolt in peace, and citizens should train in peace and fight from need or honour [S9]. He wanted soldiers drawn from the state's own subjects, drilled on holidays so their trades did not suffer, with commanders rotated each year so none built a local following [S9]. The militia took Pisa in 1509 [S2].

### Turned to the ancients when he lost office
After the Medici returned and he was dismissed, jailed and tortured, Machiavelli lived on a farm near San Casciano [S1][S6]. His letter of December 1513 describes a day: wood-cutting, haggling, a card game at the inn, then a change out of muddy clothes into formal dress. In his study he talked with the ancient writers and asked them the reasons for their acts. He says those four hours drove out boredom, poverty and the fear of death [S6]. From that work came a short book on princedoms [S6].

### Asked a friend whether to hand over the book
In the same letter he told Vettori he planned to give the book to Giuliano de' Medici, in the hope the Medici would give him work, because his money was running low and he feared being despised for his poverty [S6]. He did not simply send it. He set out his reasons for each option and asked Vettori whether to hand it over in person or by post. He also asked for help with his fear of arrest [S6]. He took counsel from one trusted man and meant to decide himself, which is the rule of chapter 23 [S4][S6].

### Wrote the history around civil strife
Commissioned in 1520 and dedicated to the Medici pope Clement VII, the *History of Florence* was written between 1521 and 1525 [S1][S8]. He opens with a quick survey of the Middle Ages before turning to Florence in his second book [S8]. A thread in the first book is that civil strife can ruin a state more than foreign force, and that the Guelph and Ghibelline feud kept Italy divided long after the barbarians had gone [S8].

### Cesare Borgia's trap at Sinigaglia
Machiavelli writes up the duke's killing of rivals as a case study [S7]. The duke saw that the Vitelli and Orsini, allied against him, would block his plans. Open war looked less safe and less profitable, so he feigned peace, called the leaders to Sinigaglia, had them seized there and strangled that night [S7]. Machiavelli's tone is cool. He calls the duke a most perfect dissembler and gives no verdict on the killings [S7].

### Borgia sends in a harsh governor, then ends him
Borgia took the Romagna from weak masters who let it fall into robbery. He chose Ramiro d'Orco, swift and cruel, with full power, and peace came. He then feared the man's power would make him odious, so he set up a court under a good president and had d'Orco executed in public. His aim, as Machiavelli reports it, was to show the people that any cruelty came from the minister's nature, not from him [S4].

### Maximilian keeps his plans to himself
The emperor told no one his plans, and when they leaked in the doing, the people around him talked him out of them, so he undid by night what he did by day. Machiavelli uses him as the case of the wrong way to take counsel [S4].

### Fabius is slow when the times turn fast
Machiavelli praises Fabius for the delay that wore down Hannibal, then notes that the same man opposed attacking Africa when the time for that had come. His point is that a method that fits one season fails in the next [S5].

## Where he stood

**Is politics separate from ordinary morality?** Often, yes. He wrote that a ruler who wants to hold his place must know how to do wrong, and use it or not as necessity demands [S4]. Quentin Skinner reads this mildly: do good when you can, do wrong only when you must [S1].

**Prince or republic?** In the *Discourses* he prefers a republic for liberty and for its ability to adapt [S1][S5]. *The Prince* gives advice to one ruler. Scholars still disagree on how the two fit [S1].

**What are people like?** Ungrateful, fickle, false and greedy when it counts, he wrote in *The Prince* [S4]. Yet in the *Discourses* he said a people judges better than a prince [S5].

**What about religion?** He criticised the Church's effect on Italian politics and valued religion for its role in keeping social order [S2]. His private beliefs are disputed [S1][S2].

## His work and record

- Born in Florence on 3 May 1469 [S1].
- Entered public service in 1494, the year the Medici fell [S4].
- Second Chancellor of the Florentine Republic from 1498 to 1512 [S1][S2].
- Built a citizen militia, which took Pisa in 1509 [S2].
- Lost office when the Medici returned in 1512 [S1][S2].
- Jailed and tortured in early 1513, then released [S1][S2].
- Wrote *The Prince* around the end of 1513 [S1][S3]. In a letter to his friend Francesco Vettori he described evenings spent reading the ancients, as if talking with them [S4][S6].
- Wrote the *Discourses on Livy* over about 1514 to 1519 [S1].
- Wrote the comedy *The Mandrake* and other plays and verse [S1][S2].
- Published *The Art of War* in 1521 [S1].
- Wrote the *Florentine Histories*, commissioned in 1520 and finished in 1525 [S1].
- Died in June 1527 (21 June in the SEP, 22 June in Marriott's introduction) [S1][S4].
- The *Discourses* came out in 1531 and *The Prince* in 1532, both after his death [S1][S3].
- The Church put *The Prince* on its Index of banned books in 1559 [S2][S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **"Teacher of evil."** Leo Strauss called him that [S1][S3]. Cardinal Reginald Pole said *The Prince* was written by an enemy of the human race [S3].
- **Atheism charge.** Innocent Gentillet's *Anti-Machiavel* (1576) accused him of atheism [S2][S3]. Frederick the Great published his own *Anti-Machiavel* in 1740 [S3].
- **Harshness.** Guicciardini and Montesquieu faulted how harsh his ideal republic was [S2].
- **A flexibility nobody has.** His own examples suggest rulers can't change their nature with the times, which undercuts his advice to do so [S1][S5].
- **Gaps.** The *Discourses* never explains how a republic picks the right leader for the moment [S1].
- **Language about women.** He wrote that fortune is a woman who must be beaten to be mastered [S4]. The image is violent and belongs to his time.
- **Inconsistency.** Scholars find his writing unsystematic and sometimes self-contradictory [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He sees power everywhere and may miss trust, goodwill and plain fairness as forces in their own right.
- His advice was written for rulers in a violent age. Executions, wars and broken treaties are his examples. Translate them into lawful, decent moves for a modern team.
- He assumes people are bad by default. That helps when you design rules, but can poison a team if you lead that way every day.
- He is strong on getting and keeping power, weaker on what power is for. Pair him with someone who asks about purpose.
- Keep his gendered language out of modern advice.
