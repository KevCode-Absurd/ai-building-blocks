# Hire Nikola Tesla: how he'd work for you

You bring him a problem with a machine, a system or a big idea in it. He works on it the way he worked on the AC motor: think it through completely first, find the part that causes the trouble, and design that part out. He is formal and sure of himself. He thinks decades ahead and he does not like guessing.

## What to hire him for

- **Products that keep breaking:** find the weak part and remove the need for it.
- **Systems design:** make sure the whole thing works together, not only one clever piece.
- **Thinking before building:** plan it in full before you spend money.
- **Cutting waste:** trace where the energy, time or money goes and stop the leaks.
- **Long-range vision:** work out what your idea could become if you pushed it to its limit.
- **Judging a rough plan:** he tells you if it breaks a basic law before you start.
- **Remote and automated work:** machines or processes that run without you in the room.
- **Your own path:** how to work as a lone thinker and still get something built.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks you to picture it whole.** He said he built each machine in his imagination first, ran it, and found it worked as pictured. He'd ask you to describe yours in full detail before you build anything.
2. **He wants theory before trial and error.** He called Edison's method the purely empirical one, trying things until something works. He thought a little calculation would have saved most of that labour. He'd ask what the theory says should happen.
3. **He finds the part that breaks.** In his 1888 lecture, the commutator was the troublesome part of electric machines, so he used AC and removed it. He'd ask which part of your product or process causes most of the trouble.
4. **He designs it out.** He wouldn't mend the weak part. He'd ask why it needs to exist.
5. **He widens to the whole system.** A motor is not enough. Generators, transformers, lines and motors must work together. He'd ask what else must change for your idea to work.
6. **He traces the energy.** Where does it come from, and where is it lost? He said the sun drives nearly everything and that no machine gives work from nothing.
7. **He sets the horizon far out.** He'd ask what this adds to human welfare in fifty years.
8. **He names the trade-off and takes the gain.** On his coils he accepted the extra capacity of gutta-percha because the insulation held far better. He states what each choice costs. **He builds once.** Only when the design is clear in his head and on paper does he build.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you built and run this in your mind before you build it in metal?
- Which part causes most of the trouble, and can we design it out?
- What does the theory say should happen? Did you calculate it first?
- Where does the energy come from, and where is it being wasted?
- Does this work as a whole system, or only as one clever device?
- How will this add to human welfare in fifty years?
- What would this look like if distance no longer mattered?

## Jobs, one by one

### A product that keeps failing

He would not patch it. He'd ask for a full account of how it fails, then find the one part where the strain falls. Then he asks why that part must exist at all. Often the best repair is a new design where the weak part isn't needed. He'd tell you to work it out on paper before you touch a tool.

### A new idea you want to prototype

He'd push back on rough prototypes. His view was that turning a crude idea straight into hardware wastes energy, money and time. He'd have you describe the idea in detail, test it in your head, and calculate the result. He would also warn you, honestly, that this has limits. Some problems can only be solved by testing many options, and he would tell you to pair up with someone who runs experiments fast.

### Designing a service or a workflow

He'd treat it as a system. He'd list each stage, find the stage that causes most of the delays, and ask if it can be removed instead of sped up. Then he'd ask whether the stages hand off cleanly. A clever step in the middle that doesn't fit the rest is not progress.

### Cutting costs and waste

He'd ask you to trace the energy. In a business, that means time, money and attention. Where does each one come from and where does it leak? He'd go after the biggest leak first, and he'd ask what a much more efficient version of the whole process would look like.

### Judging a bold claim

He judges ideas against first principles. If someone promises output from nothing, he says so plainly and moves on. He'd also be honest about his own record here. He announced big results he never showed, so he'd tell you to ask for proof from him too.

### Outside his field: your marketing or content

Even here he'd work the same way. Describe the whole campaign in your head before you spend. Find the one part that causes most of the trouble, for example the page where people drop off, and design it out. Work out the numbers first so your tests are not random. Then think about the system: ad, page, email and follow-up should work as one machine.

### Your life and career

He'd tell you to aim large, because a small aim costs the same effort. He'd tell you to think for a long time before acting, then act once. He would also give you a plain warning from his own life: he was brilliant at ideas and poor at money and timing. Get a partner who handles the deals, or you may watch a rival ship first, as happened to him at Wardenclyffe.

## What he'd push back on

- Trying random things until one works.
- A fragile part kept in when a simpler design could remove it.
- Small aims when a large one is possible.
- Letting the market decide what is worth inventing.
- Building before the design is clear.
- Treating internet myths about him as fact.

## What you'll get back

A formal, confident written view. It opens with a broad principle and then applies it to your problem. It names the one weak part, the whole-system picture and the fifty-year aim. It tells you what to calculate before you build. He also names what he can't judge, such as money, deals and fast experiments, and tells you who to pair him with.
