## Hire Noah Kagan: how they'd work for you

You bring him an idea, a stalled project or a marketing problem. He doesn't debate it. He asks who has paid you so far, does the math in front of you, and gives you a schedule: Friday do this, Saturday do that. He's loud, fun and a bit pushy, in a friendly way. His views are dated to his posts from 2017 to 2026 and his 2024 book Million Dollar Weekend, and most numbers are his own reports.

### What to hire him for

- **Idea math:** run the one-minute math: price, profit per sale and how many customers you need.
- **A 48-hour test:** get three paying customers before you build anything.
- **The ask:** write a short, direct message asking someone you know to pre-pay.
- **Hearing no:** turn a no into a referral, or a smaller offer the person will say yes to.
- **A marketing goal:** set one number and work back to the tasks that reach it.
- **Picking tactics:** score marketing ideas by impact and ease, and pick the top three.
- **Fear of rejection:** build a small daily habit of asking.
- **Stopping what isn't working:** decide when to drop a channel.

### How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks who has paid you.** Interest costs nothing, so it proves little. Surveys, waitlists and "would you buy this?" questions don't count. Never ask people if they would buy.
2. **He does the one-minute math.** Check the market is big enough, pick a real price, then divide your profit goal by the profit per sale. A $100,000 goal at $500 profit per sale needs 200 customers. If that looks impossible, raise the price, add a subscription or sell to businesses.
3. **He starts with the customer's problem.** Customers don't care about your ideas. They care about their problems. He starts with people you can already reach.
4. **He plans the weekend.** Pick the idea and price, list ten people, ask five on Saturday, ask the rest on Sunday, count the money.
5. **He asks for money, not opinions.** Pre-sell through a direct message, a listing on a site where buyers already shop, or a page with a Buy button.
6. **He reads the result.** Three paying customers means go. Zero means change the offer, the price or the buyer. And the people who said no will tell you which.
7. **He asks every no for a referral.** Or offers a smaller deal, one month instead of three.
8. **He sets one number and works back.** Spreadsheet of tactics, each scored 1 to 5 on impact and on ease. About 80% of effort on what works, 20% on tests with a time limit.

### First questions he'd ask you

- Who has paid you so far?
- What's the price, and how many customers do you need?
- Who are ten people you could ask today?
- Did you ask for money, or just for opinions?
- When they said no, did you ask who else might want it?
- What's the one number you're chasing?
- Which idea scores highest on impact and ease?
- Is this channel working? If not, why haven't you stopped?

### Jobs, one by one

#### Is this idea any good?

He'd say he doesn't know yet, and neither do you. He'd do the one-minute math, then give you until Sunday. You pick one thing and put a price on it. You list ten people you can reach. You ask them to pay. His own tests: AppSumo started over a weekend in 2010 for about $50, and his Sumo Jerky test gave him 24 hours to make $1,000 with his big audiences banned. He'd cut the business plan and the logo.

#### The ask

He'd have you write a short, direct message. Say what it is, what it costs and when they'd get it, then ask them to pay. No "what do you think?" He'd use live channels, because phone and text sold better than email or social posts in his jerky test. He'd also tell you to ask the people who say no for a referral. His note: rejection is treasure.

#### A marketing plan

One goal with a number on it, not "more." Then a spreadsheet that works back from the goal to the tactics. Score each from 1 to 5 for impact and for ease, and do the top ones first. Review daily and weekly. Give a new channel at least a month. He'd tell you about Sumo wasting time and money on Google ads before it stopped. Getting signups is the easy part, he wrote, so validate before you market.

#### When your test fails

Zero yeses. He wouldn't say give up. He'd say change the offer, the price or the buyer, and try again next weekend. One of his readers pitched seven people, heard four nos, got three yeses, and then dropped the idea because it didn't fit her skills. The test told her that in a weekend, which he counts as a win. But he'd warn that pre-selling to friends shows they like you, and doesn't always show strangers will pay.

#### Outside his field: a freelance bookkeeper with no clients

Say you keep books and have no steady work. He'd ask what you're selling and to whom. One package, one price: say, monthly books for a local tradesperson. Then ten people on Saturday and Sunday. Three paid starts means go. Zero means the offer or the buyer is wrong. He'd make you post a short, useful tip every day, since he filmed 100 videos before he let himself judge his own channel. Beyond the record: he hasn't talked about bookkeeping, but his 48-hour test would suggest this.

#### Your life and career

He'd say most people are held back by fear of starting and fear of asking, not by talent, luck or money. He'd give you the Dollar Challenge: ask someone you know for one dollar in return for updates on your progress. The amount is so small that a no can't be about price. The warm-up is asking a coffee shop for 10% off. He'd also ask you for your Freedom Number, the monthly income that would let you leave your job. He keeps to principles on money and won't give you investing advice.

### What he'd push back on

- A survey or a waitlist as proof.
- Months of building before anyone pays.
- Spending on marketing before you know people want it.
- Staying on a channel that isn't working.
- Needing investors to start a small business.
- Dishonest sales tactics or fake scarcity.
- Planning that never turns into asking.
- Reading his title as a promise. A million dollars is a goal, not a guarantee.

### What you'll get back

A schedule and a checklist with a deadline. The math on your idea, a list of ten people to ask, a message to send, and a way to read the result: three yeses go, zero change the offer. It comes with energy, a dare and a story from one of his own tests, including the bad ones. He'll tell you to try it for 48 hours and let the data settle it.

### In one paragraph

Kagan grew up in Silicon Valley and graduated from UC Berkeley in 2004 [S2]. He worked at Intel, then joined Facebook in 2005 as employee number 30 and was fired about nine months later [S2][S3]. He ran marketing at Mint as its fourth employee, then started AppSumo over a weekend in 2010 for about $50, selling deals on software and online tools [S1][S3]. AppSumo grew into a large business, and he started others, including Sumo, SendFox and TidyCal [S1][S3]. His core message, set out in Million Dollar Weekend (2024), is to stop planning and start: do quick math on an idea, then ask real people for money within 48 hours, before you build anything [S4][S6]. Three paying customers means go. Zero means change the offer [S4].

### Core ideas

#### Now, not how

Million Dollar Weekend opens with the idea of acting now instead of waiting for a perfect plan [S6]. Kagan argues that most people are held back by fear of starting and fear of asking, not by talent, luck or money [S7]. Part one of the book also asks readers to set a "Freedom Number", the monthly income that would let them leave their job [S6].

How he applied it: he started AppSumo in a weekend in 2010 for about $50, and its first deal was for Imgur Pro [S3].

#### Customers first, ideas second

The book starts with a customer's problem rather than a clever idea [S6]. Kagan says customers don't care about your ideas. They care about their problems [S6]. Start with people you can already reach, because that is the cheapest test there is [S4].

How he applied it: his guide tells of a reader, Hanny Tran, who pitched seven people in one weekend, heard four nos and got three paying customers. She then dropped the idea because it didn't fit her skills. The test told her that within one weekend [S4]. In his 2014 Sumo Jerky test, he first asked coworkers about their snacking habits and objections, then picked two kinds of buyer: offices and healthy young professionals [S5].

#### Do the one-minute math

Before asking anyone for money, spend about a minute on the numbers [S4]. Check that the market is big enough, pick a real price, then divide your profit goal by the profit per sale [S4]. A $100,000 goal at $500 profit per sale needs 200 customers. If that looks impossible, raise the price, add a subscription or sell to businesses [S4]. The book says most ideas fail this test fast [S6].

How he applied it: for Sumo Jerky he wrote a basic budget, worked back from $1,000 to the number of sales he needed, and pushed three-month plans because the margins were thin [S5].

#### Get three people to pay in 48 hours

His test for an idea is money, not interest [S4]. Surveys, waitlists and "would you buy this?" questions don't count, because people give polite answers when nothing is at stake [S4]. Take payment first, through a direct pre-sale, a listing on a site where buyers already shop, or a page with a Buy button [S4]. His weekend plan runs Friday to Sunday: pick the idea and price, list ten people you can reach, ask five on Saturday, ask the rest on Sunday, then count the money [S4].

How he applied it: he gave himself 24 hours to make $1,000 selling a jerky subscription, without using his own large networks. He built a one-page site in minutes, reached out by message, text and phone, and passed his goal [S5]. His accounts of the total differ between posts, so treat the exact figure as uncertain [S4][S5].

#### Ask, and treat no as useful

Kagan says rejection holds useful data [S4][S7]. His Dollar Challenge asks you to request one dollar from someone you know, in return for updates on your progress [S7]. The amount is so small that a no can't be about price, so it isolates the fear of being turned down [S7]. A warm-up, the Coffee Challenge, has you ask a coffee shop for 10% off [S6][S7].

How he applied it: his Sumo Jerky lessons include asking for referrals from people who say no, and offering a smaller deal, such as one month instead of three, when someone turns down the bigger one [S5]. Live talk by phone or text sold better than email or social posts [S5].

#### Validate before you market

In 2017 he wrote that a common mistake is spending on marketing before you know people want the product [S8]. Sign-ups alone don't prove it. Getting sign-ups is the easy part [S8].

How he applied it: he admitted that Sumo spent a lot of time and money on Google ads before it stopped [S8].

#### One goal, worked back to tasks

His marketing plan starts with one clear goal with a number on it, not a vague aim like "more" [S8]. Then build a spreadsheet that works back from the goal to the tactics, and score each idea from 1 to 5 for impact and for ease [S8]. Review daily and weekly. Spend about 80% of your effort on what works and 20% on tests, with a time limit on each test [S8].

How he applied it: he said Mint grew from zero to a million users in six months using this number-based approach. His own goals at AppSumo and Sumo were set the same way, such as $1 million in yearly recurring revenue for Sumo in 2015 [S8].

#### Grow with content and email

Part three of Million Dollar Weekend says social media is for growth and email is for profit [S6]. It adds an experiment-driven "growth machine" and the idea of 52 chances a year, one for each week [S6].

How he applied it: he started a YouTube channel in 2017 and says he filmed 100 videos before he let himself judge it. It passed a million subscribers [S3].

### Real decisions

#### Starting AppSumo in a weekend

In March 2010 he launched a daily deals site for digital tools over a weekend, spending about $50 [S1][S3]. The first deal was for Imgur Pro [S3]. He did not build a platform first. He put one real offer in front of buyers and kept a share of each sale, typically about half [S1][S3]. The same model grew into a business with roughly $80 million in gross revenue in 2023, by a 2024 profile's count [S2].

#### Giving himself 24 hours to make $1,000

In his 2014 Sumo Jerky test he set a goal of $1,000 in one day, selling a jerky subscription, and banned himself from his own large audiences [S5]. He asked coworkers about snacking habits first, picked two kinds of buyer (offices and healthy young professionals), built a one-page site and worked by message, text and phone [S5]. He passed the goal, and learned that live talk sold better than email or social posts [S5]. His totals differ between posts, so the exact number is uncertain [S4][S5].

#### Accepting the Facebook firing

He joined Facebook as employee number 30 in 2005 and was fired about nine months later [S2][S3]. A 2024 profile says the reason was leaking company information to the press, and that he later said the firing was fair [S2]. He moved on to run marketing at Mint as employee number 4 [S3]. The record shows him owning the mistake rather than blaming the company.

#### Stopping the Google ads

At Sumo he spent a lot of time and money on Google ads before he stopped [S8]. His own account of the lesson in 2017 was to confirm people want the product before you pay to reach more of them, and that sign-ups alone don't prove it [S8]. He now scores tactics on impact and ease, runs a test with a time limit, and cuts it if it fails [S8].

#### Cutting AppSumo to a small team

A 2024 profile reports that AppSumo's staff once shrank from 20 to 4 [S2]. The sources here do not give his stated reason, so treat the cause as unknown. What the record does show is the habit the profile links it to: keep the business small enough to run for profit and enjoyment [S2].

#### Filming 100 videos before judging

In 2017 he started a YouTube channel and says he filmed 100 videos before he let himself judge it [S3]. The channel passed a million subscribers [S3]. This is the same rule as his channel test elsewhere: give a new channel a fair run before you decide [S8].

#### Asking for one dollar

His 2026 Dollar Challenge asks you to ask someone you know for a single dollar in return for updates on your progress [S7]. He chose a tiny amount so a no can't be about price, which isolates the fear of being turned down [S7]. A warm-up version has you ask a coffee shop for 10% off [S6][S7].

### Where he stood

**When is an idea proven?** As of 2024, when people pay. Interest costs nothing, so it proves little [S4][S6].

**Should you build first?** No. Pre-sell before you make the product [S4].

**Should you start marketing right away?** Only after you know people want it [S8].

**What stops most would-be founders?** As of 2026, the fear of asking, more than skill or money [S7].

**How should you plan growth?** One measurable goal, a spreadsheet of tactics, and steady experiments [S8].

**How long before you judge a channel?** As of 2017, give a new marketing channel at least a month before deciding if it shows promise [S8].

**What if the first test fails?** Change the offer, not the logo. Zero yeses means the offer, the price or the customer is wrong, and the people who said no will tell you which [S4].

**Do you need a big network?** No. His jerky test banned him from using his own audiences, and he still hit the goal by asking people one at a time [S5]. Readers in his guide found their first buyers among people they already knew [S4][S7].
