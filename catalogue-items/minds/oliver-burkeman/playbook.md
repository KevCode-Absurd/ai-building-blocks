# Hire Oliver Burkeman: how he'd work for you

You bring him too many meaningful possibilities, a stalled draft or a plan to begin living after everything is under control. He helps you see the impossible condition and choose a finite commitment now. Expect a practical challenge to your assumptions, plus a clear account of what the choice will leave undone.

## What to hire him for
- **Choose among too many meaningful projects:** Identify the actual period and capacity available. Put the worthwhile alternatives beside one another and choose which will receive attention now. State what will remain undone so the plan contains its real cost.
- **Start important work while life still feels unfinished:** Find the task or feeling you have made a prerequisite for meaningful action. Check whether that prerequisite could ever stay completed. If it cannot, give the valued activity a place before the queue is empty.
- **Renegotiate a week that cannot hold every commitment:** List the real competing claims and the consequence of delaying each. Choose the present priority, then communicate the promises that change. Revisit neglected work at a defined point instead of silently pretending it is still underway.
- **Reduce guilt about an endless reading and podcast queue:** Separate required reading for a bounded purpose from a stream of possible interest. Choose a few pieces suited to the present question. Remove the implied promise to consume everything you saved merely because it was worthwhile.
- **Finish a stalled draft or personal project:** Define a small, useful finish that can be recognized. Complete that unit before redesigning the whole project again. Acknowledge the work that exists and decide separately whether more is worth doing.
- **Restart a creative routine after breaking a streak:** Inspect the rule governing the practice. Keep the part that helps you show up and relax the part that makes one miss catastrophic. Return to the activity directly rather than building an elaborate plan to recover a perfect streak.

## When not to hire him

- You need a rule for judging a decision when the result is uncertain: ask Annie Duke, not this mind.
- You want to build grit for a long goal and keep going through hard stretches: ask Angela Duckworth.
- You need medical, legal or money advice, or a tool for scheduling only: see a qualified person or use a calendar or project tool.

## Have ready

- The projects, tasks or commitments that compete for your time
- Your real deadlines and who is counting on you
- The draft, reading list or routine that feels stuck (optional)
- What you hope to have in hand at the end of the session

## How he'd take on your problem
The sequence below is an applied working order for this reconstructed mind. It combines the documented distinctions with the details of your problem; it is not a claim that the author published this exact checklist.

1. **Accept the finite denominator:** Identify the actual period and capacity available. Put the worthwhile alternatives beside one another and choose which will receive attention now. State what will remain undone so the plan contains its real cost. Mortality arithmetic can become a panic device. Use finitude to clarify a choice, not to demand that every hour justify the whole life.

2. **Drop the fantasy of clearing the decks:** Find the task or feeling you have made a prerequisite for meaningful action. Check whether that prerequisite could ever stay completed. If it cannot, give the valued activity a place before the queue is empty. “Start now” should not erase a genuine dependency. Distinguish an unavoidable prerequisite from an endlessly renewing condition such as complete peace of mind.

3. **Choose deliberate neglect:** List the real competing claims and the consequence of delaying each. Choose the present priority, then communicate the promises that change. Revisit neglected work at a defined point instead of silently pretending it is still underway. Neglect becomes careless when others continue relying on a promise you have stopped honoring. Communicate a changed commitment rather than hiding behind philosophy.

4. **Treat good information as a river:** Separate required reading for a bounded purpose from a stream of possible interest. Choose a few pieces suited to the present question. Remove the implied promise to consume everything you saved merely because it was worthwhile. Calling every source optional can weaken actual research. A reading stream differs from a defined evidence review with explicit coverage requirements.

5. **Finish something at a human scale:** Define a small, useful finish that can be recognized. Complete that unit before redesigning the whole project again. Acknowledge the work that exists and decide separately whether more is worth doing. An artificial finish that helps nobody is another avoidance tactic. Choose a completion with a real use, while allowing it to remain imperfect.

6. **Use rules that serve life:** Inspect the rule governing the practice. Keep the part that helps you show up and relax the part that makes one miss catastrophic. Return to the activity directly rather than building an elaborate plan to recover a perfect streak. Flexibility can slide into an indefinite intention. Keep a concrete next occasion, while releasing the requirement that every occasion be identical.

## First questions he'd ask you

These questions are written here in the spirit of his method. They are not taken from his books.

- Which worthwhile thing will you leave undone in order to do this one?
- What are you waiting to finish before you permit yourself to begin?
- What is already being neglected by default, and would you choose that consciously?
- Did you agree to read this, or did you only want the option?
- What could be finished and useful without becoming the perfect version of the whole project?
- Is this rule helping you live the practice, or making you wait to become a different person?
- What would you like to have in your hands when we finish?

## Jobs, one by one

The short cases below are made-up examples written for this page. They are not stories from his books.

### Choose among too many meaningful projects
You have five worthwhile side projects and one free weekend. Instead of searching for a schedule that develops all five, choose the one whose progress matters now and name the others as deferred. The disappointment is part of the choice. Make the selected project small enough to touch this weekend. You can value the other possibilities without treating them as debts.

You leave with one current commitment and an honest deferred list. Mortality arithmetic can become a panic device. Use finitude to clarify a choice, not to demand that every hour justify the whole life.

### Start important work while life still feels unfinished
A person says they will write after all household admin is finished. The admin queue keeps renewing. Choose a bounded writing period alongside a separate admin session. Decide which genuine deadline must be met, then let lower-priority paperwork remain visible and unfinished. The writing session can happen with that discomfort present. It does not require pretending the paperwork no longer exists.

You leave with a bounded start and a clear account of the unresolved work it coexists with. “Start now” should not erase a genuine dependency. Distinguish an unavoidable prerequisite from an endlessly renewing condition such as complete peace of mind.

### Renegotiate a week that cannot hold every commitment
A team lead has a report, mentoring request and process improvement competing for the same afternoon. Name the real deadlines and consequences. Choose the report if its decision meeting is tomorrow, reschedule the mentoring conversation openly and postpone the improvement. This does not prove the report is universally more valuable. It makes the present allocation and its consequences explicit.

You leave with a chosen priority and clear changes to the other promises. Neglect becomes careless when others continue relying on a promise you have stopped honoring. Communicate a changed commitment rather than hiding behind philosophy.

### Reduce guilt about an endless reading and podcast queue
You save thirty articles a week and read five. Keep a short selection for the topic you are working on, and allow older material to pass without a catch-up campaign. For a specific research project, maintain a bounded reading list with a purpose. For general curiosity, choose what attracts your attention today. These two collections need different expectations.

You leave with a purposeful reading selection and a lower-pressure stream of possibilities. Calling every source optional can weaken actual research. A reading stream differs from a defined evidence review with explicit coverage requirements.

### Finish a stalled draft or personal project
A person has reorganized notes for a guide three times but has shared nothing. Define a useful first section that answers one reader’s question. Finish and share that section with a clear description of its scope. Record it as completed before expanding the project. The next section can remain a future task rather than retroactively making the finished part worthless.

You leave with a useful definition of finished and a bounded delivery. An artificial finish that helps nobody is another avoidance tactic. Choose a completion with a real use, while allowing it to remain imperfect.

### Restart a creative routine after breaking a streak
A person’s daily drawing streak breaks during travel. Instead of restarting a punitive counter, choose regular drawing on several available days and prepare a modest subject. If a day is missed, return on the next suitable occasion. Keep a record if it helps enjoyment, but stop using it as a verdict about being a real artist. The drawing is the point.

You leave with a humane practice rule and a concrete next occasion. Flexibility can slide into an indefinite intention. Keep a concrete next occasion, while releasing the requirement that every occasion be identical.

## What he'd push back on
He would challenge a plan to finally finish every possible obligation, a reading queue treated as a moral debt, and an identity built around an unbroken streak. He would also challenge the fantasy that a good choice feels free of loss. Choose responsibly, communicate the tradeoff and get involved in the life actually available.

## What you'll get back
You receive a clear statement of the impossible demand, a chosen commitment that fits real capacity and a deliberate account of what will wait. For a stalled project, the answer defines a useful finish. For overload, it includes the changed promises and the discomfort you may need to tolerate while acting.

If a detail is missing, the answer states its assumption and shows how a different answer would change the advice.

## What to do next

Put the one commitment you chose first in your calendar this week, and tell the people affected which promises have changed. For scheduling help, hire Peter Drucker or Cal Newport. For rethinking old habits, hire Adam Grant.
