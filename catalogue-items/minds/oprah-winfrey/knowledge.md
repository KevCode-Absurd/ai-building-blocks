## In one paragraph

Winfrey grew up poor in Mississippi, Milwaukee and Nashville, worked in radio as a teenager and later anchored local news [S4]. Being taken off the news desk in Baltimore pushed her into talk shows [S2][S8]. In 1984 she took over a Chicago morning show that became *The Oprah Winfrey Show*, which ran nationally from 1986 to 2011 and was the highest-rated daytime talk show in US history [S6]. She built Harpo Productions, a magazine, a book club that could turn any book into a bestseller, and her own TV network, OWN [S4][S5]. Her method was connection: she believed people most want to feel seen and understood [S1], she trusted her gut over other people's advice [S9], and she wanted viewers to leave believing they were responsible for their own lives [S2]. Critics faulted her for giving airtime to guests with weak health claims and for backing a memoir that turned out to be invented [S4][S5].

## Core ideas

### Everyone wants to be seen

In her 2013 Harvard speech, Winfrey said that across thousands of interviews, the common thread was that people want to be validated and understood [S1]. After almost every interview, she said, guests would ask her if it had been okay, even the famous and powerful [S1].

How she applied it: in 1991 she said she wanted guests to keep their dignity on the show, even when she disagreed with them [S2]. At Duke she described her time with Nelson Mandela: on the advice of a friend she mostly listened, and she was struck that he asked what her show was about [S9]. Her style made the talk show more personal and confessional, a shift critics called "Oprahfication" [S4].

### Failure is a change of direction

Winfrey told Harvard graduates there is no such thing as failure. In her view, failure is life trying to move you in another direction. Feel the loss, learn from the mistake, and then take the next right step [S1].

How she applied it: in Baltimore she was taken off the anchor desk, partly because she cried over the stories she covered, and the move to a talk show felt like coming home [S8][S11]. When OWN was widely called a flop in its first year, she called it the worst period of her professional life, and she later turned it around [S1].

### An inner compass: listen to the whisper

Winfrey argued that the key to life is to develop an internal moral and emotional guide, like a GPS, that tells you what to do [S1]. At Stanford's business school she called instinct an emotional GPS and said her choices blend feeling and logic [S12]. In 1997 she told Wellesley graduates to listen for "the whisper" early, before a problem grows loud [S8]. At Duke she said every experience presents a choice between what is right and what is popular, and that she trusts her own gut more than anyone's advice [S9].

How she applied it: for a hard decision she told students to get still, ask what the next right step is, take it, and repeat [S12]. She urged graduates to ask what makes them come alive [S1].

### You are responsible for your life

In 1991 Winfrey said her intention was for viewers to understand that they are responsible for their own lives, and that triumph is always possible [S2]. At Duke she said the same about energy: you are responsible for the energy you bring into a room [S9].

How she applied it: she said she had stopped giving airtime to guests whose message did no good [S2].

### Reading and education

Winfrey said that being able to read saved her life and that education is what freed her [S2]. Her grandmother taught her to read early, and books showed her other ways of living were possible [S2].

How she applied it: she started Oprah's Book Club in September 1996, with *The Deep End of the Ocean* [S5]. Over about 15 years it picked 70 books [S5]. On giving, she said she backs education only where it truly helps the people served, not out of vague good intentions [S12].

### Prepare, practice and stop imitating

Winfrey said her comfort on camera came from years of practice and from learning that on-air mistakes matter less than people fear [S2]. At Wellesley she said her early blunders, such as mispronouncing a word, came from not reading her material first, and that she stopped copying Barbara Walters once she began to laugh at herself [S8]. Before each show she took a few minutes to sit quietly and focus [S2].

How she applied it: she started in radio at 17 after winning a local contest [S2]. She also said luck is what happens when preparation meets an opportunity [S12].

### Give the audience a moment they'll never forget

Winfrey's show became known for big, shared moments. Her "Favorite Things" giveaway began in 1996 [S6]. At the 2004 season opener, every audience member got a new car [S6]. Her book picks and favourite things could change a product's fate overnight [S5][S6].

How she applied it: the show won 47 Daytime Emmys before she stopped entering it in 2000, and the final episode in 2011 drew its highest rating in 17 years [S6]. Her 1990s Wildest Dreams tour asked women to write their dreams, and she and her team made several come true [S8].

### Find common ground, and tell the truth

At Harvard, Winfrey called for empathy and face-to-face talk across divides [S1]. At the 2018 Golden Globes she called speaking the truth the most powerful tool people have, and she told one woman's story in full, that of Recy Taylor, to show why [S10]. She said she had tried in her work to show how people really behave, including shame, love, anger and perseverance [S10].

How she applied it: she urged graduates to seek out face-to-face conversation and work with people they disagree with on shared problems such as food, housing and education [S1].

### Serve first; purpose shows up in the work

At Colorado College she told graduates to keep the job that pays the rent even if it is not their passion, because an early job is not the mission and purpose becomes clear through experience [S11]. She said purpose is revealed daily, as the thread through what you do, and that service means asking how your work serves others as well as you [S11]. Maya Angelou once told her that a legacy is every life you touch [S11].

How she applied it: she told a young woman she mentored to keep her job and do that work well until she could do the work she wanted [S11].

## Real decisions

### Moving from the news desk to talk (Baltimore, late 1970s)
She struggled as a news reporter in Baltimore and was taken off the anchor desk, partly because she cried over the stories she covered [S8][S11]. She called it a painful failure, and the next job, co-hosting a talk show, felt like coming home [S2][S8]. Her reason for taking the lesson was that the failure pointed her somewhere better [S2]. She had started in radio at 17 and learned her TV style partly by copying Barbara Walters, until she let go of imitating her [S2][S8].

### Buying her own studio
While filming *The Color Purple* she felt boxed in by her contract and short of time. Her lawyer, who became her business partner, said she could own her show and pointed to a studio. She first dismissed the idea, then bought it so she could act and host at the same time [S2]. She also said she wanted to keep hosting only while it meant something, and not "stay in the ring too long" [S2].

### Choosing guests and pulling shows
In 1991 she said she wanted viewers to leave believing they were responsible for their own lives, and she no longer gave airtime to racists. She shelved an interview with a mass murderer because it would serve no good purpose, and said a postpartum-depression episode should not have aired in the afternoon [S2]. In 2009 she described pulling a show about the Columbine killers even after promos aired, because it would bring more darkness into the world [S9]. In 2019 she described booking Ku Klux Klan members early on, believing she could expose them, then seeing they were using her platform, and changing course. She also took the blame when a guest revealed a pregnancy on air, and resolved that the show should serve viewers, not just entertain [S11].

### Wanting a part so badly she created it
She was not an actress and wanted the role of Sofia in *The Color Purple*. She read the novel, gave copies to people she knew, and talked up the film before she had any link to the producers. Quincy Jones spotted her on television [S2]. Her lesson: intense wanting and persistence can make an opportunity happen [S2].

### Starting a book club, and confronting a faked memoir
In September 1996 she started Oprah's Book Club with *The Deep End of the Ocean* [S5]. In 2006 James Frey's *A Million Little Pieces*, a club pick, was found to be largely made up, and she confronted Frey and his publisher on air [S5].

### Asking women what they dreamed of (Wildest Dreams tour)
On a tour with Tina Turner, women sent about 77,000 letters about their dreams. Oprah noticed that people with deeper hardships tended to dream smaller [S8]. The team made several come true, such as paying off a debt, giving a house to a woman who had fled abuse, and sending a woman to Egypt [S8]. Her message afterwards was to dream bigger [S8].

### Building her own network, then turning it around
She launched OWN on 1 January 2011 [S4]. A year later most of the media called it a flop. She called it the worst stretch of her professional life, felt embarrassed, and said she used that low point to turn the network around [S1].

### Telling a young woman to keep the job
A young woman she mentored got a job, a used car and an apartment, then was offered a promotion that did not feel like her purpose. Winfrey told her to keep the job and do the work well until she could do the work she wanted [S11]. Her stated reasons were that the first job is not the whole mission, and that purpose is revealed through experience [S11].
