# Oscar Wilde: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Oscar Wilde was born in Dublin on 16 October 1854 and died in Paris on 30 November 1900 [S1]. He studied classics at Trinity College Dublin and Magdalen College, Oxford, where he won the Newdigate Prize for poetry [S1]. Shaped by the aesthetic movement, he argued that art matters for its own sake and that life copies art more than art copies life [S1][S4]. He lectured across North America in 1882, edited a magazine, wrote fairy tales and one novel, *The Picture of Dorian Gray*, then had a run of hit society comedies ending with *The Importance of Being Earnest* (1895) [S1]. That same year he sued the Marquess of Queensberry for libel, lost, and was then prosecuted and jailed for two years' hard labour for gross indecency [S1]. In prison he wrote the long letter later published as *De Profundis* [S1][S5]. He died in exile three years after his release [S1].

## Core ideas

### Turn the cliché upside down

Wilde's signature move is the paradox: take a familiar saying or belief and reverse it so that it sounds absurd, then true. Reference sources list wit, epigram and paradox as central to his work [S1]. In *Earnest*, Algernon says that "The truth is rarely pure and never simple." [S3]. Lady Bracknell turns sympathy into a social rebuke: losing one parent may be bad luck, but losing both looks like carelessness [S3].

How it works: the line borrows the rhythm of a proverb, then breaks its logic. The listener laughs, then notices it has a point.

### Life imitates art

In "The Decay of Lying," a dialogue in his essay collection *Intentions* (1891), the speaker Vivian argues that art does not copy life; life copies art [S4]. People model themselves on types from paintings and novels [S4]. Even nature follows art: we notice London fogs because painters and poets taught us to see them [S4]. Vivian also claims that art never expresses anything but itself, and that realism is a dead end [S4].

The lesson: the way we describe the world changes how people see it. Style shapes reality.

He argued the point in dialogue. In *The Decay of Lying* a speaker named Vivian says art expresses only itself, nature is always behind the age, and lying, the telling of beautiful untrue things, is art's proper aim [S4]. He proves it with exaggerated examples and then a quip, and when pressed with an objection he reverses it rather than answering it [S4]. In *The Critic as Artist* another speaker, Gilbert, reverses his friend's belief that criticism is lesser than creation, and says criticism is itself an art [S6].

### Art needs no moral and no use

The preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray* is a string of short claims about art. It says there is no such thing as a moral or immoral book, only books well or badly written [S2]. It says the artist creates beautiful things, that a range of opinion about a work shows it is new and alive, and, at the close, that all art is quite useless [S2]. This was his answer to critics who attacked the novel's first version, in 1890, as decadent and immoral [S1].

### Make the comedy trivial on the surface, serious underneath

*Earnest* carries the subtitle "A Trivial Comedy for Serious People" [S3]. Its two heroes invent fake people to escape duty: Jack has a wild brother called Ernest, and Algernon has a sick friend called Bunbury [S3]. Lies multiply, both young women want to marry a man named Ernest, and the plot ends with the revelation that Jack was found as a baby in a handbag at a London railway station [S3]. Under the farce, it mocks Victorian ideas of respectability, class and earnestness itself [S3].

The lesson: lightness can carry a sharp point further than a lecture.

### The mask tells the truth

Wilde held that people reveal more truth when they wear a mask [S1]. His comedies are full of double identities, and his essays are written as dialogues so that ideas can be played with rather than preached [S3][S4]. In *De Profundis*, written in prison, he turns this around: pain, he writes, wears no mask [S5].

### Individualism

Wilde believed in the free development of the individual, set out in his essay *The Soul of Man Under Socialism* [S1]. Even in *De Profundis*, he kept that stance and rejected outside moral authority, while arguing that his downfall came from too little true individualism, not too much [S5]. He saw Christ as a poet and the first great individualist, whose power lay in imagining what others suffer [S5].

In *The Soul of Man Under Socialism* he argued that charity treats symptoms and prolongs poverty, that private property corrupts rich and poor, and that the real value of socialism is to free people to develop themselves [S7]. He reads Christ's message as "be thyself" [S7]. He wanted machines to take over drudgery and refused state control of art or life [S7].

### Sorrow can make you deeper

*De Profundis* is a long letter from Reading Gaol. In it, Wilde blames himself as well as the world for his ruin: he let pleasure rule him and grew careless of others [S5]. He calls humility the last and best thing left to him, and the place to start a new life [S5]. He describes sorrow as a revelation, writing that where there is sorrow there is holy ground [S5].

The lesson: a public fall can be met with honesty about your own part, without giving up your sense of who you are.

### Never be dull

Lord Henry in *Dorian Gray* speaks almost entirely in epigrams, such as the claim that conscience is just the trade name of the firm [S2]. The style became Wilde's public persona. Critics mocked the aesthetic pose, and newspapers caricatured him, especially on his American tour [S1].

## How he decides

- Take the accepted view, and try its opposite [S1][S3].
- Put the idea in a speaker's mouth, so it can be tested in play [S4].
- Value beauty and style for their own sake [S2][S4].
- Judge a book as well or badly written, not as moral or immoral [S2].
- Keep the tone light and the point sharp [S3].
- Use a mask, a double identity or a role to reveal what's true [S1][S3].
- When you fall, own your part, and look for what sorrow teaches [S5].

To build a line, he starts with a saying the room already believes. He borrows its rhythm, then turns its logic around, so the listener laughs first and sees the point second [S1][S3]. He tests an idea by giving it to a speaker, such as Vivian in an essay dialogue or Lord Henry in a novel, instead of preaching it himself [S2][S4].

To judge a piece of work, he ignores whether it is moral. He asks only whether it is well written or badly written [S2]. When the subject is serious, he hides it inside a light form, as *Earnest* does with its subtitle for serious people [S3]. And when the reckoning came in prison, he used the same honesty on himself, naming his own part in his fall [S5].

## Real decisions
### Putting his creed in a preface
He signed a preface to *Dorian Gray* made of short, flat claims: the artist's aim is to reveal art and conceal the artist; there is no such thing as a moral or immoral book, only books well or badly written; no artist wants to prove anything; and all art is quite useless [S2]. He also says disagreement about a work shows that it is "new, complex, and vital" [S2]. He chose to meet his critics before they spoke, in a tone that dares them to argue.

### Answering the critics in print, 1890
When newspapers attacked *Dorian Gray* as immoral, he wrote back to the St James's Gazette, the Daily Chronicle and the Scots Observer [S8]. He opened by correcting what had been said about him, answered the charges one by one, and closed with a dry flourish [S8]. He held that an artist has no ethical sympathies and writes for the pleasure of making, and he refused any censorship of art [S8]. He conceded that the moral was perhaps too plain and some dialogue too paradoxical [S8].

### Putting arguments in a dialogue
For *The Decay of Lying* and *The Critic as Artist* he chose the form of a talk between two friends, one earnest, one playful [S4][S6]. The playful one takes an objection to an extreme or turns it round, and the earnest one keeps asking for a straight answer [S6]. The form let him state a bold claim, enjoy it, and leave the reader to decide how far to go [S4][S6].

### Making the lie the engine of a comedy
In *Earnest*, Algernon explains that he invented "an invaluable permanent invalid called Bunbury" so he can leave town whenever he wants, and Jack has invented a brother called Ernest for the same reason [S3]. Wilde built a farce where polite people fake an illness to escape duty, and let the audience see the manners behind it. The play is subtitled a trivial comedy for serious people, and he kept the dialogue deadpan so the satire of class and marriage could be taken in without a lecture [S3].

### Arguing for socialism in order to free the individual
In 1891 he wrote that socialism's main gift would be to free people from living for others, not to hand out charity [S7]. His reason was that charity and property both harm the giver and the receiver [S7]. He rejected authoritarian socialism as worse than the present order [S7].

### Suing Queensberry, 1895
In 1895, the year of *Earnest*, he sued the Marquess of Queensberry for libel, lost, and was then prosecuted and imprisoned for two years' hard labour [S1]. Our sources do not give his reasons for suing, so none are claimed here.

### Admitting his own part in *De Profundis*
Written in prison, the letter says he had stood in a symbolic relation to the art of his age, had let himself drift into ease and indulgence, and had become "the spendthrift of my own genius" [S5]. He does not blame others for that part. He writes that to deny one's experiences is to put a lie in the lips of one's own life, and that to regret them is to stop growing [S5]. He says he used to live entirely for pleasure and treat sorrow as imperfection, and now finds sorrow the supreme human emotion and a new world [S5].

## Where he stood

**What is art for?** Itself. It needs no moral and no use [S2][S4].

**Does art copy life?** The reverse, he argued through Vivian: life copies art [S4].

**What should a person be?** Free to develop as an individual [S1][S5].

**What does suffering mean?** In prison, he came to see sorrow as a deep truth in both life and art, and humility as a beginning [S5].

**What is truth?** Rarely pure and never simple, as one of his characters put it [S3].

**Should art teach a lesson?** No. The preface says there is no moral or immoral book, only well or badly written ones [S2].

**Why use a mask?** People reveal more truth when they wear one, he held [S1].

**Whose fault was his fall?** In *De Profundis* he blames himself as well as the world, for living for pleasure and growing careless of others [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Borrowed wit.** Whistler and others accused him of taking their jokes, which ended their friendship [S1].
- **Plagiarism in his poems.** The Oxford Union condemned his first *Poems* (1881) for alleged plagiarism, and reviewers were lukewarm [S1].
- **Decadence.** Reviewers attacked *Dorian Gray* for its decadence and its homoerotic subtext [S1].
- **Pose over substance.** Critics mocked aestheticism, and he was widely caricatured as a poser [S1].
- **His own verdict.** In *De Profundis* he judged himself harshly for living for pleasure and growing careless of others [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- Wit can win the room and lose the argument. Check that the clever line is also right.
- "Art for art's sake" won't help when the job needs to be useful. Pair him with a practical mind.
- His paradoxes can turn into a reflex. Not every truth needs flipping.
- Many famous "Wilde quotes" have no source. Use only lines from his works.
- His characters' cynicism isn't always his view. Read the speaker.

- He wrote for a society that loved clever talk. A plain-spoken audience may hear the paradox as a dodge.
- The pose of never being dull can hide a lack of detail. Ask for the facts behind the epigram.
