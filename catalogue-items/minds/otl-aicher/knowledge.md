## In one paragraph

Aicher was a German graphic designer whose life was shaped by his opposition to the Nazis [S1][S2]. After the war he helped found the Ulm School of Design, known as the HfG, led its visual communication department and later served as its director [S1][S2]. He became a pioneer of corporate design, shaping the identities of Braun and Lufthansa, and he led the design of the 1972 Munich Olympics, where his sports pictograms became world famous [S1][S2]. His approach was systematic: a few strict rules for colour, type, grid and imagery, applied by a team, so everything belonged to one family [S2][S7]. Behind the systems sat a moral aim. He wanted "a comprehensive interaction between life and design," and he saw design as a way to build a better, more reasonable society [S2].

## Core ideas

### Design should serve life and society

Aicher's wartime experience left him convinced that designers have a social duty [S5]. As a teenager he refused to join the Hitler Youth and was arrested for it in 1937. His close friends Hans and Sophie Scholl were executed for their part in the White Rose resistance [S1][S2]. He framed his life's work as a move away from despotism and ideology toward reason and sense [S2]. He saw the designer as an author and instigator who serves society and works as part of a team [S7].

How he applied it: after the war he and Inge Scholl, whom he married in 1952, helped rebuild civic life through adult education in Ulm, which led to the design school [S1][S2]. He even wrote in lowercase letters to express equality and reject the power of an absolute state [S5].

### Feeling and atmosphere before features

For Munich 1972, Aicher said his team looked for a feeling instead of features and an atmosphere instead of symbols [S3]. He described the visual work as a form of stage direction, where colours and symbols were parts of a staging [S3]. He wanted the Games to feel unforced, open, carefree and relaxed, and celebratory in a playful rather than an official way [S4].

How he applied it: he ruled out anything that looked like nationalism or militarism [S3]. He wanted to keep the good side of the 1936 Berlin Games while wiping out their dark meaning [S4].

### Colour carries meaning, so choose it on purpose

Aicher treated colour as a moral and political choice. His team had to decide which parts of the colour wheel could build a credible identity for a different Germany [S3]. He linked blue with peace and with the Bavarian landscape, and he left out reds, blacks and golds, which he tied to the Nazis, to Caesar and to dictatorship [S3].

How he applied it: Munich's palette was built on light blue and white, inspired by the Alps, deliberately unlike the red and black of Berlin 1936 [S5].

### A few strict rules, then room to play

His systems rested on a small set of tools: colour, type, page format and grids [S3]. The Munich identity joined bold colour, the Univers typeface, imagery and icons into one system [S1][S7]. The rules were firm, but he encouraged play inside them. The aim was consistency, not rigid dogma [S3].

How he applied it: at Lufthansa in the early 1960s he kept the airline's flying crane but placed it in a circle and set the name in Helvetica. The design has largely survived [S5]. He led the whole house style, from aircraft paint to luggage labels [S2].

### Signs that work across languages

Aicher's pictograms reduced each sport and service to a simple figure drawn on a grid, so anyone could read it regardless of language [S4][S5]. Between 1967 and 1972 he and Gerhard Joksch used a simple grid to design the sports symbols [S6]. His Olympic pictograms directly shaped later sign systems, including the U.S. transportation pictograms [S1].

How he applied it: he extended the same picture language into signage for Frankfurt Airport, working with the lighting firm ERCO, and it is still widely seen in airports and public buildings [S5].

### Lead a team, trust the team

Aicher ran large teams. For Munich he led a department of at least 82 people, and he trusted his team leads and drew out their best work, though he could be demanding [S3]. His teamwork approach is what won him the role of overall designer for the Games [S2].

How he applied it: he favoured close working ties between designer and client, as in his long partnership with Klaus Jürgen Maack at ERCO [S7].

#### First among equals

A designer on the Munich team recalled that Aicher tried to be first among equals, not the grand boss [S11]. The team began with weekend work at his woodland retreat in late 1965, moved into an open-plan studio in 1967 with desks he designed himself, and grew to about eight project leaders, each owning an area such as pictograms, signage or the mascot [S11]. The team's stated goal was to show Germany as a changed, social-liberal, post-national republic in cheerful colours and democratic forms [S11].

### Ordinary days matter more than "cultural Sundays"

In his later writing he traced the history of thought, design and building to ask how people can arrange life in a humane, civilised way [S8]. He argued for renewing modern design, which he felt had worn itself out in purely aesthetic visions, and he held that the ordinary working day matters more than cultural showpieces [S8].

How he applied it: he moved his studio to Rotis, an old mill in the Allgäu, in the early 1970s and worked there for the rest of his life [S5]. He wrote books on the kitchen, the car and design [S1], and from 1980 he consulted for the kitchen maker Bulthaup [S1].

### A typeface between two worlds

Late in life Aicher designed the Rotis type family, named after his home. It was a semi-serif design, an attempt to bridge sans serif and roman letters [S2][S5]. He also wrote *Typographie*, a long study of type's history and uses [S5].

How he applied it: Rotis came out in 1988 [S1]. He also designed logos for the University of Konstanz and Munich Airport [S1].

### The world as design, and making as real work

In his essays Aicher said the world can be seen in three ways: as a fixed order, as a process of evolution, or as something people design. He chose the third [S10]. He held that Western philosophy had focused on knowing and neglected making, and that society wrongly ranks thinking and managing above making [S9][S10]. For him real work is creative and self-directed: your own concept, design, execution and review, in a loop that refines both the idea and the person [S9][S10]. Most people, he wrote, have only a job and no longer any work [S9]. He called design the mark of creativity and said a philosophy of making and design was still missing [S9][S10]. He also rejected the idea that aesthetics belongs only to art: everything concrete and real has aesthetic relations, and design should be a way of life, not decoration [S10].

How he applied it: he argued that civilised culture is something people must make and fight for, against apparent practical constraints and thin cultural substitutes [S8][S10].

## How he decides

- Choose colours for what they mean, and rule out the ones with bad history [S3].
- Fix a few core tools: colour, type, format, grid [S3].
- Allow play inside the rules, aiming for consistency over dogma [S3].
- Draw signs on a grid so they read the same everywhere [S5][S6].

## Real decisions

### Refusing the Hitler Youth, 1937

As a teenager he refused to join the Hitler Youth and was arrested in 1937 [S1][S2]. In 1945 he deserted the army and hid at the Scholl family's home [S1].

### Co-founding the Ulm School of Design, 1953

With Inge Scholl and Max Bill he co-founded the school, which opened in 1955 and ran until 1968 [S1][S2]. It grew out of adult education he and Scholl started to rebuild civic life [S1][S2].

### Redrawing Lufthansa in the early 1960s

He kept the airline's flying crane, placed it in a circle and set the name in Helvetica, and led the whole house style from aircraft paint to luggage labels [S2][S5].

### Choosing the colours of Munich 1972

He linked blue with peace and the Bavarian landscape, built the palette on light blue and white, and left out the reds, blacks and golds he tied to dictatorship [S3][S5].

### Drawing the pictograms on a grid

Between 1967 and 1972 he and Gerhard Joksch designed the sports symbols on a simple grid. A 2025 study found the production was rushed and the figures do not match their own grid, though it still ranks them among design's high points [S6].

### Moving the studio to Rotis

In the early 1970s he moved his studio to Rotis, an old mill in the Allgau, and worked there for the rest of his life [S5]. He later named his type family for it [S2][S5].

## Where he stood

**What is design for?** For life and for a better society. He wanted a full interaction between life and design [S2].

**Should national design show power?** No. For Munich he ruled out anything nationalist or military and wanted a relaxed, open feel [S3][S4].

**Is a design system a straitjacket?** Not in his hands. He set firm rules and encouraged play within them [S3].

**What matters more, culture on show or daily life?** Daily life. He put the ordinary working day above cultural showpieces [S8].

**Who is the designer?** An author and instigator who serves society as part of a team [S7].

## His work and record

- Born in Ulm in 1922 [S1].
- 1937: arrested for refusing to join the Hitler Youth. In 1945 he deserted the army and hid at the Scholl family's home [S1].
- After the war studied sculpture in Munich, and opened a studio in Ulm in 1947 [S1].
- 1952: married Inge Scholl [S1].
- 1953: co-founded the Ulm School of Design with Inge Scholl and Max Bill. It opened in 1955 and ran until 1968 [S1][S2].
- Worked on identity for Braun, and in the early 1960s on Lufthansa [S1][S2][S5].
- 1966: hired to lead the design of the 1972 Munich Olympics [S4]. Created the pictograms, colours, emblem and Waldi, the first official Olympic mascot [S1].
- 1972: redrew the Sparkasse savings bank logo into the red version still used in Germany [S5].
- Early 1970s: moved his studio to Rotis [S5].
- From 1980: consulted for Bulthaup [S1].
- Books include *The Kitchen is for Cooking* (1982), *Critique of the Automobile* (1984) and *The World as Design* (1991) [S1][S8].
- 1988: released the Rotis typeface [S1].
- Died in Günzburg in 1991, after being hit while mowing grass (motorbike or car, sources differ) [S1][S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Hard to work with.** A review of his biography notes his frequent clashes with more intellectual staff at Ulm, and describes him lecturing clients on how to run their companies, even on how to choose flowers [S2].
- **Rotis.** His biographer criticised how much more money went into promoting Rotis than into designing and producing it [S2].
- **Rushed pictograms.** A 2025 study found that the Munich pictograms don't match their own grid. Limbs built from straight and 45-degree parts can't have even thickness, and the production was rushed with no check against the grid [S6]. The same writer still ranks the pictograms among design's high points [S6].
- **Reason over feeling.** His systems can seem cold. Yet his own account of Munich put feeling and atmosphere first [S3], so the charge fits some of his work better than others.

## Blind spots to watch

- Strict systems can crush local voices. Small teams may need looser rules than an Olympic Games.
- His moral reading of colour came from a specific history. Colours carry different meanings in other places and times.
- He could lecture clients. Pair him with someone strong on listening and persuasion.
- Grids and rules don't guarantee quality. As the pictogram study shows, someone still has to check the final work [S6].
