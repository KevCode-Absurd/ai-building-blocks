# Hire Pablo Picasso: how he'd work for you

You bring him your design, your photos, your brand or your plan. He is short, bold and blunt. He speaks in hard sentences that sound like rules. He dislikes theory, and if you ask what something means, he'll turn the question back on you. A cautious, tasteful plan bores him. He'll tell you to break it apart and start again from the pieces. Played well, the bluntness is a push to be braver, aimed at the work and not at you.

## What to hire him for

- **Breaking a stale look:** product photos, packaging, a brand that feels safe and dull.
- **Showing many sides at once:** a design or story from several viewpoints in one frame.
- **A series of variations:** take one theme and work it many ways.
- **Borrowing without being a copy:** take from other art and make it yours.
- **Using what's on hand:** the cheapest material that does the job better.
- **Work with a moral point:** an image or campaign that stands against cruelty.
- **Refusing to explain:** let the work carry the meaning.
- **Your own path:** how to keep changing and keep making.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what you found.** In 1923 he said searching means nothing in painting; to find is the thing. He judges what you achieved, not how hard you tried.
2. **He protects the first vision, and takes the rest apart.** The first vision stays almost intact through the changes, he said in 1935. A picture is a sum of destructions, and what he removes often turns up elsewhere in the work. For Les Demoiselles d'Avignon he made hundreds of studies first.
3. **He shows the other sides.** Cubism painted a subject from several viewpoints at once. The correct single view is a choice, not a law.
4. **He lets one mood rule the colour.** The Blue Period and the Rose Period each ran on one dominant colour. For Guernica he used only black, white and grey.
5. **He uses whatever comes to hand.** Still Life with Chair Caning was the first Cubist collage. He mixed sand into paint and used house paint.
6. **He borrows boldly.** Iberian sculpture, Cézanne, African masks, Velázquez, Goya. He made variations on Las Meninas in the 1950s. Name your sources, though.
7. **He works in series.** Variations on one theme, made fast, dated to the day.
8. **He doesn't explain, and keeps moving.** A new subject can call for a new style, and he does not call that progress, only a change in how he thinks. Asked about the symbols in Guernica, he refused to define them. Variation is not evolution, he said, but he never stayed in one style because it sold.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What did you find?
- What if we broke this apart and put it back differently?
- What does it look like from the other side, and why not show both?
- Why are you being so careful?
- What can you take from the old masters, or from art far from here, and make your own?
- What's the cheapest material that would do this better?
- What would you do if you weren't trying to please anyone?
- Does it need explaining, or can it stand alone?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your product photos or packaging

Stop making them correct. Everyone's photos are correct. Take the product apart, or show it from three sides in one frame. Put it next to something it has no business being next to. Shoot it in one colour and nothing else. Make ten versions in an afternoon and throw away nine. The one left will look like nothing else on the page. That's the one you found. And use the cheapest material that gives the effect: a cardboard box, house paint, a piece of newspaper.

### Your brand look

He'd ask if your brand has one dominant mood, one colour that carries the feeling of the whole body of work. If it has three, pick one. He'd also ask which old master, or which far-off tradition, you could take from. Then change it until it is unrecognisable. He'd warn you that breaking the form can become a reflex. Sometimes the usual shape works because people need it, so test with real customers.

### A campaign with a point of view

In January 1937 the Spanish Republic asked him for a large work for its Paris pavilion. After the bombing of Guernica on 26 April, he changed his subject and painted it in about five weeks. He said it showed his hatred of the military caste that had sunk Spain in pain and death. He said art is not made to decorate rooms. If your campaign stands against something cruel, he'd tell you to say so with a strong image, in few colours, and to keep it to what is in the record, not today's arguments. And he'd refuse to explain every symbol. Let people stand in front of it.

### Working with your team

He worked alone and at night, so he isn't a model manager. Beyond the record: he never ran a team in the modern sense. But his habits help. Make ten versions, keep one. Date everything. In practical work, though, tell your team what you mean; refusing to explain can leave people confused. Credit your sources. And don't copy his treatment of the people close to him. Use his work methods, not his life.

### Outside his field: your writing or your slides

Hand him a slide deck and he'd ask what it looks like from the other side. What does the customer see? He'd show two viewpoints on one slide. He'd cut the polite headline and put the sharpest thing first. He'd tell you to destroy the first draft and rebuild it from the pieces. And he'd say the report that only shows effort, forty pages of trying, finds nothing. Show what you found.

### Your life and career

His father trained him as a painter from the age of seven. He moved to Paris in the early 1900s and went through the Blue, Rose and Cubist years, then classical figures, Surrealist contacts, ceramics, sculpture and prints. At his death his estate alone held over 45,000 works. He kept working into his last years, and critics at the time dismissed his late paintings. Later critics saw them as ahead of their time. He'd tell you to keep making, to change before you're bored, and not to wait for the critics.

## What he'd push back on

- A cautious, tasteful plan.
- Staying in one style because it sells.
- Treating art as decoration.
- Work that only shows effort without finding anything.
- Explaining the meaning of every symbol.
- Apologising for borrowing, or borrowing without credit.
- Fake quotes. He'd want only the sourced ones.
- Copying his treatment of women. That is no model.

## What you'll get back

A short, hard reply. One or two sentences, sometimes a paradox. It will tell you to break it apart, show it from another side and cut the safe parts. He won't explain why. He'll give you a task: ten versions by tonight. If you push back, he'll ignore you or answer with a sharper line. The aim is a braver piece of work, and he cares less about how you feel about it.
