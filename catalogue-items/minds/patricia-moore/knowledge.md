# Patricia Moore: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Statements about this living designer refer to the checked publications. Exercises and hypothetical applications are written by us, not personal advice from Moore.

## In one paragraph

Patricia Moore, born in 1952, is an industrial designer and gerontologist. Her public work asks how ordinary products, environments and social encounters affect independence across age and ability. The World Design Organization describes her travels disguised as older women from 1979 to 1982 as formative to that approach. Her useful contribution here is attention to the whole experience of a mundane task, including the dignity of the person doing it and the response of other people. [S5][S3]

## Core ideas

### Begin with a moment of ordinary frustration

In the 2026 interview, Moore recalls observing her grandparents' difficulties with mobility and a refrigerator handle. She connects those childhood observations to her later design concerns. [S1]

Our application begins with an ordinary event rather than an age category. Describe the last time someone wanted to open a package, prepare a meal or enter a shop and could not do it comfortably. What were they holding? What did the object require? Where did help become necessary? The purpose is to find a specific demand that can change. Do not turn a single difficulty into an assumption that the person needs help with everything. A small obstacle can deserve attention precisely because the activity itself is an ordinary part of life.

### The encounter includes how people treat you

Moore's interview describes both kindness and dismissive treatment during her disguised travels. The Royal College of Art account also recounts her experience of being overlooked at a conference about housing for older people. [S1][S4]

For our service review, map the human exchanges as carefully as the physical route. Does someone address the visitor directly or speak only to a companion? Can a person ask a question without announcing private information across a room? Is there a way to say that more time is needed? A comfortable chair does not repair a humiliating interaction. Record the exact behaviour and its effect without guessing the employee's motives. A useful improvement might involve the order in which staff offer information as well as a change to the room.

### Being designed for should not mean being left out

The RCA account frames Moore's objection as the absence of older people from decisions about their own lives. It reports her insistence on equity and the diversity of people's wishes and needs. [S4]

Our research plan asks participants about the activities they value before showing a proposed solution. Invite them to reject the premise. If a family wants to reorganise an older relative's kitchen, ask which routines work, which items must remain familiar and what assistance is actually wanted. The relative should not become a source of data for a decision already made by everyone else. Separate shared concerns, such as a blocked route, from preferences on which people can differ. Preserve a clear record of who decides what.

### Usefulness does not require a joyless appearance

Asked to define good design in 2019, Moore includes beauty alongside empathy and inclusion. In the 2026 conversation she describes older women's wish for clothing appropriate to important occasions, rather than accepting a narrow practical offer. [S2][S1]

An original product brief therefore includes appearance, pleasure and self-presentation with handling and comfort. A tool that works but makes its owner feel labelled or embarrassed may not be the preferred option. Ask the person what they like; do not assume every visibly specialised product is unwanted. Compare choices in use rather than deciding that either attractive or clinical-looking is inherently better. The aim is a useful object that fits a person's life, with enough choice to reflect their taste rather than the designer's idea of what they should accept.

### Design work needs collaborators

In her Cooper Hewitt answers, Moore rejects the idea of a self-made career and describes learning through other people. She also identifies collaboration with Dr. Wendy Logan on a mammography unit as personally significant. [S2]

Our transfer to a career project is a collaborator map. Who understands the task through daily experience? Who knows the materials, service operation or technical requirements? What can you contribute, and what authority do you lack? A student can make a useful observation and a clear brief without pretending to validate medical equipment. A community organiser can coordinate a conversation without becoming an access engineer. The portfolio should make those contributions visible and credit them. Competence grows through a well-formed relationship with expertise, not through a heroic solo narrative.

### A service can bring the opportunity closer

Moore's 2026 discussion of mobile mammography emphasises the travel, time and income burdens involved in reaching a service. This is an account of access logistics, not a screening recommendation in this profile. [S1]

Our everyday application is a library, repair event or community appointment. Trace the effort required before the person reaches the service: travel, booking, time away from work, waiting and the return journey. Could a different location, time or delivery arrangement remove a burden? Compare the operations required by each option. Bringing a service closer can create new issues of staffing, privacy or reliability, so state those rather than treating proximity as a complete solution. The useful unit of design is the opportunity someone can actually use.

## How she decides

The checked public accounts emphasise experience, direct attention to people and practical collaboration across disciplines. Moore's award record recognises that design approach; an award does not establish that a specific intervention will work for an unknown user. [S2][S3][S4]

Our working sequence is to identify an important ordinary task, listen to the person, follow the effort and notice where the encounter undermines choice. Then compare a physical change, a different service arrangement and a more respectful way of offering help. Ask which outcome the person prefers. A proposal should explain how it changes the task rather than simply calling itself empathetic.

For an original kitchen example, someone struggles to open a particular jar. Ask when it happens and what they already do successfully. Compare authorised product options, alternative packaging and assistance the person actually wants. Consider holding, twisting, cleaning and storage, not only the instant of opening. Do not prescribe a home-made tool that creates another hazard. The output is a task-and-choice comparison with any specialist question identified, rather than a verdict that age makes one product universally suitable.

## Where she stood

The World Design Organization names Moore the recipient of its 2022 World Design Medal and records the ceremony in Tokyo in October 2023. It describes a career spanning products, environments and communication. [S3]

Our interpretation focuses on a principle that can be carried into an ordinary visit: treat the person's preferred life as the starting point. If a relative wants to attend a celebration, ask what makes attendance comfortable and meaningful. Avoid turning the planning conversation into a list of everything that might be wrong with them. Discuss the journey, seating, timing, food and opportunities for rest as practical arrangements. Ask before offering help, and make declining it easy. A considerate plan leaves room for the person to choose differently on the day.

## Their work and record

The checked biographical account identifies Disguised: A True Story and Ageing, Ingenuity & Design among her books. Cooper Hewitt records the 2019 Design Mind award and identifies her as lead industrial designer for the Phoenix METRO Light Rail system shown in its article. [S5][S2]

These examples show the breadth of the work rather than providing instructions for transport or clinical design. In this profile the transfer is to observing a use sequence, including users in decisions and writing a better brief. Do not infer a universal result from the presence of an impressive client or project. The present task still needs evidence from its own users, setting and constraints.

## Where she was wrong or criticised

The RCA account describes simulation exercises involving constrained movement, vision and dexterity. [S4] Our explicit limitation is that a short simulation cannot reproduce a lifetime of ageing or disability, the skills people develop or the differences between individuals. It may reveal that a particular task is harder under a constraint, but it cannot establish that you now know another person's life. This profile does not recommend risky restriction, deception or coercion as an everyday research method.

The 2026 interview also includes medical and technological hopes. [S1] This profile does not treat interview claims as clinical evidence or repeat speculative health benefits as promises. For a health-related decision, use appropriate current evidence and qualified advice. The design question remains the person's experience and access to an opportunity, with the limits of design kept clear.

## Blind spots to watch

Empathy can become a confident story told by the observer. Keep checking it against the person's own account. A family member's good intention does not grant authority over another adult's choices. Similarly, a designer may notice an awkward object but miss cost, language, transport or the user's attachment to a familiar routine. Include those conditions in the brief.

Do not make independence mean doing everything alone. People may reasonably choose companionship, paid support or a specialised aid. Nor should a broadly usable design erase a need for individual accommodation. The final output should state the important task, observed burdens, desired choices, possible responses and what has not been established. Dignity is made practical by the arrangement and the encounter, not by attaching a flattering adjective to the user.
