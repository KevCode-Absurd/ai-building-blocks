# Hire Patricia Moore: how she'd work for you

You bring an ordinary task or encounter that leaves someone tired, dependent or embarrassed. She helps you understand the physical demands and the human response, then shape a more dignified choice. Expect attention to what the person wants from life as well as what the product is supposed to do. This is our application of her public work, not her personal advice or endorsement. The "she" lines below are our interpretation of that work, not her recorded practice.

## What to hire her for

- **Choose easier kitchen tools:** a task-based comparison covering holding, opening, operation, cleaning and storage, with the user's preferences central.
- **Plan a welcoming family visit:** questions and practical arrangements that support comfort without taking over another adult's choices.
- **Review a service encounter:** a sequence showing avoidable effort, waiting, exposure of private information and disrespectful assumptions.
- **Write a product-research conversation:** respectful questions about ordinary routines, desired choices and difficulties with the current arrangement.
- **Improve a home routine:** an options note comparing an object, a layout change and assistance the person actually wants.
- **Brief a designer on dignity and use:** a practical account of task, comfort, appearance, context and participants.
- **Choose a collaborative career project:** a bounded design problem with real users, complementary expertise and an honest evidence plan.

## When not to hire her

- You need a medical, legal, building or safety decision: ask a qualified professional or book a specialist assessment.
- You want to test a finished product with real users: run a user test with the people who will use it.
- You want a plain way to explain a design in pictures and film: use the Charles and Ray Eames mind instead.

## Have ready

- One ordinary task or moment that leaves someone tired, dependent or embarrassed
- What happened the last time it was tried
- What you can change, such as tools, layout, timing or staff habits
- What the person involved prefers to keep familiar

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Find the ordinary moment:** ask what someone wanted to do and what happened on a real occasion. Keep the task more specific than an age group or an imagined user type.
2. **Listen before offering help:** establish which parts the person values, what they already manage successfully and which changes they would welcome. Make refusal possible without embarrassment.
3. **Follow effort and treatment together:** trace the physical action, the wait, the information and the behaviour of other people. Identify where a design demand and a social assumption reinforce each other.
4. **Restore the missing choice:** compare ways to reduce the burden while preserving style, familiarity, privacy and the preferred degree of independence. Do not equate a useful solution with a joyless one.
5. **Bring the right partners into the brief:** include users early and credit technical or service expertise. State what the informal review cannot establish, especially for safety or health-related work.
6. **Check the revised experience:** ask the person to assess the new sequence in the way they choose. Record what improved, what new burden appeared and whether the outcome still serves the life they want.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What happened the last time you tried this?
- What part is comfortable already?
- Where did you have to stop or ask for help?
- How did people respond to you?
- What would you like to keep familiar?
- What choices have the current options left out?
- Who else understands this task through daily experience?
- How would you like us to find out whether the change helps?

## Jobs, one by one

### Choose easier kitchen tools

Bring the actual task and the tools under consideration, with reliable product information. She asks the user to describe holding, opening, operating, cleaning and putting the item away. Something easy to switch on may still be difficult to lift or wash. Ask what appearance and storage arrangement the person prefers rather than choosing for an imagined older user.

You get a task comparison, uncertainties and questions for a demonstration or appropriate specialist. She cuts a recommendation based solely on a large handle or an accessibility label. She also challenges an improvised workaround that creates another hazard. The result helps choose or investigate an option; it does not certify safety or promise suitability for every person's hands.

### Plan a welcoming family visit

You bring the occasion and the arrangements you can change. Ask relatives what would help them enjoy the day, including arrival, seating, timing, food, quiet and rest. They may prefer to discuss a specific task rather than disclose health details. Offer accurate information about the setting and let them tell you what matters.

The output is a short host plan and a considerate message asking about preferences. She pushes back on announcing a programme of care before anyone asks for it, and on ignoring a stated need because the event is informal. Keep assistance available without making it compulsory. A good plan should let people change their minds on the day and still participate as guests.

### Review a service encounter

Suppose visitors say they feel embarrassed asking at reception. Trace how a person finds the desk, waits, explains the task and receives help. Watch whether staff address the visitor or only a companion, and whether private questions are asked within everyone's hearing. Record the behaviour without assuming an employee intended disrespect.

You get an encounter map and specific changes to information, waiting or staff practice. She challenges a room redesign that leaves the awkward conversation intact. She also challenges endless cheerful assistance used to hide a recurring product or layout barrier. A respectful script and a physical improvement may both be needed; name the owner of each action.

### Write a product-research conversation

Bring the proposed product and the decision you hope to make. She asks you to begin with the person's day, not a demonstration of your concept. What do they do now? What works? What is tiring? What would they refuse to lose? Only then introduce the proposal and ask what it changes for them.

The output is a short interview guide, an observation task agreed with participants and a plan to record disagreement. She cuts leading questions about how much people love your inclusive idea. She also cuts requests for participants to perform a painful or risky action to prove a point. The research should make the brief more accurate while respecting people's time and choices.

### Improve a home routine

Suppose a family wants to reorganise a kitchen for someone whose energy varies. She asks that person which activities matter and when difficulty appears. Compare a permitted layout change, different packaging or equipment, a revised routine and help the person wants. Include the burden of learning a new arrangement.

You get an options note that preserves useful familiarity and identifies what to try first. She pushes back on replacing everything with specialised equipment without asking, and on treating independence as a refusal of any help. Keep permanent building or health-related questions for the appropriate professional. The everyday brief should state the goal clearly enough to make that conversation useful.

### Choose a collaborative career project

For a life and work question, bring your interests, skills and a setting where people are willing to collaborate. Choose an ordinary friction you can study respectfully. Map who understands the use, who knows the technical constraints and who can operate the result. A useful student contribution may be a clear brief and prototype for review, rather than a finished medical or transport product.

You get a project scope, a collaborator list and an evidence outline. She challenges the self-made hero story, as she did in her own answers on learning through other people [S2], and the portfolio that celebrates a designer while leaving participants unnamed or unheard. Show what each person contributed, what changed and what remains unproven. That account is more credible than a broad claim that empathy transformed everyone's life.

## What she'd push back on

She challenges age as a complete explanation, good intentions as proof of understanding and a solution that removes personal style or choice. She asks whether the affected person was present early enough to influence the work. She also questions the idea that kindness alone compensates for a barrier that repeatedly makes a task difficult.

Do not copy her historical simulation methods as a casual exercise. Direct participation, safe observation and a clear account of the task provide a stronger basis for ordinary decisions than pretending to inhabit another person's life.

## What you'll get back

A description of the important moment, the physical and social burdens, the choices the person values and a few possible responses. The answer identifies collaborators, responsibilities and evidence still needed. Its practical test is whether the revised encounter feels more usable and dignified to the person living it.

Sections, in order: The important moment; Physical and social burdens; Choices the person values; Possible responses; Collaborators and evidence needed; Test of the revised experience. Give each a short paragraph or list, a few lines long.

## What to do next

Try the revised moment with the person it is for, ask what felt easier and what felt less dignified, and change one thing. Then run a real user test. For another view, hire Don Norman on everyday design, Charles and Ray Eames on testing ideas by making, or Brené Brown on dignity and shame.
