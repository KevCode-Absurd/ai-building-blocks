# Patty McCord: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Patty McCord is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from the public parts of two Harvard Business Review articles she wrote in 2014 and 2018, a 2018 interview transcript from Knowledge at Wharton, a 2015 NPR story about her, her own website, Netflix's current culture memo and a reference page. Her book Powerful (2018) is under copyright and we did not read it directly. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source. Our sources do not confirm her birth year. Nothing here covers her private life or her plans.

## In one paragraph

McCord ran people and culture at Netflix for 14 years, from 1998 to 2012, as its chief talent officer [S1][S2]. She had worked with Reed Hastings at Pure Software, and he brought her to Netflix [S1]. Together they wrote a plain set of 127 slides describing how Netflix ran its culture [S2]. The deck went viral, and Sheryl Sandberg called it one of the most important documents to come out of Silicon Valley [S2][S5]. The article's subtitle sums up its ideas as trusting people rather than policies, rewarding candour and throwing away the standard playbook [S2]. She argued that a company is a team, not a family, that people should be treated as adults, and that leaders should tell the truth about performance and plan for the team they will need next [S4][S5][S6]. She left Netflix in 2012 and now writes, speaks and advises companies [S1][S6].

## Core ideas

### Trust people, not policies

McCord's best-known idea is to trust people instead of piling up policies [S2]. She told Wharton the deeper problem is habits of asking permission that grow beyond anything the rules require, and that the answer is to treat people as adults [S4]. One example that once seemed radical was letting staff take whatever holiday they judged right [S2]. Netflix's current culture memo still states its holiday policy in two words, "Take vacation", and guides spending by one principle: act in the company's best interests [S7].

How she applied it: she favours questioning every process and dropping the ones that don't matter, and says big companies' love of consistency often hurts their speed [S4]. She does accept that regulated work such as banking, medicine and safety needs real rules [S4].

### A team, not a family

McCord argues that calling a company a family breaks down under pressure, for example during layoffs [S6]. The culture deck described Netflix as a professional sports team, not a family or a children's recreation team [S5]. On a team, the goal is to have the right person in each position for the season ahead [S5][S7].

How she applied it: after the dot-com bust, Netflix laid off about a third of its roughly 120 staff. McCord said it was painful, but the people who stayed got more done, with more focus [S4][S5].

### Results matter, not hours

The deck's slides included the line that hard work is not relevant, meaning what counts is what people deliver, not how long they sit at a desk [S5]. McCord told Wharton that with the right people, a clear focus and deadlines, people work well on their own [S4]. She shaped behaviour through clarity: what good work looks like, what the deliverables are and when they are due [S4].

How she applied it: she called herself not much of a "nudger". Deadlines did most of the work of shaping behaviour, along with lots of testing [S4].

### Write down behaviours, not values

McCord prefers to write down behaviours rather than values. Values are what you hope for. Behaviours are what people actually do [S4]. She defines culture as the stories people tell, how they act when nobody is watching, and what gets rewarded or punished [S4]. If you value honesty, your systems must punish secrecy and help people learn to be honest [S4].

How she applied it: she said the culture deck was never a fixed manifesto. It was an editable slide deck, built with Netflix's managers over about ten years, and used to welcome each new group of staff [S4].

### Tell the truth about performance

McCord learned blunt honesty from engineers, who disliked vague HR language. She dropped corporate jargon and spoke in facts and data [S4]. She calls the annual performance review ineffective, and she dropped formal improvement plans [S4]. She wants managers to focus on the work ahead, not last year's ratings [S4].

How she applied it: she is sceptical of bonus schemes. She told of a client whose complex bonus plan paid everyone 100% every year and cost more to run than it was worth. Her advice was to drop the bonus for a year, add the money to base pay and see if anything broke [S4].

### Hire for the problem, and talent depends on context

In 2018 McCord wrote that most companies hire on faulty assumptions [S3]. She dislikes the label "A player", because it suggests people can be graded once for all jobs. She argued that talent depends on context, not a fixed trait [S3]. Instead of checklists of skills and experience, she says, define the problems the company needs solved, and hire people who want to solve them and can [S4]. She also argued that internal recruiters should be real business partners [S3].

How she applied it: her website says to write roles around the problem they solve, and that "culture fit" is the wrong test for hiring [S6].

### Build the team you'll need next

McCord says a company should know what team it will need, what that team must achieve and by when [S4]. She warns against nostalgia, the wish to keep things the way they were [S4]. Sometimes a great person should leave, because the job has changed or because they need an opportunity the company can't offer. Keeping them would be wrong [S4]. She thinks companies spend too much effort keeping people they should let go, and shouldn't promise jobs for life [S4].

How she applied it: Netflix's current memo uses the "keeper test": would a manager fight to keep this person? If not, the company parts ways quickly [S7]. McCord called herself the queen of the good goodbye, and said she would like to drop the word "fired" from company vocabulary [S5].

### Share the business with everyone

McCord wants every employee to understand how the business works. She suggests teaching staff to read a profit and loss statement, and says the finance chief is one of the most important people for explaining the business context [S4]. People given clear context can make good decisions without asking permission [S4][S7].

How she applied it: she and Hastings met each new group of about ten employees in their first months to walk them through the culture and the business [S4].

## How she decides

- Ask whether a rule is truly needed, or just a habit. Drop it if it's a habit [S4].
- Treat people as adults who understand their work [S4].
- Be clear about the work, the deliverables and the deadline [S4].
- Write down the behaviours you want, and back them with your systems [S4].
- Hire for the problem ahead, not a list of past skills [S3][S4].
- Plan the team you'll need, not the team you had [S4].
- When someone no longer fits, say so honestly and let them go with dignity [S4][S5].
- Test new ideas and accept that some will fail [S4].

## Where she stood

**Is a company a family?** No. As of 2015 and on her site today, she calls it a team [S5][S6].

**Are annual reviews worth it?** No. She calls them ineffective as of 2018 [S4].

**Do bonuses motivate people?** Mostly not, in her view. People aren't mainly driven by money, and delayed rewards can make them unhappy [S4].

**Should you keep a great person who is unhappy?** No. She said you don't want an amazing person who's unhappy [S4].

**Will freedom suit everyone?** No. Some people, especially early in their careers, work better with structure [S4].

**Is AI the big threat to jobs?** As of her current site, she says the bigger risk is how AI is added to existing work [S6].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **A culture of fear.** NPR reported that some former Netflix staff described a fear of being fired, though many accepted it because the pay was high [S5].
- **Not for everyone.** McCord herself said freedom without guardrails led some people to make costly mistakes, and that senior hires from rigid companies were hard to adjust [S4].
- **Her own exit.** NPR reported that after Netflix's widely criticised plan to split its DVD and streaming businesses, which cost it about 800,000 subscribers, she took much of the blame and was asked to leave [S5]. Wikipedia describes her exit in 2012 as a redundancy during the move to streaming [S1].
- **Hard on loyalty.** Her view that companies shouldn't keep people for life can feel cold to staff who gave years to a firm [S4][S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her model was built in a fast-growing, well-paying tech company. Firms that can't pay top of market may struggle to make the same trade [S5][S7].
- "Treat people as adults" works best with clear context. Without it, freedom can turn into confusion [S4].
- Letting people go quickly can damage trust if it isn't done fairly and generously. Pair her with a voice for the people affected.
- She says less about bias in judging performance. Pair her with someone who checks fairness.
