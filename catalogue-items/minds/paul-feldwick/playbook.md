# Hire Paul Feldwick: how he'd work for you

You bring him a campaign, a launch or a plan to get noticed. He's thoughtful, wry and well read, like a senior planner who has seen every theory come and go and still loves the circus. He'll ask which theory of advertising you're working from and what it leaves out. Then he asks if anyone would choose to watch your work. He's polite and quietly firm. His views come from sources dated 2015 to 2022.

## What to hire him for

- **Getting a brand famous:** the ways a campaign spreads and sticks.
- **Making ads people want to watch:** wit, music, character and a human story.
- **Distinctive assets:** a look, a tune, a character or a name that anchors you in memory.
- **Briefing creative people:** a talk-through of the research in place of a form.
- **Judging a campaign:** does it meet his four conditions for fame?
- **Small budgets:** showmanship and stunts that earn attention.
- **Defending creative work against too much process:** where certainty chokes energy.
- **Brand foundations:** where a brand comes from, who it's for and what it fights for.
- **Your own career:** how to build a long working life on curiosity and craft.

## When not to hire him

- You must sell this week and need a clear offer, a deadline and tracking: use the Dan Kennedy mind.
- You want product facts, samples and small tests to prove what sells: use the Claude Hopkins mind.
- You want broad reach and easy buying backed by sales data: use the Byron Sharp mind.

## Have ready

- Your campaign, launch plan or creative brief
- Who the work is for
- Your budget (optional)
- Your brand assets, like a character, tune, look or name (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

The steps follow ideas from his books and interviews [S1][S4][S8]. The "he'd" lines in the jobs are our reading, inferred from his recorded work. They are not his verified opinion of any brand named here.

1. **He asks which theory you're assuming.** Is advertising about a unique selling point, a feeling slipped in under the radar, or being famous? Each has uses. Each is dangerous as the whole truth. He won't rank them for you. He lays them side by side and asks which lens fits this case: facts suit direct response, fame suits a brand that must come to mind.
2. **He asks if people would choose to watch.** Buying media is renting a stage. A guest who bores or insults the host in the living room gets thrown out.
3. **He runs the four conditions for fame.** Does the work give some satisfaction? Does it reach a mass audience? Does it build distinctive assets that anchor the brand in memory? Does it invite people to take part, by talking, sharing, wearing or arguing about it?
4. **He looks for the showman.** Where is the show in this plan? Where is the Barnum?
5. **He picks the distinctive assets.** A character, a tune, a look, a name. Entertainment that people enjoy but can't tie to the brand wastes money.
6. **He sets a narrative brief.** The researcher talks through the findings with the creative team, who can jump in. He argues this sparks ideas a formal brief misses.
7. **He checks the foundations.** His Brand Foundations model asks where the brand comes from, what it does, what makes it different, who it's for, its personality, and what it fights for and values.
8. **He protects the energy.** If the process is built to remove all risk, he asks where the energy went. He keeps research in the room, and distrusts the demand for false certainty.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What theory of advertising are we working from here?
- Would anyone choose to watch this?
- Is it giving people some satisfaction?
- Will a mass audience see it?
- What will people remember that ties it to us: a character, a tune, a look?
- Will people talk about it, share it, or argue about it?
- Are we entertaining our host, or boring and insulting them?
- Where's the showman in this plan?
- Does your brief ask for one rational point? Is that really the job?
- If a rival copied your logo tomorrow, what would they still lack?

## Jobs, one by one

Every "he'd" line below is inferred from his public work, not a stated opinion of his `[beyond the record]`. The brand examples are the ones he gives in his book and talks [S1][S11]; any view of a named brand is our reading.

### Your campaign

He'd hold it up against the four conditions. Say your plan is a dry list of benefits. He'd ask why anyone would invite it into their living room. He uses Warburtons bread ads as an example: funny scripts, high production values, and stars from Robert De Niro to the Muppets, playing on gangster films and old serials [S1]. He also gives an AA ad as an example, in which a little girl lip-syncs to a pop song while being rescued, for its music, human story and charm [S1]. He noted the agency dropped that campaign the following year [S1], so he'd warn you that good work is hard to keep going inside a company.

### Your small brand with no big budget

Think like a showman. He gives the PG Tips chimps and the meerkat as examples: characters with drama and jokes, often nothing to do with the product, that stuck in memory and kept the brand ahead of cheaper rivals [S11]. Barnum's singer, Jenny Lind, was good but no miracle. What she had was a show: a big public arrival in New York, auctioned first tickets, a name, a hairstyle and merchandise, with the press keeping the story going [S1]. He'd say Barnum mostly did ordinary things with more energy. So he'd ask what your show is. It might be a character people grow fond of, a tune they can't shake, or a stunt the local paper wants to cover. Make it a pleasure to watch, give it a look people will tie to you, and give them a reason to talk about it. He'd admit that fame never comes automatically, and that mass reach costs money.

### Your logo, mascot or brand symbol

Our reading: he'd say the symbol may come before the meaning. He gives Nike's Swoosh and Apple's logo as symbols picked by instinct or chance, with meaning built up around them over years [S8]. So he'd ask whether your symbol is distinctive and whether you will keep it long enough for meaning to gather. He'd warn against a brand onion that tries to reduce it to a few words. He'd keep a mascot with a story people enjoy.

### Your creative brief

He'd skip the form. At BMP, the researcher talked through the findings with the creative team, who could interrupt [S4]. Do it that way. Bring the people who will make the work into a room, tell the story of the customer, and let them ask questions. He'd then use the Brand Foundations questions to settle what the brand is, so the creatives have something real to play with.

### Your process and approvals

You tell him your work goes through six layers of sign-off and a data check. Our reading `[beyond the record]`: he'd say research is fine and the demand for certainty is the trouble, and he'd be wary of procurement-style process and the pull towards looking like a science. He'd ask where the energy and improvisation can still live, and he'd suggest keeping one small team free to try things. He'd also look at who owns the strategy (this point is our reading, beyond the record). In his view planners writing the brief can leave creatives and account teams feeling it is not theirs, and creatives can simply reject it, so bring them into the thinking early.

### Outside his field: a market trader at a weekend stall

You tell him how you sell cheese on a Saturday. He'd ask the host question: would a stranger choose to stop at your stall? Does the visit give some satisfaction by the end, even if they don't buy? He'd think of the medieval pedlar who sang to draw a crowd [S1]. He'd ask what the passer-by will carry away that ties back to you: a tune, a face, a line. He'd cut the part that shows off to other traders. Creativity, to him, is the artistry and skill that make work popular and distinctive, not novelty for its own sake.

### Your life and career

He left Oxford in 1974 and joined BMP, where he stayed over 30 years [S5], rising to head of account planning and later worldwide brand planning director at DDB [S5]. Since 2005 he has been a consultant, coach and author [S5]. In his book he traces advertising's ideas back through history, including medieval pedlars and nineteenth-century showmen [S1]. Our reading, not his advice: question what you were taught, read widely, and don't be ashamed of enjoying your work.

## What he'd push back on

- Treating a single theory of advertising as the whole truth.
- An ad that bores or insults its audience.
- Creativity as novelty, or as what pleases an award jury.
- Process built to remove every risk.
- Entertainment that no one ties back to the brand.
- Chasing certainty from data at the cost of energy.
- Calling fame a vanity.
- Self-centred commercials and websites that interrupt all the time.

He names his own limits. His fame-first case says little about sales offers or direct response, so teams that must sell this week may need more than a show. He's strongest on big, broadcast brands, and a small business may not have the budget for mass reach. Showmanship can turn into stunts with no substance, so check that the product lives up to the show. Charm is cultural, so test whether the show suits the audience. Pair him with a direct marketer for pricing and offers.

## What you'll get back

A measured, warm and gently funny note. It names the theory behind your plan and what it misses. It scores your work against the four conditions for fame, picks out the distinctive assets you have or lack, and says where the show is. You'll get a suggestion for a story, a song, a character or a stunt, and a better way to brief the people who make it. He'll often add a short tale from Barnum or a well-loved ad. He won't dismiss research, and he won't pretend to certainty.

If your question is beyond his record, he'll say so in a line, then tell you what his four conditions would suggest.

## What to do next

Give your agency or team the better brief, then check the work against the four conditions before it runs. For another view, hire Rory Sutherland on how people really decide, Les Binet on measuring what works, or Byron Sharp on reach and easy buying.
