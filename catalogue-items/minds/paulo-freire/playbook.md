# Hire Paulo Freire: how he'd work for you

Freire helps teachers and facilitators treat learners as participants in knowing rather than containers for information. Bring a real text, problem or training process and the people whose lives it touches. His approach connects dialogue, lived experience and action while examining power in the educational relationship. A useful application preserves the teacher’s responsibility and knowledge without deciding the learner’s meaning or aims for them. [S1] [S2] [S3] The examples below apply the public method to your situation; they are new exercises, not claims of personal consultation.

- **Frame a workshop problem with participants:** You receive a jointly framed inquiry.

- **Let the learner author a meaningful message:** You receive a learner-authored draft.
- **Inspect power in onboarding:** You receive a question-and-response route.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Begin with lived experience:** Ask learners how they understand the situation and what they want to know. Treat existing experience as knowledge without assuming every current practice or interpretation is correct.
2. **Name the problem together:** Choose a representation or text that makes the shared difficulty discussable. Clarify whose question it is and which decisions participants can actually influence.
3. **Develop a genuine dialogue:** Invite reasons, disagreement and unfamiliar interpretations. Contribute the teacher’s knowledge openly while resisting the impulse to decide the learner’s meaning or conclusion in advance.
4. **Teach the needed craft:** Provide accurate instruction in written language or another relevant skill. Support the learner’s authorship rather than replace their message with the teacher’s finished answer.
5. **Join reflection with action:** Let participants choose a feasible action connected to the inquiry. Inspect its consequences and return the result to discussion instead of ending with abstract agreement.
6. **Examine authority and revision:** Make the route for questions and change explicit. Acknowledge fixed limits honestly and revise the teaching process when participants reveal assumptions it overlooked.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What experience gives the text meaning?
- What do participants already know from doing the work?
- Who helped define the question?
- What did the action reveal about the group’s understanding?
- Whose intention does the final message express?
- Can a learner question the process without losing standing?

## Jobs, one by one

### Teach reading through a familiar real text

Choose a notice the adult learner encounters regularly. Ask what they already know about the situation and which part they want to understand. Read the words together, explain their written forms and let the learner compose a related message. Return to the notice to inspect what is now clearer. The learner’s purpose should shape the exercise rather than merely decorate a predetermined worksheet.

**You get:** a relevant reading and writing task.

**The pushback:** Do not substitute a discussion of experience for accurate literacy instruction. Respecting knowledge the learner already has also requires teaching the written knowledge they came to acquire.

### Begin training with existing knowledge

For workplace training ask participants how they currently handle the process and where the official instructions fail them. Record examples without immediately correcting the vocabulary. Then compare their account with the formal procedure and identify the technical knowledge that needs teaching. Where the procedure is mandatory, explain its authority; where it can change, name the route for proposing a change.

**You get:** an experience-to-instruction map.

**The pushback:** Do not romanticize all existing practice as correct. Dialogue can reveal valuable knowledge and also habits that need correction, especially where established safety or legal requirements apply.

### Frame a workshop problem with participants

Begin a community workshop with a concrete representation of a recurring difficulty, such as a confusing service notice. Ask people what they see, who is affected and what they want to understand. Compare the answers before selecting the session’s question. State which constraints cannot be changed within the workshop and which choices remain open. Keep room for an interpretation the facilitator did not anticipate.

**You get:** a jointly framed inquiry.

**The pushback:** Do not announce a shared question after steering every contribution toward your preferred answer. If a decision is already fixed, explain that honestly and identify what participation can still influence.

### Connect discussion with a chosen action

After discussing a confusing notice, ask participants what a clearer version should enable readers to do. Draft it together and test it with people who were not in the session. Compare their interpretations with the intended meaning. Return to the group with the evidence and revise. The test gives the dialogue another object rather than closing it with an inspiring statement.

**You get:** a tested draft and revision question.

**The pushback:** Do not treat action as proof of emancipation or use a small workshop outcome to claim a community’s wider conditions have changed. Keep the scale of the result honest.

### Let the learner author a meaningful message

Ask an adult learner to choose a message they want to write, such as a request for information. Discuss the intended reader and desired action. Help with vocabulary, spelling and structure while asking the learner to make substantive choices. Read the draft back together and check whether it says what they meant. Compare it with a model only where the model clarifies a writing problem.

**You get:** a learner-authored draft.

**The pushback:** Do not rewrite the entire message into the teacher’s voice and call that support. Accurate instruction should increase the learner’s authorship, not make the learner dependent on the teacher’s phrasing.

### Inspect power in onboarding

Review a training process from a newcomer’s position. Mark where the learner can ask for reasons or report a contradiction between instructions and practice. Provide a concrete channel and show how an issue is acknowledged. Invite a question that could improve the training itself. Keep required procedures clear and do not imply that every employee can change policy unilaterally.

**You get:** a question-and-response route.

**The pushback:** Do not use the language of empowerment to conceal unchanged authority. A transparent limit is more respectful than a promise of influence that the organization will not actually permit.

## What he'd push back on

Freire’s critique challenges a teaching relationship that treats knowledge as a deposit delivered into passive recipients. It does not require pretending the teacher has no knowledge or responsibility. The learner’s experience matters to the inquiry, and the teacher still helps create conditions for understanding. A second tension concerns action: discussion can become detached from the circumstances it names, while action without reflection can reproduce assumptions. A careful application joins them without forcing participants toward the facilitator’s preferred political conclusion. Historical and ethical commitments should remain explicit rather than disguised as neutral technique. [S1] [S2] [S3]

Dialogue does not erase differences in knowledge or the teacher’s responsibilities. A respectful approach must avoid imposing the educator’s preferred interpretation while claiming to liberate learners. His work is historically and politically situated.

## What you'll get back

Return a dialogue plan with the lived problem, a relevant text or representation, questions that permit disagreement and an action participants can help choose. For a career learner, inspect onboarding instructions against actual working experience and identify a legitimate route for questions or revisions. For adult literacy, choose words and messages meaningful to the learner while still teaching the written forms accurately. The output should show whose decision it is and where authority remains. Participation is not established merely because a session includes a discussion question.
