# Peep Laja: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Peep Laja is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own website, podcast transcripts from 2015 and 2021, short written answers he posted on a Q&A site, two podcast summaries from around 2023 and 2024, and his companies' own pages. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its source. His main blog posts sit on a site that blocked our reader, so we could not use them. We also could not find his birth year or birthplace in a source we could open, so both are left out. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after these sources.

## In one paragraph

Laja is an entrepreneur who started in software, marketing and sales, then ran a general marketing agency before he narrowed in on conversion optimization, the work of getting more visitors to take action [S1]. In 2011 he and Viljo Vabrit founded CXL, an agency that was renamed Speero in 2020. Around it grew CXL, an online school for marketers, and later Wynter, a research platform where business buyers react to a company's messaging [S1][S2][S3]. His core belief is that marketers guess too much. He wants decisions based on research into what real buyers think, tested with sound numbers, and messages built on clarity, relevance, value and real difference [S4][S7]. By the 2020s his focus had moved from page tweaks up to positioning, messaging and business strategy [S1][S9].

## Core ideas

### Research first, then change things

Laja's starting point is that you should learn what is wrong before you fix anything. His agency, now Speero, treats research as the core of testing, used to find what matters to users and customers [S2]. In 2015 he warned that many people running tests pick random elements to try, rather than testing ideas that come from research [S4].

How he applied it: he says people often build a page without thinking about the campaign first. He wanted the ad, the traffic source and the page planned together, so the ad's promise matches the page [S4].

### Clear beats clever, and talk about their problems

A visitor should know within a few seconds what a page is about. Laja says many pages fail this test [S4]. Common faults are a vague page, a weak value proposition and copy about the company's own history instead of the visitor's problems [S4]. He admits writing a good value proposition is hard work [S4].

How he applied it: he cut his own homepage down to a single offer. He reported that its sign-up rate rose from about 2% to about 12% [S4].

### Score a message in layers

Laja breaks a message into parts and works on each one on its own. Clarity means "I get it." Relevance means "it's for me." Value means "I want what it promises." Differentiation means "I see how it's different." Friction, meaning doubts and worries, is the one to reduce [S7]. He calls this method Message Layers and recommends testing a message again after each rewrite, to see if each layer improved [S7].

How he applied it: Wynter, the company he founded, scores messaging on clarity, relevance, value and differentiation, and shows buyers' reasons next to the scores [S3][S8].

### Message testing is small, fast and qualitative

Laja argues that waiting for a full sales cycle, or tracking only conversion rates, makes feedback on a message slow [S7]. A message test asks the buyers you want to win how a message lands [S7]. Because it is qualitative research, it doesn't need a big sample [S6][S7]. This makes it workable even if you only sell to a few large companies [S7].

How he applied it: he built Wynter after writing sales copy for CXL and finding no good way to ask his ideal customers whether they found it interesting or boring [S8]. Wynter claims to verify each panel member and to use no AI-made respondents [S3].

### Run tests with real statistics

Laja is hard on sloppy testing. He says many people don't understand sample sizes, mix up statistical significance with whether a result is valid, and declare tests winners when they actually lost [S4]. In early results he is careful. In one case he called a 30% lift in a test too early to judge because the sample was small [S5].

How he applied it: he suggested that a site needs a fair amount of traffic, on the order of 30,000 visits a month, before testing one change at a time is the safe route [S4].

### Iterate instead of redesigning

Full redesigns are risky and costly, Laja argues, and they often fail to lift conversions [S4]. He calls a redesign a resource black hole. He recommends first checking how each page performs. Keep the layout of pages that work and rethink only the weak ones [S4]. A full redesign makes sense mainly if a site is badly out of date or gets little traffic [S4].

How he applied it: he pointed to Amazon, which changes its site step by step through testing, and to a retailer whose sales fell after a big redesign [S4].

### Start with small yeses

Most visitors will never buy, and you can't rush the ones who might [S4]. So Laja starts with micro-conversions, small steps such as joining an email list [S4]. He tested several ways of asking for email on his blog. A box that slid in as people scrolled beat a fixed sidebar offer, and using several methods together did not make them compete with each other [S4][S5].

How he applied it: he said that combining three sign-up methods brought about 30% more subscribers each month than one method alone [S4]. He also said content and referrals, not paid ads, built the trust needed to sell his costly consulting [S4].

### Positioning and messaging come before copy

By the 2020s Laja was saying that copywriting has little effect on its own if the positioning and messaging under it are weak [S9]. In his words, messaging sits one level above copy [S6]. In crowded markets, many tools offer the same features in the same words. Standing out takes a truly different position and message [S9]. He suggests finding a group your big rivals ignore, such as small firms when a rival chases large ones, because full overlap with a bigger player risks getting crushed [S9].

How he applied it: he recommends a strategic narrative, a story about how the world is changing that creates a need for your product, and revisiting it every two to four years [S9]. He also argues that business buyers are people too, so business messaging doesn't have to be stiff [S9].

### Listen for patterns in what customers say

In a young company there isn't enough data for numbers to mean much, so Laja leaned on qualitative data. He drew it from sales demos, customer interviews and live chat, and looked for themes that kept coming up [S6]. He found sign-up counts less useful, because most people who signed up did nothing [S6].

How he applied it: he said he was running 15 to 20 demos a week himself at Wynter's start. Repeated customer questions and paying beta users shaped the company's pivot [S6].

## How he decides

- Research what buyers think before changing a page [S2][S4].
- Plan the campaign, the ad and the page together [S4].
- Check that a stranger gets the page in a few seconds [S4].
- Score the message on clarity, relevance, value and difference, and cut friction [S7].
- Test messages with a small group of real buyers [S6][S7].
- Respect sample size, and don't call a test early [S4][S5].
- Fix weak pages one at a time instead of redesigning everything [S4].
- Get positioning right before you polish copy [S9].
- Look for patterns across many conversations, not one loud opinion [S6].

## Where he stood

**Should you redesign your website?** Usually not all at once. Find the weak pages and test changes, unless the site is very outdated or has little traffic [S4].

**Are best practices enough?** No. He criticised testing random elements and wanted ideas drawn from research on your own visitors [S4].

**How do you know a message works?** Ask the buyers you want to convince. Small, fast message tests beat waiting months for conversion data [S7].

**What matters more, copy or messaging?** Messaging and positioning. Copy builds on them [S6][S9].

**How do you stand out in a crowded market?** Take a different position, aim at a group your rivals ignore, and say it in a different way [S9].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

Our sources record little outside criticism of him. The points below are fair limits, including mistakes he has named himself.

- **Wynter's first version.** He said the company's original name and its focus on "copy testing" were wrong, because marketing leaders cared more about messaging and positioning [S6]. It also began with online shops testing site copy, who saw little lasting value, before it moved to business software firms [S8].
- **Claims about himself.** The "most influential" title appears on his own site and his own agency's page [S1][S2]. Treat it as a claim from his side.
- **Advice that needs traffic.** Much of his testing advice assumes a site with real traffic [S4]. A small site may never reach the sample sizes he insists on.
- **Panel quality.** He said outside research panels had fraud problems, which is why Wynter built its own [S8]. Wynter's quality claims come from Wynter itself [S3].

## Blind spots to watch

- He is strongest on the page, the message and the test. He says less about product, price or service, which can matter more than the copy.
- Small message tests show how a message lands. They don't prove people will pay. Pair them with real sales data.
- His world is mostly business software. Shops, local services and creative work may need different methods.
- A habit of testing everything can slow down a team that needs to act on a clear, obvious fix.
