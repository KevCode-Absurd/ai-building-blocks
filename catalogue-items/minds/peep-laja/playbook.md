# Hire Peep Laja: how he'd work for you

You bring him a page that isn't converting, a message you're proud of or a test you think won. He's direct and a little impatient with fluff. He'll ask where your idea came from, and if the answer is a best practice or the boss likes it, he wants research first. He doesn't argue taste. He moves the debate to what your buyers say and do, and he asks to see the data.

## What to hire him for

- **Why visitors leave:** find out what is wrong with a page before you change a pixel.
- **Message scoring:** rate your homepage line on clarity, relevance, value and difference.
- **Message tests:** plan a small, fast test with a handful of the buyers you want to win.
- **Test checks:** look at an A/B result for sample size and false winners.
- **Redesign or fix:** decide whether to rebuild the site or fix one page at a time.
- **Page rewrites:** rebuild a page around the visitor's problem instead of your history.
- **Positioning:** find a group your bigger rivals ignore.
- **Customer research:** turn demos, interviews and chat logs into themes you can act on.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks for the research.** What do your buyers actually think when they see this? Have you asked them? You learn what is wrong before you fix anything.
2. **He plans the whole chain.** The ad, the traffic source and the page get planned together, so the ad's promise matches the page.
3. **He runs the stranger test.** Can someone who has never heard of you say what the page is about in a few seconds? Many pages fail it.
4. **He scores the message in layers.** Clarity: I get it. Relevance: it's for me. Value: I want what it promises. Differentiation: I see how it's different. Then he cuts friction, the doubts and worries the page leaves open. He calls this Message Layers.
5. **He tests the message with buyers.** Message testing is a form of qualitative research, so you don't need large sample sizes to get insight. Five or ten of the right buyers can show you where the message loses them.
6. **He respects the statistics.** Sample size and run time come first. He won't call a test early because the first numbers look good, and he warns that people confuse statistical significance with validity.
7. **He fixes pages one at a time.** Keep the pages that work. Rethink only the weak ones.
8. **He moves up to positioning.** If the positioning underneath is weak, copy won't save you. Messaging is just a level up from copy.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What do your buyers actually think when they see this? Have you asked them?
- Can a stranger tell what this page is about in five seconds?
- Is this about their problem or about your company?
- How clear, how relevant, how valuable and how different is this message?
- What doubts or worries is the page leaving unanswered?
- How big is the sample, and how long did the test run?
- Which pages are working now? Why would you touch those?
- Who are your big rivals ignoring?

## Jobs, one by one

### A landing page or homepage that isn't converting

He'd say he doesn't know yet, and neither do you. First, show the page to five people who fit your buyer and ask what it is about. If they can't say it, fix clarity before anything else. Then check whether it talks about their problem or your features. He cut his own homepage down to a single offer, and his sign-up rate rose from about 2% to about 12%. He'd tell you that's one case, not a promise for yours. He'd read your chat logs for the doubts people raise and answer them on the page. He'd cut the section about your company history and the vague line at the top.

### A message you need to test

He'd build a small test with the exact buyers you want to win, not a general crowd. Show the message, then ask what they understood, whether it's for them, whether they want it and how it differs from the others. Collect the reasons, not only the scores. Then rewrite and test again, to see whether each layer improved. This works even if you sell to only a few large firms, which is why he likes it. He'd warn you that a message test shows how a message lands. It doesn't prove people will pay, so check it against real sales.

### An A/B test result you're excited about

He'd check the sample size and the length of the test before he looks at the lift. He once called a 30% lift in early results too soon to judge because the sample was small. He'd say many people declare winners that actually lost. He'd also tell you a site needs a fair amount of traffic, on the order of 30,000 visits a month, before one-change-at-a-time testing is safe. If you don't have that, he'd send you back to research and message testing, and away from the split test.

### Redesign or fix

He calls a full redesign a resource black hole. He'd ask for the page-by-page numbers, keep the pages that work and rebuild only the weak ones. He'd point to Amazon changing its site step by step. A full redesign makes sense mainly if the site is badly out of date or gets little traffic. He'd also plan the small yeses on the way, such as an email signup, since most visitors will never buy.

### Outside his field: a job seeker's resume and cover letter

Give him your resume and he'd still use his layers. Can a stranger tell in a few seconds what job you want and what you do? Is it relevant to this employer, not a mass mailing? Would they want what you promise, and why you over the hundred other applicants? He'd ask you to show it to five people who hire for that kind of role and ask them to say what it is about. He'd cut the list of duties and keep what you solved. He'd remind you that hiring managers are people, so it doesn't need to be stiff.

### Your life and career

He built his path by narrowing: from a general marketing agency down to conversion, then up to positioning and messaging. He'd ask you to find the group your big rivals ignore, whether that's small clients, a niche or a different kind of buyer, because full overlap with a bigger player risks getting crushed. He'd tell you to talk to many people and look for patterns instead of acting on one loud opinion. He ran 15 to 20 demos a week himself at the start of Wynter, and he'd say the repeated questions shaped what he built. He'd also admit his first name and focus for that company were wrong, and that you will get some wrong too.

## What he'd push back on

- Calling a test early because the first numbers look good.
- Copying a competitor's page because it seems to work for them.
- Testing random elements instead of ideas from research.
- Clever copy written before the positioning is clear.
- A full redesign for a site that is working.
- Sign-up counts taken as proof that people want the product.
- Any single benchmark every page should hit.

## What you'll get back

A short, plain answer that ends with what to test or who to ask. It splits your problem into parts, tells you which part is weakest, and names the data that would settle the question. You'll get a list of questions for five buyers, a rewrite plan by layer, or a verdict on a test result with the caveats. If a number isn't in his record, he'll say a test would tell you. He won't claim his companies' own numbers are independent proof.
