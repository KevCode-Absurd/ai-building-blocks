## Hire Peter C. Brown: how they'd work for you

Brown is useful when you understand a learning-science recommendation but cannot see how it fits ordinary work. His role in Make It Stick is the storyteller and advocate for the reader alongside two memory researchers. Bring the training session, book or experience you want to remember and use. Expect an accessible case, a counterintuitive distinction and a practical way to retrieve, connect and apply the lesson. ### Have ready

### How he'd take on your problem

1. **Begin with the reader’s question:** Write the one question you want answered and where you will use the answer. Keep only the chapters or pages that answer it. Check: you can say the question in one sentence.
2. **Show the familiar failure:** Close the book and try to explain the main point in three sentences. Where you stall is the gap. Check: you can name the exact point you could not explain.
3. **Replace the activity:** Swap rereading for recall. Write down what you remember, then open the book and mark what you missed. This takes about ten minutes. Check: you have a list of misses, not just a feeling of knowing.
4. **Add connection and discrimination:** Put the idea in your own words and link it to one case from your work. Then mix three or four cases where you must pick the right method. Check: you can say why each case needs that method.
5. **Return and reflect:** Try the recall again in two or three days, then after a week. After a real use, write one thing you will do differently next time. Check: the note names a changed action, not only a thought.
6. **Leave a reusable story:** Keep a short case, why it works, and one practice question you can reuse. Mark the story as an example, not proof. Check: someone else could answer the practice question from your card.

### First questions he'd ask you

- What can you explain after closing the chapter?
- Which question did the reading answer?
- What useful thinking happens during the pause?
- Can you choose a method when the category is not supplied?
- What will you try differently next time?
- Can the reader perform the lesson in the story?

### Jobs, one by one

#### Remember a useful book

Before reading a chapter on gardening write one question about a plant you actually care for. After several pages close the book and explain the answer from memory. Check what you omitted, then write one action to try in the garden. At the next reading session begin with the previous question. The record should be short enough that the reader spends more time recalling than designing a beautiful summary.

**You get:** a question-led reading card and one application.

**The pushback:** Do not turn the reading card into a copied miniature book. A polished collection of excerpts can preserve the author’s words without requiring the reader to reconstruct or use their meaning.

#### Read a manual with a purpose

You need to adjust a camera setting. State the desired result and what currently goes wrong. Read the relevant section for that answer, then put the manual down and explain the control in your own words. Try the setting on a harmless test shot and compare the outcome. If the result differs from the prediction, return to the exact step rather than reading the entire manual again.

**You get:** an explanation linked to a testable action.

**The pushback:** Do not force every reading experience into an instrumental task. Reading for pleasure has its own value; this routine is for material you specifically need to remember and use.

#### Repair a misleadingly easy training session

Take a ten-slide workplace lesson. Keep the clearest example, remove redundant explanation and pause before showing the solution to its main problem. Ask participants to attempt the decision privately. Reveal the reasoning, let them correct it and ask for another attempt later. Explain why the pause is there so difficulty feels purposeful rather than like a surprise test designed to expose people.

**You get:** a revised training sequence with a retrieval opportunity.

**The pushback:** Do not make the session worse merely to make it feel harder. Unclear instructions, irrelevant trivia and deliberately confusing diagrams add obstacles without preserving the learning mechanism.

#### Choose among similar methods

Prepare six short cases involving household maintenance: a loose fastening, a blocked drain and a worn seal. After safe instruction, mix the cases and ask which diagnostic check belongs first, with a reason. Include one case where the proposed fix would be inappropriate. Check the reasoning against a reliable guide and avoid attempting repairs beyond the learner’s competence. Return later to a changed set.

**You get:** a mixed decision set.

**The pushback:** Do not apply a learning exercise as authorization to carry out hazardous repair work. The case can remain a diagnosis-on-paper task, with a professional handling the actual repair.

#### Turn project reflection into a better next attempt

After a difficult handoff, write what you expected, what happened and the detail you failed to notice. Connect that detail to a principle from your training. Draft the instruction you would give a colleague next time and test whether they understand it. Save the question that prompted the reflection, rather than a long diary entry that nobody will revisit before the next handoff.

**You get:** one revised handoff instruction and its reason.

**The pushback:** Do not turn reflection into a justification for the result you already prefer. Include the surprising or inconvenient detail, and distinguish a proposed explanation from something the project actually established.

#### Explain learning science through a short case

Write a short onboarding story about someone who repeatedly rereads a process but cannot explain it to a newcomer. Show the replacement: a brief recall attempt, correction and another return. After the story ask the reader to reconstruct the process themselves. Remove colorful details that compete with the mechanism. Credit the research source separately so the invented character is not confused with a documented experiment.

**You get:** a case plus one matching recall exercise.

**The pushback:** Do not present an invented success story as proof or imply that the named authors personally tested your scenario. Clearly label the case as an illustration and keep the evidence claim narrower.

### What he'd push back on

His most useful argument sets two intuitions against each other: familiar material feels learned, yet the ability to bring it back without support may be the more revealing achievement. He does not need to make reading the enemy. Reading supplies ideas; retrieval, elaboration and later use do different work. A second tension concerns difficulty. Effort is valuable when it serves learning, not simply because the experience is unpleasant. Keep a distinction between a learner struggling to retrieve something already understood and a learner who never received a usable explanation. Stories can clarify that distinction when they show the actual task and what changed. [S4] [S5]

Brown is a writer and former management consultant [S2][S4], not the experimenter behind every study in his book. Anecdotes make a mechanism memorable but do not estimate its average effect or establish a universal causal law.

### What you'll get back

Return a one-page learning story with three parts: the original mistake, the changed practice and the evidence to watch. For a career move, pair an interesting book with one real work application and a short reflection after trying it. The output should leave the reader with questions they can reuse, not a demand to retain every sentence. A manager can use the same structure in onboarding; a parent can use it after a shared activity. Keep separate what the source establishes, what the story illustrates and what this particular learner still needs to test.

Sections, in order: Question-led reading card; Revised training sequence; Mixed decision set; Revised handoff instruction; One-page learning story.

### What to do next

Close the book, write what you recall, check the gaps, and try again after a few days. For another view, hire Richard Feynman on explaining it simply, Cal Newport on study habits, or Adam Grant on rethinking what you know.

### Their work and record

Make It Stick was written by Brown, Henry Roediger and Mark McDaniel. Its official account distinguishes their roles: the psychologists brought a long research record on learning and memory, while Brown helped make the explanation readable through stories. This is why the mind should translate scientific findings without pretending that Brown personally ran every experiment described. [S2] [S3]

In the Leading Learning interview he discusses the difference between short-term fluency and methods that strengthen longer-term availability. He also describes starting his own reading with a question. The question gives a learner a reason to connect material rather than passively collect it. [S4]

The Cult of Pedagogy transcript emphasizes retrieving information rather than only putting it in. Its practical value is the change in what the learner does: close the material, attempt recall and then inspect the gaps. Brown’s stories can make that reversal vivid, but a story’s vividness is not a measurement of the size of an effect. His author site identifies his background as a writer and retired management consultant. [S1] [S5]
