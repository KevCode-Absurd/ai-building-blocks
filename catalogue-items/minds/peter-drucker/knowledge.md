## In one paragraph

Drucker was born in Vienna in 1909, earned a doctorate in law in Frankfurt, and left Germany in 1933 when the Nazis came to power [S1][S2]. He moved to the United States in 1937 [S2]. His study of General Motors, *Concept of the Corporation* (1946), and *The Practice of Management* (1954) helped turn management into a field of study of its own [S1][S2]. He taught at New York University and then at Claremont for more than 30 years, and advised GE, IBM, Sears, the Red Cross and the Salvation Army, among others [S1][S2]. His big ideas are still in daily use: the customer as the reason a business exists, management by objectives, the knowledge worker, planned abandonment, and the habit of asking basic questions before giving answers [S1][S2][S6]. He saw well-run institutions as society's protection against tyranny [S2]. He died in 2005, days before his 96th birthday [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### A business exists to create a customer

Drucker held that a company's first duty is to serve customers, and that profit is a condition for survival, not the goal [S1]. In *The Practice of Management* he wrote that the only valid definition of a business purpose is to create a customer [S6]. He also faulted economists for looking almost only at price, when what matters is what a product does for the buyer [S1].

How he applied it: the questions he put to clients were basic ones, such as who the customer is and what the business really is [S2].

### Ask the right question before the clever answer

He thought many management tools of his day, such as downsizing, outsourcing, benchmarking and reengineering, were "how to do" tools. They improve how existing work gets done, but they don't ask what work should be done at all [S4]. His 1994 article "The Theory of the Business" opens with that point [S4].

How he applied it: he worked as a consultant through close relationships with top executives, and he assumed his clients were smart and meant well [S1]. When a company struggled, he looked for old assumptions, narrow thinking or misunderstandings, rather than bad people [S1].

### Management by objectives and self-control

In 1954 Drucker set out management by objectives: people agree goals with their managers, tie them to the goals of the whole organisation, and are judged on results [S1][S7]. The point was self-control. People who help set their own goals, and choose how to reach them, take more ownership [S7]. He drew together older ideas, including Mary Parker Follett's, into one system [S7].

How he applied it: the idea spread widely, and later goal systems such as OKRs, used at Google, grew from it [S7].

### The knowledge worker

Drucker saw that the economy was moving from manual work to knowledge work, and he predicted the decline of the factory worker [S1]. He used the phrase "knowledge work" in 1959 [S2][S8]. He argued that the most valuable asset of an institution in the 21st century would be its knowledge workers and how productive they are [S8].

How he applied it: he listed what makes knowledge workers productive. Ask first what the task is. Let them manage themselves. Make innovation part of the job. Keep them learning and teaching. Judge quality as well as quantity. Treat them as assets, not costs [S8].

### Manage yourself

In "Managing Oneself" (in the January 2005 Harvard Business Review), Drucker argued that success in a knowledge economy goes to people who know themselves: their strengths, their values and how they work best [S3]. Great achievers always did this, he wrote, but now most people would have to [S3]. Careers would last about 50 years, so people must find where they can contribute most and stay alert and engaged, even if that means changing the kind of work they do [S3].

How he applied it: he taught his last class in 2002, at 92, and kept writing almost to the end [S1].

**What he told executives to do in his late interviews.** In 1997 he said computers make managers more inwardly focused, and advised chief executives to spend time working in the marketplace [S9]. He said a leader should ask what the institution most needs and not what the leader likes, and he named Truman and Jack Welch as examples [S9]. He said big companies would struggle to attract strong graduates [S9]. He said being global and being diversified do not mix, and cited Coca-Cola as a model of focus [S9]. Asked to predict, he said he only looks out the window and sees what is visible but not yet seen [S9].

**Management is not only for business.** In 1998 he wrote that management is no more business management than medicine is obstetrics [S10]. He traced it to the military, government and nonprofits, and held that the differences between kinds of organisation are small, about a tenth of the work [S10]. In 1989 he had already argued that American nonprofits were becoming its management leaders on strategy and on board effectiveness [S11].

### Planned abandonment

Organisations hold on to yesterday's successes, so Drucker told them to drop, on purpose, products and practices that no longer work [S1]. He warned that success makes obsolete the behaviour that produced it, and that an old success that has outlived its use can do more harm than a failure [S6].

How he applied it: as early as 1946, he urged General Motors, then seen as the world's most successful company, to rethink its long-held policies. Its chairman, Alfred Sloan, ignored the book, and GM executives shunned Drucker for years [S1].

### Effectiveness is a habit, not charisma

Drucker argued that an effective executive doesn't have to be a "leader" in the usual sense [S5]. The best chief executives he worked with were very different in personality and style [S5]. He pointed to Harry Truman, who had no charisma at all, as one of the most effective U.S. presidents [S5]. He judged a team's morale by performance, not by whether people got along [S6].

How he applied it: he once told Donald Keough, a former president of Coca-Cola, not to tell him they'd had a wonderful meeting [S2]. A good meeting, for Drucker, was one that changed what people did.

### Management serves society

Drucker grew up watching society collapse in Europe between the wars, and he came to see good management as a guard against that [S2]. He wanted "a functioning society" built on effective institutions [S2]. He called management a liberal art that draws on history, psychology, philosophy and religion [S1]. He favoured decentralised companies over command and control, and saw workers as assets [S1].

How he applied it: he wrote that management has authority only while it performs [S6].

### The third sector

Beyond business and government, Drucker saw nonprofits as a major sector of society [S1]. He thought they could be very well run, and that volunteering gave people purpose and a sense of community [S1][S2].

How he applied it: he advised the Red Cross and the Girl Scouts, and created an award for innovation by nonprofits in 1991 [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask who the customer is and what they value [S2].
- Ask what the work should be before asking how to do it better [S4].
- Agree clear goals, then let people steer themselves [S7].
- Build on strengths, your own and your team's [S3].
- Drop what no longer works, even if it once made you famous [S1][S6].
- Judge by results, not by style or charm [S5][S6].
- A decision is needed when a condition will get worse if nothing is done [S6].
- Do not risk disaster this year for a grand future [S6].
- Think before you act; action without thought leads to failure [S1].
- Know the cost of the whole chain, not only your own part of it [S1].

## Real decisions

### Studying General Motors from the inside, 1943 to 1946

Drucker spent time inside GM and wrote *Concept of the Corporation* from it [S1][S2]. He urged GM, then seen as the best-run firm in the world, to rethink long-held policies [S1]. Chairman Alfred Sloan called the critique dead wrong and ignored the book, and GM executives shunned Drucker for years [S1]. He kept the work and went on to make management a field of study [S1].

### Refusing the "wonderful meeting"

He told Donald Keough of Coca-Cola not to report that a meeting had been wonderful [S2]. His test for a meeting was whether it changed what people did next [S2][S6].

### Answering the Wall Street Journal

When *The Wall Street Journal* reported in 1987 that he sometimes got facts wrong, he did not fight the charge. He said he used anecdotes to make a point, not to write history [S1].

### Taking the nonprofit sector seriously

Drucker advised the Red Cross and the Girl Scouts and in 1991 created an award for innovation by nonprofits [S2]. His stated view was that nonprofits could be run very well and that volunteering gave people purpose and a sense of community [S1][S2].

### Telling ServiceMaster what its business really was

In the 1997 interview he recalled telling ServiceMaster's leaders that their real business was to train the least-skilled workers and make them able to do the job [S9]. He used the case to show his habit of reframing a business around the people and results it actually produces, not the product on the price list [S9]. This is his basic question, what is our business, applied to a cleaning firm.

### Sending chief executives into the marketplace

He argued that computers were making managers more focused on the inside of the firm, and so advised CEOs to spend time working in the marketplace [S9]. His reason was that results exist outside the walls, among customers and non-customers [S9]. The 1963 article opens in the same spirit: the manager's first duty is to get the best economic result from the resources at hand, and everything else depends on it [S13].

### Managing experts as associates, like volunteers

In 1998 he held that knowledge workers often know more about their specialty than their boss does, so they cannot be managed as subordinates: they are associates, and you lead them [S10]. He suggested treating them like volunteers, giving them challenge, mission, training and visible results, since pay mostly prevents dissatisfaction and does not drive performance [S10]. He also asked for one clear boss per person, a flat structure and a transparent chain of authority [S10].

### Choosing to teach and write into old age

He taught his last class at 92 and kept writing almost to the end, which fits his view that a working life now runs about 50 years and the person must keep themselves engaged [S1][S3].

## Where he stood

**What is profit for?** It is a condition for staying alive and a test of performance. The purpose is the customer [S1][S6].

**What makes a good leader?** Effectiveness, which can be learned. Charisma is optional [S5].

**Who is the key worker of the future?** The knowledge worker, who must be managed as an asset and largely trusted to manage themselves [S8].

**What should a successful company fear?** Its own success, because it makes yesterday's methods feel safe [S6].

**Why does management matter?** Because performing, responsible institutions protect society from tyranny [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Loose with facts.** In 1987 *The Wall Street Journal* reported that he sometimes got facts wrong, and he replied that he used anecdotes to make a point, not to write history [S1].
- **Forecasts.** He predicted that America's financial centre would move from New York to Washington, which didn't happen [S1].
- **Management by objectives.** Critics say it is hard to put into practice and can put control ahead of creativity [S1]. W. Edwards Deming argued that targets, applied without understanding the whole system, push people to hit numbers at the cost of quality [S7]. Deming also felt practitioners ignored Drucker's own call to see the system [S7].
- **GM.** Alfred Sloan, GM's chairman, called Drucker's critiques dead wrong [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He thought in big institutions. A one-person business may need simpler tools than a full goals system.
- His stories teach well but may not be accurate. Don't repeat them as facts.
- Goals and measures can be gamed. Pair him with someone who watches quality and the system as a whole.
- He wrote mostly before the internet economy. Test his forecasts against what actually happened.
