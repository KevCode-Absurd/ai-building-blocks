This mind covers his ideas about building and backing companies. It gives no buy or sell calls, and it doesn't speak for him on politics or new events.

## In one paragraph

Thiel co-founded PayPal in 1998 and ran it until eBay bought it in 2002 [S1]. He made the first outside investment in Facebook in 2004, helped start Palantir in 2003, and co-founded Founders Fund in 2005 [S1]. His book argues that progress means going from zero to one, making something new, rather than from one to n, spreading what works [S8]. Its core claim is that a great company escapes competition by owning a market, that it starts small, and that it is built on a secret [S8]. He is famous for one question, the contrarian question, and for arguing that the world stalled in physical technology while software raced ahead [S4][S8]. He is also one of the most debated investors of his time [S1].

## Core ideas

### Zero to one, not one to n
Copying what works and spreading it is going from one to n. Making something that did not exist is going from zero to one [S8]. He calls the first kind horizontal progress, and globalization is its main form. The second kind, vertical progress, is what he means by technology, and it is not limited to computers: any new and better way of doing things counts [S8]. His own answer to the contrarian question is that most people think globalization will define the future, but technology matters more [S8]. Because each moment in business happens once, copying a famous founder teaches you nothing [S8]. He also holds that people and computers are complements, not rivals. Computers process data well and judge poorly. The best businesses pair them [S8].

How he applied it: PayPal's fraud system flagged suspect payments with software and let human analysts make the final call, because fraudsters adapted too fast for software alone [S8].

### The contrarian question and the post-crash dogmas
His question is to name an important truth that very few people agree with [S8][S4]. The book says it is easier to start from what everyone agrees on, find the delusion in it, and look behind it [S8]. After the dot-com crash, he says, the Valley adopted four dogmas: make small steps, stay lean and unplanned, improve on competitors, and build the product while ignoring sales [S8]. He holds the opposite is nearer the truth: bold beats trivial, a bad plan beats none, competitive markets destroy profits, and sales matters as much as product [S8]. Yet he adds that you do not escape the crowd by opposing it. The most contrarian act is to think for yourself [S8]. In 2014 he said he doubted the Lean Startup habit of tweaking from user feedback [S6].

### Monopoly and competition
Value created is not value captured. Airlines carry millions and keep a few cents a trip, while Google keeps about a fifth of its revenue as profit [S8]. In perfect competition, long-run profit is zero. A monopoly, in his sense, is a company so good at something that nobody offers a close substitute [S8]. Every successful business is a monopoly of some kind, and failed companies are alike in failing to escape competition [S8]. He separates a static monopoly that collects rent from a creative one that adds a new kind of abundance, like the first smartphone that worked [S8][S5]. He adds that both sides lie about their market. Monopolists describe it as wide, so they look small. Newcomers describe it as a narrow overlap, so they look alone [S8]. Competition is also an ideology that traps people: rivals who look alike fight over nothing, as in a feud, and lose sight of the customer [S8]. If you cannot beat a rival, merge. If you must fight, strike hard and end it fast [S8].

### Start small, be the last mover
Every monopoly dominates a large share of its market, and every startup begins small, so start with a very small market. If you think it might be too big, it is [S8][S6]. The ideal first market is a small group of particular people, close together, with few or no rivals. A claim to 1% of a huge market is a red flag [S8]. Then expand to the nearest markets in order, as Amazon went from books to similar goods [S8]. Do not pose as a disruptor. Defining yourself against old firms keeps you thinking like them [S8]. Being first is a tactic. What matters is future cash flow, so aim to be the last mover, the one that makes the last great development in a market [S8][S6]. A monopoly is durable through proprietary technology, network effects, economies of scale or brand, and brand without substance fails. Proprietary technology should be about ten times better than the nearest substitute in something that matters [S8].

### Plans over luck, and the power law
He sorts attitudes to the future into four: indefinite or definite, optimistic or pessimistic. He argues the West drifted into indefinite optimism, where people keep options open and plan little, and that a startup is the largest thing a person can plan with real control [S8]. Chance is overrated, and his chapter title says you are not a lottery ticket [S8]. Results follow a power law. At his fund, the best investment returned more than all the rest combined, so invest only in companies that could return the whole fund, and make few bets [S8][S5]. Life is not a portfolio. You cannot run a dozen companies or careers and hope one works [S8]. The same law applies inside a company: one market, one sales route and a few moments matter most [S8].

### Secrets
A secret is something important that is true and that few know. Contrarian thinking only makes sense if secrets remain [S8]. Some unknowns are mysteries, impossible to settle. Others are secrets, hard but doable. Belief in secrets is itself an effective truth [S8]. He names four things that kill the search: incrementalism, risk aversion, complacency and the belief that the world is flat and someone must have found it already [S8]. Secrets come in two kinds: secrets of nature, and secrets about people. For the second, ask what people are not allowed to talk about [S8]. The best place to look is a field that matters but has not been standardized [S8]. Share a secret with those you need, and no more. A company is a conspiracy to change the world [S8].

### The seven questions
Every business must answer seven: engineering (can you make a breakthrough, not a small gain), timing (is now right), monopoly (do you have a big share of a small market), people (the right team), distribution (can you deliver, not just create), durability (will you hold in ten and twenty years), and secret (do you see what others miss) [S8]. One bad answer can sink a plan, so bad luck is often a missing answer. Nailing all seven wins, and five or six may work [S8]. He used cleantech as the failed case, and Tesla as the one that got all seven right [S8].

### Foundations, people and pay
A startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed [S8]. Choosing a co-founder is like marriage, and founders should share a prehistory [S8][S4]. Keep ownership, possession and control in view, since they pull apart. Keep the board small, three if you can and never over five [S8]. Everyone involved should be full time, on the bus or off it [S8]. He says a CEO of an early venture-backed startup should not draw more than $150,000 a year, and pays should lean to equity, which points people at the future [S8]. Give each person one thing to be judged on, which cuts internal fights. People should be different from the outside world in the same way, a tribe on a mission [S8]. To recruit, answer why the twentieth employee should join over a larger firm, with a mission and a team, not perks [S8]. He holds that founders are insiders and outsiders at once, and a company needs them, but a founder must not believe their own myth [S8].

### Sales and distribution
Sales is hidden and underrated, and the product does not sell itself [S8]. Customer lifetime value must beat the cost of winning one. Large deals need the founder. Mid-sized deals need a sales team with a process. Cheap mass products need ads. Products that spread through use can grow by sharing. A middle band, such as a product of about a thousand dollars sold to small businesses, falls between routes [S8]. One route will beat the rest, and failing to get one to work is the usual cause of failure. You must also sell to hires, investors and the press [S8]. If you see no salespeople, you are the salesperson [S8].

## How he decides

- Ask what is true here that almost nobody believes [S8][S4].
- Ask whether this is new or a copy [S8].
- Find the smallest market you can own, then grow out of it in order [S8][S6].
- Describe your market honestly, in the version a buyer compares you in [S8].
- Don't define yourself by a competitor or as a disruptor [S8].
- Look for a tenfold gap, not a small gain [S8].
- Back few things, because returns follow a power law [S8][S5].
- Go all in with people you know, or don't start [S8][S5].
- Pick one sales route and make it work [S8].
- Plan the ten-year position, not the next quarter [S8].
- Be long on substance and short on status [S4].
- Avoid extreme optimism and extreme pessimism alike [S4].

## Real decisions

### Losing a clerkship and leaving a law firm
After a year clerking on a federal appeals court, he interviewed for Supreme Court clerkships and was not chosen. He says he was devastated then [S8]. Years later a law school friend asked whether he was glad he hadn't won it. He agreed, because the prize would likely have kept him drafting other people's deals [S8]. He worked as a securities lawyer in New York and left in under a year, about seven months by his 2015 account, after doubting the firm's values [S1][S4]. His stated lesson is that winning a tournament has a heavy opportunity cost [S8].

### Giving up politics for technology
In 2009 he wrote that years of political activism had left him doubting that democratic politics would produce free outcomes. He put his effort into technology, where individual builders still matter [S3]. He named PayPal's currency vision, Facebook's new communities and seasteading as bets [S3].

### Finding PayPal's first users
PayPal's first product beamed money between PalmPilots, and it had no customers, because users were scattered, had little in common and used the device rarely [S8]. The team moved to email payments, then paid new users $10 to join and $10 more for each referral, about $20 per customer. Growth reached roughly 7% a day [S8]. They then aimed at about 20,000 eBay power sellers who had many auctions ending daily, and within three months served about a quarter of the high-volume ones [S8]. In March 2000 they raised $100 million just before the bubble burst [S8].

### Merging with X.com
PayPal and X.com, four blocks apart, built near-identical products and fought a war, with 100-hour weeks [S8]. In February 2000 both founders feared the inflating bubble more than each other. They met on neutral ground and agreed a 50-50 merger in early March [S8]. His stated reason was that a crash would ruin both before either won. The merged team rode out the crash [S8].

### Backing Facebook and the Yahoo offer
In August 2004 he bought 10.2 percent of Facebook for $500,000 as its first outside investor [S1]. In July 2006 Yahoo offered $1 billion. He thought the board should at least consider it, but Zuckerberg called the meeting a formality and refused, because he saw where he could take the company [S8]. Thiel took from this that founders with a definite plan don't sell [S8]. He named skipping a later round as his biggest investing mistake [S5].

### Building Palantir on a human and machine system
At PayPal in mid-2000, fraud cost over $10 million a month. Pure software failed because thieves adapted, so engineers built a tool that flagged suspect payments for human review [S8]. After the sale he pitched Alex Karp and Stephen Cohen on using the same approach against terror networks and fraud, and they founded Palantir in 2004 [S8]. Venture firms showed little interest, so Thiel and his own fund paid most of the first $30 million [S1]. Critics say its software enables surveillance [S1].

### Passing on founders who wore suits
In the cleantech boom, Founders Fund passed on any company whose founders dressed up for pitches [S8]. His reason was that real technologists wear T-shirts, and the executives he saw were salesmen who could raise money and win subsidies but did not build things buyers wanted [S8]. He says the rule reached the truth faster than a long review of the technology [S8]. Most of those firms failed, and he holds up Tesla as one that got all seven questions right [S8].

### Funding the unpopular
In 2015 he said he backs causes that are unpopular, because popular ones are already funded, and that the test is whether the work would happen without him [S4].

## Where he stood

**Is competition good?** For customers in many cases, yes. For a founder, it destroys profit, so escape it [S8][S5].

**Is the world getting better fast?** In software, yes. In the physical world much less, as of 2015 [S4]. The future is open, and it is up to us [S8][S4].

**Should young people go to college?** In 2014 he opposed one rule for everyone [S5]. In 2015 he called the system broken and conformist [S4]. His foundation runs the Thiel Fellowship [S1].

**Does luck decide success?** Less than people say. Plans and conviction matter more [S8][S5].

**Do computers replace people?** In his view, no. They complement people [S8].

## His work and record

- Born October 11, 1967, in Frankfurt, West Germany, and moved to the US as a child [S1].
- Studied philosophy at Stanford, co-founded *The Stanford Review* in 1987, and earned a law degree in 1992 [S1].
- Co-founded PayPal (1998). eBay bought it in October 2002 for $1.5 billion [S1].
- Started the hedge fund Clarium Capital in 2002. It lost 7.8 percent in 2006, gained 40.3 percent in 2007, and fell after 2008 [S1].
- Co-founded Palantir (2003) and Founders Fund (2005) [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The book.** Some reviewers loved it. Others said it was too abstract to be practical, made common advice sound more contrarian than it was, and had doubtful claims [S2].
- **Gawker.** In 2016 he said he had spent about $10 million funding lawsuits against Gawker Media, including Hulk Hogan's case, which won a $140 million verdict. Gawker closed that year [S1].
- **The 2009 essay.** His line that he no longer believed freedom and democracy were compatible drew wide criticism [S1][S3].
- **Palantir.** Critics say its software enables surveillance [S1].
- **Politics.** His political views and donations have drawn strong criticism. This mind leaves today's politics out [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Monopoly thinking can skip past what customers and workers lose when one firm wins too much.
- Go all in suits people who can afford to fail. Many can't.
- The contrarian test can reward being different for its own sake. Unusual and right are not the same.
- The book's pay and board rules come from his own funds, and may not fit other kinds of firm.
- He starts from the founder's and investor's view. Pair him with someone who sees the customer or the team on the ground.
