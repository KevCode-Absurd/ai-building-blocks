# Philip Kotler: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Philip Kotler is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own website, including a page of his sayings and his blog (2019 to 2024), a 2016 interview with the American Marketing Association, a 2015 Kellogg article based on his interview comments, and reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" the year it comes from. His textbooks are long and under copyright, so we summarise their ideas rather than quote them. Several books of his deal with democracy and capitalism. We describe his business ideas from them and stay out of today's politics. Nothing here speaks for him on later events.

## In one paragraph

Kotler is an American economist who became the best-known teacher of marketing in the world [S1]. He earned a master's in economics at the University of Chicago in 1953 and a PhD in economics at MIT in 1956, studying under Milton Friedman, Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow [S1]. He taught at Northwestern's Kellogg School from 1962 to 2018 [S1]. His 1967 textbook, *Marketing Management*, was the first to bring economics, organisation theory and psychology into marketing [S1]. He popularised the four Ps of the marketing mix: product, price, place and promotion [S1][S7]. Then he kept widening the field. He argued that marketing works for charities, causes, places and ideas, not just products [S1]. He named social marketing and demarketing in 1971 [S1]. In later decades, as of 2015 to 2024, he urged business to serve all its stakeholders and the planet, not only its owners [S5][S6][S4].

## Core ideas

### Marketing is far more than selling

Kotler rejects the idea that marketing just means advertising [S5]. For him it starts before there is a product. His site puts it as the homework you do first [S3]. Good marketing studies needs so well that the offer almost sells itself. In 2016 he recalled Peter Drucker's view that strong marketing can make hard selling unnecessary [S6].

How he applied it: his sayings include that hard-sell marketing is a contradiction, and that the only way to serve your company is to serve your customer [S3].

### Choose who you serve

A core part of his toolkit is segmentation, targeting and positioning. Split the market into groups, choose the ones you will serve, and decide how you want them to see you [S5]. His sayings warn against selling to everyone, and say that aiming for the average loses [S3].

How he applied it: he described the field's growth from this toolkit toward a discipline built around customers' goals and hopes [S5].

### Use the whole mix

The marketing mix asks you to plan the product, its price, where it is sold and how it is promoted, together [S7]. E. Jerome McCarthy first set out the four Ps in 1960. Kotler made them famous, valuing the model as a clear way to teach [S7][S1]. The mix is a reminder that a price cut or an ad campaign is only one lever among four.

How he applied it: his *Marketing Management*, first published in 1967, was in its 15th edition by 2015 and spread the model to generations of students [S5][S1].

### Anything can be set apart

Kotler holds that there is no such thing as a commodity. A commodity is just a product waiting to be set apart from the rest [S3]. He likes to say every business is a service business, so a chemical firm is really a chemical services firm [S3].

How he applied it: on price, his advice is that you don't sell through price, you sell the price [S3]. And on share, he says earn it rather than buy it [S3].

### Market ideas, not just products

Kotler argued that marketing tools work for non-profits, government, causes, people and places [S1]. In 1971 he and Gerald Zaltman built on an older question, whether you could sell brotherhood like soap, and the term social marketing was born [S5][S1]. It uses marketing to change behaviour for the good of society [S1].

How he applied it: he and Nancy Lee later applied social marketing to health, safety, education and community causes [S5].

### Demarketing

Also in 1971, Kotler and Sidney Levy proposed demarketing: using marketing tools to reduce demand when it is too high or harmful [S1][S5]. Their example was persuading people to save water during a shortage [S1]. He prefers this to heavy rules from government, and thinks companies can lead it too [S5].

How he applied it: he has pointed to firms that discourage overconsumption, such as Patagonia's repairs and recycled materials [S5].

### Marketing with a higher purpose

Kotler was among the first to argue that companies should weigh customers' long-term welfare alongside profit [S1]. As of 2015, he said making a difference in customers' lives is marketing's core purpose [S5]. He urged firms to weigh profit, people and the planet, and to build good into the business model rather than add it on as charity [S5].

How he applied it: he described three stages of marketing. The first sells on function, the second adds emotion, and the third serves community and higher hopes [S5]. He proposed a "Good Company" index that would rank firms on sustainability, fair wages and sensible executive pay [S5].

### Serve all stakeholders

In a 2016 interview he said companies should serve their whole range of stakeholders, not just shareholders, and that this also supports long-term profit [S6]. The people who make, use or are touched by a product shape the brand's value [S6]. Social media gives them a louder voice [S6]. He calls this brand activism, which goes beyond corporate social responsibility [S1][S6].

How he applied it: he asked brands what they are doing to make the world better, and pointed to Unilever as an example [S6]. In 2018 he and Christian Sarkar published *Brand Activism* [S1].

### Markets change faster than marketing

Kotler warns that success breeds failure. His sayings include that the main cause of company failure is company success, and that calling a market "mature" shows a lack of imagination [S3]. Markets, he says, change faster than marketing does [S3].

How he applied it: his 2020 book *Never Stop* tells how Fujifilm survived losing its film market through innovation and marketing [S4]. In 2019 he wrote with Sarkar that ecosystem thinking would reshape strategy [S4].

## How he decides

- Start with the customer's need, before the product [S3][S5].
- Choose a target group, and don't try to serve everyone [S3].
- Find a way to stand out, even for a plain product [S3].
- Plan product, price, place and promotion together [S7].
- Earn share, don't buy it [S3].
- Weigh people and the planet next to profit [S5].
- Use marketing to change behaviour when society needs it [S1][S5].
- Keep changing, because the market will [S3].

## Where he stood

**What is marketing?** A customer-centred discipline that starts before the product, not just advertising [S3][S5].

**Is it only for business?** No. It works for causes, charities, places and ideas [S1].

**Should marketing ever reduce demand?** Yes, through demarketing, when demand is too high or harmful [S1][S5].

**Who should a company serve?** As of 2016, all its stakeholders, not only shareholders [S6].

**Is behavioural economics new?** As of 2015, he said marketers were the original behavioural economists [S5].

**Is a market ever "mature"?** He doubts it. On his site he calls the label a sign that a company has stopped looking for new needs [S3].

**How much should you spend on advertising?** Enough to matter. One of his sayings warns that spending too little is itself a waste [S3].

**Should firms do good on the side or in the model?** In the model. As of 2015, he said corporate social responsibility often treats good works as an add-on, while sustainable marketing builds them into how the business makes money [S5].

**Is capitalism working?** In his books from 2015 onward he argued it needs reform, starting with paying workers enough to buy what they make [S5][S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The four Ps look from the seller's side.** Critics call the mix a supply-side model with real limits [S7]. Robert Lauterborn offered the four Cs in 1990, built around the consumer [S7]. Booms and Bitner added three more Ps for services in 1981 [S7].
- **Borrowed credit.** McCarthy, not Kotler, first proposed the four Ps [S7]. Some lines on his sayings page echo others, such as Drucker's view on selling [S3][S6].
- **Thin record.** Reference pages on him include few critical views, and some have errors [S1]. Read praise of the "father of modern marketing" with care.

## Blind spots to watch

- His frameworks are broad and tidy. A small team may need one sharp move more than a full plan.
- He writes for large firms and classrooms. Test his advice against a small budget.
- His later work leans toward big themes, such as capitalism and democracy. Keep advice on the business problem in hand.
- Pair him with Byron Sharp on evidence about how brands grow, and with a direct-response writer on what makes people act now.
