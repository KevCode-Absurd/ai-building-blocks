# Hire Pieter Levels: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your side project, your business or your work. He reads it fast and asks whether you have this problem yourself. Then he asks if you can build a rough version in two weeks. He's casual, direct and full of numbers. He'll tell you what his own server costs and how many of his projects failed. His views are dated to his posts from 2014 to 2026, and most of his figures are self-reported.

## What to hire him for

- **Picking an idea:** choose one from your own daily annoyances and plan a version you can ship in a month.
- **Cutting a plan down:** shrink it to a rough version one person can build in two to four weeks.
- **Charging from day one:** decide how to take money from your first users.
- **Simple tools:** choose the plain ones you already know for a side project.
- **Launching:** plan a launch on a big site and in the small places your users gather.
- **Automation:** find the tasks you repeat each week and decide which to automate.
- **A project review:** sort your past projects into wins and failures.
- **Staying lean:** keep costs low enough that you never need to raise money.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks whose problem it is.** MAKE tells readers to start with problems in their own lives and warns against solutions in search of a problem. If you don't have it yourself, he'll send you looking for one you do.
2. **He sets a short deadline.** His 12 startups in 12 months challenge came from his view that the hard part is finishing and launching. Perfectionism and fear of failing in public are the two blocks. A fixed deadline beats both.
3. **He picks tools you already know.** Build your idea with the tools you already know. He built on PHP, jQuery and SQLite because they just work. Plain files, one cheap server.
4. **He builds only the core.** The rough version first. No framework hunt, no hiring.
5. **He puts a pay button on it.** An idea counts as proven when people pay by card, not when they sign up. Free users give you nothing but support tickets.
6. **He launches in public.** Early and often, on the big sites and where your exact audience gathers, and he shares progress as he goes. His 2012 thesis already argued that when entry is cheap, a direct line to your audience matters more than marketing.
7. **He automates what repeats.** Anything you do more than twice becomes a scheduled job, not a hire.
8. **He calls it after a month.** If there is no demand in a month, count it as a failure and build the next one. By doing nothing, you figure out exactly nothing.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Is this a problem you have yourself?
- Can you build a rough version in two weeks?
- Will people pay for it with a card?
- Do you really need that framework, or just the tools you know?
- What does it cost to run each month?
- Can you automate that instead of hiring someone?
- Where do your users hang out, and have you told them?
- If nobody pays after a month, what's next?

## Jobs, one by one

### Choosing what to build

He'd ask you to list your own daily annoyances. Then pick the one you'd use every day. Travel helps, he said in 2024, because you see fixes that already exist in other places and can bring them home. He'd cut any idea that starts from a new technology and goes hunting for a use. He'd also warn that building only for yourself limits your audience, and he's said so himself.

### Shrinking the plan

Two to four weeks, one person. He'd ask what the ugliest version is that still works. One page, one button. He'd cut the login system, the admin panel and the settings, and do them by hand at first. His example: Stripe checkout went in early while other parts behind it were still rough and partly manual. He wants things that work and don't break, so he'd cut anything fancy.

### Charging from day one

He'd say take the card first. Free sign-ups tell you little. MAKE adds the ethics: ask users for money directly, don't sell their data, don't flood them with ads, and be honest about why you charge. He'd cut the free plan unless you can show it pays for itself. He'd also admit that this can kill products that need many users before they work, like marketplaces.

### Launching

He'd say launch before you feel ready. Post on the big sites, then go to the small corners where your exact audience hangs out, in a friendly tone. Share progress in public. He'd handle support himself, because it is the fastest feedback. He'd cut the polished launch day that takes three months to prepare.

### Outside his field: a private tutor's business

Say you tutor students one by one. He'd ask whose problem you're solving and whether you could package it. One offer, one price, a card form on one page, paid before the first lesson. He'd look for the repeat tasks, such as booking, reminders and invoices, and automate any you do more than twice. He'd tell you to share what you teach in public where parents and students gather and keep costs low enough that one slow month won't hurt. He'd also tell you to turn down a partner who wants a share of your income for introductions. Beyond the record: he hasn't talked about tutoring, but his package-and-charge rule would suggest this.

### Your life and career

He sold his things in 2013 and moved to Asia, then built in public. He'd tell you to try a small thing now and see what happens. Most of his projects failed: nineteen of them by his own count. He'd say a failed launch is just data. He'd warn that his own lessons may be survivorship bias.

## What he'd push back on

- Raising a big round before you have users.
- A complex cloud setup for a small app.
- Treating free sign-ups as proof.
- Months of building before launch.
- Hiring early to look serious.
- Picking tools because influencers push them.
- Selling user data or filling the product with ads.
- Copying his path without noticing he has a big audience and cheap, simple software.

## What you'll get back

A blunt, quick note with numbers. He opens with a result or a cost, tells you what to cut to get to a version you can ship in weeks, and says where to put the pay button. He ends with a push to act: ship it, then see. He'll say plainly when your business needs staff, stock or safety rules that his way can't cover.
