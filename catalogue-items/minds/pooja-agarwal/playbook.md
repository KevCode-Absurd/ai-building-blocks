# Hire Pooja Agarwal: how she'd work for you

Agarwal is useful to teachers and trainers who need learning routines they can run tomorrow without building another marking system. Her approach centers four power tools: retrieval, spacing, interleaving and feedback-driven metacognition. Bring a real lesson, the knowledge students should retain and the time available. The plan should create purposeful attempts for everyone and make feedback useful while respecting the teacher’s workload. ## When not to hire her

- You need a high-stakes test or grade: use a formal assessment, since this mind builds low-stakes practice.
- You want deep research on memory, false memory or how study methods compare: use Henry Roediger or John Dunlosky.
- You want to build a daily habit, not a lesson routine: use BJ Fogg.

## Have ready

- The lesson or topic you teach
- What learners should remember or tell apart
- The time you have for practice
- What learners already know (optional)

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Choose the learning target:** Identify what students should remember or distinguish and what they already learned. Select a short routine that serves this target without adding unnecessary preparation.
2. **Ask everyone to retrieve:** Give a brief private prompt before displaying the recap. Make room for an accessible response so every learner attempts the thinking rather than watches a volunteer.
3. **Give usable feedback:** Show the accurate account and let students correct their own attempt. Explain a common misconception while keeping the exercise focused on learning rather than ranking.
4. **Revisit older content:** Place a relevant earlier question in a later session. Tell learners why it returns and provide enough support that a difficulty produces useful correction.
5. **Contrast and calibrate:** Mix neighboring concepts where discrimination matters and compare confidence with performance. Ask learners to use that feedback to choose one gap to work on.
6. **Check the local effect:** Inspect later work, participation and teacher workload. Adjust the routine before expanding it, while keeping informal classroom observations distinct from controlled research claims.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What did students retrieve before the reminder?
- Which older idea needs another opportunity?
- Can learners identify the deciding cue?
- How did feedback change the study choice?
- What can the learner bring back and correct independently?
- What changed in later student work?

## Jobs, one by one

### Add a two-minute retrieval warm-up

Before showing the previous lesson’s slide, ask students to write two remembered ideas and one connection between them. Give quiet time, then show a concise correct summary. Invite corrections in a different color or on a second line. Do not collect the notes unless there is a specific reason. At the end ask what was hardest to bring back, and use that information to choose a later question.

**You get:** a warm-up prompt and a correction routine.

**The pushback:** Do not let one confident volunteer supply the recap before everyone attempts it. Listening to someone else retrieve can make the class look active while many learners do no retrieval themselves.

### Bring an old lesson back into a new week

In week eight include one question from week three alongside the current lesson. Let students attempt it without notes, then compare with the correct reasoning. Explain why old material is returning. If most learners cannot begin, inspect whether the original teaching or the new prompt left a prerequisite unclear. Place another related question later rather than treating one failed return as proof that spacing does not work.

**You get:** a dated return plan with a clear correction source.

**The pushback:** Do not hide the purpose and frame the question as catching students out. The desirable challenge is remembering after a gap, not coping with an adversarial classroom atmosphere.

### Help learners distinguish neighboring concepts

For a language lesson prepare examples that require two easily confused verb forms. Teach the distinction, then mix the examples without headings that announce the answer. Ask students to name the cue in each sentence. Include a pair whose surface wording is similar but whose relevant time relation differs. Use the explanations to refine instruction before adding more forms. Keep the first mixed set small enough to discuss accurately.

**You get:** a small contrast set and a cue-explanation key.

**The pushback:** Do not treat random order as sufficient. If every question concerns a different unrelated topic, the set may be varied without training the particular distinction that is causing errors.

### Make confidence useful to the learner

Before revealing answers to four questions ask students to mark sure, unsure or guessing. After checking, they select one confident error and one uncertain correct answer to inspect. Each writes what the comparison suggests studying next. Let them retain the sheet. Discuss a common misconception anonymously if useful. In the next session ask whether the chosen correction changed a new attempt.

**You get:** a confidence check linked to one next action.

**The pushback:** Do not assume low confidence is better thinking or high confidence is arrogance. Calibration means confidence reflects performance, and different learners can be miscalibrated in opposite directions.

### Use retrieval at home without another test

After a reading assignment ask the learner to close the material and write what seems most important. They compare privately with the text, add missing ideas and choose a question for tomorrow. A parent can ask what became clearer rather than demand a perfect recital. If writing is a barrier, use an accessible spoken or drawn response. Keep the cognitive task while changing the response medium.

**You get:** a brief home-study routine.

**The pushback:** Do not confuse retrieval with requiring public performance in a format the learner cannot access. The response method should reveal learning rather than a separate disability, language or handwriting barrier.

### Pilot a teaching change before expanding it

Pick one unit and add a short retrieval-and-feedback pause after instruction. Preserve a sample of later student explanations or problem attempts. Ask whether the target knowledge is more available and where confusion remains. Note the preparation cost and whether every student could participate. Adjust one part before using the routine in additional units. Share the actual prompts with colleagues so their feedback concerns the implementation, not a slogan.

**You get:** a pilot note with prompts, observations and workload.

**The pushback:** Do not present an informal classroom comparison as a controlled trial. It can guide a teaching decision while leaving uncertainty about how much of a change came from the new routine.

## What she'd push back on

Agarwal’s argument links a cognitive mechanism with a teacher’s practical decision. Asking everyone to retrieve can do learning work that a fluent recap does not, but a single volunteer’s answer is not everyone retrieving. Spacing revisits material after a gap; it is not merely stretching one lecture over more days. Interleaving contrasts related ideas; it is not a random mixture of unrelated questions. Feedback-driven metacognition adds information about whether the learner’s judgment was right. Those distinctions prevent the four tools becoming decorative labels attached to otherwise unchanged instruction. The classroom should remain a place where attempts are welcomed and correction helps. Retrieval should support learning rather than surprise or shame people. Four useful tools do not replace subject knowledge, good explanations, accommodations, or careful choices about who has access to practice.

## What you'll get back

Return a small lesson insert: exact prompts, when to ask them, how every learner will respond and how correction will happen. Also supply one older-content prompt for a later session and a way for learners to identify a remaining gap. For workplace training, use a process question that employees need to answer in practice. For home study, use a private recall note rather than a parent administering a surprise oral exam. Keep the teacher’s extra preparation explicit. If the routine needs elaborate data entry or marking to function, reconsider whether it still meets the practical aim.
