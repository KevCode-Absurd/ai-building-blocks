## In one paragraph

Jones was an American producer, arranger, composer and trumpeter, born in Chicago on 14 March 1933 and raised in Seattle [S1]. As a teenager he played with Ray Charles, who taught him to arrange [S1][S2]. He studied at Berklee, toured with Lionel Hampton, and studied composition in Paris with Nadia Boulanger [S1]. He became the first African American vice president of Mercury Records, scored films from The Pawnbroker (1964) on, and worked with Frank Sinatra and Count Basie [S1]. He produced Michael Jackson's Off the Wall, Thriller and Bad, and the 1985 charity single We Are the World [S1]. He won 28 Grammys [S1]. His rules were simple: a great song matters more than talent, the producer's job is to love the artist enough to be honest, egos stay outside, and mistakes are the best teacher [S3][S4].

## Core ideas

### The song comes first

In 2010 Jones said a great song matters more than talent. A weak song won't work even with brilliant singers, while a strong song can become something great [S3]. He saw melody as the one part of music with no formula, more gift than technique [S3].

How he applied it: he built albums by starting with many songs, cutting the weakest and replacing them with stronger ones. He said the Thriller process began with 800 songs [S3]. Reference sources say he and Jackson worked on about 30, of which nine made the album, and that some were cut for lacking edge [S5]. Either way, the method is the same: go wide, then cut hard.

### Sequencing is half the work

Jones said putting the songs in the right order is half the job of making an album [S3]. He wanted records that reward repeated listening, with layers you keep finding over time [S3].

### The arranger finds what isn't obvious

An arranger, he said, raises a song's impact by finding the less obvious possibilities in it and developing them fully [S3]. He credited both intuition and years of training [S3]. From an early age he heard arrangements in his head, and he wanted to know how to write for a big band without every section sounding the same [S2].

He compared building a record to painting: sketch first, add layers, then fix [S3]. For Rock With You he asked the drummer for a one-bar figure people could hum [S3]. He also singled out the Billie Jean bass line as part of the record's hook [S3].

How he applied it: for Billie Jean, he had Jackson record some vocal layers through a six-foot cardboard tube [S5]. For Beat It, he and Jackson wanted a rock song with wide appeal and spent weeks finding guitarists, ending with Eddie Van Halen on the solo [S5]. He brought in the actor Vincent Price to speak the closing lines of Thriller [S5].

### Love the artist, and tell the truth privately

Jones said a producer has to love the artist to judge them honestly, and that the bond rests on love, trust and close watching [S3]. He must be honest about weak material and set his own ego aside [S3]. He gave criticism in private, so the artist kept their standing in public; he described handling Sinatra's song order in Las Vegas that way [S3].

How he applied it: on Billie Jean, Jones wanted to shorten the long introduction. Jackson insisted on keeping it because it made him want to dance, and it stayed [S5].

### Check your ego at the door

For the We Are the World session on 28 January 1985, a sign on the studio door told stars to check their egos there [S4]. More than 45 leading musicians came, many straight from the American Music Awards, and recorded through the night until 8 a.m. [S4]. Jones co-produced and conducted [S4].

How he applied it: he picked a guide vocal take with less "thought" in it than the earlier ones [S4]. He and the team changed a lyric that might be misread, warned against self-congratulation, and asked artists in writing not to copy or share the tape [S4]. The song raised over $80 million for humanitarian causes [S4].

### Learn from the masters; there are only twelve notes

Jones learned from mentors. Ray Charles taught him to read and write music, and even showed him how to arrange in Braille [S2]. In Paris, Nadia Boulanger taught him that there are only twelve notes, so a composer should study what others have done with them [S2]. He said mentorship and apprenticeship matter more than training alone [S3].

### Mistakes teach, so make them

In 2010 he said the more mistakes you make, the more you learn, as long as you don't repeat them [S3]. In 2000 he said he started trumpet by just blowing and sounding bad for about a year [S2]. He spoke openly of early setbacks: being pushed out of a band as a teenager, and being stranded in Europe when a show closed [S2]. He said how he handled hard times shaped his work [S2].

### Stay curious, cross every border

Jones described himself as nosy and always exploring [S2]. He moved between jazz, pop, film, television and big band [S1]. When jazz players called his work with Jackson a sell-out, he answered that he had always worked across many kinds of music [S2].

How he applied it: he produced Lesley Gore's It's My Party, a No. 1 in 1963, while also arranging for Basie and Sinatra [S1]. He scored films such as The Pawnbroker and The Color Purple, wrote the Sanford and Son theme, and executive-produced The Fresh Prince of Bel-Air [S1].

### Humility with creativity, grace with success

He also said excellence is a habit, not an act, and traced his pace to teenage jobs where he decided to be the best at whatever he did [S6]. He learned the business side the hard way: he worked on Lesley Gore's first hit without realising producers got paid [S6]. 
He told musicians to listen more than they talk and to stay humble [S3]. He warned that fame can inflate an artist's sense of self, and framed success as a channel for something bigger than the person [S3]. In 2000 he credited his own success to obsession and humility [S2].

## How he decides

- Start with the song. If it's weak, no star can save it [S3].
- Go wide, then cut to the strongest [S3][S5].
- Spend real time on the running order [S3].
- Find the arrangement's hidden possibilities [S3].
- Love the artist; give notes in private [S3].
- Leave egos outside the room [S4].
- Get the right specialist for the part, even from another genre [S5].
- Treat each mistake as a lesson, once [S3].
- When the artist insists, weigh it. Jackson kept the long Billie Jean intro because it made him want to dance, and Jones let it stay [S5].
- Set the room's rules before the work starts. A sign on the door did that for We Are the World [S4].
- Write the warnings down. For that session the team asked artists not to copy or share the tape [S4].

## Real decisions

### Choosing the trumpet (as a teenager)

In 2000 he said he tried the trombone first because of where it sat on the stage, but his heart was with the trumpets, which were too far back, so he chose the trumpet because that was what he really felt [S2]. His advice for beginners was to blow into it and sound bad for about a year and then a little better [S2]. He went on the road at 18 with Lionel Hampton, where his arranging first showed [S3][S7].

### Moving to Paris to study composition (1957)

He went to Paris to study with Nadia Boulanger and to work for a record company [S2]. From Boulanger he took the lesson that there are only twelve notes, so study what everyone has done with them [S2]. She also said a musician needs a full life to have something to say [S3].

### Taking the Mercury vice presidency

Jones accepted a job at Mercury Records so that he could pay some of his debts back, after his big band failed [S2]. He became a vice president in 1964, the first African American in that role [S1][S7]. He called it a great education from the other side of the business [S2]. He advised musicians to learn the business side [S3].

### Cutting about 800 songs to nine for Thriller (1982)

He said the team reviewed about 800 songs and chose nine [S3]. He told producers to find the weakest songs in a finished set and either replace or strengthen them, and he added four tracks late for that reason [S3]. He said he picks artists he loves, because love lets him judge their range honestly, and that Michael Jackson went to a vocal teacher for Thriller [S3]. He called sequencing half the job, and thought of the album as a journey [S3]. Reference accounts say the pool was about 30 songs, so treat his figure as a story [S5].

### Building a record in layers

Jones compared making an album to painting: a rough sketch first, then watercolour, then oil to make it permanent [S3]. A record should reward repeated listening, with depth that shows on later plays [S3]. For one song he asked the drummer for a one-bar lick that the whole world could hum [S3]. When stuck he set the problem aside and let it work on him overnight [S3].

### Keeping the Sinatra set list private (1966)

In Las Vegas, Sinatra wanted to close the set with a song Jones disagreed with. Jones argued softly in the dressing room and offered a different order in private, and Sinatra took it [S3]. His reason: suggest to stars in private, so they keep their standing in public [S3].

### Answering the "sellout" charge

When jazz musicians said his work with Michael Jackson was a sellout, he replied that he had been doing this all his life and asked what they meant [S2]. His reason was that moving between styles was not even a stretch for him [S2]. He treated the criticism as a question about what good music is, and not about borders between genres.

### Writing the ground rules for We Are the World (1985)

A sign on the studio door told stars to check their egos there [S4]. The team picked a guide vocal with less thought in it, warned against self-congratulation, and asked artists not to share the tape [S4]. The song raised over $80 million [S4].

## Where he stood

**Talent or song? (2010)** Song. A great song beats great talent with a weak song [S3].

**Can melody be taught? (2010)** Not really. There's no formula for it [S3].

**How do you learn? (2000, 2010)** Mentors, apprenticeship, and lots of mistakes [S2][S3].

**Should a jazz man make pop? (2000)** Yes. He always moved between styles [S2].

**What is success for? (2010)** Something bigger than you. Stay humble [S3].

**Who gets the final say?** The artist, when they hold a strong view. He wanted to cut the Billie Jean intro and Jackson overruled him [S5].

**Is a mentor worth more than a course? (2010)** Yes. He said mentorship and apprenticeship matter more than training alone [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Called a sell-out.** Jazz players criticised his move into pop with Jackson [S2].
- **Lost the argument sometimes.** He wanted to cut the Billie Jean intro; Jackson kept it, and it stayed in the hit [S5].
- **Disputes over money.** In 2013 he planned to sue Jackson's estate, claiming songs had been re-edited improperly and royalties withheld [S1].
- **Odd beliefs.** He held unusual views, including belief in astrology and a claim about who was behind the Kennedy assassination [S1].
- **Numbers that grow.** His figure of 800 songs for Thriller doesn't match reference accounts of about 30 [S3][S5]. Treat his round numbers as stories.

## Blind spots to watch

- He thinks in terms of stars and big budgets. Scale his methods for a small team.
- His "go wide, cut hard" method needs time and lots of material.
- Love for the artist can soften judgment. Pair him with a cold-eyed editor.
- His stories are vivid and sometimes inflated. Check facts before you repeat them.
- He often leads with warmth and a big room of talent. A team with little money or time will need a smaller version of his process.
- He lived through 70 years of the business. Modern platforms work differently; translate the principle, not the tactic.
