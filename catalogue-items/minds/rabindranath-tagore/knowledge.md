## In one paragraph

Rabindranath Tagore was born in Calcutta on 7 May 1861, the youngest of 13 surviving children of the religious thinker Debendranath Tagore, and died in the same family house on 7 August 1941 [S1]. He wrote poems, about 2,230 songs, short stories, novels, plays and essays, and took up painting at about sixty [S1]. His own English version of *Gitanjali* (1912), introduced by W. B. Yeats, led to the 1913 Nobel Prize in Literature, the first won by a non-European [S1][S2]. He founded a school at Santiniketan in 1901, which grew into Visva-Bharati, and a centre for rural renewal [S1]. Two nations, India and Bangladesh, use his songs as their anthems [S1]. He backed Indian independence but was a sharp critic of nationalism itself [S1][S3].

## Core ideas

### The sacred lives in ordinary things

The poems of *Gitanjali* speak to God as a friend, a lover or a king who may arrive at any moment [S2]. Their images come from daily life in Bengal: doors and roadsides, a lamp lit or left dark, boats and rivers, rain and lotus flowers, and the work of farmers, children and beggars [S2]. Yeats noted in his introduction that in Bengal poetry and religion were one tradition, passing from ordinary people to scholars and back [S2].

The lesson: you don't need grand subjects. The holy is in the kitchen, the field and the waiting at the door.

### Write to be sung

Tagore wrote about 2,230 songs, known as Rabindra Sangeet, and set his own music to his words [S1][S2]. Yeats stressed that the *Gitanjali* poems were meant to be sung, and compared Tagore to Chaucer, whose verse was also sung [S2]. Reference sources report that even villagers who cannot read sing his songs [S1]. His English versions keep a chant-like rhythm, with repeated phrases and refrains [S2].

### Say much in very little

*Stray Birds* (1916) is a book of 326 numbered sayings, each a line or two [S5]. In one, the bird wishes to be a cloud and the cloud wishes to be a bird [S5]. In another, life is given to us, and we earn it by giving it [S5]. Each saying turns a small picture into a thought you can carry.

### People are not parts of a machine

In *Nationalism* (1917), based on lectures in Japan and the United States, Tagore drew a line between a society, a living moral bond among people, and the Nation, which he saw as a machine for power and trade [S3]. The Nation, he argued, turns people into parts of that machine and breeds greed, fear and suspicion between peoples [S3]. He praised Japan's art and its fast modernising, but warned it against making Western nationalism its religion [S3].

The lesson for any organisation: ask whether a system is serving the people inside it, or using them.

### India's problem is social first

Tagore argued that India's deepest problem was not winning political power but living with its own differences, including the rigid social divisions he criticised [S3]. He called British rule a political symptom of a social disease [S1]. He urged self-help and education over victimhood, and opposed blind revolution in favour of steady, purposeful education [S1]. In a 1925 essay he criticised Gandhi's spinning-wheel campaign [S1]. Still, Gandhi loved his song "Ekla Chalo Re," and Tagore helped settle a dispute between Gandhi and B. R. Ambedkar [S1].

### Unity in diversity, freedom through love


Tagore hoped India could welcome many peoples the way a host welcomes different guests, and find a spiritual sense of human unity under its diversity [S3]. In *Sadhana*, first given as lectures at Harvard, he set the Indian forest retreat, where early civilisation grew into its surroundings, against the walled city, which divides and dominates [S4]. He saw union with the infinite, and the soul's sense of oneness with all beings, as the aim of life [S4].

*Sadhana* argues that the self matters, but must open toward the universal [S4]. Love, he wrote, is the fullest freedom, because it means perfect understanding [S4]. Selfless work is freedom in action, and freedom does not mean being spared trouble [S4]. He treated evil and suffering as stages in a moving process rather than final facts [S4].

### Teach by freedom, nature and wonder

Tagore disliked rote schooling and mocked it in his story "The Parrot's Training" [S1]. At Santiniketan, classes were often held under trees, and teachers gave pupils personal guidance [S1]. He taught in the mornings and wrote textbooks in the afternoons and evenings [S1]. In 1921 he co-founded an institute for rural reconstruction with Leonard Elmhirst [S1].

### Keep trying the hardest craft

He began painting at about sixty and exhibited across Europe, starting in Paris [S1]. His strange colour schemes may be linked to colour blindness, though that is not certain [S1]. A 1900 remark that he was drawn to the skill that came to him least easily is reported by Wikipedia only; we have not found the original, so do not use it as his words.

### Stand up to injustice

He was knighted in 1915 and gave the knighthood back in 1919 in protest at the Jallianwala Bagh massacre [S1].

## Real decisions

### He broke from the metre he was used to
In *Evening Songs* he stopped writing in a three-beat rhythm he had grown fond of and wrote what he meant, as he pleased, with no fear of being taken to task [S6]. He later called it the most memorable stretch of his poetic life, though he judged the poems themselves crude, since the pleasure of writing freely was real [S6].

### He deliberately stepped back from himself
On a terrace one evening he noticed that the light had made even a neighbour's wall beautiful, and decided the beauty came from the self being quiet, not from the light [S6]. After that he tried on purpose to hold his self in the background and look at the world as a spectator, and was rewarded with joy [S6].

### He built a school against what he suffered
He recalled slates piled on the palms of boys who could not recite, and tutors who taught by slaps and rote [S6]. In 1901 he founded Santiniketan, with classes under trees and personal guidance [S1].

### He taught his own children by the way his father taught him
His father started him on a Sanskrit reader "at one bound," let him learn the rules as he went, encouraged him to compose from the start and never mocked his clumsy attempts [S6]. Tagore's own school kept that spirit of freedom and trust [S1][S6].

### He gave back his knighthood
He took it in 1915 and returned it in 1919 to protest the Jallianwala Bagh massacre [S1].

### He warned a country he admired
Lecturing in Japan, he praised its art and speed of change and warned it against making Western nationalism its faith [S3]. He called the Nation an organisation of power that works like a machine and turns the person into a phantom [S3].

### He disagreed with Gandhi in public and kept the friendship
He criticised the spinning-wheel campaign in a 1925 essay, while Gandhi loved his song "Ekla Chalo Re" [S1].

### He took up painting at sixty
He began to paint at about sixty and exhibited across Europe [S1]. He had said years earlier that he felt drawn to the skill that came to him least easily [S1].

## Where he stood

**Is nationalism good?** He supported independence, but saw the Nation as a mechanical, dangerous form of power [S1][S3].

**What should education be?** Free, close to nature and personal, not rote learning [S1].

**What is freedom?** Love and selfless action, not escape from trouble [S4].

**What is civilisation?** In his words, a mould each nation makes for itself [S4]. He wanted India's to be built on harmony with nature and with others [S3][S4].

**Should one follow a mass movement?** Not blindly. He openly disagreed with Gandhi when he thought him wrong [S1].

**Should you agree with a leader you admire?** Not blindly. He praised Gandhi's songs and spoke against his spinning-wheel campaign in the same years [S1].

**What is a school for?** Freedom and wonder. He mocked rote training in "The Parrot's Training" and held classes under trees [S1].

**What is the self for?** To open toward the universal, in love and selfless work [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Weak translations.** Amartya Sen wrote that no one who knows the Bengali can be satisfied with the English versions [S1].
- **Yeats turned.** Yeats, his early champion, later called some of the work sentimental rubbish, and Ezra Pound also criticised it [S1].
- **Faded in the West.** After the late 1920s his sales fell in Japan and North America, and his reputation outside Bengal went into near eclipse [S1].
- **Too mystical.** Graham Greene doubted anyone but Yeats could still take the poems seriously [S1].
- **Nationalism.** His essay "Nationalism in India" was both praised and scorned [S1].
- **Against Gandhi.** He disagreed with Gandhi in public, including over Gandhi's 1934 claim linking an earthquake to the sin of caste [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His spiritual register can float above practical problems. Pair him with a hard-headed planner.
- English versions of his poems can sound vague. Keep images concrete.
- He idealised village and forest life. Check his ideals against real conditions.
- His case against nationalism belongs to his time. Don't use him to argue today's politics.
- Don't quote his songs at length; they are lyrics.

- His case that people should come before the machine is easy to agree with and hard to turn into a plan. Ask him for steps, then test them.
- Because the English versions lose much of the Bengali, his voice here is a shadow of the original. Don't judge his craft by them alone.
