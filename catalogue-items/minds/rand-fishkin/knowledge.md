# Rand Fishkin: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-21. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Rand Fishkin is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from interviews he gave between 2018 and mid-2026, a conference speaker biography and a reference page [S1] to [S11]. Eight of these are interviews or podcast pages in which he explains his own choices: four on marketing and zero-click search from 2021 to 2025 [S2][S3][S4][S5], and five on money, Moz and SparkToro, from 2018 to 2026 [S7][S8][S9][S10][S11]. Several are show-note pages, so they give his points in summary, not word for word. His own company blogs, at SparkToro and Moz, could not be opened by our reader, and neither could his book, so we have not used them. Treat each view as "as of" the interview it comes from. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about later events, and nothing covers his private life beyond what he said about work.

## In one paragraph

Fishkin built his name in search marketing. He started the SEOmoz blog in 2004, co-founded the company with his mother, and ran it as CEO from 2007 to 2014 as it grew into Moz [S1]. He raised about $30 million of venture money along the way, and later called that a big mistake [S8][S11]. He stepped down as CEO in 2014 while battling depression, left Moz in early 2018, and started SparkToro the next day [S11]. SparkToro is a small, profitable audience research company funded by about 35 angel investors, not funds [S7][S10]. His marketing view moved with the web. He now argues that search engines and social platforms keep people on their own pages, so fewer searches end in a click, and he tells marketers to study where their audience already pays attention and build the brand there [S2][S3].

## Core ideas

### The zero-click internet

Fishkin says a large share of searches now end with no click to any website, because Google answers the question on the results page [S2][S3]. In a February 2025 interview he put it at nearly two-thirds of searches [S2]. In another conversation that month he gave about 60%, up from roughly half in 2019 [S3]. He credits the term "zero-click" to Gabriel Weinberg of DuckDuckGo, in 2011 [S3]. In mid-2026 he said answers now sit inside Google, AI tools and social platforms, so a customer's journey often ends without a site visit [S11].

How he applied it: he argues the same pull applies everywhere. Social sites such as LinkedIn, Facebook and YouTube show posts with outside links to fewer people, to keep users on the platform [S3][S5].

### Influence people where they already are

If people won't click through, you have to reach them on the platforms they use [S3]. His advice is to make content that helps and persuades people right where they are, on social sites, in communities and on other people's websites [S3]. In 2026 he told small businesses to pick one or two channels that fit their audience, their skills and their interests, and said offline marketing still works [S11].

How he applied it: he described much of good SEO as a kind of public relations, getting your message placed and repeated on sites your audience already reads [S3].

### Do the audience research almost nobody does

He notes that nearly every marketer runs search, ads, email and content, yet very few do deep audience research [S2]. That gap is an edge for anyone willing to fill it [S2]. Research should show what a group reads, watches, listens to and follows, and how that differs from the average person [S3][S4].

How he applied it: SparkToro pulls together public social and web profiles to show which podcasts, sites and accounts a given audience follows [S4]. Its first big problem was demand: almost nobody searched for what it did, so the company had to create demand by working with people who already reached marketers [S4].

### Search demand comes late

Fishkin argues that people, especially business buyers, rarely start with a search. They meet ideas first through podcasts, publications, social posts and webinars, and those ideas later turn into searches [S4]. Topics are often discussed for months or years before anyone types them into Google [S4].

How he applied it: he tells search marketers to invest in topics early, while people are talking about them on social platforms [S4].

### Brand over raw traffic

He told marketers in 2025 to stop obsessing over direct traffic and invest in the brand [S2]. In 2026 he called traffic a vanity metric. He argued that brand compounds over time, while tricks such as spamming Reddit or YouTube stop working once platforms adjust [S11]. He also said leaders overvalue attribution and wrongly credit Google for branded searches that other exposure created [S11].

How he applied it: he pointed to HubSpot, whose stock hit record highs while its reported web traffic fell. Traffic down and revenue up, he said, is a reason to celebrate [S3].

### Measure lift, and tie it to business goals

Fishkin sees measurement moving away from exact click-by-click tracking and back toward lift: growth in searches for your brand name, brand sentiment and conversion rates [S2]. He wants marketing goals tied directly to specific business goals [S5]. In 2026 he suggested tracking mentions, impressions and AI visibility across press, search, AI answers and social channels [S11].

How he applied it: he warns that "how did you hear about us" surveys are biased toward channels you already use. He cited a test where 30% of a food brand's customers chose a celebrity chef as their source, though the chef had never mentioned the brand [S3].

### Venture money is a poor default

Fishkin says he was drawn to venture capital partly from feeling like an outsider who wanted to play with the big kids [S7]. He now argues that most funded companies don't return the investors' money, and that the model rewards a few huge winners [S7][S8][S10]. He says the pressure to grow fast pushed Moz toward spending, hubris and loss of focus, and that it hurt staff with little equity value and customers who were served after investors [S8].

How he applied it: he points to rivals such as Semrush and Ahrefs, which took market share from Moz while taking no venture money or only late. Ahrefs, he says, never took outside money [S7]. He also says founders choose their model and should not feel venture funding is the only road [S10].

### Small, profitable and open on purpose

At SparkToro he chose a small team, low costs, and angel money that gets paid back [S7][S10]. He rejects the stigma on "lifestyle businesses", saying owner-run profitable companies can bring more money and more happiness [S10]. He values openness because he dislikes secrecy and half-true stories [S10]. He published the legal documents for his fundraising so others could copy them [S7][S8].

How he applied it: he says he is happier and earns more today, although revenue is a small share of Moz's roughly $50 million a year [S11].

### Focus and sharp positioning

Looking back at Moz, he said his biggest strategic mistake was drifting away from self-service SEO software for small and mid-size businesses into broad web marketing, which left the company mediocre at a bunch of things instead of excellent at one [S9]. He called weak positioning his biggest marketing mistake at SparkToro and said the company changed its description several times [S11].

How he applied it: he warns against launching a product that barely works. If it doesn't impress, it can cost you future customers and fans [S5].

## How he decides

- Start with data on where your audience spends attention, not with guesses [S2][S3].
- Show up in those places, in proportion to how much the audience uses them [S3].
- Tie every marketing goal to a business goal [S5].
- Judge results by lift: brand searches, sentiment, conversion [S2].
- Treat self-reported "how did you hear about us" answers with caution [S3].
- Hire only after revenue shows real demand, not before [S10].
- Prefer quiet, profitable markets and a team you can keep small [S7][S11].
- Test a trend by how much of it can be measured, and don't chase every one [S2][S7].

## Real decisions

### Taking venture money for Moz (2007 and 2012)

Moz's first round was only $1.1 million in 2007, and it helped build early tools such as MozRank and Domain Authority [S1][S11]. A much larger raise followed, $18.1 million in 2012 by Wikipedia's count, bringing the total to about $30 million [S1][S8]. He says he wanted to play with the big kids, and the bigger round shifted the company toward a low-odds, billion-dollar outcome with pressure to return roughly ten times the money [S7][S11]. Later he called it a big mistake, though he owned two of four board seats and had veto rights, so he says the pressure came from his own choices as much as from investors [S11].

### Passing on a sale to HubSpot

He regrets not selling Moz when HubSpot offered to buy it. He says he was overconfident the company would be worth more later [S10]. His lesson is to notice when your growth rate, not your revenue, is your most valuable asset, and to think about selling while growth is high [S10]. He adds that selling a first company early cuts risk for the founder and staff and makes a second one easier to start [S10].

### Stepping down as CEO (2014)

When growth slowed in 2014, Moz had internal trouble and Fishkin was dealing with depression. He stepped down as CEO [S8][S11]. He stayed on, but later said the conflict with leadership peaked after layoffs in 2016. He left about a year later after helping launch Link Explorer [S9]. He says the board had been pushing him out for about 18 months before he left [S7].

### Starting SparkToro the day after leaving (2018)

He left Moz in early 2018 and announced SparkToro the next day, about ten hours between jobs [S7][S11]. He wanted the news to be his own, and he needed health insurance [S7]. He also wrote *Lost and Founder* that year to show how startup life differs from the picture painted by media and venture culture [S9].

### Raising $1.3 million from angels, on his own terms

He raised about $1.3 million from 33 to 35 individual investors, with checks of $25,000 to $100,000 each [S7][S10]. SparkToro is an LLC with no formal board. Founder pay is capped until investors are repaid in full, and profits are paid out [S10]. He says investors were paid back about four years after launch and now get pro-rata profit distributions [S7]. He paid lawyers an extra $10,000 to prepare the documents and shared them openly [S7].

### Keeping the company small

SparkToro has three people and under $2 million in revenue, a size he picked, not a stage on the way to something bigger [S7]. He says he will hire only after revenue shows real demand, instead of raising money and building a team ahead of it [S10]. He also refuses constant long hours and puts sleep, friends, family and travel first [S10].

### Starting another small company the same way

He later helped raise $2.15 million from almost 40 investors at similar check sizes for Snackbar Studio, his game company. He describes it as a different structure, a C corporation with higher risk and higher potential return [S7]. He also sits on the board of another firm that used the SparkToro-style model [S7].

## Where he stood

**Is SEO dying?** No, as of 2025. But he expects it to grow more slowly or shrink as companies see other options, and he wants SEO teams to broaden beyond rankings [S3].

**Is Google getting worse for users?** He was sceptical of that claim, and said data showed searches per user had grown [S3].

**Traffic or brand?** Brand. He told marketers to stop obsessing over direct traffic [S2].

**Should you optimise for AI tools?** It depends on whether your audience uses them heavily, as of 2025 [S3].

**Is a personal brand an asset?** At small and mid scale it helped him raise money and gain trust. At larger scale he thinks it can become a drag and a risk for the company [S7].

**Should every founder raise venture money?** No. Start small, self-fund or use angels first, since you don't yet know which model fits [S10].

## His work and record

- Left the University of Washington in 2000 to work as a web designer at his mother's small marketing firm [S1].
- Started the SEOmoz blog in 2004, which became a leading resource for search marketers [S1].
- Served as CEO of SEOmoz from 2007 to February 2014 [S1].
- Under his lead the company grew from 7 to 134 staff and from $800,000 to $29.3 million in revenue [S1].
- Renamed SEOmoz to Moz in 2013 [S1].
- Co-founded Inbound.org with Dharmesh Shah. It was sold to HubSpot in 2014 for no profit [S1].
- Made Whiteboard Friday videos at Moz, which built the brand and the field [S4].
- Founded SparkToro in 2018 [S1].
- Wrote *Lost and Founder: A Painfully Honest Field Guide to the Startup World*, published in 2018 [S1][S9].
- Co-wrote *The Art of SEO* and *Inbound Marketing & SEO* [S1].
- Named to BusinessWeek's 30 Under 30 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Venture funding.** By his own account, taking about $30 million was his biggest mistake, and it hurt employees and customers [S8][S11]. A fair note: he also says he chose it, and had board control [S11].
- **Selling Moz.** He says he was overconfident and should have taken the HubSpot offer [S10].
- **Data sources.** His first studies of zero-click search used clickstream data from Jumpshot [S3]. Jumpshot, owned by the antivirus maker Avast, was shut down in January 2020 after reporters found it resold browsing data from users of free antivirus software. US regulators later fined Avast $16.5 million [S6]. Nothing in our sources says Fishkin knew how the data was gathered, but the episode raises fair questions about clickstream data.
- **Moving numbers.** His zero-click figures differ between interviews, from about 60% to nearly two-thirds [S2][S3]. Different studies and dates may explain this. Quote the figure with its source and date.
- **He sells what he recommends.** He argues for audience research and runs a company that sells audience research tools [S2][S4]. That doesn't make the advice wrong, but weigh it.
- **Hard to measure.** His shift toward brand and lift makes results harder to prove than click-based tracking [S2]. Teams with strict budgets may struggle to defend it.

## Blind spots to watch

- He thinks about marketing at the scale of data sets. A tiny local business may learn more from ten customer conversations.
- He is strong on where to reach people, less on what to say. Pair him with April Dunford or Donald Miller for the message.
- His small-company, no-venture path works for software that can be built by a few people. Capital-heavy businesses may not fit it.
- His view of search is shaped by Google's changes. Check the latest data before you act.
- Brand-first advice needs patience and cash. A business that needs sales this month may need direct response too.
- His points on AI tools are from 2025 and 2026 and will date fast.
