# Rand Fishkin: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Rand Fishkin is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from interviews he gave from 2021 to 2025, a conference speaker biography and a reference page [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5][S6]. His own company blogs, at SparkToro and Moz, could not be opened by our reader, so we have not used them. Treat each view as "as of" the interview it comes from, mostly early 2025. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about later events, and nothing covers his private life or plans.

## In one paragraph

Fishkin built his name in search marketing. He started the SEOmoz blog in 2004, co-founded the company with his mother, and ran it as CEO from 2007 to 2014 as it grew into Moz [S1]. In 2018 he founded SparkToro, a tool for audience research [S1][S4]. His thinking moved with the web. He now argues that search engines and social platforms keep people on their own pages, so fewer searches end in a click [S2][S3]. His answer is to study where your audience already pays attention, show up there with useful content, invest in the brand, and measure lift instead of chasing every click [S2][S3]. He also wants marketing goals tied directly to business goals [S5].

## Core ideas

### The zero-click internet

Fishkin says a large share of searches now end with no click to any website, because Google answers the question on the results page [S2][S3]. In a February 2025 interview he put it at nearly two-thirds of searches [S2]. In another conversation that month he gave a figure of about 60%, up from roughly half in 2019 [S3]. He credits the term "zero-click" to Gabriel Weinberg of DuckDuckGo, in 2011 [S3].

How he applied it: he argues the same pull applies everywhere. Social sites such as LinkedIn, Facebook and YouTube show posts with outside links to fewer people, to keep users on the platform [S3][S5].

### Influence people where they already are

If people won't click through, you have to reach them on the platforms they use [S3]. Fishkin's advice is to make content that helps and persuades people right where they are, on social sites, in communities and on other people's websites, rather than making traffic your only goal [S3].

How he applied it: he described much of good SEO as a kind of public relations, getting your message placed and repeated on sites your audience already reads [S3]. Some audiences rely on word of mouth, like asking a neighbour for a builder, and marketers should show up in those places in proportion to how much the audience uses them [S3].

### Do the audience research almost nobody does

He notes that nearly every marketer runs search, ads, email and content, yet very few do deep audience research [S2]. That gap is an edge for anyone willing to fill it [S2]. Research should show what a group reads, watches, listens to and follows, and how that differs from the average person [S3][S4].

How he applied it: SparkToro pulls together public social and web profiles to show which podcasts, sites and accounts a given audience follows [S4]. He also suggests using browsing data to see which sites and tools a target group uses more or less than average, and he names other tools that can help [S3].

### Search demand comes late

Fishkin argues that people, especially business buyers, rarely start with a search. They meet ideas first through podcasts, publications, social posts and webinars, and those ideas later turn into searches [S4]. Topics are often discussed for months or years before anyone types them into Google [S4].

How he applied it: he tells search marketers to invest in topics early, while people are talking about them on social platforms [S4]. At SparkToro he faced a product almost nobody searched for, so the company had to create demand by working with people who already reached marketers [S4].

### Brand over raw traffic

He told marketers in 2025 to stop obsessing over direct traffic and invest in the brand [S2]. The goal is to link your name to the problem you solve and stay in your audience's mind [S2]. Falling traffic doesn't have to mean falling business [S3].

How he applied it: he pointed to HubSpot, whose stock hit record highs while its reported web traffic fell. A pattern of traffic down and revenue up, he said, is a reason to celebrate, not to blame the marketing team [S3].

### Measure lift, not every click

Fishkin sees measurement moving away from exact click-by-click tracking and back toward measuring lift [S2]. He points to signals such as growth in searches for your brand name, brand sentiment and conversion rates [S2].

How he applied it: he warns that "how did you hear about us" surveys are biased toward channels you already use. He cited a test where 30% of a food brand's customers chose a celebrity chef as their source, though the chef had never mentioned the brand [S3].

### Tie marketing to business goals

He wants marketing goals tied directly to specific business goals, and improvements judged by business results [S5]. He has argued that much marketing money is spent without sense, citing political advertising as an example of heavy spending with weak returns [S5].

How he applied it: he warned against launching a product that barely works. If it doesn't impress, it can cost you future customers and fans [S5].

### Don't chase every trend

In 2025 he advised marketers not to try to predict every future trend. Strategy should rest on how your current audience behaves and who influences it [S2]. He also said no single approach fits every business [S2].

How he applied it: on optimising for AI tools, he said it depends on the field. Business technology buyers use AI tools a lot, so those marketers should pay attention, and brands should check that they show up accurately in AI answers [S2][S3]. He cited a study finding that 70% of ChatGPT prompts had no direct equivalent in Google search [S3].

## How he decides

- Start with data on where your audience spends attention, not with guesses [S2][S3].
- Show up in those places, in proportion to how much the audience uses them [S3].
- Make content that helps people even if they never visit your site [S3].
- Tie every marketing goal to a business goal [S5].
- Judge results by lift: brand searches, sentiment, conversion [S2].
- Treat self-reported "how did you hear about us" answers with caution [S3].
- Move early on topics people discuss before they search for them [S4].
- Prefer large-scale data over anecdotes [S3].

## Where he stood

**Is SEO dying?** No, as of 2025. But he expects it to grow more slowly or shrink as companies see other options, and he wants SEO teams to broaden beyond rankings [S3].

**Is Google getting worse for users?** He was sceptical of that claim, and said data showed searches per user had grown [S3].

**Traffic or brand?** Brand. He told marketers to stop obsessing over direct traffic [S2].

**Should you optimise for AI tools?** It depends on whether your audience uses them heavily, as of 2025 [S3].

**Where do competitors come from?** Anyone competing for the same attention, not only firms selling similar products [S4].

## His work and record

- Left the University of Washington in 2000 to work as a web designer at his mother's small marketing firm [S1].
- Started the SEOmoz blog in 2004, which became a leading resource for search marketers [S1].
- Served as CEO of SEOmoz from 2007 to February 2014 [S1].
- Under his lead the company grew from 7 to 134 staff and from $800,000 to $29.3 million in revenue, and raised $1.1 million in 2007 and $18.1 million in 2012 [S1].
- Renamed SEOmoz to Moz in 2013 [S1].
- Co-founded Inbound.org with Dharmesh Shah. It was sold to HubSpot in 2014 for no profit [S1].
- Made Whiteboard Friday videos at Moz, which built the brand and the field [S4].
- Founded SparkToro in 2018 [S1].
- Wrote *Lost and Founder: A Painfully Honest Field Guide to the Startup World*, published in 2018 [S1].
- Co-wrote *The Art of SEO* and *Inbound Marketing & SEO* [S1].
- Named to BusinessWeek's 30 Under 30 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Data sources.** His first studies of zero-click search used clickstream data from Jumpshot [S3]. Jumpshot, owned by the antivirus maker Avast, was shut down in January 2020 after reporters found it resold browsing data from users of free antivirus software. US regulators later fined Avast $16.5 million [S6]. Nothing in our sources says Fishkin knew how the data was gathered, but the episode raises fair questions about clickstream data.
- **Moving numbers.** His zero-click figures differ between interviews, from about 60% to nearly two-thirds [S2][S3]. Different studies and dates may explain this. Quote the figure with its source and date.
- **He sells what he recommends.** He argues for audience research and runs a company that sells audience research tools [S2][S4]. That doesn't make the advice wrong, but weigh it.
- **Hard to measure.** His shift toward brand and lift makes results harder to prove than click-based tracking [S2]. Teams with strict budgets may struggle to defend it.

## Blind spots to watch

- He thinks about marketing at the scale of data sets. A tiny local business may learn more from ten customer conversations.
- He is strong on where to reach people, less on what to say. Pair him with April Dunford or Donald Miller for the message.
- His view of search is shaped by Google's changes. Check the latest data before you act.
- Brand-first advice needs patience and cash. A business that needs sales this month may need direct response too.
- His points on AI tools are from 2025 and will date fast.
