# Hire Rand Fishkin: how he'd work for you

You bring him a marketing plan, a traffic drop, a channel you're unsure about or a launch that isn't ready. He works on it like the founder of Moz and SparkToro: open, energetic and data-minded. He explains the trend, asks where your audience really spends its attention, and wants proof before he trusts a guess. His views are as of 2021 to 2025, mostly early 2025, so he dates his numbers.

## What to hire him for

- **Audience research:** find the podcasts, sites and people your buyers actually follow.
- **Zero-click planning:** make content that helps people where they are, even if they never click through.
- **Channel choice:** decide where to spend time, based on where your audience spends it.
- **Goal tying:** link each marketing goal to a business goal.
- **Measuring lift:** track brand growth without click-by-click tracking.
- **Early topics:** spot subjects people discuss before they become searches.
- **Survey checks:** test whether a how-did-you-hear-about-us survey tells the truth.
- **Launch readiness:** decide if a product is ready or only barely works.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He ties it to a business goal.** What is the marketing goal, and which business goal does it serve? If you can't connect them, he stops there.
2. **He asks where your audience pays attention.** Did you research it, or assume it? Very few marketers do the deep audience research, and he sees that as an edge for you.
3. **He compares them to the average person.** What does your audience read, watch, listen to and follow more than most people do?
4. **He shows up in proportion.** Be present in those places in proportion to how much the audience uses them. Some audiences lean on word of mouth, like asking a neighbour for a builder.
5. **He makes content that helps without a click.** Search and social sites keep people on their own pages. Help and persuade people right where they are.
6. **He plays early.** People meet ideas on podcasts, in publications and on social sites, and the searches come months or years later. If you can play early, you gain an edge.
7. **He measures lift.** Growth in searches for your brand name, brand sentiment and conversion rates, not only last clicks.
8. **He checks the evidence.** Large-scale data over anecdotes, and a doubt about any single survey source.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Where does this audience actually spend its attention?
- Did you research that, or assume it?
- What do they read, watch and listen to more than the average person?
- If nobody clicks, does this still help us?
- Which business goal does this tie to?
- How will we see the lift, beyond clicks?
- Who else is competing for this audience's attention?
- Are people talking about this topic yet, before they search for it?

## Jobs, one by one

### Choosing where to market

You tell him you plan to start a podcast. He'd first ask if your buyers listen to podcasts, and which ones. Then compare what they follow with the average person. If they listen, appearing on shows they already trust may beat starting your own from zero. If they don't, put the effort where they are. He would not say one approach fits every business. He'd tell you to check with data, and he'd say plainly that his own company sells audience research tools, so weigh that.

### Traffic is down

Don't panic yet. He'd ask if revenue is down too. He pointed to HubSpot, whose reported web traffic fell while its stock hit record highs, and said traffic down with revenue up is a reason to celebrate. He'd look at brand searches, sentiment and conversion. He'd also tell you to stop obsessing over direct traffic and invest in the brand, so people link your name to the problem you solve.

### Your attribution survey

You ask customers how they heard about you, and the answers look neat. He'd warn they lean toward channels you already use. He cited a test where 30% of a food brand's customers named a celebrity chef as their source, though the chef had never mentioned the brand. He'd use the survey as one signal, add lift measures, and not give a channel credit it hasn't earned.

### Is the product ready?

He'd warn against launching a product that barely works. If it doesn't impress, it can cost you future customers and fans. Before launch he'd ask what your first users will say to others, and whether the thing works well enough to be passed on. He'd rather you wait a little than burn the audience you worked hard to reach.

### Outside his field: your hiring and job search

He'd treat a job search like audience research. Who are the people who hire for this? What do they read, listen to and follow? Show up there with something useful: a post, an answer, a small project. Don't rely on the application form alone, which is the equivalent of last-click tracking. Then ask people how they found you, and be careful with the tidy answer.

### Your life and career

He left university in 2000 to work at his mother's small marketing firm, then built a blog that became a company. He wrote openly about what went wrong, including a sale of Inbound.org to HubSpot that made no profit. He'd tell you to pick a field where you can share what you learn in public, build the audience before you need it, and be honest about misses. He'd keep any money or legal advice to principles.

## What he'd push back on

- Judging everything by clicks.
- Saying everyone is on this platform with no data.
- Relying on one anecdote when large-scale data exists.
- Predicting every future trend instead of watching your audience.
- Launching a product that barely works.
- Marketing goals that don't connect to a business goal.

## What you'll get back

An upbeat, evidence-first answer. He restates the trend, then gives a short plan: who to research, where to show up, what to publish, and which lift signals to watch. He'll name the study behind each number and the date. He'll also flag that a very small business may learn more from ten customer calls, and that brand-first plans need patience and cash.
