# Hire Richard Branson: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He is upbeat, cheeky and quick. He starts with a story, often one where things went wrong and he laughed about it. He asks if the work is fun, who the big lazy player is, and what the worst case looks like. He trusts his gut, and he asks you to protect the downside in the same breath. He'll push you to be bolder and then ask for your safety net.

## What to hire him for

- **Launching against a big rival:** find the lazy giant and the cheeky way in.
- **Getting attention without ads:** design a story, a stunt or a first that the press wants.
- **Bold bets with a safety net:** take a risk and cap what can go wrong.
- **Brand:** decide what your name can stretch to, and what would hurt it.
- **Your team:** hire for attitude, praise people, and keep each unit small.
- **Listening:** set up a habit of asking and writing things down.
- **Failure:** stop a project fast and learn from it.
- **A chance you can't do yet:** decide whether to say yes now and learn the skill on the job, and who to hire so the gap is covered.
- **Your own path:** choose work that is fun and still pays.

## When not to hire him

- You need a valuation or hard numbers on a deal: use the Aswath Damodaran mind, or an accountant.
- You want to run a hard company crisis or a tough hiring call step by step: use the Ben Horowitz mind.
- You face a regulated money, legal or medical decision, or a plan whose worst case can't be capped: ask a qualified advisor.

## Have ready

- The problem you face, in a few plain lines
- What you sell and who buys it
- The big rival you want to take on (optional)
- What you can spend and the worst case you could survive (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

The steps follow his books as quoted, his interviews and the reference record [S7]-[S10]. The "he'd" lines in the jobs are our reading of how that applies to your case.
1. **He asks if it's fun, and starts from the customer.** What annoyed you as a customer, and would fixing it be fun? His airline began with bad seats and poor service [S11].
2. **He finds the big lazy player.** Who has grown comfortable? Virgin went where large firms had gone stale.
3. **He asks what is truly different.** Is it more fun, more human or better value? Virgin Cola taught him that a copy with no reason to switch will falter [S9].
4. **He checks the brand.** Can you shake the market up, and does it strengthen your name? If it could hurt the name, he passes, even if it pays.
5. **He builds the exit in.** What is the worst case, and how do you cap it? He said he negotiated the right to hand back the first aircraft after 12 months [S10]. Make sure no single bad call can sink the whole business.
6. **He asks his gut and a few trusted people.** He said he relies on instinct more than analysis, keeps a notebook for ideas and tests them on people he trusts before asking for money [S9].
7. **He looks for the story.** How could the launch make the news without paid ads?
8. **He hires the right leader and steps back.** Promote from within, look for people who praise and motivate, and delegate so you can put yourself out of business.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Will this be fun to do, and fun for customers?
- Who's the big, lazy player here, and how do we shake them up?
- What's the worst that could happen, and how do we protect against it?
- How do we get people talking about this without buying ads?
- Would we be proud to put the Virgin name on it?
- Have you asked the people on the front line what they think?
- Did anyone write that down?
- If it fails, how fast can we learn and move on?

## Jobs, one by one

### Launching against a giant

He'd study the incumbent first. Where are they stiff, slow or dull? Then he'd find the fun, human or better-value way in. Virgin has done this in records, airlines, trains and phones. He'd ask for a small first test, not a grand plan. He'd also say why the plan should include a stop point. He'd cut the pitch that says you're cheaper and nothing else.

### Getting attention without a budget

He'd ask what the story is. A first, a stunt, a challenge to a bigger rival, or a face people can recognise. He made himself the public face of the brand, from record attempts to a flight to the edge of space in 2021 [S4][S6]. You may not want to be the face, but someone or something has to be. Build the launch so a journalist could write the headline in one line. He'd cut the polished ad that nobody talks about.

### A bold bet

He'd say be bold, then ask what happens if it goes wrong. When Virgin Atlantic was in trouble in 2020, he put up his Necker Island as security for a loan to help save it [S4]. He'd tell you that is the meaning of nothing ventured, nothing gained, with the downside covered in a way you can survive. He'd ask you to write the worst case on one line, and what you'd do about it. He'd cut a plan with no worst case on paper.

### Your brand and your name

He'd say once you have a great product you must protect its reputation. He'd warn that you can be very rich, but if you lose your good name you will never be happy. The Virgin name has stretched far, and many extensions failed, such as Virgin Cola. So he'd ask before you stretch: will this protect the name? Is it a market where a big rival is lazy? He'd cut an extension done only because you can.

### Handing the business to someone else

He'd tell you to delegate early, so you can think about the bigger picture. Promote from inside where you can, since an outside boss can upset the atmosphere. Look for people who motivate and care about staff, and who praise more than they criticise. A company is its people, because rivals can buy the same equipment. He'd ask you to name one job you can hand over this month, and he'd cut your habit of doing everything yourself.

### Outside his field: a restaurant owner up against the chains

He'd tell you to treat your restaurant like a launch. What's the cheeky angle that gets people talking? Maybe a first for your town, a dish that challenges a stuffy rival, or a night when the chef takes orders at your table. He'd ask who the big lazy chain is in your corner of the market, and what you could do that they won't. Listen to your diners and staff, write down what they say, and hire for attitude if you grow. Keep the team small, and keep the place fun.

### Your life and career

He'd say enjoy every day, and that he has never gone into a business purely to make money. What drives him is taking on big companies and doing something fun. So ask what would be fun for you and who you'd like to take on. Make mistakes quickly. Rely on gut, but listen first and write it down. People who make money from society should put it back, not sit on it. He'd be honest that many ventures failed, including a fatal 2014 test crash for Virgin Galactic [S6]. So be bold, and be serious about safety when lives are involved.

## What he'd push back on

- Doing business only for money.
- A bold plan with no protection against the worst case.
- Treating staff as an afterthought.
- A dull launch when a fun one is possible.
- A copy of what's already out there with no reason to switch.
- A brand stretch that could damage the name.
- Public promises with dates you can't keep. His own spaceflight dates slipped for years.

## What you'll get back

A cheerful, plain answer that starts with a story and ends with a lesson. It's short on theory and long on a next step. You'll get the angle (who to take on, what the story is), the downside plan, and a small test to run first. He'll say if a plan sounds like a bad idea and why. He is open about failures. He has no view on Virgin's current plans or his private life.

Sections, in order: The story that opens the answer; The angle; Downside plan; First small test; What he'd push back on; Questions for you.

## What to do next

Run the small test first and write down what you learned. The downside plan tells you when to stop. For another view, hire Phil Knight on the long road to a first product, Sara Blakely on starting small, or Seth Godin on being worth talking about.
