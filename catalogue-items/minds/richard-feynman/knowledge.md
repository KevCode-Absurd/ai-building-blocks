# Richard Feynman: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Feynman's books and talks are still in copyright, so this is our summary of his ideas. The few quotes are short and sourced. His popular memoirs were compiled with Ralph Leighton from stories he told, so treat their anecdotes as his own telling, not as checked history [S1].

## In one paragraph

Richard Feynman was an American physicist who helped build the atomic bomb as a young man, won a share of the 1965 Nobel Prize for his work on quantum electrodynamics, and became famous as a teacher and explainer [S1][S3]. He invented a way of drawing particle interactions, now called Feynman diagrams, that turned pages of hard math into pictures [S1]. Three ideas run through his public talks. Don't fool yourself, because you are the easiest person to fool [S4]. Test everything against nature, because nature can't be fooled [S5]. And if you can't explain something simply, you probably don't understand it yet [S2]. He put all three to work in 1986, when he showed the country why the space shuttle Challenger was lost [S1][S5].

## Core ideas

### The first rule: don't fool yourself

In his 1974 commencement talk at Caltech, Feynman said the key to real science is a kind of honesty that goes beyond not lying. You must work hard not to fool yourself, and he warned that you are the easiest person to fool [S4]. That means leaning over backwards: reporting everything that might make your result wrong, including other explanations and data that don't fit [S4]. It also means testing an idea against facts other than the ones that gave you the idea in the first place [S4].

How he applied it: he told the story of the charge of the electron. After Robert Millikan measured it, later results crept slowly toward the right value instead of jumping there. He thought researchers checked hard for errors when their numbers disagreed with Millikan's, and checked less when they agreed [S4].

### Cargo cult science

Feynman described islanders who, after a war, built bamboo runways and control towers and waited for planes that never came. They had copied the form of an airport without the thing that made it work [S4]. He said some research does the same. It follows the outward steps of science but lacks the integrity that makes science work [S4].

How he applied it: he praised a 1937 experiment by a researcher named Young, who kept removing clues from a maze until he was sure rats were finding food for the reason he thought. Young found the rats could hear the floor, so he laid sand to remove even that clue. Feynman complained that later researchers ignored this careful work [S4].

### Nature can't be fooled

On the commission that investigated the Challenger accident, Feynman found a huge gap in how people rated the shuttle's risk. Working engineers put the chance of losing a shuttle at about 1 in 100. Management put it at about 1 in 100,000 [S5]. He thought management's figure was far too hopeful, perhaps to protect funding [S5]. His appendix to the report ends with the warning that reality must come before public relations, because nature cannot be fooled [S5].

How he applied it: at a televised hearing he squeezed a piece of the O-ring rubber in a clamp and dipped it in ice water, showing that it lost its springiness in the cold [S1][S2]. The commission concluded the seal had failed in unusually cold weather [S1].

### A warning sign is a clue, not a margin

Before the accident, officials had seen the O-rings partly burn through on earlier flights. Because those flights came back safely, they treated the damage as acceptable [S5]. Feynman argued the opposite. The damage meant the design was not working as intended, and past luck said nothing about the next flight [S5]. Officials also said that rings burned a third of the way through had a "safety factor of three." He said this misused the term, since nobody understood the burning well enough to predict it [S5].

### Build from the bottom up

Feynman compared two ways of designing an engine [S5]. In the bottom-up way, used for most aircraft engines, you test the materials and parts first, find problems early and fix them cheaply. The shuttle's main engine was built top-down: the whole engine was put together before its parts were well understood. Every crack or fault then became slow and costly to find and fix [S5]. He also noted that the rules for certifying the engine had been loosened over time [S5].

How he applied it: he praised the shuttle's flight software, which had been tested carefully and checked by an independent group, as an example of good practice [S5].

### If you can't explain it simply, you don't understand it

He was known as the "Great Explainer" [S2]. He held that a topic you can't explain in a first-year lecture is a topic you don't yet fully understand [S2]. He disliked learning by rote and stressed clear thinking [S1]. He argued against rote learning at an education conference in Brazil [S2], and criticised California school textbooks, including the "New Math" use of set notation [S1].

How he applied it: his lectures to Caltech undergraduates in the early 1960s were edited into *The Feynman Lectures on Physics* [S1]. They sold well for decades, though they were never widely adopted as textbooks [S1]. He won the Oersted Medal for teaching [S2].

### Draw it

Feynman diagrams are simple sketches that track how particles interact. They let physicists see and organise calculations that had been very hard to handle [S1][S3]. He developed this approach at Cornell in the late 1940s. Some physicists were sceptical at first [S1].

### Ask what physics allows

In a 1959 talk, "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom," Feynman asked how small we could make things. He worked out that the whole *Encyclopaedia Britannica* could fit on the head of a pin, and argued that nothing in the laws of physics forbids arranging atoms one by one [S6]. The barrier was engineering, not natural law [S6]. He offered two $1,000 prizes: one for shrinking a page of text 25,000 times so an electron microscope could read it, and one for a working electric motor no bigger than 1/64 of an inch on each side [S6].

### Tell the public the truth

Feynman thought scientists owe the public an honest account of their work, even when that makes funding harder [S4]. He criticised publishing only the results that look good, and giving government advice that only supports what officials already want [S4]. His Challenger appendix made the same point for NASA: be realistic about weaknesses, cost and schedule, and be honest with the public, even if it costs support [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask what the evidence says, and look hardest for evidence against your idea [S4].
- Report everything that could make your result wrong [S4].
- Test the idea on facts other than the ones that suggested it [S4].
- Repeat the experiment before you build on it [S4].
- Talk to the people doing the work, not only their managers [S5].
- Treat surprises and small failures as clues [S5].
- Test the parts before you trust the whole [S5].
- Explain it to a beginner. If you can't, go back and understand it better [S2].
- Check what nature actually forbids before you call something impossible [S6].

## Where he stood

**Is looking scientific the same as being scientific?** No. Copying the steps without the honesty is cargo cult science [S4].

**Should past success make us feel safe?** No. Getting away with a flaw doesn't fix it [S5].

**Who should we believe on risk?** Look at the data and ask the engineers. In the shuttle case, their estimate was far closer to reality than management's [S5].

**What should students learn?** How to think and understand, not how to memorise [S1][S2].

**Is it worth thinking about things that seem impossible?** Yes, if the laws of physics allow them. He thought very small machines were one such case [S6].

## His work and record

- Born 11 May 1918 in Queens, New York City [S1].
- Graduated from MIT in 1939 and earned a PhD at Princeton in 1942 under John Wheeler [S1].
- Worked on the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos. Hans Bethe made him a group leader in the theoretical division at 24. He helped plan safe storage of nuclear material at Oak Ridge and was present at the Trinity test [S3].
- Taught at Cornell from 1945 to 1949, where he developed his diagram approach to quantum electrodynamics [S1].
- Chose Caltech over the Institute for Advanced Study, partly because he wanted to teach [S2].
- Gave "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom" on 29 December 1959 at a meeting of the American Physical Society at Caltech [S6].
- His undergraduate lectures of 1961 to 1964 became *The Feynman Lectures on Physics*, edited by Robert Leighton and Matthew Sands [S1].
- Shared the 1965 Nobel Prize in Physics with Julian Schwinger and Shin'ichirō Tomonaga [S1][S3].
- Wrote for general readers, including *The Character of Physical Law* (1965) and *QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter* (1985) [S1].
- Gave the "Cargo Cult Science" commencement talk at Caltech in 1974 [S4].
- Served on the Rogers Commission on the Challenger disaster in 1986. The commission at first resisted including his report, which became Appendix F [S1][S2].
- Died 15 February 1988 [S1][S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Self-made legend.** Murray Gell-Mann complained that Feynman spent a lot of effort generating stories about himself. Gell-Mann also objected to Feynman's account of their joint work on the weak force, which led to a correction in later editions of a Feynman book [S1].
- **Treatment of women.** He has been criticised for a chapter in his memoir about how he treated women at a bar in 1946, and for the language he used about them [S1].
- **Teaching for beginners.** Accounts of his famous lectures vary. Colleagues said many first-year students found them too hard, while older students and staff loved them [S1].
- **Not every idea worked.** His work on quantum gravity went little past known results, he did not solve superconductivity, and Gell-Mann dismissed his parton model as unclear [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- He clashed more than once with the Challenger commission's chairman [S1]. His outsider style found the truth there, but it can make teamwork harder.
- His stories about himself are entertaining and not always reliable. Don't treat a Feynman anecdote as a fact [S1].
- "Explain it simply" can be misused to dismiss ideas that are truly hard. Use simplicity as a test of understanding. Hard ideas can still be true.
- He was a physicist. In fields full of people, like management or medicine, evidence is messier. Pair him with someone who studies human judgment.
- He spoke for his era on women and science. Leave that out of modern advice.
