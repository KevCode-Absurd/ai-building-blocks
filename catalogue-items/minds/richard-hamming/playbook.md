# Hire Richard Hamming: how he'd work for you

Hamming helps you question whether your learning and working time is aimed at a problem worth solving. Bring a list of current commitments and one ambitious question with a possible opening. His technical lectures combine problem choice, preparation, communication and reflections from research life. A useful application should sharpen judgment about where to work, while resisting the implication that a significant life must imitate an exceptional research career. [S1] [S2] The examples below apply the public method to your situation; they are new exercises, not claims of personal consultation.

## What to hire him for

- **Choose a problem worth a learning project:** You receive an importance-based project comparison.
- **Find an attackable opening:** You receive a bounded experiment.
- **Keep important questions available:** You receive a small live problem list.
- **Reframe a resource constraint:** You receive a constraint-to-options note.
- **Balance focus with useful conversations:** You receive a focus-and-conversation rhythm.
- **Make a useful result understandable:** You receive a tested explanation.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **List problems and responsibilities:** Separate necessary commitments from discretionary projects. State why each ambitious question matters and who could use a good answer rather than rely on prestige alone.
2. **Choose an important opening:** Select a question with a plausible first attack. Identify the knowledge and resources the experiment requires, keeping ambition and tractability in the same decision.
3. **Investigate the constraint:** Ask whether the missing resource suggests another design or is a genuine blocker. Test a responsible alternative without romanticizing deprivation or concealing a need to reduce scope.
4. **Protect concentrated investigation:** Give the selected problem sustained attention and inspect what the first attempt reveals. Keep other important questions available without pretending that listing them constitutes progress.
5. **Invite relevant connections:** Arrange purposeful exposure to people and ideas outside the current route. Use new information to revisit the live problem rather than allow conversation to become an avoidance routine.
6. **Communicate and evaluate use:** Explain the problem, method, evidence and limitations to the intended reader. Test whether the result can be understood or used, and let that reveal the next technical or communication gap.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Why does this problem deserve attention?
- What is the first informative attack?
- Which unresolved question does this idea touch?
- Can another design supply the needed function?
- Where will a relevant unfamiliar idea enter?
- Who can now use or evaluate the work?

## Jobs, one by one

### Choose a problem worth a learning project

List your main discretionary projects. For each write the consequence of a good answer, the person who could use it and why the question remains unresolved. Select one whose value you can explain without relying on a fashionable label. Keep necessary operational work visible rather than pretending it can all disappear. Identify what learning the selected problem genuinely requires.

**You get:** an importance-based project comparison.

**The pushback:** Do not use significance as a license to dismiss routine or care work. A person’s responsibilities can be essential even when they do not resemble a celebrated scientific breakthrough.

### Find an attackable opening

You want to reduce a recurring reporting delay. Map the process and identify one repeated step you can inspect. Gather a small sample, propose a change and predict what should happen. Build a limited prototype or test before declaring a transformation project. If the opening requires knowledge you lack, make that learning explicit and decide whether obtaining it is worthwhile.

**You get:** a bounded experiment.

**The pushback:** Do not confuse a small first experiment with a claim that the entire problem is easy. Its purpose is to reveal an opening and constraints, not promise the final result in miniature.

### Keep important questions available

Maintain a short page of unresolved questions in your field or work. Beside each note what makes it difficult and what kind of information might open it. When reading or talking with colleagues, revisit the page and record relevant connections. Choose one connection to investigate rather than collecting every interesting reference. Retire questions that no longer matter or belong elsewhere.

**You get:** a small live problem list.

**The pushback:** Do not treat maintaining a list as progress on every problem it contains. The list is preparation for selecting an attack; actual investigation still requires focused work and feedback.

### Reframe a resource constraint

Your team cannot add another person to prepare a recurring report. Separate the information users need from the manual steps currently producing it. Ask whether a smaller report, a clearer process or limited automation could serve the same purpose. Prototype one change and inspect errors and maintenance costs. If no responsible alternative exists, state the resource gap rather than romanticize it.

**You get:** a constraint-to-options note.

**The pushback:** Do not generalize Hamming’s anecdote into the claim that all resource shortages are beneficial. Some constraints are genuine blockers and should be escalated or the project reduced.

### Balance focus with useful conversations

Protect a focused block for the selected problem. Arrange a regular short conversation with someone who knows a different part of the work. Bring a concrete unresolved question rather than a broad networking agenda. Record any new connection and inspect it during the next work block. Adjust the balance if meetings become avoidance or solitude leaves you repeating a dead end.

**You get:** a focus-and-conversation rhythm.

**The pushback:** Do not turn an open door into constant interruption or a closed door into an identity. The practical issue is whether the arrangement helps this problem progress.

### Make a useful result understandable

Write a one-page account of your result for the intended user. State the problem, the change, evidence that it works and conditions where it may fail. Ask the reader to explain what they would do next. Revise where they misunderstand. If the result is technical, provide enough supporting detail for qualified review while keeping the main decision legible.

**You get:** a tested explanation.

**The pushback:** Do not replace substance with performance of importance. A compelling pitch that hides uncertainty may attract attention while making the work less dependable for the people expected to use it.

## What he'd push back on

The useful tension is ambition with an attack. An important question without a plausible approach can remain a badge of seriousness rather than work; a tractable task with no importance can consume a career. Hamming’s advice joins the two. Another tension lies between concentration and openness. Protecting every minute can also cut off the ideas that make an important connection possible. His emphasis on commitment needs a modern boundary: health, care responsibilities and ethical obligations are not evidence that a person deserves to accomplish less. Retain the challenge to choose deliberately without importing every value judgment from the research anecdotes. [S1] [S2]

His advice comes from an unusually resourced research career and often values exceptional achievement over balance. It cannot justify burnout or the belief that essential maintenance and care work have no worth. His anecdotes are observations, not controlled experiments.

## What you'll get back

Return a problem portfolio with importance, current tractability, required learning and the next experiment written beside each item. Select one primary question and preserve a few others for later connections. For a career review, distinguish necessary maintenance from discretionary work whose purpose has become unclear. Include a communication artifact for the selected result: who needs it, what they need to understand and how it will be tested in use. The portfolio should help allocate attention, not rank the worth of people whose work has different aims.
