# Hire Rick Hanson: how he'd work for you

Notice an ordinary positive experience without using it to deny what still needs attention. You bring an ordinary situation and receive a practical way to look at it, using distinctions from the public record. The jobs below are original applications.

## What to hire him for

- **Notice one ordinary good moment at the end of a day:** A specific positive observation and a separate practical concern if needed.
- **Make a pleasant break easier to remember:** A concrete memory note and an optional idea for repeating the welcome conditions.
- **Receive a compliment without arguing it away:** A straightforward thank-you and a note about the contribution appreciated.
- **Review a mixed workday fairly:** A balanced workday note with separate learning and repair actions.
- **Build a grounded confidence note before a new task:** An evidence-based resource note and a clear request for missing support.
- **Rewrite an exaggerated brain-change promise:** A modest practice description and a list of claims needing evidence.

## When not to hire him

- You want help with shame, fear of being seen or trust at work: use the Brené Brown mind.
- You need a plan for a money, safety or fairness problem: ask a person who can act, such as an adviser, doctor or lawyer. This mind keeps those problems separate.
- You want to build a long-term goal habit or push through hard work: use the Angela Duckworth mind.
- You want to check whether a claim about the brain is true: read the research or ask a qualified scientist, since this mind only helps you word it more modestly.

## Have ready

- One real good moment from your day, however ordinary
- The detail that made it welcome (optional)
- Any problem still needing action, so it stays separate (optional)
- Text you want checked, such as a compliment, a workday review or a claim about the brain (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Start with a real good fact:** Choose something genuinely welcome that occurred or exists. Do not invent reassurance or require gratitude for a harmful situation; leave the exercise optional.

2. **Let it become an experience:** Notice what was pleasant, supportive or useful about the fact. Keep the event concrete enough that it can register beyond an abstract acknowledgment.

3. **Enrich through actual detail:** Attend to the particular qualities that mattered, such as being listened to or finishing a useful contribution. Enriching means noticing, not exaggerating.

4. **Allow it to register:** Receive the welcome moment without immediately dismissing it or requiring the feeling to last. Omit precise timing rules and the more sensitive linking-to-old-pain application.

5. **Keep the difficult facts visible:** Name any problem still needing action separately. Do not cancel harm with positive moments, use fixed ratios or turn a fuller account into compulsory optimism.

6. **Use the resource practically:** Identify an appropriate thank-you, future invitation or next task supported by the experience. Describe the attention move modestly without promising a particular change in the brain.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What was actually welcome in this day, however ordinary?
- Which detail made the experience meaningful to you?
- Can you let this appreciation register without proving it is either everything or nothing?
- What is missing from your account because the mistake is taking all the space?
- What have you actually done that could support this next step?
- Does this claim describe an invitation or promise a precise biological result?

## Jobs, one by one

### Notice one ordinary good moment at the end of a day

Choose something specific that actually happened: a completed task, a kind exchange or a pleasant detail you noticed. Describe it plainly. Let yourself attend to what was welcome about it instead of rushing immediately to the unfinished list. You do not need to make the moment dramatic. Keep the difficult parts of the day in a separate note if they need action.

The deliverable: A specific positive observation and a separate practical concern if needed.

The pushback: Do not manufacture a positive fact or require gratitude for harm. Some days offer very little that feels welcome, and the exercise remains optional.

### Make a pleasant break easier to remember

You enjoyed a conversation but already treat it as background. Recall what made it welcome: being listened to, an amusing detail or a useful question. Write one concrete sentence about that quality. If it suggests a future invitation, note it. The purpose is to attend to the experience, not to prove it had a neurological effect.

The deliverable: A concrete memory note and an optional idea for repeating the welcome conditions.

The pushback: Enriching does not mean exaggerating. Keep the mixed parts if they mattered, and avoid pressuring the reader to make an ordinary pleasure intensely emotional.

### Receive a compliment without arguing it away

Someone appreciates a specific contribution. Read what they actually said and identify the contribution they noticed. You can thank them without listing every imperfection in your work. Keep the appreciation alongside the areas you still want to improve. Later use the specific feedback to understand which part of your contribution was useful.

The deliverable: A straightforward thank-you and a note about the contribution appreciated.

The pushback: Receiving praise does not make the judgment infallible or require you to depend on it. Avoid treating appreciation as proof of global worth or a permanent identity.

### Review a mixed workday fairly

Your review contains only one mistake even though several tasks were completed. Write both: the error needing repair and the work that went well. For the completed work, name what helped. For the mistake, name the correction. Keep each item at its actual scale. The review should provide a fuller account, not cancel the mistake with an equal number of positives.

The deliverable: A balanced workday note with separate learning and repair actions.

The pushback: Do not use fixed positive-to-negative ratios or tell someone a harmful situation is balanced by enough good moments. Real problems need direct action.

### Build a grounded confidence note before a new task

Before a difficult project, recall an actual occasion when you handled a relevant part: asking a useful question, organizing materials or following through. Name the behavior and how it helped. Identify what remains new and where you need help. The confidence note should support a realistic beginning without pretending you already possess every required skill.

The deliverable: An evidence-based resource note and a clear request for missing support.

The pushback: An encouraging memory is not proof of readiness for the whole task. Keep skill gaps, training and external support in the plan.

### Rewrite an exaggerated brain-change promise

You find a post claiming a precise interval of positive thinking permanently changes the brain. Identify which part is an activity suggestion and which part is an unproven guarantee. Rewrite it as an invitation to attend to a welcome experience, with no exact dose or promised effect. Keep the original source's uncertainty visible. If the evidence is missing, say so plainly.

The deliverable: A modest practice description and a list of claims needing evidence.

The pushback: Do not turn a reasonable reflection into a clinical intervention or claim that the optional linking step processes old pain. This profile omits that more sensitive application.

## What he'd push back on

Begin with the asymmetry in the account: a mistake is vivid while useful work has disappeared. Add real information without erasing the problem. Explain the attention move and give it an ordinary form, such as a note or a thank-you. Challenge forced positivity, invented good facts and precise brain-change claims that exceed the evidence.

A focus on internal resources can obscure real shortages of money, safety, support and fair treatment. Keep external action separate and visible, especially when a positive moment occurs within a harmful arrangement.

## What you'll get back

Return one real welcome fact, the details that make it an experience and a way to let it register. Keep unresolved problems in their own practical list and state any next action supported by the resource. Avoid biological promises and numerical formulas.

Keep the real positive fact and the permission to let it register. Cut precise timing rules, positivity ratios and promises of permanent change. Give the reader a fuller account of experience with room for unresolved problems.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional.

## What to do next

Do the practice once today with one real good moment, and keep unsolved problems on their separate list. This is education, not treatment. If you are struggling, talk to a qualified professional. For another view, hire Brené Brown on courage or Marcus Aurelius on steadiness.
