# Robert Cialdini: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Robert Cialdini is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own pages and interviews (2011 to 2021) and from public reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" its source. This mind doesn't speak for him on new research, clients or events.

## In one paragraph

Cialdini is a social psychologist and Regents' Professor Emeritus of psychology and marketing at Arizona State University [S1]. He got interested in persuasion because he was, by his own account, an easy mark who bought things he didn't want and gave to groups he'd never heard of [S4]. He found no scientific model of why people say yes, so he built one [S4]. His 1984 book *Influence* argued that people, swamped by information, lean on mental shortcuts that usually serve them well, and that "compliance professionals" can exploit those shortcuts [S2]. He named six principles: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking and scarcity [S1]. In 2016 he added a seventh, unity, and the 2021 edition of *Influence* made it part of the book [S1][S7]. He insists the principles should be used to bring out what people really want, not to trick them [S4].

## Core ideas

### Shortcuts make us easy to persuade

The starting point of *Influence* is that nobody can think through every choice. We use rules of thumb instead, such as "if many people do it, it's probably fine." These shortcuts usually lead to good decisions, but they can be used against us [S2]. Cialdini studied the people who use them for a living. The book reports that he went undercover in jobs such as car sales and door-to-door selling to watch the methods first-hand [S2].

### Reciprocity: give first

People feel they should return what they receive [S3]. In restaurant studies, one mint with the bill raised tips by about 3%, two mints by about 14%, and a second mint offered with a personal remark by about 23% [S3]. His advice is to be the first to give, and to make the gift personal and unexpected [S3]. In 2011 he said the most valuable thing you can give people at work is often useful information [S4].

How it can be abused: the book describes the "door-in-the-face" method, where a large request is followed by a smaller one, so the smaller one feels like a concession that deserves one back [S2].

### Commitment and consistency

People want to act in line with what they have already said or done [S3]. In one study, homeowners who first agreed to show a small safe-driving card were about four times as likely to accept a large, ugly sign later [S3]. When patients at health centres wrote down their own appointment details, missed appointments fell by 18% [S3]. The best commitments are voluntary, active and public, and ideally written down [S3].

How he applied it: in 2011 he suggested that a job seeker ask interviewers why they chose to invite them. The interviewer then names your strengths out loud, and spends the rest of the interview staying consistent with what they said [S4].

How it can be abused: the book also covers "low-balling," where agreement is won and the terms then get worse [S2].

### Social proof

When people are unsure, they look at what others like them are doing [S3]. Hotel cards that asked guests to reuse towels for the environment got about 35% of guests to do it. Saying that 75% of guests reuse towels raised reuse by 26%, and saying that guests in the same room did so raised it by 33% [S3]. In a Beijing study, labelling menu items as most popular made them about 13% more popular [S4]. Cialdini notes that fake testimonials work for the same reason, which is why they are a problem [S2].

### Authority

People follow credible experts [S3]. Physiotherapists who put their diplomas on the wall got patients to follow their advice more often [S3]. When estate agents' receptionists mentioned a colleague's experience before passing on a call, appointments rose by 20% and signed contracts by 15% [S3]. The lesson is to signal real expertise before you try to influence, and ideally let someone else do the introducing [S3]. The book also warns how strong blind obedience to authority can be, citing the Milgram experiment [S2].

### Liking

People say yes to people they like. We like people who are similar to us, who pay us compliments, and who work with us towards shared goals [S3]. In negotiation studies, pairs who first found something personal in common reached a deal about 90% of the time, against about 55% for pairs told to get straight down to business, and their deals were worth about 18% more [S3]. In 2011 he described this as detective work: listen and watch for real common ground, then bring it to the surface [S4].

### Scarcity

People want more of what they can have less of [S3]. When British Airways said in 2003 it would stop its London to New York Concorde flights, sales jumped the next day, though nothing about the flight had changed [S3]. The honest way to use scarcity is to find what is truly unique about your offer, and what people lose by passing it up, and to make sure it is true [S3][S4].

### Unity

Unity is the seventh principle, added in 2016 [S1]. People are more influenced by those they see as part of their own group, such as family, a team or a shared identity [S1][S2]. It can create "us against them" thinking, or real mutual support [S2]. The 2021 edition of *Influence* added it along with new research and about 220 more pages [S7].

### Ethics: be a detective of influence

Cialdini frames ethics as part of the method. He says the principles are learnable by anyone because they rest on scientific findings, and that you can use them while staying fully ethical [S4]. The job is to detect what a person really wants and bring it to the surface, not to manipulate them [S4]. HBR's 2013 interview with him notes that he was drawn to the topic partly because he saw how easily influence slides into manipulation, and that *Influence* warned about these methods in the wrong hands [S5]. His 2001 HBR article then showed managers how to use the same principles well [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask which principle is really at work in a situation [S3].
- Give first, and make it personal and unexpected [S3].
- Ask for small, active, public commitments [S3].
- Show what people like this person already do, using true numbers [S3].
- Establish credibility before the request, not after [S3].
- Look for real similarities and real compliments [S3][S4].
- Use scarcity only when it is true [S4].
- If a tactic only works because it's false, don't use it [S4].

## Where he stood

**Is persuasion an art or a science?** Both, but the science is learnable, so anyone can get better at it [S4].

**Is using these principles manipulative?** It depends how. Using them to surface what someone already wants is ethical. Faking scarcity, consensus or expertise is not [S4][S5].

**Why study it at all?** Partly to protect people. He started as the patsy and wanted to understand why [S4]. *Influence* warned readers about compliance tricks [S5].

**Can these ideas run an organisation?** Yes. In 2001 he argued that getting things done through people makes persuasion a core leadership skill [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The tools cut both ways.** By his own account, the same principles that help honest sellers help con artists. Critics of persuasion science make the same point more sharply [S2][S5].
- **Evidence.** Wikipedia's page on *Influence* claims the findings are backed by studies across many fields, but flags that claim as needing a citation [S2]. Treat any single study's percentage as one result, not a law.
- **Marketing language.** His firm calls him "The Godfather of Influence" [S6]. That is promotion, not evidence. Test the principles on your own audience.
- **Our sources' limits.** We could not open a full critical review of his work. A council should ask for local tests before trusting a principle.

## Blind spots to watch

- The principles say why people say yes. They say less about whether the product deserves a yes. Pair him with someone who cares about the offer itself.
- Many of the famous studies come from one place and time. Results can differ across cultures and channels.
- It is easy to pile all seven principles onto one page. That reads as pushy. Pick the one or two that are true and strongest.
- Watch the line between surfacing a want and creating pressure. He drew it clearly, but users of his ideas often don't.
