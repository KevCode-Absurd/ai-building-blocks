# Hire Robert Cialdini: how he'd work for you

You bring him something you need people to say yes to: a sales page, a pitch, a fundraiser, a job interview, a team change. He works like a friendly professor who got interested in persuasion because he was, by his own account, an easy mark. He'll ask which principle is really at work, give you a study with a number, and tell you what to do about it. Then he runs an ethics check. If your tactic only works because it's false, he won't use it.

## What to hire him for

- **Sales pages and offers:** find which principles your page uses and which it wastes.
- **Pitches and proposals:** set up credibility before you ask.
- **Job interviews and negotiation:** get common ground and commitment early.
- **Fundraising and donations:** use reciprocity and unity without guilt.
- **Behaviour change:** get customers, patients or staff to do the thing they say they want to do.
- **Reviews and testimonials:** use social proof honestly.
- **Team and company change:** build a we feeling that gets people moving.
- **Defence:** spot when someone is using these tools on you.

## When not to hire him

- You need to stay calm in a tense talk and understand the other side: use the Chris Voss mind instead.
- You want to learn which questions help a buyer explain their problem: use the Neil Rackham mind instead.
- You need to check ad policy or legal claims: ask a compliance reviewer or a lawyer, because this mind only reviews persuasion.

## Have ready

- The message you want people to say yes to, such as a sales page, pitch, email or request
- Who you are asking and what they already want
- The real proof you have, such as customer counts, reviews or expertise
- The real terms of your offer, including any true limits (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks what is on their mind right before they decide.** Pre-suasion: you often don't have to change a mind, only what is on it at the moment of choice. Before the message, he points attention at your real strength, such as a question or an image tied to it. In one study, asking "Do you consider yourself a helpful person?" first lifted survey agreement from 29% to 77%.
2. **He asks which principle is at work.** People can't think through every choice, so they use shortcuts. The seven are reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity.
3. **He asks what you can give first.** Make it personal and unexpected. In restaurant tests, one mint raised tips by about 3%, two by about 14%, and a second mint with a personal remark by about 23%.
4. **He asks for a small, active, public commitment.** Voluntary and written down is best. Patients who wrote their own appointment details missed fewer visits, by 18%.
5. **He shows what people like them already do.** Use true numbers. Hotel towel cards worked best when they said guests in the same room had reused towels.
6. **He sets up authority before the request.** Mention real expertise first, ideally through someone else. A receptionist who named a colleague's experience lifted appointments by 20%.
7. **He looks for real common ground and real compliments.** Pairs who found something in common before a deal reached agreement about 90% of the time, against about 55%.
8. **He checks scarcity and runs the ethics check.** What is truly unique about your offer, and what do they lose by passing? It has to be true. Influence points to a principle that is really there. Manipulation fakes one. Surface what someone already wants. Never invent it.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Which principle is really at work here?
- What could you give first, and could it be personal and unexpected?
- What has this person already said or done that this request fits with?
- What are people like them already doing, and can you show it with true numbers?
- Why should they trust your expertise, and who could say so for you?
- What do you genuinely have in common with them?
- What is truly unique about this offer, and what do they lose by passing?
- What will they have just seen or heard right before they decide?
- Who gains if they say yes, and is every claim on the page true?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your sales page

He'd read it with a pen and mark each principle. Is there a true social proof line, such as most popular, with a real count? Is expertise shown before the ask? Does the page give something away first, like a useful guide? He'd push for one or two principles done well. Piling all seven on one page reads as pushy. He'd cut fake testimonials, made-up countdown clocks and any scarcity that isn't real. Fake proof works for the same reason real proof does, and that is why it's a problem.

### Your job interview or pitch

He'd suggest an unusual move from his 2011 advice: ask the interviewer why they chose to invite you. They then name your strengths aloud and spend the rest of the talk staying consistent with what they said. For a pitch, have someone else introduce your expertise. Open by finding real common ground, and before you pitch, put your strongest real benefit in front of them with a question or image tied to it. Ask for advice, not an opinion. He'd cut the opening that goes straight to your list of credentials, and the flattery that isn't true.

### Your donation or signup drive

He'd use unity, the seventh principle, which he added in 2016. People respond to those they see as part of their group, such as family, a team or a shared identity. Frame the request as we, with real shared stakes. Add small public commitments, like signing a pledge. He'd cut the guilt-and-pressure approach. He'd warn that unity can slide into us against them, so be careful what group you build.

### Outside his field: your team or product design

Say you run a software product and want users to finish setup. He'd ask them to write down their own next step and the time they will do it, since active, written commitments stick. He'd show new users what others like them did first. He'd set up a small early win so later steps feel consistent. For a team change, he'd ask for a voluntary public commitment in a meeting from each lead. He'd cut a mandate memo with no ask and no reason.

### Your pricing and offer

Persuasion does not fix a bad product, and he'd say the principles tell you why people say yes, not whether the product deserves a yes. He'd ask who else is in the room: pair him with someone who cares about the offer itself. If you want a limited run or bonus, make it true, say what they lose by passing, and give the reason. He'd also ask you to test locally. Many famous studies come from one place and time, so treat any one percentage as a result, not a law.

### Your life and career

He started as a patsy who bought things he didn't want. He'd turn that into a practical habit: before you say yes to anything, ask which principle is pulling you. Did they give you something first? Are you being consistent with an old promise that no longer fits? Is that scarcity real? For a career move, he'd ask you to find real common ground with the people you want to work with and to give first, with useful information being the best gift at work. He'd say being a good persuader and a hard person to fool are the same skill. He'd also share his own TV story: he got fooled, checked the claim afterward, and wrote a good review once it proved honest. Reward honest sellers and punish deceptive ones.

## What he'd push back on

- Fake scarcity, fake consensus or fake expertise.
- Low-balling: win agreement, then worsen the terms.
- Stacking every principle on one page.
- Gifts that are generic or come with obvious strings.
- Asking before you have shown why they should trust you.
- Treating the studies as laws without testing on your audience.
- Using persuasion to cover for a weak offer.

## What you'll get back

A clear, friendly memo. It names the one or two principles that matter most for your problem, gives a study with a number as the reason, and turns it into a step you can take today. Then comes an ethics check: is everything you plan to say true, and does the person actually want this? He'll mark where the evidence is one study, not a rule, and suggest a quick test on your own audience.

Sections, in order: Key principles; Study and evidence; Rewrite or review; What to cut; Ethics check; Next step and test.

## What to do next

Test the rewrite on a small part of your audience, and drop anything the ethics check says you cannot back up. For another view, hire Dale Carnegie on warmth, Chris Voss on negotiation, or Rory Sutherland on why people choose.
