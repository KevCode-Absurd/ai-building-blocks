## In one paragraph

McKee is an American writing teacher, born in Detroit on 30 January 1941 [S1]. He studied English and theatre at the University of Michigan, acted on Broadway as a young man, and worked in Los Angeles as a story analyst and TV writer from 1979 [S1]. In 1983, as a Fulbright Scholar at the University of Southern California, he began his Story seminar, opening it to the public a year later [S1]. Tens of thousands of people have since taken it, from screenwriters to novelists to business leaders [S1][S3]. His books are Story (1997), Dialogue (2016), Storynomics (2018, with Tom Gerace), Character (2021) and Action (2022) [S1]. He teaches principles, not formulas [S4]. His core claims are that talent is not enough and a story must be well told [S4], that character shows itself only through choices under pressure [S5], that to speak is to act [S6], and that narration should enrich a story that is already dramatised, not replace it [S2].

## Core ideas

### Principles, not formulas

McKee presents Story as a set of guiding principles rather than rigid rules [S4]. He wants writers to rediscover the basic tenets of the art and to stop recycling Hollywood formulas [S4]. The aim is original work that holds audiences around the world for decades [S4]. Talent alone isn't enough; the story has to be well told [S4][S8].

How he applied it: his course moves from basic principles to advanced ones, and is meant for writers of page, stage and screen, including journalists and nonfiction writers [S4].

### Structure is built from units that turn

His course breaks structure into beats, scenes, sequences, acts and whole movements [S8]. Design, in his teaching, means progressive complications, set-ups and pay-offs, turning points, and above all the nature of choice [S8]. Each unit should move the story, and the turns should come from what characters decide.

How he applied it: his Story course ends with a scene-by-scene screening and analysis of Casablanca [S8].

### Character is revealed under pressure

In Character (2021), McKee argues that people's true natures hide behind their surface traits [S5]. True character comes out only through the choices a person makes under pressure [S5]. Major characters are different at heart from how they first appear, and the writer reveals that inner nature through the design of events and of the cast [S5].

How he applied it: the book moves from the main character's dimensions, complexity and arc to the major characters around them, then to the supporting roles [S5]. His course also treats the old debate of structure versus character as one subject, along with character dimensions and cast design [S8].

### Dialogue is action

In Dialogue (2016), McKee's position is that to say something is to do something [S6]. Every line carries a hidden desire and intent; characters talk to meet a need and pursue a purpose, and speech is the tactic they use [S6].

How he applied it: the book studies scenes from Macbeth to Breaking Bad to show speech that works on the page, stage and screen [S6].

### Show the story; let narration add, not replace

In his 2012 essay, McKee split voice-over into two kinds [S2]. "Telling" narration takes the place of drama: a character recites the story while the pictures illustrate the words [S2]. "Counterpoint" narration enriches a film whose story is already fully dramatised on screen [S2]. His test: strip all the narration out. If the story is still beautifully told, the narration may be adding something. If the film falls apart without it, the narration is doing work the scenes should do [S2].

How he applied it: he judged that the voice-over in Oliver Stone's Savages was telling narration that recited unsurprising life histories, and that the film would collapse without it [S2]. In Beasts of the Southern Wild, by contrast, a girl's narration set grace against the story's brutality, while the images alone told the tale [S2].

### Meaning: premise, controlling idea, counter idea

McKee teaches that a story expresses its truth through its premise, a controlling idea, and a counter idea that argues back [S8]. A story's energy comes from its substance, and he urges writers to work from the inside out [S8]. His tagline for Story is to write the truth [S4].

He also treats a premise as something to test before committing to a full draft. His method moves from inspiration to finished draft through step outlines and pitches, checking whether the idea can grow into a full story that holds an audience from start to end [S8].

### Setting and genre are choices, not wallpaper

His course teaches setting, the world of the story, as a way to avoid clichés [S8]. It treats genre as a set of conventions of setting, role, event and value, with both limits and inspirations [S8].

### Story persuades better than data

In Storynomics (2018), McKee and Tom Gerace argue that interruptive advertising is losing its power, and that brands should move from an ad-centred to a story-centred approach [S7]. Story matches how the mind takes in and organises reality; data alone doesn't persuade unless it sits inside a story with a point of view and values [S7]. In the book's pitch, data says what is happening and story says how and why [S7]. The page asks marketers to think in layers, not in straight lines of numbers, and to find the protagonist's point of view and values [S7]. In the 2003 conversation he said stories move people more deeply than argument, and that an executive can reach listeners better by dropping the slide deck and telling a good story [S10]. His definition of a story: it expresses how and why life changes [S10].

### Discipline and courage

His site describes his teaching as asking writers to write daily, line by line and page by page, even when afraid [S3]. They must risk rejection, ridicule and failure, study the craft carefully, and then write boldly [S3].

## How he decides

- Test the premise before writing the draft [S8].
- Outline step by step, scene by scene [S8].
- Make each scene turn on a choice [S8].
- Put the character under pressure and see what they choose [S5].
- Treat every line of dialogue as an action [S6].
- Cut the narration; see if the story still stands [S2].
- Prefer an original story to a recycled formula [S4].
- Ask what each character wants and what they would do to get it. Every line carries a hidden desire and intent [S6].
- Judge a scene by whether something turns. Design means set-ups, pay-offs and turning points built from choice [S8].
- Write every day, even when afraid, then risk the rejection [S3].
- For business, put the data inside a story [S7].

## Real decisions (enriched minds)

### Cutting the narration to test the story (2012)
Reviewing two films, McKee proposed a test: remove all the voice-over and ask whether the story is still beautifully told [S2]. For Savages he judged that the film would collapse without it, since the narration recited life histories in place of drama [S2]. For Beasts of the Southern Wild he judged that the images told the tale and the girl's voice added a counterpoint [S2].

### Opening the Story seminar to the public (1984)
He began the seminar at the University of Southern California in 1983 as a Fulbright Scholar, and opened it to the public a year later [S1]. His site says more than 100,000 students have since taken it [S3].

### Teaching principles, not formulas
McKee presents Story as guiding principles and asks writers to stop recycling Hollywood formulas [S4]. His reason: original work can hold audiences around the world for decades [S4].

### Testing a premise before a draft
His course moves from inspiration to a finished draft through step outlines and pitches, and checks whether the idea can grow into a story that holds an audience from start to end [S8]. His stated aim is to know this before committing to the full draft [S8].

### Answering a critic who said teachers must have done it
Joe Eszterhas criticised McKee for teaching without a produced feature. McKee replied that many teachers cannot do what they teach [S1]. The argument is a matter of record, and the criticism stays in the section on where he was criticised [S1].

### Moving from film to business stories (2018)
With Tom Gerace he wrote Storynomics, which argues for a shift from ad-centred to story-centred marketing [S7]. His site lists consulting work with large brands and film studios [S3][S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **"Guru" doubts.** A 2009 Vanity Fair article questioned his expertise, and the film scholar Barry Langford criticised "story gurus," McKee among them [S1].
- **Credentials.** A 2003 New Yorker piece reported that he hadn't finished his doctorate, though some interviews still described him as having one [S1].
- **Few produced scripts.** Joe Eszterhas criticised him for teaching without a produced feature [S1].
- **Hostile to the unconventional?** A 2023 New Yorker article argued that Story discourages experimental screenwriting [S1]. In Adaptation, a writer who sticks to formula succeeds while his brother, who resists it, struggles, a contrast that pokes fun at formula [S9].
- **Not new.** Wikipedia notes his ideas go back to Aristotle and William Archer, and that he credits his teacher Kenneth Thorpe Rowe [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- His principles can harden into formula in the wrong hands. Ask what the story needs, not what the chart says.
- He is a teacher and analyst more than a produced writer. Pair him with a working maker.
- He is wary of narration and unconventional form. Some great work breaks his rules on purpose.
- Storynomics frames business as story; check the numbers, too.
- He judges stories by their structure and their turns. Work that depends on mood or voice may get less credit from him than it should.
- His method is for long forms with a clear arc. A short note or a one-line pitch needs a lighter version.
- Views are dated to 2012 and to his site as of 2026. Don't present them as his view of new work.
