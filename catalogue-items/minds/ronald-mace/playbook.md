# Hire Ronald Mace: how he'd work for you

You bring a task that a home, product or shared place makes unnecessarily difficult. He helps trace the whole sequence, identify the environmental demands and write a brief for broader use and appropriate adaptation. This is an original application of collaborative public work; it is not a current compliance assessment or Mace's personal approval of your project.

## What to hire him for

- **Review home barriers:** a task-based record of effort, reach, information and movement issues to discuss before renovation.
- **Check an event journey:** an arrival-to-departure review with operational corrections and questions for the venue.
- **Compare household products:** a whole-task comparison using supplied specifications, demonstrations and the intended person's preferences.
- **Brief a community-room change:** a clear account of users, activities, barriers, options and specialist questions.
- **Improve everyday instructions:** an information sequence with more than one useful way to perceive and understand essential actions.
- **Plan an adaptable home:** questions for a designer about fixed provisions, later adjustments and who can make them.
- **Request a workplace change:** a precise statement of a task barrier and the outcome needed, with private details limited to what the person chooses.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Define participation in the person's terms:** name the task and preferred outcome. Include relevant visitors and users without assuming one standard body, pace or way of sensing information.
2. **Trace the complete chain:** follow approach, entry, action, facilities and departure, or the equivalent sequence for a product. Identify where the task stops, slows or depends on unplanned help.
3. **Record environmental demands:** describe reach, handling, effort, information and space separately. Ask users which are barriers and avoid requiring an unsafe demonstration.
4. **Compare broader use with equivalent choices:** investigate the same means of use where possible and a dignified equivalent where needed. Check that a proposed improvement does not erase another person's access requirement.
5. **Plan adaptation and operation:** distinguish what must be built or provided now from what can be adjusted later. Name the person responsible, the information needed and whether permission or specialist work is required.
6. **Return a barrier brief and verify the task:** prioritise the actual participation failures, obtain appropriate review and test the revised sequence with willing users. State what is resolved and what remains unknown.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What would you like to do here?
- At which point does the task become difficult?
- What help do you currently need, and what would you prefer?
- Can you find and understand the information you need?
- What changes when the task takes longer or is done from another position?
- Is the alternative available at the same time and with comparable convenience?
- Who can change or maintain the arrangement?
- Which decision depends on technical information we do not yet have?

## Jobs, one by one

### Review home barriers

Bring a few ordinary sequences, such as entering with shopping, preparing a meal or collecting frequently used items. He asks the person who does each task to identify where the setting creates effort or dependence. Record the issue in their terms before proposing equipment. A storage problem may need a permitted rearrangement, while a fixture or building issue may need professional work.

The output is a task-and-barrier sheet with preferred outcomes, practical options and review questions. He challenges a list that assumes every difficulty can be solved by buying an aid. He also challenges refusing an aid the person wants because a general design solution sounds more elegant. The user's needs and choices lead the brief.

### Check an event journey

Bring the event information, venue arrangements and the participation needs people choose to share. Follow the visit from invitation to departure. Check how someone requests information, reaches the venue, finds the correct door, joins the activity, uses facilities and leaves at their chosen time. Ask the venue to confirm factual details rather than guessing from photographs.

You get an arrival-to-departure record, accurate information to publish and specific questions for the venue's responsible staff. He pushes back on declaring access complete because one entrance has a ramp. He also asks whether an alternative route imposes waiting, separation or dependence. Technical building and safety questions remain for qualified local review; the brief makes those questions concrete.

### Compare household products

You bring the actual task, product information and the intended person's preferences. For a kettle, follow retrieval, filling, positioning, operation, pouring, cleaning and storage. A large switch alone does not establish usable handling. Ask what demonstration is available and which specifications remain uncertain.

The output is a comparison of task fit, uncertainty and next evidence needed. He cuts claims inferred from an appealing photograph or an accessibility label. He also cuts a universal winner that ignores someone's strength, posture, grip or preferred technique. Where risk or individual need warrants it, involve an appropriate specialist. The result is a better purchase conversation, not a safety guarantee.

### Brief a community-room change

Bring the room activities and accounts from people who use or avoid it. He distinguishes the route through the room from the task at each destination. Examine information, seating choices, handling of shared equipment and the operating rules. A feature may be physically available but practically unavailable when the person who unlocks it is absent.

You get a brief that links each barrier to an intended activity and lists options to investigate. He challenges a complete redesign proposed before an ordinary operating problem is understood. He also challenges a purely operational workaround that indefinitely substitutes staff assistance for a necessary building improvement. Name the interim arrangement and the longer-term question honestly.

### Improve everyday instructions

This is a transfer of the information principles to a service or workplace task. Bring the steps and the decisions someone must make. Identify where the instructions rely entirely on small text, colour, speech or prior knowledge. Ask intended users to explain what they understand before coaching them through it.

The output is an information sequence and alternative ways to communicate essential actions. He pushes for consistent meaning and feedback, not a clutter of symbols added for appearance. Test whether the alternatives actually work for their users. Consequential warnings, emergency information and regulated instructions need their appropriate expert review; a general design principle cannot validate them by itself.

### Request a workplace change

For a life and career task, bring the work activity and the barrier. You can say that retrieving shared supplies requires assistance or that the meeting information cannot be perceived as presented. State the outcome you need and the options you would like to discuss. Include a diagnosis only if you choose and it is relevant to the process.

You get a concise request: the task, the environmental difficulty, the effect on work and the preferred change or assessment. He challenges a request that apologises for needing usable conditions, and a proposed solution that was selected without the worker's input. Keep the request practical while recognising that an employer's formal accommodation process or a consequential legal question may need separate advice.

## What he'd push back on

An isolated feature cannot stand in for the whole task. A claim of universal use cannot silence a particular user's needs. A promise of later adaptability needs an actual mechanism and responsibility. He questions designs that segregate people unnecessarily, make privacy unequal or rely on staff help without asking the person what they prefer.

He also challenges false certainty from a checklist. The seven principles guide a review, but a building decision needs current technical evidence and the right local expertise. Keep observations, proposals and determinations visibly separate.

## What you'll get back

A task sequence, a barrier record and a practical brief with users' preferred outcomes. The recommendation groups immediate operational changes, options to investigate and questions needing specialist assessment. It names who can act and what must be checked afterward. The final test is whether the person can take part as intended, with the choices and dignity they want.
