# Ryan Holiday: what he knew and believed

Ryan Holiday is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from reference pages on him and his books [S1][S2], an introduction to Stoicism on the Daily Stoic site [S3], and three essays on his own blog from September and October 2026 [S4][S5][S6]. Treat each view as "as of 2026", the date of those essays. His books are known here through reference summaries, not read in full. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after October 2026, and nothing here covers his private life.

## In one paragraph

Holiday is a marketer turned author who has made the ancient philosophy of Stoicism, above all the thought of the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius, popular as a practical guide to life [S1]. He dropped out of college at 19, worked with the authors Tucker Max and Robert Greene, and became director of marketing at American Apparel [S1]. His first book, *Trust Me, I'm Lying* (2012), confessed how he had gamed the media [S1]. From 2014 he turned to Stoic themes: *The Obstacle Is the Way*, *Ego Is the Enemy*, *The Daily Stoic* and a four-book series on the Stoic virtues that ended in 2025 [S1]. By 2026 he wrote that Stoicism's real purpose is to make people better and more useful to others, more than tougher or more productive [S4]. He keeps coming back to the same message: there are no shortcuts, and the slow, hard work is the point [S5][S6].

## Core ideas

### What stands in the way becomes the way

The title of his 2014 book comes from a line in Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations*: an obstacle to action can itself move action forward [S2]. Holiday built the book on three disciplines: perception, action and will [S2]. Perception means seeing the problem calmly, without panic. Action means directed effort on the task in front of you. Will means enduring what you can't change [S2].

How he applied it: he used John D. Rockefeller's calm investing through market panics as a model of perception, and Amelia Earhart taking a flight offer even as a second choice, and unpaid, as a model of action [S2].

### Split what you control from what you don't

At the heart of Stoicism, as Daily Stoic explains it, is the habit of sorting what you can affect from what you can't, and putting your effort into the first [S3]. Your judgments, attitude and emotions are yours. Most events are not [S2][S3].

How he applied it: in 2026 he wrote that he would never have chosen a pandemic, market turmoil or political unrest. He leaned on Seneca's idea that fortune does as she likes, so the job is to prepare and respond well [S4].

### The four virtues, with justice at the centre

The Stoics named four main virtues: practical wisdom, self-discipline, courage and justice [S3][S4]. Daily Stoic treats justice as the most important, because it shapes the others [S3]. Holiday wrote a book for each of the four, from *Courage Is Calling* (2021) to *Wisdom Takes Work* (2025) [S1].

How he applied it: by 2026 he read obstacles as chances to practise virtue, not only as chances to win. He wrote that even a terminal diagnosis or a death can be met with endurance, kindness and decency [S4].

### Stoicism is for being better, not only tougher

Holiday said he first came to Stoicism at 19 hoping it would make him more productive and harder to knock down [S4]. Ten years after *The Daily Stoic*, he wrote that its real demand is to make people better. He pointed out that Marcus writes about serving others more than almost anything else [S4].

How he applied it: he described asking whether a business choice helps others, not only whether it is good for business. He gave examples such as stopping paid advertising and speaking against injustice even when it cost him [S4].

### Discipline includes saying no

As his books took off, Holiday kept saying yes to every offer [S4]. The pandemic made him count the cost. He closed his marketing company and cut back on talks, travel and consulting [S4]. Saying no to some things, he argued, is how you commit to others.

How he applied it: he described keeping a sign that says "No" on his wall as a reminder [S4].

### Know when you have enough

Seneca called endless wanting a kind of poverty [S4]. Holiday wrote that his own goals kept moving after each success, so his happiness kept moving too [S4]. Comparing yourself with others, he warned, drains the value from what you have done.

How he applied it: he noted that another author's book sold more in its first year than *The Daily Stoic* had sold in its whole life, and used that to show how comparison robs you [S4].

### Small steps, every day

Holiday cited Zeno's teaching that well-being comes through small steps, and Seneca's aim to find one small thing each day that made him wiser [S4]. Daily Stoic calls Stoicism a practice and a routine, built on exercises such as journaling, choosing discomfort on purpose, imagining setbacks in advance and reflecting on death [S3].

How he applied it: he designed *The Daily Stoic* as a page-a-day book, much as Marcus wrote daily notes to himself in the *Meditations* [S4].

### No shortcuts to wisdom

Holiday argues that real learning is slow and hard, and that the difficulty is what makes it worth having [S5][S6]. He calls speed reading a scam and says no great reader he knows credits their reading to speed [S5]. He told Seneca's story of a rich Roman who bought slaves to memorise poets for him, and read it as a warning about handing your thinking to AI. Having someone else lift the weight doesn't make you strong [S6].

How he applied it: he described reading thousands of letters and documents himself for a biography, even with research help, following the historian Robert Caro's advice to turn every page [S6].

### Learn from a master

Holiday says he learned how to read from Robert Greene, for whom he worked as a research assistant [S6]. Greene pushed him to ask where a quote came from, whether the source was reliable and whether other sources agreed [S6]. Good reading, Holiday says, means questioning, checking, following footnotes and keeping a system for notes [S5][S6].

How he applied it: he pointed to commonplace books kept by Joan Didion and Emerson, and to notes in the margins by Montaigne and John Adams, as the habits of active readers [S5].

## How he decides

- Ask what is up to you here, then act on that part [S3].
- Look for the virtue the moment calls for: wisdom, discipline, courage or justice [S4].
- Ask whether the choice helps others, not only you [S4].
- Say no to protect what matters most [S4].
- Notice when the goalposts move, and ask whether you already have enough [S4].
- Focus on the task in front of you, not the whole mountain [S2].
- Do the slow work yourself. Be wary of anything that promises a shortcut [S5][S6].
- Re-read the old books. You change, so they read differently [S4].

## Where he stood

**What is Stoicism for?** As of 2026, making people better and more useful to others. Toughness and output are side effects [S4].

**Is Stoicism about having no emotions?** No. Daily Stoic calls that a common misreading. The aim is resilience, self-mastery and wisdom [S3].

**Should you read fast?** No. He says people who read a lot simply spend a lot of time reading [S5].

**Can AI do your learning for you?** He argues no. Outsourcing the reading leaves you without the strength the reading builds [S6].

**What does death teach?** At 29 he read memento mori as a push to hurry. At 39 he read it as a call to slow down and be present [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Philosophy or self-help?** A *New York Times* writer said his approach sounds more like business than philosophy. A *Guardian* writer argued he pins Stoic quotes to modern stories so loosely that little real philosophy is left [S1].
- **His past.** At a Stoicism conference in 2016, a professor asked whether his conduct fitted Stoic ideals, given that his first book was about manipulating the media [S1].
- **His own reply.** He has defended popularising Stoicism as a self-help strategy [S1]. By 2026 he wrote that he had first used it to get ahead, and now saw it as a call to serve [S4].
- **Selling the message.** His 2026 essays on slow reading end by promoting a paid reading course [S5][S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- He turns hard philosophy into short, usable lessons. Some depth gets lost on the way [S1].
- His stories pick winners who turned obstacles into wins. Not every obstacle can be turned.
- Stoic calm can slide into accepting what should be changed. His stress on justice is the check [S3].
- He writes as a successful author who can afford to say no. Others may have fewer choices [S4].
- Pair him with someone who works on systems, or with a scholar who reads the Stoics closely.
