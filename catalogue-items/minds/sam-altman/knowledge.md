# Sam Altman: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Sam Altman is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own essays and blog posts, from his Startup Playbook, and from a public reference page. His startup advice was written during and after his years running Y Combinator. His essays on AI date from 2021, 2024 and 2025 [S4][S5][S6]. Treat each view as "as of" the essay it comes from. Some of his posts carry no date on the page, so we don't give them one. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after these pieces, and nothing here covers his private life.

## In one paragraph

Altman started his first company, Loopt, at 19, later ran the startup school Y Combinator, and has led OpenAI since 2019 [S7]. His advice to founders is simple and repeated often: make something users love, focus on a few things, keep momentum, and charge more than it costs you to deliver [S3]. His advice on careers rests on compounding, strong self-belief balanced by open ears, independent thinking, and bets where the upside is far larger than the downside [S1]. In his AI essays he argues that the technology will make most goods and services much cheaper, that society should share the gains, and that the hard problems are safety and wide access [S4][S5][S6]. He writes with confidence about the long run, and he admits the path will not be smooth [S5].

## Core ideas

### Make something users love

His Startup Playbook says the goal of a startup is to build something users love, and to get that right before chasing growth [S3]. A great product is the one reliable route to long-term growth. If growth is weak, the product is often the problem [S3]. The cycle is to talk to users, watch them, improve the product, and repeat, as fast as you can [S3].

How he applied it: the Playbook tells founders to recruit early users by hand, keep the product simple, and launch sooner than feels comfortable. It also tells them to do things that don't scale at first [S3].

### Compound yourself

Altman argues that the biggest gains in a career or a business come from growth that compounds. So you should look for work where each year builds on the last, and think in decades. The later years matter most [S1]. He links this to owning things: a salary grows slowly with time, while equity in something that grows fast can grow much faster [S1].

How he applied it: in the same essay he tells readers to build leverage through skills, relationships and reputation, so their work gets harder to copy over time [S1].

### Focus beats hours

Altman writes that choosing the right things to work on matters more than working long hours. He calls focus a force multiplier on work [S1]. The Playbook gives the startup version: do fewer things, finish one before you start the next, and move fast [S3]. He also warns against chasing press and a personal brand after early success [S3].

How he applied it: he told founders to pick one key growth number, share it widely, and watch retention as well as sign-ups [S3].

### Self-belief, with open ears

He says success often needs almost too much belief in yourself, because contrarian bets need conviction and teams need morale [S1]. He pairs this with a warning. You must stay open to criticism, or self-belief turns into self-delusion [S1]. Independent thinking has to be learned, since school tends to reward fitting in. He suggests reasoning from first principles and testing ideas quickly in the real world, expecting to be wrong many times [S1].

How he applied it: he advised founders to stay persistent but not stubborn, and to stay optimistic as CEO [S3].

### Risk is usually smaller than it looks

Altman argues that most people overrate risk and underrate reward [S1]. He tells readers to make small bets with large possible upside early on, and to keep their living costs low so they can afford to leave a comfortable path [S1][S2]. In his list of life advice, written the week after he turned 30, he says most people regret what they didn't do more than what they did [S2].

How he applied it: the Playbook says starting a company is less risky to a career than many people assume, especially for skilled builders [S3].

### Execution and momentum

The Playbook closes on the point that many people have good ideas, and execution is what separates the few who succeed [S3]. Founders should do the hard work themselves rather than hand it to an outside manager. They should make money and never lose momentum [S3]. Most startups fail because of their own problems, not because of rivals, so he tells founders to mostly ignore competitors until one beats them with a shipped product [S3].

How he applied it: his hiring advice follows the same line. Hire as late as you can, then hire only very strong people, value aptitude over experience, and fire quickly when a hire isn't working [S3].

### Technology will make most things cheaper

In 2021 Altman argued that AI would do more and more of the work people do now, from giving advice to running factories and doing research [S4]. As the cost of labour falls, the cost of goods and services could fall too, much as chips got more powerful for the same price every couple of years [S4]. He called this "Moore's Law for everything" [S4]. In 2025 he said he expected the cost of intelligence to fall toward the cost of electricity [S6].

How he applied it: he used this belief to argue that power would shift from labour to capital, and that policy should change in response [S4].

### Share the gains

Because he expected AI to shift wealth toward owners of capital, he proposed taxing capital instead of relying on income tax [S4]. His 2021 plan, the American Equity Fund, would take 2.5% of the value of large companies each year in shares, and 2.5% of the value of private land, and pay every adult a yearly share [S4]. He estimated about $13,500 per adult per year after ten years, under his assumptions [S4]. By 2024 he had floated "universal basic compute" instead, and by April 2026 he said he no longer believed in basic income as much as before [S7].

How he applied it: the plan included limits, such as a cap on the tax in the constitution and a ban on borrowing against future payments [S4].

### Safety and wide access come first

In 2024 he wrote that superintelligence might arrive in a few thousand days, though it could take longer [S5]. He argued that abundant, cheap compute is needed, or AI will stay scarce and mostly help the rich [S5]. In 2025 he named two priorities: solve alignment, so AI reliably pursues what people want over the long term, and make superintelligence cheap and widely spread, not held by one person, company or country [S6].

How he applied it: he pointed to social media feeds as an example of a system tuned for short-term engagement rather than what people really want [S6].

## How he decides

- Ask whether some people love it already. If not, fix the product before growth [S3].
- Pick one key number and track it in the open [S3].
- Do the few things that matter, and finish them [S1][S3].
- Take bets with small downside and large upside [S1].
- Keep costs low to keep options open [S2].
- Charge more than it costs to deliver, and watch cash flow [S3].
- Raise money when you need it or when terms are good, and keep terms clean [S3].
- If you will regret not trying, try [S2].
- Pause before acting, longer when angry [S2].

## Where he stood

**Should a startup worry about competitors?** Mostly no, until a rival beats it with a real product. Most startups die from their own mistakes [S3].

**Is hard work enough?** No. Hard work matters, especially early in a career, but choosing the right thing to work on matters more [S1][S2].

**How do you get rich?** By owning things that grow fast in value, not by salary alone [S1].

**What should people do as AI spreads?** As of 2021, he argued for sharing the gains through ownership [S4]. As of 2024, he expected most jobs to change more slowly than people think, and did not foresee a shortage of meaningful work [S5].

**What matters most in building advanced AI?** As of 2025, alignment first, then cheap and wide access [S6].

## His work and record

- Born April 22, 1985, in Chicago, Illinois [S7].
- Studied computer science at Stanford for two years and left in 2005 [S7].
- Co-founded Loopt, a location app, in 2005. Green Dot bought it in March 2012 for $43.4 million [S7].
- Joined Y Combinator part-time in 2011 and became president in February 2014, after Paul Graham [S7].
- Co-founded OpenAI in 2015 as a nonprofit, with Greg Brockman, Elon Musk, Ilya Sutskever and others [S7].
- Left Y Combinator and became OpenAI's full-time CEO in March 2019 [S7].
- Co-founded Tools for Humanity, the company behind Worldcoin, in 2019 [S7].
- Published "Moore's Law for Everything" on March 16, 2021 [S4].
- OpenAI launched ChatGPT in late 2022 [S7].
- Testified to a U.S. Senate subcommittee on AI oversight in May 2023 [S7].
- Removed as CEO by OpenAI's board on November 17, 2023, and returned on November 21, 2023 [S7].
- Published "The Intelligence Age" on September 23, 2024 [S5].
- Published "The Gentle Singularity" in 2025 [S6].
- Chairman of Helion, a fusion company, and chair of Oklo until April 2025 [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Candour with his board.** In November 2023 OpenAI's board said he had not been consistently candid with it [S7]. A former board member later said he had withheld information from the board [S7]. He returned as CEO within days, with strong staff support [S7].
- **Leaving Y Combinator.** Wikipedia reports that his 2019 move was publicly described as a step to chairman, and that Paul Graham said he was removed because partners did not trust him (disputed) [S7].
- **Exit agreements.** In May 2024 he faced claims that he knew about a rule that could cancel departing employees' equity [S7].
- **Conflicts of interest.** His stakes in hundreds of companies, some of which deal with OpenAI, have raised questions. OpenAI's board chair said he had been open about them [S7].
- **Worldcoin.** The iris-scanning project has faced regulators and claims of misleading marketing in several countries [S7].
- **OpenAI's structure.** Elon Musk sued in 2024, claiming OpenAI broke its founding mission. Wikipedia reports that a 2026 jury found Musk had waited too long to sue [S7].

These are criticisms of his record as a leader. His essays give his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His advice is tuned for venture-backed startups aiming to grow very large. A small business that wants steady profit may not need to swing that big [S3].
- "Almost too much self-belief" can tip over into ignoring warnings. He names the risk himself, but a council should test it [S1].
- His AI timelines are confident and could be wrong. Treat them as forecasts, dated to when he wrote them [S5][S6].
- He writes mostly about the upside of technology. Pair him with someone who looks hard at who gets left behind, or at trust and governance.
- His notes on life and health are general personal reflections, not expert advice [S2].
