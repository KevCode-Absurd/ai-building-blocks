## In one paragraph

Walton bought a small variety store in Newport, Arkansas, in 1945, lost the lease, and started again in Bentonville in 1950 [S4]. In 1962 he opened the first Walmart in Rogers, Arkansas [S3]. His formula was simple: the lowest prices he could manage, a wide range always in stock, stores in small towns that bigger chains ignored, and warehouses and trucks of his own so goods arrived cheaply [S4]. He treated staff as "associates" and partners, shared profits and information, and praised people often [S1][S2]. He ran lean, watched costs closely and liked doing the opposite of what everyone else did [S1]. By 1980 Walmart had $1 billion in yearly sales, and it went on to become the largest company in the Fortune 500 in 2002 [S3]. Its size also drew heavy criticism over wages, unions and the effect on small-town shops [S5].

## Core ideas

### Low prices, every day

Walton built Walmart on the idea of the lowest prices, anytime, anywhere [S3]. He aimed to match or beat the prices in cities a four-hour drive away, so shoppers in small towns had no reason to leave [S4]. He believed a store could win with low prices and good service, which many rivals doubted [S2].

How he applied it: when he accepted the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1992, he said that working together could lower the cost of living for everyone [S2]. Walmart turned this into its purpose: saving people money so they can live better [S2].

### Go where the big chains won't

Rival discount chains chose large cities. Walton chose small towns [S4]. There was less competition there, and a store big enough to serve the whole town could keep other merchants from moving in [S4].

How he applied it: the chain grew from 190 stores in 1977 to 800 in 1985 [S4].

### Control the supply chain

Walton put his stores within a day's drive of regional warehouses and ran his own trucks [S4]. Buying in volume and delivering efficiently let him sell name brands at a discount [S4].

How he applied it: Walmart opened its first distribution centre and home office in Bentonville in 1971 [S3].

### Run lean

Walton's ninth rule was to control expenses better than competitors [S1]. He argued that cost discipline can be your biggest advantage, because an efficient business can survive mistakes that would sink a wasteful one [S1].

In his memoir he put it in customer terms: every dollar the company wastes comes out of the customer's pocket, and every dollar saved puts it a step ahead of rivals [S7].

How he applied it: he bought a small second-hand plane to scout for store sites, and he and his son John logged thousands of flying hours doing it [S4].

### Treat staff as partners

Walton's second rule was to share profits with associates and treat them as partners [S1]. In return, he wrote, they will treat you as a partner and perform beyond what you expect [S1]. His fourth rule was to tell them everything you can, because the more they know, the more they care [S1]. His fifth was to praise people often and sincerely, because praise costs nothing and is worth a great deal [S1].

How he applied it: in his early Ben Franklin stores he had managers invest in their own stores, sometimes about $1,000, which made them think like owners [S4].

### Pay is not enough

The third rule says money and ownership alone don't motivate people for long [S1]. Set high goals, encourage friendly competition, keep score, and change things up so you don't become too predictable [S1]. The sixth rule says to celebrate wins, find humour in failures and have fun, because enthusiasm spreads [S1].

How he applied it: Walton brought in the Walmart cheer in the 1970s after visiting a factory in South Korea, where workers began the day with a company cheer [S6]. He liked that a cheer put executives, associates and fans on the same level, and said nobody was too important to lead one [S6].

### Listen to everyone

The seventh rule is to listen to everyone in the company and find ways to get them talking [S1]. The people on the front line, who talk to customers every day, know what's really happening [S1]. Pushing responsibility down lets good ideas rise up [S1].

How he applied it: running stores at a distance forced him to delegate to local managers [S4].

### Exceed customer expectations

The eighth rule is to give customers a little more than they expect, and to make good on every mistake [S1]. Don't make excuses. Apologise [S1]. Walton also built a "servant leadership" idea into the company, based on his faith [S4]. In a 1989 letter he went further. He wrote that exceeding customer expectations must be the company's strategy, because specialty chains such as Office Depot and Toys-R-Us were taking on Walmart one department at a time [S8]. His example of a good result was a lawn and garden manager who drove about 20 miles with the missing hardware for a customer's picnic table and helped her put it together, which Walton said won a customer for life [S8]. His fix for a bad return trip was a rule he called replace, plus one: replace the shoes, and add something such as socks to make up for the hassle [S8].

How he applied it: the company cheer ends by asking who is number one, and answering that it's the customer [S3].

### Swim upstream

The tenth rule is to go the other way when everyone else is doing the same thing [S1]. Your niche may be in the opposite direction [S1]. The first rule ties it together: commit to your business, believe in it more than anybody else, and others will catch your passion [S1].

How he applied it: he went to small towns when others went to cities [S4]. He also borrowed freely and openly from rivals, and said his one-stop-shop idea came from the Meijer chain [S4]. He wrote that he learned the most in his early years from studying what a competitor, John Dunham, was doing across the street [S7].

## Real decisions

### Buying a variety store in Newport at 26, and buying direct
In 1945, after the army, Walton bought a Ben Franklin variety store in Newport, Arkansas, with a $20,000 loan from his father-in-law and $5,000 he had saved [S4]. He took the franchisor's two-week training, then found he could buy goods cheaper from other suppliers than from the franchisor [S11][S13]. His method was to price low and sell much more, so profit came from volume rather than margin [S11][S13]. Sales grew from $80,000 to $225,000 in three years, and he repaid his father-in-law within 30 months [S4][S11].

### Losing the Newport lease and starting again
The Newport lease cost 5% of sales and had no renewal option. The landlord, who wanted the store for his own son, refused to renew, though he paid $50,000 for the stock and fixtures, which Walton called fair [S4][S11]. Walton said he read his leases much more carefully after that [S11]. He moved to Bentonville and opened a new store on 9 May 1950. Walmart's account says he chose it because his wife Helen liked small-town life and the hunting was good [S2][S4].

### Leaving Ben Franklin to start Walmart (1962)
By 1960 he ran 15 stores with about $1.4 million in sales and was looking for a bigger payoff [S12]. The franchisers would not back discounting, so he went his own way [S13]. He studied discounters such as FedMart, ignored the view that discounting needed big towns, and opened the first Walmart in Rogers, Arkansas, in 1962 at age 44 [S4][S12]. He wrote that many of his best opportunities were created out of necessity: with little money, he went to small towns the big chains skipped [S12]. The first store earned far less than top discounters, but the gap showed him a large opening [S13].

### Flying to scout, and giving managers a stake
Tired of driving between stores, he bought a small second-hand plane to scout sites [S4]. He asked managers to invest in their own store, often up to about $1,000, so they would act as owners [S4]. He admitted he had been too strict on pay early on, then loosened policy, first for partner managers and later for the rank and file, who shared in profits and stock gains [S12]. Encyclopedia.com says he first paid the minimum wage and that his wife Helen persuaded him to add incentive bonuses, stock discounts and profit sharing [S15]. Reviewers note he came to believe that well-treated employees treat customers better [S13].

### Building the warehouse and trucks first
To make small-town stores work, he placed them within a day's drive of regional warehouses and ran his own trucks [S4]. Delivering to stores directly was inefficient, so a central warehouse became the base for expansion [S13]. A reviewer notes that an executive had to push him to buy key equipment because he disliked spending [S13]. He took the company public in 1970 to pay off the debt from expansion [S13]. The chain grew from 190 stores in 1977 to 800 in 1985 [S4].

### Studying rivals with a notepad, then copying
Walton visited rivals' stores constantly, from the Sterling store across the street in Newport to Kmart, which he called his laboratory [S11][S13]. He asked clerks questions and wrote the answers on a yellow pad [S13]. He told managers to look for what rivals did right and take it [S13]. He said openly that his one-stop format came from Meijer and that Sam's Club came from Sol Price's warehouse model [S4][S12]. He dropped failed trials such as Hypermart quickly [S14].

### Bringing a factory cheer to the stores (c. 1975)
Walton visited a manufacturing plant near Seoul where workers began the day with a company cheer, and he brought the idea home [S6]. Walmart's museum records his reason: a cheer puts everyone on the same level, and nobody should be too important or too puffed up to lead one [S6]. The date is uncertain, because a May 1976 company letter about trips to Japan, Korea and Taiwan casts doubt on 1975 [S6].

### Writing to associates: make exceeding expectations the plan (1989)
After a Saturday morning meeting on how specialty chains were attacking each department, Walton wrote to all associates [S8]. He proposed a pledge that every customer would get a greeting from at least ten associates. He proposed letting service desk associates handle refunds and exchanges on the spot, with manager approval perhaps only at $25 and above. He wanted knowledgeable staff in specialty departments for twelve hours a day. He asked for fewer hassles and a "do it right the first time" approach, and said the company must also find the system failures behind unhappy customers [S8]. His reason was that survival would depend on exceeding what customers expect as rivals improve. He closed by admitting the letter was a little heavy and signed it "Sam" [S8].

## Where he stood

**What does a store owe its customers?** Low prices, good service, a little more than they expect, and an apology when it gets things wrong [S1][S2].

**How should you treat staff?** As partners, with shared profits, shared information and frequent praise [S1].

**Where should you compete?** Where others aren't, as with small towns [S4][S1].

**What should you do about competitors?** Study them, borrow what works, and beat them on cost and service. He said openly that his one-stop-shop format came from a rival chain [S4]. Cost control, he argued, is the edge that lasts, because the lean business survives the mistakes that sink the wasteful one [S1].

**What do you do when you get it wrong?** Own it. Make good on the mistake and apologise to the customer, without excuses [S1]. Celebrate the wins, and find the humour in the failures so people keep trying [S1].

**How big should the boss's role be?** Smaller than you'd think. Share information, push decisions down to store managers, and let ideas come up from the front line [S1][S4].

**Where should goods come from?** In his later years Walmart ran a "Buy American" campaign, looking for US makers who could match foreign prices [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Small towns.** Critics say Walmart's low prices pulled shoppers away from local Main Street shops [S5]. Walton's own answer, in his memoir, was that many small stores were bound to disappear anyway, and that Walmart had kept a number of small towns from dying [S7]. Readers can judge both sides.
- **Wages.** Walmart has long been criticised for low pay and thin benefits. A line attributed to Walton, undated, describes the business as built on a low-wage, low-benefit model [S5].
- **Unions.** In 1970 Walton hired a consultant to fight a union drive at stores in two Missouri towns, and the company later built a union-avoidance programme [S5].
- **Buy American.** Soon after his death, reports said "Buy American" signs had been placed over imported goods [S5].
- Many later criticisms, such as the 2011 sex discrimination case, came after his death [S5]. They are about the company he built rather than his own choices.

## Blind spots to watch

- Low price above all can squeeze workers, suppliers and towns. Pair him with a voice that weighs those costs [S5].
- His rules praise sharing with staff, while his record on pay and unions was criticised. Check the gap between the words and the practice [S1][S5].
- His playbook suits a business that can win on scale and cost. A small premium brand may need a different game.
- His thrift cut both ways: reviewers say Walmart lagged on distribution centres and that he resisted some equipment until others pushed [S12][S13].
- He saw the world through the store. Big shifts outside it, such as online shopping, came after him [S3].
