### How they'd take on your problem

1. **Taste and name the problem:** You taste the food and describe the missing quality before recommending anything. Is it dull, heavy, harsh, dry, pale or unevenly cooked? Reserve a little unchanged food as a reference so a comparison can reveal what the first description concealed.

2. **Inspect salt:** You ask what seasoning entered, in what form and at what stage. Compare a small adjusted portion before changing the pot. The aim is clearer flavour in this food, not a universal instruction to add more salt regardless of the eater or dish.

3. **Inspect fat:** You identify the fat’s flavour and the texture its handling is meant to produce. Ask whether the goal is crispness, creaminess, tenderness or another quality. A problem created by cooking technique cannot always be repaired by pouring additional fat into the finished dish.

4. **Inspect acid:** You look for a suitable sour contrast already present or available. Try a small amount in one portion and compare it with the original. Explain which quality changes, keeping the adjustment within the dish’s flavour and avoiding the assumption that brightness means sharpness.

5. **Inspect heat:** You identify the transformation the food still needs: browning, softening, setting or something else. Ask how gentle or intense the cooking has been and what the pan allows. Choose the next action from the food’s state instead of hiding a heat problem beneath seasoning.

6. **Retaste and keep the useful cue:** You compare the new result with the reference and stop when it suits the intended meal. Write down the adjustment and the sensory cue that justified it. The useful lesson is a decision you can recognise again, rather than another rigid recipe to memorise.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Bland soup

Taste a small portion, compare salt and acid separately, and make the smallest change that makes the flavors clearer. With bland soup, reserve the pot and take three small samples. Leave one unchanged, adjust seasoning in another and add a little appropriate acid to the third. Taste the differences before touching dinner. The comparison teaches which missing quality the word bland was hiding, and it avoids trying several corrections at once in a pot that cannot be restored.

#### Dry roast vegetables

Check cut size, coating and heat before adding more ingredients, then choose tenderness and browning as separate goals. A roast-vegetable problem may be about texture rather than flavour. Inspect whether the vegetables were meant to brown or soften gently, whether the pieces are consistent and whether the tray is crowded. Adding more spice cannot replace the intended heat treatment. Describe the desired transformation and then choose a method that can produce it in the available oven.

#### Cooking without a recipe

Start with a familiar combination and identify the fat, acid and heat process before improvising the remaining parts. A weekly learning plan can use one meal repeatedly while changing only a meaningful variable. Keep the dish familiar enough that the change is perceptible and the result remains useful as dinner. Write a short before-and-after observation. This makes the kitchen a place to learn through eating rather than an endless test requiring a different elaborate recipe each night.

#### Explaining a confusing project

Find the few variables that recur across cases, name them plainly and show how each changes the result. In a project outside cooking, the transfer is the act of finding a few recurring variables. Ask which questions appear across several examples and give each a plain name. Do not pretend that a product has a literal equivalent of salt or heat. The useful result is a clearer diagnostic conversation supported by examples from that project.

#### Building confidence after a failure

Describe the failed texture or flavor without blaming yourself, and change one controllable element on the next attempt. When a timer rings, ask the cook to report what changed in colour, smell, sound and texture. A time in a recipe is a useful prompt to check; it cannot describe every pan or ingredient. Record one reliable cue beside the timing. That note turns a single cooking experience into a more portable piece of judgment.
