## In one paragraph

Blakely planned to be a lawyer, did badly on the LSAT, worked briefly at Walt Disney World and tried stand-up comedy, then sold fax machines door to door for Danka, where she became a national sales trainer at 25 [S1]. Annoyed by pantyhose in the Florida heat, she cut the feet off a pair to wear under trousers and saw an idea [S1][S2]. At 27 she spent two years and her $5,000 in savings building it while keeping her job [S1]. She pitched mills that turned her away, worked on her own patent application, bought the trademark online for $150, and sold the product to Neiman Marcus by modelling it for the buyer in a restroom [S1]. After Oprah Winfrey named Spanx one of her "Favorite Things" in November 2000, sales took off [S1][S2]. In 2012 Forbes called her the youngest self-made female billionaire [S1]. In 2021 Blackstone bought a majority stake at a $1.2 billion valuation, and she gave every employee $10,000 and two first-class plane tickets [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Solve your own problem

Blakely started from something that bugged her. She liked the smoothing effect of control-top pantyhose but hated the seamed feet under open-toed shoes [S1]. Cutting the feet off at a party gave her the look she wanted, even though the legs rolled up [S1].

In her own telling, the spark was a frustrated shopper with white trousers and nothing to wear under them [S9]. The name came to her in the car, and she pulled over because she knew it was right [S7].

How she applied it: in 2011 she said she had noticed that the hosiery industry was run by men who didn't wear the products they made [S1]. Being the customer was her edge.

### Bootstrap, and keep your day job

Blakely started Spanx with about $5,000 of her own savings, and kept selling fax machines while she developed it for two years [S1][S3]. She quit Danka only after the Oprah mention made sales take off [S1].

She took no outside investment as the company grew, and she says she hired people to cover her weaknesses [S9].

How she applied it: Spanx sold $4 million in its first year and $10 million in its second [S1]. She stayed the main owner until Blackstone bought a majority stake in 2021 [S1][S2].

### Do it yourself, cheaply, then pay for the hard part

Blakely did much of the early work herself. She worked on her own patent application and paid a patent attorney $750 to finalise it [S1]. She bought the Spanx trademark for $150 on the US patent office website with a credit card [S1]. She first called the product "Spanks" and changed the spelling at the last minute [S2].

How she applied it: early on she handled the marketing, logistics and positioning herself [S1].

### Keep knocking, and use sales skill

Years of door-to-door sales taught Blakely to handle rejection [S1]. Mills in North Carolina turned her down. Two weeks later, a mill owner in Asheboro called back because his three daughters liked the idea [S1].

In her telling she made cold calls and went door to door to find a manufacturer, and most meetings ended politely with no deal [S7]. In 2026 she said she wrote her own patent because she could not afford a lawyer, and that she once scrambled to fill a first large order [S10]. When she retells a rejection she mimics the buyers' questions, with dry comedy [S8].

How she applied it: when a Neiman Marcus buyer gave her a meeting, she took her into the ladies' restroom and showed the difference the product made under white trousers [S1][S2]. Spanx went into seven Neiman Marcus stores, then Bloomingdale's, Saks and Bergdorf Goodman [S1].

### Care more than anyone

In a line quoted by Forbes, Blakely says she didn't have the most experience or the most money, but she cared the most [S3]. Her advantage was commitment, not resources.

She says she trusts her gut and that being authentic matters [S8].

How she applied it: she persuaded friends to go and find Spanx in department stores, and mailed them thank-you cheques for doing it [S1].

### Put it where the customer will see it

Blakely wanted Spanx displayed with shoes, not hidden in the hosiery section [S1]. The product was about the outfit, so it should sit where women planned outfits.

How she applied it: she sent a gift basket to Oprah Winfrey's show with a note explaining the product. In November 2000 it was named one of Oprah's "Favorite Things" [S1]. In 2001 she signed with the TV shopping channel QVC, where she could show the product in action [S1][S2].

### Turn a weakness into a different path

Blakely has said she is a really bad test taker, and that failing the LSAT twice kept her out of law school [S4]. Instead of a legal career, she went into sales, and from sales into her own company [S1].

She retells the setbacks in her 2026 commencement speech: two failed LSAT attempts, a job fair that turned her down, missing the height rule for a Disney character role, and years selling fax machines door to door. She also describes a roadside moment of thinking she was in the wrong movie, and calls investing in herself her first big decision [S10].

How she applied it: her failed plan pointed her toward work where her people skills mattered more than exam scores [S1][S4].

### Share the win

When Blackstone bought its majority stake in October 2021, the deal team and the board were all women [S2]. Blakely gave each of her roughly 750 staff $10,000 and two first-class plane tickets to anywhere [S1].

How she applied it: in 2013 she became the first female billionaire to join the Giving Pledge [S1]. Richard Branson gave her $750,000 to help start her foundation, after she appeared on his TV show *The Rebel Billionaire* [S1]. She later gave $1 million to the Oprah Winfrey Leadership Academy for Girls [S1]. She started the Sara Blakely Foundation in 2006 to support women through education and training, and in 2020 pledged $5 million to women-run small businesses during the pandemic [S1].

### Keep inventing

Blakely didn't stop at shapewear. In 2013 she said she wanted to design the world's most comfortable high heel before she retired [S1]. In August 2024 she launched Sneex, high-heeled sneakers starting at $395 [S1][S3].

## Real decisions

### Cutting the feet off pantyhose and trusting the idea

She cut the feet off control-top pantyhose so her white trousers would look smooth with no lines and no bulky girdle [S8]. She had no design or sewing background [S7].

### Funding it with her own money

She started with her own savings and no outside investors [S8][S9]. She kept her selling job while she built the product [S1][S3].

### Changing the pitch mid-meeting

She got a Neiman Marcus buyer on the phone, then a ten-minute slot in Dallas. When the buyer was losing interest, she asked her to come to the restroom, put the product on in a stall and showed the difference. Neiman Marcus placed a small order [S7][S8].

### Paying friends to be the first customers

She asked friends near the first stores to buy the product and offered to pay them back [S8]. She says she paid friends to buy it and put it near store registers to build buzz [S7].

### Writing her own patent

She wrote her patent application herself because she could not afford a lawyer [S10]. Another source adds that she paid an attorney $750 to finalise it [S1].

### Selling to men too

She noticed that the men who ran the mills did not wear the products. She later launched men's products instead of leaving men out, and co-developed a cheaper line with Target [S8].

### Hiring her weaknesses

Instead of trying to be good at everything, she brought in colleagues to cover what she was weak at [S9]. She later hired a separate CEO [S2].

## Where she stood

**Do you need investors to start?** Not always. She started with about $5,000 and kept her job while she built it [S1][S3].

**Do you need industry experience?** No. She saw the gap because she was the customer and the industry wasn't [S1].

**What matters more than money?** Caring the most, as she put it to Forbes [S3].

**Should a founder run everything forever?** No. She brought in Laurie Ann Goldman as CEO from 2002 to 2014 [S2], and she stayed on as executive chair after the 2021 sale [S1].

**What role does luck play?** A real one. The Oprah mention came after she sent a gift basket and a note, a small move that happened to land [S1]. Our reading of her record: keep giving luck small chances to find you.

**How should a founder share success?** Generously, with staff and with other women starting out [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

Our sources record no major scandal or public controversy around Blakely herself [S1][S2]. Some things to weigh fairly:

- **Selling control.** By selling a majority to a private equity firm in 2021, she gave up control of the company she built [S1][S2].
- **Shapewear itself.** Shapewear sits inside a wider debate about body image and pressure on women's looks. Our sources don't cover that debate, so this mind should not claim to settle it.
- **Survivor's story.** Her path depended on rare breaks, such as the Oprah mention [S1]. Many good products never get one.

## Blind spots to watch

- Bootstrapping works when costs are low. Products that need factories, stock or long trials may need outside money.
- A founder's story can hide the team that scaled the business. Spanx had a separate CEO from 2002 to 2014 [S2].
- "Care the most" is a strength, but it isn't a business model. Check the numbers too.
- Her story is often told as one woman alone. In fact friends, a mill owner's family, buyers, a TV host and later a full team all played a part [S1][S2].
- Big-break marketing, like an Oprah mention, can't be planned. Build steady channels as well [S1].
