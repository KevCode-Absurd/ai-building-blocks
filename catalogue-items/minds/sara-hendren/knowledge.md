# Sara Hendren: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. This living-person portrait uses the dated writing and interviews below. The exercises are our applications, not projects she has approved. The checked sources do not establish a birth year.

## In one paragraph

Sara Hendren works across art, design research and disability. Her 2020 book What Can a Body Do? examines encounters between bodies and the built world at several scales. Northeastern's faculty record describes her teaching and collaborations, including work with disabled artists. Her useful contribution is to ask what a person wants from life before deciding what device or arrangement they need. Assistance can be ordinary, inventive and expressive; a person's worth cannot be reduced to the speed or economic output a design produces. [S5][S2]

## Core ideas

### Capacity belongs to a body in a world

Hendren's title announcement describes bodily possibility as a relationship with products, environments and systems. It credits philosophical and disability-studies influences rather than claiming the question as her invention. [S4]

Our home-routine exercise begins with a scene instead of a diagnosis. Someone wants to choose a book, sit comfortably and read in the evening. Record the lighting, storage, furniture, time available and help they already use. Ask which part of the scene is awkward and which is satisfying. Do not assume the body alone is the object to change. Moving an object, changing an expectation or arranging another person's help may matter as much as purchasing equipment. The description should leave the person recognisable as someone with tastes and purposes, rather than a list of functions to restore.

### Assistance is part of ordinary life

In the 2023 On Being conversation, Hendren discusses human dependence and the supports that make an independent life possible. She also warns against collapsing distinct disabled experiences into the claim that everybody is disabled. [S1]

Our assistance map lists the help already making an ordinary week possible: transport, reminders, shared meals, tools and other people's time. Its purpose is to remove the shame attached to requesting help, without pretending everyone's constraints are identical. Next ask who chooses the help, who can decline it and what happens when it is unavailable. A family arrangement that appears generous can still remove choice. Agree on how to offer assistance before touching someone's belongings or taking over a task. Independence can include directing support, not simply doing everything alone.

### A tool for one person can be worth making

In her interview with Alice Wong, Hendren describes designs ranging from individual adaptations to widely distributed products. She disputes the assumption that mass manufacture is the only meaningful measure of design, while acknowledging the affordability that scale can provide. [S2]

Our personal-aid brief names a particular activity and a particular owner. The person might want to arrange flowers, write a card or keep using a familiar hobby material. Ask what they value about the activity before offering a digital replacement. Compare existing aids, a change in setup and an appropriate custom collaboration. Include cleaning, storage and who can repair or replace the item. A bespoke response does not have to become a business to count as useful. Equally, uniqueness is no excuse for an expensive object that its intended user dislikes. Success belongs to the chosen activity, not the designer's exhibition.

### Disabled people are already designing

On Being describes Engineering at Home, Hendren's collaboration with anthropologist Caitrin Lynch, documenting Cindy's everyday adaptations. Hendren explicitly rejects a blanket conclusion that advanced prostheses are bad; the interest lies in which tools serve which wishes. [S1]

Our documentation exercise asks a maker whether they want an adaptation recorded at all. If they do, describe its purpose, their decisions, materials and remaining frustrations in their words. Ask how they wish to be credited and who may see the record. Do not turn a family member into an inspirational story or claim ownership of their ingenuity because you photographed it. An archive can include failed versions and things that need help from a professional. Its value is to make situated knowledge visible, including knowledge that usually receives less attention than a laboratory invention.

### Beauty is part of access

In the New America interview, Hendren discusses a stage-scale ramp developed with wheelchair dancer Alice Sheppard and students. She treats it as creating artistic possibility, extending the purpose of assistance beyond entry to a room. [S3]

Our community-art review asks what participants are actually being invited to enjoy or create. Getting through the door is necessary, but the activity may still assume one posture, pace or way of manipulating materials. Ask participants about the experience they want, including challenge, surprise and pleasure. A quieter area should remain connected to the event rather than becoming a place where people are parked. Describe the artistic and social possibilities as well as the practical arrangements. This does not mean every preference can be met at once; it means beauty is a legitimate part of the brief.

### Time is a designed expectation

Hendren's book discussion moves from limbs and objects toward clocks, and her On Being interview questions the equation between speed, productivity and human worth. She credits disability scholarship for the concept of crip time. [S2][S1]

Our shared-activity plan separates the things that truly need coordination from the things that merely follow habit. A group may need a clear closing time while allowing different starting points, rest and unfinished work. Say which parts are flexible so participants do not have to negotiate every pause. Avoid treating additional time as a humiliating exception or making another person explain their body publicly. Nor should flexibility become an obligation to work endlessly. A humane schedule can have boundaries and choices together. Ask whether the activity's purpose survives when completion speed stops being its main measure.

## How she decides

The following working rules interpret her documented questions about need, agency, assistance and scale. They are not a published Hendren checklist. [S1][S2][S4]

- Describe a wanted activity before naming a solution. Include the wishes that a narrow functional specification would omit.
- Look for adaptations already in use. Their maker may understand the important constraint before the visiting expert arrives.
- Consider an object, arrangement, relationship or change of timing. A device is one possible response among several.
- Keep the person involved in choices about appearance and effort. Convenience for helpers is a separate interest that should be named.
- Judge a custom design by its actual use. Judge a distributed product by its actual reach and affordability; do not confuse the two standards.
- Give sources and collaborators credit. General usefulness should not erase the disabled people whose decisions made a design possible.

For our personal-aid comparison, use a short table: desired action, meaning to the person, present arrangement, proposed change and unanswered question. One option might keep a familiar object but alter its location. Another might use an existing product. A third might require specialist collaboration. Ask the owner to rank the options, including the choice to retain the current arrangement. A comparison can reveal that the designer's favourite solution solves the least important part of the problem. Record that finding directly rather than trying to sell the most inventive option.

## Where she stood

Hendren's 2020 interview asks readers to regard disabled experience as an intellectual and creative tradition, not merely a problem awaiting rescue. Her account draws on scholars and activists, and makes room for both individual adaptations and public infrastructure. [S2]

Our transfer to career exploration is specific. A person interested in both art and engineering could document the questions raised by one ordinary object, then learn from a maker, a user and a relevant historical source. They might produce a small exhibition, illustrated essay or revised brief. The exercise is not a claim that all disciplines are interchangeable. It tests whether the person enjoys translating between forms of knowledge and giving collaborators proper credit. Keep the learning goal distinct from claiming professional competence in accessibility, healthcare or engineering after one project.

## Their work and record

What Can a Body Do? appeared in 2020. Northeastern's faculty biography records Hendren as an associate professor in Art + Design with a joint architecture appointment, and identifies her as a co-founder of the Accessible Icon Project. It also records her previous teaching at Olin College of Engineering. [S5]

Her 2019 New America interview explains how collaborations informed her research and writing. The title announcement and the 2020 interview show her translating design and disability scholarship for a general reader rather than presenting the book as a technical manual. [S3][S4][S2]

## Where she was wrong or criticised

The checked sources do not establish a specific documented dispute that should be invented here. A real tension appears within the interviews: assistance is a common human experience, yet disabled people face distinct barriers and political histories. Hendren herself cautions against saying that all experiences are the same. [S1][S2]

Our application must preserve that distinction. A nondisabled person's passing inconvenience can prompt attention, but it does not authorise them to describe another person's life. Likewise, celebrating inventive adaptations must not excuse an organisation from removing a barrier. A moving story about a custom tool can leave a wider service failure untouched. Ask whether the suggested response expands choice or merely gives someone another job to do in order to participate. This is a limit on using the profile, not an attributed criticism from an unnamed reviewer.

## Blind spots to watch

An imaginative brief still needs an owner, resources and suitable technical assessment. This portrait cannot certify a ramp, prescribe a prosthesis or establish the safety of a homemade modification. It can help specify what a person values and identify whom to involve. Do not reproduce a story's materials as instructions for a different body or situation.

The attraction of an individual story can also hide distribution. Ask who can afford the proposed help, who maintains it and who remains outside the arrangement. Pair attention to one person's wishes with responsibility for the shared setting. Where wishes conflict, describe them openly; do not invoke a romantic idea of interdependence to make someone accept unwanted assistance or unpaid work.
