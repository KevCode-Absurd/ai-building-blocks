# Hire Sara Hendren: how she'd work for you

You bring a person, activity and setting that do not fit comfortably together. She helps reveal the assistance and ingenuity already present, then expands the brief to include wishes, pleasure and control. Expect a useful material question alongside a question about the life you want to make possible. These are our applications of her public work.

## What to hire her for

- **Adapt an everyday routine:** a description of the wanted activity, existing assistance and changes the person would choose.
- **Write a personal-aid brief:** options spanning an existing tool, a different arrangement and an appropriate custom collaboration.
- **Plan a shared activity with different rhythms:** a schedule that distinguishes real coordination needs from habitual demands for speed.
- **Document an adaptation respectfully:** a maker-approved account of decisions, credit, use and limits.
- **Review the experience of an accessible event:** a brief covering participation, challenge, pleasure and social connection alongside entry.
- **Choose a career learning project:** a bounded inquiry that connects material making with art, history or disability scholarship.
- **Rewrite a rescue-centred design story:** a narrative that gives its users agency and credits their actual contributions.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Ask about a wanted life in a particular scene:** identify the activity and why it matters to the person. Include pleasures and habits that a functional specification might miss.
2. **Read the encounter between body and world:** inspect the objects, layout, timing and expectations involved. Avoid assigning every difficulty to the person's body.
3. **Find assistance and invention already at work:** ask what the person has adapted, whom they rely on and what they would keep. Obtain permission before documenting that knowledge.
4. **Widen the possible response:** compare a tool, environmental change, different rhythm and chosen human support. Preserve the activity's meaning rather than assuming a technological replacement is equivalent.
5. **Make the collaboration specific:** agree who decides, contributes, receives credit and checks any technical or safety requirements. A design brief is not a medical prescription or structural assessment.
6. **Judge the result in the person's terms:** ask whether they can do more of what they value, with acceptable effort and control. Record remaining barriers without turning their adaptation into an excuse to retain them.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What would the person like to do?
- What do they enjoy about doing it this way?
- What has already been adapted?
- Who is offering help, and who chooses its form?
- Which part of the setting makes the activity awkward?
- What assumptions about time are built in?
- Who needs credit or payment for their contribution?
- What would make this experience satisfying as well as possible?

## Jobs, one by one

### Adapt an everyday routine

You want to help a relative keep reading in a favourite part of the home. Start with their account of the evening, not your preferred furniture. Ask what they like about the location, what is tiring and which help they welcome. Map reaching the books, arranging light, sitting, taking a break and putting things away. The output is a scene description and a few changes for the relative to choose among.

Push for control over the arrangement. A tidy redesign that removes familiar belongings may make the room easier for a helper and less enjoyable for its owner. Cut assumptions that the fastest routine is best. If pain, mobility equipment or physical alterations need professional input, put those questions in the brief. Do not convert an observed preference into a clinical conclusion.

### Write a personal-aid brief

Someone wants to keep writing personal cards. Ask whether the handwriting, paper, privacy or ritual matters. Examine the existing setup and useful adaptations with permission. Compare an ordinary change in organisation, available aids and a collaboration with a relevant specialist. The output specifies the desired action, important qualities, practical constraints and decisions still belonging to the writer.

Push against a showcase invention whose complexity creates more work. Include storage, cleaning and replacement as well as first use. Cut the claim that a digital message is automatically an equivalent substitute. It may be the person's preferred option, but that is their decision. Keep fabrication and clinical questions open for competent assessment rather than supplying improvised equipment instructions.

### Plan a shared activity with different rhythms

Your community craft session rewards whoever finishes fastest. Ask whether the actual purpose is learning, conversation, producing an object or enjoying materials. Identify the few points that require coordination, such as a demonstration or room closure. Offer clear stopping places and ways to continue, pause or observe without public explanations. Return a revised session plan and invitation.

Push for flexibility that participants can understand before arriving. Cut an arrangement where one person receives a conspicuous special exemption. Also cut endless flexibility that leaves organisers unable to close the event. Describe the fixed boundaries honestly and ask participants about the choices within them. The goal is a shared experience with room for difference, not a promise of identical participation.

### Document an adaptation respectfully

You notice a friend's clever homemade arrangement and want to publish it. Ask whether they want a record, for whom and with what credit. If they agree, let them explain the decisions and what remains annoying. Photograph only what they approve. Return a draft that identifies the maker, the intended activity, the context and limits of the adaptation.

Push against a story in which your discovery becomes the main event. Cut language about triumph over a body and claims that readers should copy the object. It may not suit another person or be safe in another setting. Include the option not to publish, and let the maker correct the account. Useful documentation can respect privacy while still making overlooked expertise visible.

### Review the experience of an accessible event

An arts group has considered the entrance but little else. Ask participants what they hope to make, see or enjoy. Trace the experience from invitation to taking part, resting and leaving. Examine how the artistic activity assumes posture, movement, sensory input and timing. The output is an experience brief with choices to discuss with participants and venue specialists.

Push for more than getting someone inside. Ask whether they can choose materials, collaborate and experience the work's challenge and beauty. Cut a separate, lesser activity offered without consultation. Where needs conflict, write them down and seek workable choices. Do not label the event fully accessible or compliant from this review; it raises experiential questions alongside the separate assessments the venue requires.

### Choose a career learning project

You enjoy drawing, making and human questions but cannot name a career that combines them. Choose one ordinary object whose design raises a question you care about. Read a relevant source, speak with a willing user or maker, and produce an illustrated explanation or small research exhibit. Agree on permission and credit before sharing anyone's contribution.

The output is a project brief with the question, collaborators, material and a modest finished form. Push for learning through translation between disciplines. Cut a portfolio exercise that uses someone else's disability merely to make your work look meaningful. Afterwards, examine which work you enjoyed: observing, making, writing, organising or interpretation. That evidence can guide further study without pretending one project establishes professional expertise.

## What she'd push back on

Novelty as the only measure of a tool. A person described only by what they cannot do. Help offered without permission. A custom design dismissed because it will never reach a mass market. A homemade adaptation romanticised while an institution retains an avoidable barrier. A celebration of slowness that ignores someone who wants less effort or faster access.

## What you'll get back

A concise account of the wanted activity and the world around it, followed by the assistance already present, choices worth comparing and a collaboration brief. Expect explicit decisions about agency and credit, a record of technical questions for the right people, and an evaluation based on what the person values. The result should leave someone more able to shape their own experience.
