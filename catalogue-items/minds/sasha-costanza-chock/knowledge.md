# Sasha Costanza-Chock: what they knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Views here are dated to the book chapter, talk and interview cited. Exercises are our adaptations. The official biography uses she/they pronouns; this profile uses they. No unestablished birth year is supplied. [S5]

## In one paragraph

Sasha Costanza-Chock is a researcher and designer associated with the Design Justice Network and the author of Design Justice, published in 2020. Their public biography describes work supporting community-led processes. The practical question is larger than whether a product is pleasant to use: who defines the problem, who bears the costs, and who has power over the result? They explicitly credit design justice to a community of practice, rather than claiming to have invented it. [S5][S3]

## Core ideas

### Follow benefits and burdens separately

In the 2019 talk, Costanza-Chock describes design justice as a framework for examining how design distributes benefits and burdens. It also concerns participation and recognition of community knowledge. [S2]

Our neighbourhood noticeboard exercise makes two lists. The first records what residents, organisers and a sponsor gain. The second records who travels, moderates, supplies information, pays or handles complaints. A convenient board may save the organiser time while assigning invisible upkeep to one resident. Do not subtract that resident's burden from somebody else's gain and call the total positive. Name the distribution and ask the people carrying the work what arrangement they accept. The output is a responsibility map that can alter the plan, including its scope and funding.

### Consultation is different from governing power

The Design Practices chapter distinguishes participation, accountability and ownership. It questions processes that represent absent people through invented personas instead of involving people in consequential decisions. [S1]

Our meeting plan states what residents can decide before inviting them. The colour of a finished sign is a small decision if its location, rules and budget are fixed. Write down which choices remain open, who must approve them and how disagreements will be resolved. Participants should be able to see where their decision goes next. A listening session may still be worthwhile when authority is limited, but call it consultation honestly. Do not promise that people are leading a project when they cannot change its central conditions or challenge an outcome.

### Begin with community work already happening

The Design Justice Network's collective principles call for attention to existing local practices and community-controlled outcomes. These principles belong to the network, not to Costanza-Chock alone. [S4]

Our service proposal begins by asking what the group already uses and why. A phone tree may work because neighbours know whom to call and can notice when someone has not answered. Replacing it with an app could remove that knowledge. Document strengths and frustrations without assuming age or informality makes a practice obsolete. Ask whether design help should support an existing arrangement, relieve a particular burden or do nothing yet. The useful result might be a clearer list, shared training or money for maintenance rather than a new platform.

### Participation can extract knowledge

In the 2022 Microsoft Research interview, Costanza-Chock contrasts community leadership with a design firm collecting workshop ideas and retaining most of the benefit. They explain why a friendly label such as codesign does not settle the power relationship. [S3]

Our survey agreement says what is being asked, how long it takes, who will use the answers and what participants receive. It distinguishes participation from permission to publish a personal story. Budget for contributions where appropriate and agree on credit before collecting material. Give participants a realistic account of what their input can change. Returning a colourful summary does not compensate for a process that never intended to share a decision. If resources cannot support the promised involvement, narrow the research question instead of asking people to donate more unpaid explanation.

### Interacting inequalities can disappear in an average

Costanza-Chock credits Patricia Hill Collins for the matrix of domination and uses it to examine interacting structures of inequality. The book chapter argues that hiring a more diverse workforce is valuable but does not by itself determine whose needs or interests a system serves. [S3][S1]

Our participation review avoids treating a category as a complete description of a person. Someone may face a language barrier alongside inflexible work hours and a requirement for identification. Those conditions interact; three separate invitation statements do not necessarily create a usable route. Ask people about the actual sequence they encounter, without demanding sensitive disclosures that the project does not need. Check whom the feedback record leaves out. An average satisfaction score can conceal a serious burden affecting fewer people, so retain the particular account alongside any summary.

### A harmful premise may need changing before a tool improves

In the interview, Costanza-Chock distinguishes reducing a tool's bias from examining its harms and whether it should be deployed. They insist that the answer depends on domain and context, rather than treating all automated systems alike. [S3]

Our group-tool decision asks whether the proposed collection or ranking is necessary at all. A neighbourhood group wanting to allocate limited places may not need a permanent database of sensitive personal details. Compare simpler arrangements and examine who could contest a mistake. Improving the interface to a questionable rule leaves the rule in place. The output is a decision about purpose, authority, alternatives and conditions for use. It may recommend not building the app. That conclusion needs reasons tied to this service, not a general claim that technology always harms or always helps.

## How they decide

These rules translate the cited work on power, community leadership and accountability into a small-project practice. They are our synthesis, not a checklist published under Costanza-Chock's name. [S1][S2][S4]

- Identify the people most affected by the outcome, including people who cannot easily attend the proposed process.
- Ask what work they already lead and what assistance they actually request before defining an external project.
- Separate an invitation to speak from authority to change a decision. State both in the project agreement.
- Record who benefits, who contributes labour and who takes risks. Do not let good intentions stand in for that account.
- Decide ownership, permissions and the ability to revise the result while collaborators still have negotiating power.
- Include a way to raise problems after delivery. Accountability cannot end with the final presentation.

Our collaboration brief uses a simple decision register. For each consequential choice, record the current proposal, affected people, decision maker, remaining disagreement and next opportunity to change it. For instance, residents may choose locations while a venue owner retains responsibility for permission and maintenance. That is a real division of authority, not an embarrassment to hide. A brief that names constraints lets collaborators decide whether involvement is worthwhile. A brief that conceals them can make a long workshop look participatory while preserving every important decision elsewhere.

## Where they stood

Costanza-Chock's public work connects design with structural power, not only individual usability. Their 2019 talk describes centring people affected by a system while technical specialists support the process. The network principles likewise prioritise community impact over a designer's intentions. [S2][S4]

Our career application concerns someone offering design skills to a local group. Ask what the group is already trying to do, what support would be welcome and whether a useful bounded role exists. A volunteer could improve an existing document under the group's direction rather than arrive with a portfolio concept seeking an audience. Agree on time, decisions and credit. The transfer is about accountable collaboration, not pretending a volunteer can resolve every structural problem. A clear, limited contribution can be more respectful than an ambitious promise whose future upkeep falls to others.

## Where they were wrong or criticised

The chapter itself acknowledges that personas can sometimes help communicate a project and that workforce diversity matters, while arguing that neither guarantees accountability. This matters because a caricature of the work could dismiss every conventional design method as useless. [S1]

The checked sources do not establish an independent named criticism that should be invented here. Our practical limitation is that community control requires specifics. A group may contain disagreements, unequal influence or people absent from its leadership. Calling a decision community-led does not prove that everyone affected agreed. Ask who selected representatives, which voices remain missing and how someone can contest a decision without losing access to the service. Where the project involves binding rights, procurement or data obligations, this profile can frame questions but cannot supply a compliance finding.

## Blind spots to watch

A power analysis can become broad enough to leave a small group unsure what to do tomorrow. Translate each finding into a decision, responsibility or honest limit. If the budget cannot support every form of participation, state the gap and adjust the claim. Do not pretend a revised invitation alone transfers control.

Technical competence still matters. Community authority over a purpose does not establish that a particular tool is secure, safe or feasible. Bring in suitable expertise under an arrangement that preserves accountability. Likewise, a strong political framework does not authorise the assistant to speak for an absent community or invent its preferences. Leave unverified views unfilled until the relevant people choose to contribute.
