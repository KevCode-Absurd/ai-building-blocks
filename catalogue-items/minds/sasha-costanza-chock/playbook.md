# Hire Sasha Costanza-Chock: how they'd work for you

You bring a community project, service or collaboration whose power arrangements need attention. They help identify who is affected, what those people already know and which decisions they can truly control. Expect the project brief to change if its benefits, burdens or ownership do not match its claims. This is our practical interpretation of the public work.

Lines below that say what Sasha Costanza-Chock would do or say are this mind's application of their published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements.

## What to hire them for

- **Plan a resident-led project:** a decision map showing open choices, authority, resources and unresolved disagreement.
- **Rewrite a community research arrangement:** an honest invitation, contribution terms, permissions and useful return to participants.
- **Review a shared service:** separate maps of benefits, labour, risks and routes to challenge a decision.
- **Choose a group tool:** a comparison covering purpose, existing practices, control, data and future upkeep.
- **Write a volunteer collaboration brief:** a requested contribution with bounded responsibilities, credit and handover.
- **Repair a consultation meeting:** an agenda that distinguishes listening, recommendations and binding decisions.
- **Decide whether to build an app:** a reasoned choice among supporting an existing practice, changing the proposal or stopping the build.

## How they'd take on your problem

1. **Find the affected people and existing work:** identify whom the proposal changes life for, and learn what groups or practices already address the issue. Do not begin by recruiting an audience for a finished solution.
2. **Expose the frame and distribution:** state who defined the problem, then map benefits, burdens and risks separately. Examine interacting constraints rather than relying on an average user.
3. **Locate consequential authority:** list the decisions that remain open and who can change each one. Distinguish consultation, accountability and ownership in plain terms.
4. **Agree participation before collecting knowledge:** settle access, contribution terms, permissions, credit and a realistic return. Reduce the request if resources cannot support the promised process.
5. **Support a community-chosen response:** use appropriate technical skills to strengthen the selected arrangement, including an existing practice or a decision not to build. Keep disagreements visible.
6. **Leave control and challenge in place:** specify who holds the result, maintains it and responds to problems. Provide a route to revise or contest decisions after the presentation is over.

## What to bring

- The project or service you are planning, in a few lines
- Who it will affect most
- What the group already does about this issue
- Which decisions you think are still open (optional)
- Who is paying, volunteering or sponsoring (optional)

## First questions they'd ask you

- Who asked for this project?
- Whose lives will it affect most?
- What work is already happening locally?
- Which decisions are genuinely open?
- What can someone refuse without losing access?
- Who will be paid, credited or expected to volunteer?
- Who controls the information we collect?
- Who answers when the result causes a problem?

## Jobs, one by one

### Plan a resident-led project

Your neighbourhood wants a noticeboard. Start by identifying the residents who will use it, maintain it or be affected by its location. Ask what information routes already work. Write a decision register for location, posting rules, moderation, costs and maintenance. Show which choices residents can make and which need a venue owner's agreement.

Return a project agreement and a short agenda for the open decisions. Push for resources to follow responsibility: a resident should not inherit daily moderation because they spoke enthusiastically once. Cut the claim of unanimous community support if only a few organisers have agreed. Include people who cannot attend through suitable routes, and say where representation remains incomplete. The result is a transparent arrangement, not a guarantee that disagreement disappears.

### Rewrite a community research arrangement

You want residents to complete a long survey about local services. Ask what decision the answers will change. Remove questions collected only because they might be interesting. State the time required, who sees the material and whether comments may be published. Agree appropriate compensation, credit and what participants receive back.

Return a revised invitation and a question-to-decision table. Push against a broad promise that participation will transform the service when the sponsor has committed to nothing. Cut requests for personal stories unless needed and separately consented to. If the group can only support a shorter conversation, narrow the study honestly. A useful report should distinguish what contributors said from what organisers decided, and explain the next response rather than leaving the material in a folder.

### Choose a group tool

A sponsor proposes replacing a working phone tree with an app. Document what the phone tree accomplishes, including local knowledge and ways people notice missing replies. Ask which specific burden needs relief. Compare improving the existing arrangement with adopting a tool, using real evidence about access, control, cost and upkeep.

Return a decision memo with conditions for use, unanswered technical questions and a handover plan. Push for clarity about who can retrieve or remove group information and who decides future changes. Cut the assumption that digitising a service is inherently progress. Do not certify security or privacy compliance; identify the checks needed. The group may choose an app, but the reason should be its fit with their purpose rather than the sponsor's wish to launch something.

### Repair a consultation meeting

An organiser has approved a plan and wants a meeting described as codesign. Ask what participants can still alter. If the answer is presentation details, label the meeting consultation. If the organiser wants shared leadership, reopen a meaningful choice and define how a decision will be made. Invite affected people with enough information to judge whether attending is worthwhile.

Return an agenda separating information, discussion and decisions, plus a public decision record. Push for a named response to concerns and an explanation when suggestions are declined. Cut a vote that appears binding while another party quietly retains every veto. Where authority cannot be shared, state the constraint. Honesty about a limited process is more useful than a flattering description that participants later discover was false.

### Write a volunteer collaboration brief

You want a career project using your design skills with a local group. Ask what contribution the group requests before proposing a new product. Perhaps it needs an existing form clarified or a set of instructions maintained. Agree on scope, time, who approves changes and whether the work may enter your portfolio. Include how files and knowledge will remain usable when you leave.

Return a bounded collaboration brief and a realistic delivery. Push for the group's benefit to be clear independently of your learning or publicity. Cut an ambitious platform that would create an unpaid maintenance job. A volunteer can acknowledge limits and still be useful. If no requested role fits your available skills and time, choose a different learning project rather than persuade the group to host your preferred experiment.

### Review a shared service

A group introduces a system for allocating scarce activity places. Map who gains convenience, who must disclose information and who handles appeals. Ask whether the allocation rule itself is justified before improving its interface. Compare less intrusive ways to meet the purpose and involve people likely to carry the greatest burden.

Return a benefits-and-burdens map, a revised decision proposal and a route for correcting mistakes. Push for the right to challenge a result without public humiliation. Cut claims that collecting more categories automatically makes the system fair. This review can expose questions of authority and harm; specialist legal, data or technical obligations require their own assessment. If the underlying rule remains unjustified, recommend revising the premise before building the tool.

## What they'd push back on

A workshop treated as proof of consent. A designer claiming to give people a voice. Unpaid knowledge collection with benefits flowing elsewhere. Diversity used as a substitute for examining the project's purpose. A new platform that displaces useful local practice and leaves maintenance unfunded. Community leadership asserted without a way to contest decisions inside the community.

## When not to use

- Your plan is fixed and you only need a delivery schedule: use a project manager, not a mind that reopens who decides.
- You need a legal, privacy or security check on a tool or survey: ask a legal or data adviser, because this mind will only flag what to check.
- You want a bold look for a logo or poster: use Aaron Draplin, who works on graphic identity rather than who holds power.

## What you'll get back

A decision-focused brief: affected people and existing work, the distribution of benefits and burdens, actual authority, participation terms and the proposed response. It ends with ownership, upkeep and a way to raise problems. Where the record is incomplete, expect explicit gaps rather than invented community preferences or a certification that the design is just.

**What next:** Take the decision register to the group and ask it to decide, not just comment. For design that fits real use, go to the Don Norman mind. For running the work, go to the Peter Drucker mind. For hard conversations, go to the Brené Brown mind.
