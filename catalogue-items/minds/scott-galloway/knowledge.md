# Scott Galloway: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Scott Galloway is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his essays on his newsletter, No Mercy / No Malice, written from 2018 to 2021, his publisher's page for *The Four*, and reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" the year of its post. He also comments often on politics and current events [S1]. We leave those out on purpose, and nothing here tells you what he thinks about news after these posts. When he talks about money, this data set gives his frameworks only. It never gives buy or sell calls.

## In one paragraph

Galloway is a marketing professor at NYU Stern, where he has taught brand strategy and digital marketing since 2002 [S2]. Before and alongside teaching he founded several businesses: the brand consultancy Prophet in 1992, the online gift shop RedEnvelope in 1997, and the research firm L2 in 2010, which Gartner bought in 2017 for $155 million [S1]. His 2017 book *The Four* asked how Amazon, Apple, Facebook and Google became so hard to avoid, and argued they tap the oldest human needs [S8]. His essays mix hard numbers, jokes and strong opinions. He argues that the age of brand-as-promise is fading, that cheap money and monopoly now shape who wins, that hype often hides weak economics, and that ordinary people build security through talent, restraint, time and spreading their bets [S3][S5][S6][S7].

## Core ideas

### The brand age is giving way to the product age

After the Second World War, he argues, the winning formula was a mediocre, mass-made product wrapped in feelings and pushed through heavy TV and radio ads. That built the brand era and the modern marketing industry [S3]. Google changed it. Once buyers could search and compare, a product had to be truly better, and cheap capital rushed to fund products that were [S3]. In 2020 he added that brands once stood in for a product's promise, but search lets people check performance at once [S7].

How he applied it: he called Apple the last great brand of the old model, able to charge far above what an iPhone costs to make because of how it makes owners feel [S3].

### From promises to performance to actions

In 2020 he argued that brand value had moved from what a company promises to how its product performs, and then from words to actions [S7]. A statement with nothing behind it now counts for little. Buyers want to see measurable commitments [S7].

How he applied it: he judged Nike's campaign with Colin Kaepernick as a calculated risk that paid off, because Nike knew its core customers were young and diverse. He saw most companies' public statements at that time as hollow by comparison [S7].

### Laddering: win on a rival's weakness

Galloway uses "laddering" for a positioning move. You pick a strength of yours that is also a rival's weakness and make it the centre of your message. It casts you in a good light and the rival in a bad one [S3]. He notes he uses the word differently from its usual meaning in marketing research [S3].

How he applied it: his example was Apple talking up privacy after the Cambridge Analytica scandal hit Facebook [S3].

### The traits that make a giant

In 2018 he set out what he called the T Algorithm, the traits a company needs to have a shot at a trillion-dollar value [S4]. Among them: it works across borders; it uses data about behaviour to tailor the product; it gets better with use, a trait he named after Benjamin Button; it is seen as likeable; it is seen as a good citizen; and it attracts top talent [S4].

How he applied it: he used the list to argue that Spotify could become the next big tech giant, as its product learns from each listener and has a social side [S4]. Treat that as a 2018 bet, not a settled fact.

### Look through hype to the economics

Galloway is at his sharpest when a story runs ahead of the numbers. He warned that cheap money lets companies with only slightly better products raise huge sums, and that the line between vision and fraud can depend on whether the money keeps coming [S3].

How he applied it: when WeWork filed to go public, he argued that its private value was an illusion. He said it had no scale benefits, pricing power, special technology or network effects, and that it had long lease costs set against short customer contracts [S6]. He mocked its "community-based" profit measure for leaving out the main costs of the business, and he attacked deals between the company and its founder [S6]. The makers of the TV series *WeCrashed* later credited his post as an "emperor has no clothes" moment [S1].

### Big tech has too much power

Galloway has long called for antitrust action to break up Apple, Meta, Amazon and Alphabet [S1]. In 2019 he described Google, Facebook, Amazon and Uber as utility-like monopolies, offered free or below cost and backed by cheap capital, and he argued that the most valuable firms would more and more be monopolies [S3].

How he applied it: in 2019 he opposed Facebook's plans for its Libra currency, citing its record on user privacy [S1].

### Follow your talent and ride a wave

For careers, he pushes against "follow your passion." He says to build a talent people will pay for and to pick your field with care, because the field matters a great deal. An average worker at a fast-growing company can outpace a great one at a slow one over a decade [S5]. Find the strongest wave and ride it [S5].

How he applied it: he points to his own record. RedEnvelope failed slowly, while his consulting firm and a stake in a fast-growing tech company paid off [S5]. He also says choice of partner matters, citing research that married people grow their wealth much faster than single people [S5].

### The algebra of wealth (a framework only)

In 2021 he defined being rich as having passive income greater than your spending, which he calls your burn [S5]. He set out four factors. Focus means building a marketable skill. Stoicism means saving more than you upgrade your lifestyle and avoiding day trading, which he calls gambling. Time means letting small, steady saving compound. Diversification means spreading bets so a mistake hurts but isn't fatal [S5].

How he applied it: he says his worst losses came from putting too much in one place and trading too much. RedEnvelope cost him about 70% of his net worth [S5]. He also credits luck and timing, including being born in California in the 1960s, for part of his success [S5]. This is a general framework, not advice to buy or sell anything.

## How he decides

- Strip out the story and ask if the product is truly better [S3].
- Check for scale, pricing power, special technology and network effects [S6].
- Look for the metric that leaves out the real costs [S6].
- Ask who is protected if it goes wrong, and who isn't [S6].
- Position on a strength that is also your rival's weakness [S3].
- Judge a brand by its actions, not its statements [S7].
- Pick the field before the job, and follow talent over passion [S5].
- Keep spending below what your money earns, and spread your bets [S5].

## Where he stood

**Do brands still matter?** Less than they did as stand-ins for a promise. Product performance and real actions now carry more weight [S3][S7].

**Is big tech good for competition?** No. He has long argued it is too dominant and should be broken up [S1][S3].

**Is high growth funded by cheap money a sign of strength?** Not by itself. Cheap capital can hide weak economics, as he argued WeWork showed [S3][S6].

**Should you follow your passion?** Follow your talent, and choose a growing field [S5].

**How do ordinary people build wealth?** Slowly, through focus, restraint, time and diversification, with some luck. He calls day trading gambling [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The breakup case.** Critics have questioned his argument for breaking up big tech. One analysis at the American Enterprise Institute called his case "not strong" [S1].
- **His own failures.** RedEnvelope went bankrupt, and he says it cost him most of his net worth [S1][S5]. His activist fund is also gone [S1].
- **Conflicts of interest.** In 2019, when he called for Twitter's chief executive to be removed, he disclosed that he owned more than 330,000 Twitter shares [S1]. He disclosed it, but readers should note the stake.
- **Bold predictions.** His style rewards confident calls, such as naming Spotify in 2018 as the likely next giant [S4]. Some calls land, like his WeWork warning [S1][S6]. Others should be checked against what happened.
- **Tone.** His jokes and insults make his posts readable but can turn a fair point into a pile-on [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- He thinks in terms of giants, public markets and venture money. A small, steady business with no plan to raise money may find his lens too big.
- His "brand age is over" view can undersell how much feeling still drives buying. Pair him with someone like Rory Sutherland or Byron Sharp.
- Punchy lines are not proof. Ask for the number behind the joke.
- His money framework is general. It is not tailored financial advice, and this mind will not tell you what to buy or sell.
