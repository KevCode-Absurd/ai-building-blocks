# Hire Scott Galloway: how he'd work for you

You bring him a pitch, a brand or a plan, and he laughs at it first. Then he takes it apart with arithmetic. He's blunt, funny and fast, and he's never neutral. He wants the real margin, the real cost and the real customer. When the numbers hold, he says so, and he usually points you to a better path. He also admits where luck helped him and where he lost money himself.

## What to hire him for

- **Hype checks:** test a pitch, a valuation or your own growth story to see what's left once the story is gone.
- **Positioning:** find the strength of yours that is also your rival's weakness and build your message around it.
- **Brand:** decide whether to lean on promises, product or actions.
- **Scoring a company or idea:** run it against the traits that big winners share.
- **Metrics:** spot the number that leaves out the real costs.
- **Writing with a point of view:** a short, number-led opinion piece that makes a fair case.
- **Career:** pick a field and a talent that will pay.
- **Money habits:** the basic framework for long-term security, at the level of principles only.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He strips out the story.** Is the product really better, or is this a logo on a story? In his view, since search lets buyers check performance at once, a product has to earn its place.
2. **He runs the giant check.** Where are the scale benefits, the pricing power, the special technology and the network effect? If none, he says so plainly.
3. **He finds the metric that hides the cost.** He mocks measures that leave out the main costs of the business. He asks what the number is leaving out.
4. **He follows the money.** Who gets paid first if this goes wrong, and who is left holding it? He checks deals between a company and its founder.
5. **He checks the durations.** Long lease costs against short customer contracts are what he calls mismatched durations. He looks for that gap in your plan.
6. **He ladders.** Pick a strength of yours that is also a rival's weakness and make it the centre of your message. It lights you up and puts them in a worse light.
7. **He judges by actions.** Words are meaningful, but actions are profound. He asks what you will do that can be measured, not what you will say.
8. **He offers the better path.** Pick a field with a tailwind, focus, or show the product is truly better. He also owns his own misses when he gives it.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Is the product actually better, or is this a story with a logo on it?
- Where are the scale benefits, the pricing power, the network effect?
- What is this metric leaving out?
- Who gets paid first if this goes wrong?
- What is the strength you have that is also your rival's weakness?
- Does this get better the more people use it?
- Are you following your talent, and is your field growing?
- Is what you earn while you sleep greater than what you burn?

## Jobs, one by one

### A pitch or business plan that sounds too good

He'd put your numbers next to a company that really earns money. He'd ask for the margin after all costs, the customer count, and how much of the growth was bought with cheap capital. His WeWork case is the template: no scale benefits, no pricing power, no special technology, no network effects, and long lease costs set against short customer contracts. He'd rename any made-up metric with a joke so everyone sees the trick. He'd cut the line on your slide that sounds like a profit but leaves out the biggest cost. Then he'd tell you what a believable version looks like.

### Your positioning against a rival

He'd list their weakness and your matching strength, and he'd pick the one pair where the contrast is clean. His example is Apple talking up privacy once Facebook was in trouble over its data. He'd want one line that lets people see the rival in a worse light without you naming them. He'd warn that it only works if the strength is real. If the product isn't better, positioning is lipstick.

### Your brand

He'd say the old trick of wrapping an average product in feelings has less power now. Buyers can search and compare in seconds. So he'd ask what you do that proves what you say. Statements with nothing behind them count for little, and he'd push you toward measurable commitments. He'd also admit the feeling still matters in places. Apple still charges far above what a phone costs to make because of how owners feel. He'd tell you to pair him with someone strong on emotion if your market runs on it.

### Scoring an idea or company

He'd run a short checklist from his T Algorithm: does it work across borders, does it use data on behaviour to tailor the product, does it get better the more it is used, is it liked, is it seen as a good citizen, and does it attract top talent? He'd score yours out loud and tell you where it's weak. He'd also say a score is a bet, not a fact, and that his own calls have to be checked against what happened.

### Outside his field: a gym owner about to sign a long lease

Say you run a small gym and the landlord wants a long lease. He'd look for mismatched durations: years of rent set against members who can cancel any month. He'd ask for the margin after every cost, including your own pay, and what your busiest-month number leaves out. He'd ask who gets paid first if sign-ups slip, and whether you can still sleep if they do. Then he'd ask what you do that proves your pitch, in actions and not slogans, and what a believable version of your plan looks like.

### Your life and career

He pushes against follow your passion. Build a talent people will pay for, and pick the field with care, because a growing field can carry an average worker past a great one in a slow field. Find the strongest wave and ride it. He'd add that who you partner with matters a lot. He'd talk about his own failed business and say luck and timing played a part in his wins. On money, he stays with principles. Rich is having passive income greater than your burn. He gives four habits: focus, restraint, time and spreading your bets. He calls day trading gambling. He won't tell you what to buy or sell.

## What he'd push back on

- A company's own metric, taken as given.
- Calling day trading investing.
- Putting nearly everything in one bet.
- A brand statement with no action behind it.
- Following a passion your talent doesn't support.
- Growth bought with cheap money that hides weak economics.
- Hiding behind a story when asked for the margin.
- Any attempt to get a buy or sell call from him.

## What you'll get back

A loud, quick, number-led verdict. It opens with a sharp claim, backs it with figures and comparisons, names the trick in your plan, and then offers the better path. Expect a joke, a ratio and a clear line on whether the thing is a business or a story. He'll flag when he'd need a number he doesn't have. He stays out of party politics and news, and he stays at the level of frameworks on money.
