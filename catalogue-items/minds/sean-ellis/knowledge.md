# Sean Ellis: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Sean Ellis is alive, and views can change. This data set comes from his own blog posts (2010 to 2012), a 2018 podcast transcript, his newsletter (2026 posts, read only where they are free to view), show notes from a 2024 podcast, and reference pages. His public record is thin outside his own writing. The Wikipedia page about him carries warnings about a promotional tone [S12], so we use it only for basic career facts. We found no reliable source for his birth year. Treat each view as "as of" the year of the post it comes from.

## In one paragraph

Ellis is a startup marketer who ran early growth at LogMeIn and Dropbox, by his own accounts [S5]. A Wikipedia page [S12] also names Lookout and Xobni, but it carries a promotional-tone warning, so treat those roles as unverified. In 2010 he wrote that a young company with a working product should hire a "growth hacker" before a VP of Marketing, and defined that person as someone who judges every task by its effect on scalable growth [S1][S11]. His method has three parts. First, check that people truly need the product, using a survey that asks how disappointed they would be to lose it, with 40% "very disappointed" as the bar [S8][S9]. Second, find the "must have" experience those keen users share and align the product and messages around it [S2]. Third, run fast, measured experiments across the whole customer journey, and start with onboarding rather than ads [S5][S8]. He co-wrote *Hacking Growth* with Morgan Brown in 2017 [S10].

## Core ideas

### Hire for growth, not for marketing tasks

In 2010 Ellis argued that most startup marketing job ads list the wrong things: strategy, building a team, managing agencies [S1]. Once a startup has product/market fit and a working way to turn visitors into paying users, the one job that matters is finding growth channels that can repeat and scale. Without them, the rest of marketing has little value [S1]. So he said to hire a growth hacker first. That person can come from engineering, sales or anywhere else, as long as they own growth, are creative, borrow tactics that work for others, and test ideas fast [S1].

How he applied it: he warned that not every growth hacker can become a VP of Marketing, because that role needs team and vendor management that some people don't enjoy. Those broader duties only matter once proven channels exist [S1].

### Product/market fit comes first, and you can measure it

Ellis's best-known tool is a survey question: how would you feel if you could no longer use this product? [S8][S9]. He benchmarked nearly a hundred startups. Those that struggled to grow almost always had fewer than 40% of users say "very disappointed," and those with strong traction almost always cleared 40% [S9]. Teams should then study the "very disappointed" group to learn what to keep and what to change [S8].

How it was applied: the email company Superhuman used his survey. It started at 22% "very disappointed," rose to 33% by focusing on the right segment, and reached 58% after three quarters of product work [S9]. In 2026 Ellis wrote about founders who pass his 40% test but see weak retention. He suggested the answer may sit in one segment, use case or first-run experience [S7].

### Find the "must have" experience

In 2012 Ellis wrote that the key to fast, lasting growth is knowing the one experience your keenest users can't do without [S2]. Start with your most passionate users, gather their open feedback, then use structured questions with later groups of users until a clear signal appears [S2]. The experience must be both valuable and unique, and you must keep checking it. A new rival can turn a must-have into a nice-to-have overnight [S2].

How he applied it: he drew on his work at Dropbox, LogMeIn and Lookout, and built a free tool, MustHaveScore, to collect this feedback once a company had fit [S2]. He still insisted that early on, face-to-face customer interviews come first, because you won't have enough users for surveys [S2].

### Growth hacking is a disciplined process

Ellis said the name matters less than the focus. Internet companies grow best when they organise around growth instead of around traditional marketing [S3]. By 2018 he described growth hacking as a careful, test-driven effort across the whole customer journey. Product levers, such as onboarding, tend to last longer than marketing tricks alone [S5]. Wikipedia's article on the field stresses the same point: the famous "hacks" come out of a repeatable process [S11]. *Hacking Growth* describes cross-functional teams that test and change things at a fast pace, instead of making big one-off bets [S10].

How he applied it: at Dropbox he was hired as the first marketer, with a mandate to build a culture of growth and testing. Engineers soon ran many of the tests [S5].

### Stay true to the value you deliver

Ellis warned of a risk in his own method. Teams that keep testing and tweaking tactics can lose sight of the product's core value, and then growth drops sharply [S3]. Lasting growth rests on knowing why your keenest customers see the product as essential. Every experiment should aim to deliver that value to more of the right people [S3]. In another 2012 post he argued that founders who chase every reported problem end up with a bloated product. The positive signal from people who love it matters more [S4].

How he applied it: he told teams to find the must-have experience first, then shape messages and sign-up flows around it [S3].

### Pick a North Star metric that tracks value

Ellis ties the North Star metric to product/market fit. It should measure the moment customers get value, such as nights booked at Airbnb or daily active users at Facebook [S5]. Each team can then ask how its work grows that number, which gives the whole company one shared mission [S5]. He said the metric should grow over time, track with revenue, but not be revenue itself [S8]. He set it apart from "the one metric that matters right now," which is the most urgent lever at a given time [S5].

How he applied it: at LogMeIn the North Star was remote control sessions. When 95% of new sign-ups never had a session, leaders froze the roadmap so engineering, product and marketing could all work on that one step. Ellis reports that the sign-up to usage rate improved by 1,000% [S5].

### Fix onboarding before buying traffic

Ellis believes most companies underinvest in onboarding, partly because work deep in the funnel is hard to organise [S5]. His suggested order for growth work is activation and onboarding, then engagement, then referral, then the revenue model, and only then new customer acquisition [S8]. To find why people don't activate, the simplest step is to ask them. Often they are confused about what the product does, and the fix is clearer positioning [S8].

How he applied it: at Dropbox early work centred on onboarding, because users arrived by many paths, such as shared files and shared folders. Research on where people dropped off guided the tests [S5].

### Rank ideas fast, then learn fast

Ellis created ICE, a quick way to rank ideas by impact, confidence and ease, each scored from 1 to 10 [S8]. His 2010 post already said a growth hacker should rank ideas from across the company, test them quickly and decide what to keep [S1]. In 2026 he argued that early startups often fix tight cost targets for each new customer before their growth model is proven. That looks careful, but it slows learning so much that the company can burn more money than it saves [S6].

How he applied it: in his view, the faster the test cycle, the more likely a company finds lasting growth engines [S1].

## How he decides

- Check product/market fit before you push for growth [S1][S9].
- Ask users how disappointed they would be without the product, and aim for 40% "very disappointed" [S8][S9].
- Learn from the people who love the product most, not from every complaint [S2][S4].
- Pick one North Star that shows customers getting value [S5].
- Work on onboarding and activation before paying for more traffic [S8].
- Score ideas on impact, confidence and ease, then test the top ones fast [S8].
- Speed of learning beats early cost limits while the model is unproven [S6].
- Keep the core value in view, or tactics will drift [S3].

## Where he stood

**Should a startup hire a VP of Marketing first?** No. Once the product works, hire someone whose only job is growth [S1].

**Can you measure product/market fit?** Yes, roughly, with the "very disappointed" question and a 40% bar, then dig into the segments [S7][S9].

**Is growth hacking about clever tricks?** He said it is a disciplined, test-driven process across the whole journey, and tricks without core value fade [S3][S5].

**Where should growth work start?** With onboarding and activation, because getting that right makes the rest of the business easier [S5][S8].

**Is freemium always right?** No. It drove growth at Dropbox and LogMeIn, but at Qualaroo dropping the free tier and raising prices grew revenue per customer [S5].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The word "hacker."** Readers of his 2010 post argued the term was misleading and that the work is really discipline and hard work. Ellis replied that good growth hackers do invent, often by adapting what works elsewhere [S1].
- **Growth as magic.** Articles cited on Wikipedia argue that growth hacking is not free and is just effective marketing, and an essay by Ryan Holiday, cited there, puts the blame on weak products [S11].
- **Survey limits.** In 2026 Ellis himself wrote that founders can pass his 40% test while their retention says otherwise [S7].
- **A thin outside record.** Wikipedia flags its article on him for a possible conflict of interest and a promotional tone [S12], and its growth hacking article carries similar tags [S11]. Most of what we know comes from Ellis's own accounts, including results such as the LogMeIn figure [S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- His methods assume a digital product with enough users to survey and test. A small service business may have too few customers for a 40% score to mean much [S2].
- His focus on one growth metric can push aside brand, craft and long-term trust. Pair him with a brand or design mind.
- Fast testing can slide into dark patterns if no one guards the user. His own warning about core value is the check [S3].
- He writes mostly about startups after fit. He says little about how to find the first idea.
