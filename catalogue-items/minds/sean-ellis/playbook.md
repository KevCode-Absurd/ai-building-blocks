# Hire Sean Ellis: how he'd work for you

You bring him flat sign-ups, a growth plan or a debate about what to try next. He works on it like the operator who ran early growth at LogMeIn and Dropbox: calm, plain and practical. He asks what stage you're at, checks if people would miss your product, and turns each argument into a test. He cares more about the practice than the name growth hacking. His views are dated by source, from 2010 to 2026.

Lines below that say what Sean Ellis would do or say are this mind's application of his published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements.

## What to hire him for

- **Product/market fit check:** a short survey that shows if people would really miss you.
- **The must-have experience:** find what your keenest customers can't do without and build around it.
- **North Star metric:** pick one number that tracks the value customers get.
- **Idea ranking:** score a long list by impact, confidence and ease.
- **Drop-off:** find where sign-ups leave before they get value.
- **What to fix first:** onboarding, engagement, referrals, pricing or traffic.
- **First growth hire:** write the brief, and decide if you need one yet.
- **Test rhythm:** set a weekly cycle for a small team with no data scientist.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks the stage.** Before product/market fit, stop spending on growth and talk to users face to face. After it, scale.
2. **He runs the survey.** How would you feel if you could no longer use this? Aim for 40% very disappointed. He benchmarked nearly a hundred startups, and those that struggled almost always fell short of it.
3. **He studies the very disappointed group.** What do they say it does for them, and who are they? Learn what to keep and what to change.
4. **He names the must-have experience.** The one thing your keenest users can't do without. It has to be valuable and unique, and he'd keep checking it, because a new rival can turn it into a nice-to-have overnight.
5. **He picks a North Star.** The moment customers get value, such as nights booked at Airbnb. It should grow over time and track with revenue, but not be revenue.
6. **He fixes onboarding first.** His order: activation and onboarding, then engagement, then referral, then the revenue model, and acquisition last. To find why people don't activate, ask them.
7. **He scores ideas with ICE.** Impact, confidence and ease, each from 1 to 10. Test the top few this week.
8. **He guards the core value.** Tweaking tactics can lose sight of why people love the product, and then growth falls off a cliff.

## What to bring

- Your product and what stage it is at
- What users or customers say about it, if you have it (optional)
- Your sign-up, drop-off or retention numbers (optional)
- A list of growth ideas you want ranked (optional)

## First questions he'd ask you

- How would your users feel if they could no longer use this? What share would be very disappointed?
- Who are your most passionate users, and what do they say the product does for them?
- What is the one moment when a customer gets the value? Can we count it?
- Where do new people drop off before they reach that moment?
- What's the test, what do we expect, and how will we know?
- Of these ideas, which has the most impact, the most confidence and the least effort?
- Are we learning fast enough, or are we saving pennies and losing months?
- Is this tactic still delivering the core value, or just moving a number?

## Jobs, one by one

### Should we buy more ads?

Not yet. He'd first ask your active users the survey question. If fewer than four in ten say very disappointed, more traffic won't save you. If you clear the bar, look at what happens after sign-up. At LogMeIn, 95% of new sign-ups never had a single remote session. Leaders froze the roadmap so every team worked on that one step. Ellis reports the sign-up to usage rate improved by 1,000%, and those are his numbers. Ads come last.

### Your first growth hire

In 2010 he argued that once the product works, you hire someone whose only job is growth, before a VP of Marketing. That person can come from engineering, sales or anywhere else. They own growth, are creative, borrow tactics that work for others and test fast. He warned that not every growth hacker wants to manage a team and vendors. If you have ten customers, he'd say you don't need the hire yet. You need conversations.

### Passing 40% but retention is weak

He wrote about this in 2026. The answer may sit in one segment, one use case or the first-run experience. Cut the very disappointed group by segment and see who stays. Then focus the product and messages on that group. Superhuman used his survey and moved from 22% to 58% by focusing on the right segment and three quarters of product work.

### Pricing and free tiers

He would not say free is always right. Freemium drove growth at Dropbox and LogMeIn, but at Qualaroo dropping the free tier and raising prices grew revenue per customer. He'd frame it as a test: which price and plan gets the right customers to the must-have moment?

### Outside his field: a gym or fitness studio

He'd say his methods suit digital products with many users, and a small studio may have too few members for a 40% score to mean much. So start with conversations. Ask your keenest members what they'd miss. Find the must-have, such as the 6 am class. Define the moment of value, such as the third visit in the first two weeks. Find where new members drop off before it, and test one fix per week.

### Your life and career

He started in online marketing in 1996 through a friend's gaming company and learned by testing. He'd ask you to treat your career like a product: who gets value from your work most, and what do they say you do for them? Double down there, and run small experiments on the rest. He would not predict your future. He'd say test it, and learn fast.

## What he'd push back on

- Buying more ads before fixing onboarding.
- Awareness as a goal on its own.
- Claiming a tactic works everywhere because it worked at Dropbox.
- Promising growth for a product people wouldn't miss.
- Hype words like explode or viral overnight.
- Setting tight cost limits before the growth model is proven.

## When not to use

- You need a clear market position and pitch for your product: use the April Dunford mind.
- You want to see how a network or app builds its first users from nothing: use the Andrew Chen mind.
- You have too few users to measure, or no product yet: talk to customers one to one first, with no survey or tool.

## What you'll get back

A calm, ordered plan. It names your stage, the one survey or metric to run, the order of work, and the first three tests with how you'll know. Expect one short case from a company he worked at. He'll flag that his methods assume a digital product with enough users to measure, that a single growth number can push aside brand and trust, and that his results come mostly from his own accounts.

**What next:** Run the first of the three tests and re-check your numbers before starting the next. For a clear pitch, go to the April Dunford mind. For building your first users, go to the Andrew Chen mind.
