# Seth Godin: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Seth Godin is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from his own blog posts, written from 2005 to 2022, his own about page, and public reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" the post it comes from. His books are still in copyright, so we sum them up in our own words and keep the few quotes short. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events after the posts we read.

## In one paragraph

Godin is an American author, teacher and former dot-com founder [S1]. He sold his first web company, Yoyodyne, to Yahoo in 1998. He then became one of the best-known writers on marketing, with books such as Permission Marketing (1999), Purple Cow (2003), Tribes (2008) and This Is Marketing (2018) [S1]. He has posted on his blog every day for more than a decade, close to 10,000 posts in all [S2]. His ideas fit together. Earn attention instead of taking it [S3]. Make the thing itself worth talking about [S4]. Serve the smallest group that can keep you going, and delight them [S11][S12]. Lead people who share a goal, and help them meet each other [S6]. Then ship the work, even when fear says wait [S7]. He describes himself as a teacher who does projects [S2].

## Core ideas

### Permission marketing

In a 2008 post he defined permission marketing as the privilege of sending messages that people expect, that are personal to them and that matter to them [S3]. He treated attention as a valuable asset. Once a person gives it, you should not waste it, because they can't take it back [S3]. He set a strict test for real permission. People would notice, and complain, if your messages stopped [S3]. He compared earning it to dating. You build trust step by step, and you keep the promise you made when people signed up [S3].

How he applied it: his company Yoyodyne, launched in 1995, ran contests, online games and scavenger hunts that people chose to join. Yahoo bought it in 1998 for $29.6 million, and he became Yahoo's vice president of direct marketing [S1].

### Be remarkable (the purple cow)

Purple Cow came out in 2003 [S1]. Its idea is that a product spreads when it is worth talking about. In 2005 he made clear that he meant the product itself, not a stunt that draws eyes to an ordinary product [S4]. He also warned about sequels. A company with a hit tends to add features to version two and keep cashing in. That rarely makes it more remarkable. A new breakthrough comes when you leave your comfort zone and build something new [S5].

How he applied it: when a casino painted real cows pink and purple as moving billboards in 2005, he wrote that he had nothing to do with it. He called the stunt harmless and funny, but said it wasn't Purple Cow marketing, because the stunt was the story, not the product [S4].

### The smallest viable audience

In 2017 he argued that trying to reach everyone tends to delight no one. Watering down a message for the average person makes it hard to stand out [S11]. His answer is to pick the smallest market that can sustain you and that you can serve well. Serving that group well pushes you to make better work, and word of mouth follows [S11]. In 2022 he called the small audience a stepping stone, not a second-best choice. Being specific about who you serve, and holding a high standard, matters more than size [S12].

How he applied it: in the 2022 post he pointed to a 14-seat restaurant in New York and an appliance repair worker who built a name in one narrow niche. Each chose its customers and gave them enough delight that they told people like themselves [S12].

### Tribes: lead, and help people connect

In 2008 he wrote that shouting for attention had stopped working. What worked was leading a small, engaged group toward something it wanted [S6]. The biggest winners online gave groups of people a way to connect with each other. People want to connect, and they want you to do the connecting [S6]. So marketers should stop acting like broadcasters and start acting like leaders. He saw the same pattern in fashion, business-to-business firms, charities and churches [S6].

In 2013 he named the strongest pull of all: the wish to belong to a group you admire. Past features and benefits, people want respect from the group they hope to join. They look for cues about how such people act. The standards that count are that group's, not the mass market's [S9].

How he applied it: he held up Bill Niman, who left a large meat co-op to run a farm that served an even narrower market, as proof that a niche is often enough [S6].

### Ship it, and quiet the lizard brain

He borrowed the idea of "the resistance" from the writer Steven Pressfield and tied it to the fearful, ancient part of the brain [S7]. In his 2010 post, this lizard brain pushes us to hold back, play safe and give up just as a project nears the finish. Groups suffer from it too, in the form of endless meetings, late launches and dull products built to avoid risk [S7]. The fear isn't going away, so the job is to notice it and ignore it [S7].

How he applied it: his own habit fits the idea. He has put out a blog post every day for more than a decade [S2].

### Pick yourself

In 2011 he told readers to stop waiting for gatekeepers such as publishers, record labels, talk show hosts and HR departments to choose them [S8]. Waiting to be picked is a habit our culture teaches. Their power is fading, and people already have the tools they need. Problems worth solving are everywhere [S8].

How he applied it: he cited Amanda Hocking, who was earning about a million dollars a year by publishing her own books on the Kindle [S8]. His own start fits too. In 1986 he left the software firm Spinnaker and funded a book packaging business with $20,000, working from a studio flat in New York [S1].

### The Dip: quit the right things

The Dip (2007) is about the low point in any project, when you must decide whether to keep going [S13]. He argued that a big problem is failing to see when a dip is really a cul-de-sac, a dead end that will never get better [S13]. To be the best in the world at something, you push through the right dip. Compromise and fear are the enemies of that [S13].

How he applied it: in April 2007 he ran a series of posts and a contest about famous quitters who left one thing to become the best at another. His list included Gandhi, Bill Gates and Jeff Bezos, and Enzo Ferrari, who quit a job and founded his company at 48 [S13].

### Marketing in four steps

In 2016 he boiled marketing down to four steps [S10]. First, invent something worth making. Second, build it so the people it serves gain from it and care about it. Third, tell the story to the right people in a way that works. Fourth, keep showing up for years to lead, organise and earn trust. He said the last step is the one most often skipped [S10].

How he applied it: he created the altMBA in 2015 as a 30-day online workshop, and says more than 60,000 people have taken his online courses [S2]. He also calls the Carbon Almanac, an all-volunteer project he edits, the most important project of his career [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask who it's for. If you can't name them, you aren't ready [S11][S12].
- Choose the smallest group that can sustain the work, then delight them [S11].
- Earn permission before you send. Then keep the promise [S3].
- Make the product itself the thing worth talking about. Skip the stunt [S4].
- Don't count on version two to stay remarkable. Make something new [S5].
- Lead the group and help its members connect [S6].
- Before quitting, ask whether this is a dip or a dead end [S13].
- When fear gets loud near the finish, ship anyway [S7].
- Don't wait to be chosen [S8].

## Where he stood

**Should you try to reach everyone?** No. Aim at the smallest market you can imagine, and serve it so well that word spreads [S11][S12].

**Is a clever stunt the same as being remarkable?** No. The thing itself should be what people talk about [S4].

**Interrupt people, or ask first?** Ask first. Permission is earned slowly and lost fast [S3].

**Broadcaster or leader?** Leader. People want you to help them find each other [S6].

**Quit or keep going?** Quit dead ends early and without guilt. Push through the dip that leads to being the best at something [S13].

**Wait for a break, or make one?** Make one. No gatekeeper is coming to pick you [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **"Advertising is broken."** In 2011 Byron Sharp of the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute argued that Purple Cow claimed marketing was broken by clutter and ad dodging, while research showed ads still worked about as well as ever [S14].
- **Old advice, new word.** Sharp also said the book mostly swapped "good" for "remarkable," and that its advice repackaged standard marketing teaching [S14]. He called it entertaining but shallow [S14]. Sharp leads a school of thought that disagrees with Godin, so read this as a rebuttal, not a neutral review.
- **Stories over data.** Much of the support for his ideas comes from hand-picked success stories, such as the 14-seat restaurant or the self-published author [S8][S12]. These show what can work, not how often it works.

## Blind spots to watch

- "Be remarkable" says where to go, not how to get there. Pair him with someone who can test ideas and size markets [S14].
- The smallest viable audience can become a reason to stay small. Ask how the small group grows [S11].
- Permission marketing assumes people will sign up. A brand nobody knows yet may need reach first. Pair him with Byron Sharp.
- His examples skew toward creators, authors and small firms. Big firms with shareholders face other limits.
- "Ship it" fits creative work. It fits less well where a failed launch hurts people, such as health or money.
