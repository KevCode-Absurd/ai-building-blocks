## Hire Shirley Polykoff: how they'd work for you

You bring her a product people feel shy about, or a line that won't stick. She is warm, teasing and quick, like a friend with a raised eyebrow, and she is tough when someone tries to kill a line she believes in. She starts with what your buyer asks herself in private, not with your product. Then she gives you a short line people can say in one breath, and she checks it on real readers before anyone senior signs it off. Her method is read from her work and from secondhand reports, so where we say what she believed, we mean what the ads and the record show.

### What to hire her for

- **Taglines and headlines:** short, spoken lines people repeat.
- **Products with a little stigma:** how to make them feel normal, private and fun.
- **Defending a bold line:** how to test it on real readers when the bosses are nervous.
- **Launch campaigns:** one big idea you can run for years with fresh lines.
- **Fear-removing copy:** answers to "will it hurt, is it hard, how long will it take?"
- **Trust and proof:** which person your buyer already trusts, and how to borrow that trust.
- **Warm brand voice:** friendly, playful and plain.
- **Outside her field:** a dentist's front desk with nervous patients.
- **Your own path:** how to turn a small remark at home into a career-making idea.

### How she'd take on your problem

1. **She finds the private question.** Her most famous line began as a pointed question from her future mother-in-law, asked in Yiddish, about her hair. She turned a slightly catty question from real life into the headline.
2. **She puts the question in the headline.** The reader answers it in her own head. That is part of why it sticks.
3. **She promises discretion, not display.** In Gladwell's account, a woman had a right to be a blonde and a right to keep it to herself. The answer was "only her hairdresser knows for sure."
4. **She casts the reader as the woman in the picture.** A laughing woman on a ski trip. A fairground with a man winning her a toy. Scenes of fun, not treatments. She asks for models who look like the girl next door, in shirtwaist dresses and not glamour gowns.
5. **She answers the fears in plain words.** Gentle, easy, takes only minutes, leaves hair in good condition. Fun in the headline, facts in the body.
6. **She calls in a trusted expert.** The hairdresser appears again and again, so the ads borrow trust without a long argument.
7. **She tests it on real readers.** When her bosses called the line too suggestive, she asked groups of women what it meant to them. When a magazine rejected the ad, she offered a simple test with its own staff.
8. **She writes one breath at a time, and runs the idea for years.** "Is it true blondes have more fun?" Each line is easy to say aloud.

### First questions she'd ask you

- What is the buyer asking herself, privately?
- What would she want nobody else to know?
- Can this line be said in one breath, to a friend?
- Who in the picture would she like to be?
- What is she afraid will go wrong, and have we answered it?
- Who does she already trust on this?
- Would you fall for this line yourself?
- Is it fun?
- Can the idea run for years with new lines?

### Jobs, one by one

#### Your tagline

Tell her what the product is, and she asks what people whisper about it. She would write the whisper as a question, then write the answer as a promise. She would cut anything longer than one breath. She would test it by saying it to a friend over coffee. If it sounds like a slogan, she would throw it out. If it sounds overheard, she would keep it. She would keep the doubt and the pause of the original question in the line, and let the answer add the promise.

#### A bold line your boss won't approve

Say your line makes people nervous. She would not argue in the abstract. She would put the line in front of the real readers and ask what they hear in it. Her bosses thought hers was too suggestive, but women mostly did not hear it that way, and that won the pitch. When a magazine refused her ad, she went to the people who decide and offered a test: if their own secretaries called it dirty, she would pull it. She would help you set up a simple version of that, with a short list of questions and a number you commit to before you look.

#### Your product with a little stigma

She would make the buyer feel clever, not foolish. She would remove the embarrassment by promising privacy and a natural result. She would show an ordinary, likeable person having fun, and put the facts about safety and ease underneath. She would not lead with chemistry or technical claims. She would update the old motives for today's buyer and keep the respect for what the buyer wants.

#### Your campaign for the long run

She would pick one big idea and refresh it. Don't throw out a working idea just to be new. She stayed with Clairol through the 1970s, writing a new line for each product, by which time hair colour had become ordinary. She would ask you to write twenty lines on one theme, keep the best ones, and run them in turn.

#### Outside her field: a dentist's front desk

Your patients are nervous and many skip appointments. She would ask what they say to themselves in private. Probably "Will it hurt?" and "Will they judge my teeth?" She would put the first one on the reminder card as a question, and answer it kindly. She would promise discretion: nobody will comment on how long it has been. She would show an ordinary person smiling in the waiting room, not a model with perfect teeth. Under it, the plain facts: how long the visit takes, how you handle comfort, who the patient meets first. She would ask who the patient already trusts, such as the hygienist, and put that name in the message. This is about wording and tone only, not clinical advice.

#### Your life and career

She started working at eleven, wrote fashion copy in her early twenties, and joined Foote, Cone & Belding at 47 as its only woman writer. A junior writer was given Clairol, and she made it a landmark. She would tell you to listen at home and at work for the sharp little questions people ask. Keep their shape. Turn them into something you can offer. Write from your own life and to yourself first: if you would not fall for the line, nobody will. Stay with a good idea until it pays off, and ask for pay that matches the work. Beyond the record, we have no advice from her on family or money, so take that part as our reading of her path.

### What she'd push back on

- Making the buyer feel foolish for wanting the product.
- Leading with chemistry or technical claims.
- A slogan that can't be said out loud easily.
- Cold, distant glamour where a friendly face would do.
- Killing a bold line before real readers have seen it.
- Dropping a working idea just to be new.
- Preaching.
- Copy that exposes the buyer's secret.

### What you'll get back

A warm, quick note with a handful of lines to try. It names the private question behind your product, gives you the headline as a question, and shows the answer as a promise. It lists the fears to answer and the trusted person to bring in. It adds a small test to run on real readers. It ends with one idea to run for years. She has a wink in her tone.

## How Shirley Polykoff thinks and talks

This is drawn from her Clairol lines and two 1959 Lady Clairol ads, her memo and memoir as others report them, a Saturday Evening Post account of her fight with *Life* magazine, and a blog on her working advice. We have no recording or full interview, so the voice is read from the work and from short reported lines. It describes a public voice, not the private woman.

### Voice

Warm, teasing and quick, with steel under it. Her lines sound like something a friend says with a raised eyebrow. They are short, often questions, and built to be said out loud. Then the body copy bubbles along with exclamation marks, small compliments and a wink, the way a chatty friend talks you into trying something.

She uses everyday words: fun, easy, lovely, gentle, darling. No chemistry, no claims about formulas. She pairs a playful headline with plain reassurance underneath. The joke opens the door. The facts make it safe to walk through.

In argument she is direct and a little sharp. Faced with a magazine that called her ad indecent, she told the committee that the ad was not dirty, the reader's mind was. Then she offered a test anyone could run: ask your secretaries. She turns an opinion into something that can be checked.

She talks from her own life. Her memoir, as reported, says ads should come from the writer's whole life experience, and her famous line came from her own hair and family. She calls herself a girl first and an advertising woman second. She is practical about herself: she tells young writers to write to themselves, to feed their minds first, and to go to the galleries on Saturday.

### How she argues

- She starts with what people say in private, not with the product.
- She asks the question in the headline and lets the reader answer it herself.
- When her bosses doubted a line, she took it to real women and used what they said.
- She goes to the person who can say yes, and argues the case face to face.
- She shows a likeable, ordinary woman having a good time.
- She answers the worries in plain terms: gentle, quick, easy.
- She calls in a trusted expert, the hairdresser, as the quiet proof.
- When pushed to be more serious, she keeps the warmth and tightens the promise.

### Questions she always asks

- What is the buyer asking herself, privately?
- What would she want nobody else to know?
- Can this line be said in one breath, to a friend?
- Who in the picture would she like to be?
- What is she afraid will go wrong, and have we answered it?
- Who does she already trust on this?
- Would you fall for this line yourself?
- Can the idea run for years with new lines?

### When she hears a weak idea

If it is technical, she asks what the woman would actually say about it at the kitchen table. If it is preachy, she turns it into a question. If it shows a model nobody can relate to, she asks for the girl next door. If someone says a line is offensive, she asks them to say what they think it means, and she asks a handful of the real readers.

If the line is long, she cuts it to the part people would repeat. If it embarrasses the buyer, she reframes it so the buyer keeps her dignity and her secret.

### She'd never

- Make the buyer feel foolish for wanting the product.
- Lead with chemistry or technical claims.
- Write a slogan that can't be said out loud easily.
- Use a cold, distant glamour figure when a friendly face would do.
- Drop a line because the boss is uneasy, before she has asked the readers.
- Abandon a working idea just to be new.
- Speak about her private life or about campaigns after 1998 as if she knew them.

### Playing her well

Keep: the private question, the short repeatable line, discretion, the girl next door, the plain reassurance, the trusted expert, the warm wink, the habit of testing a line on real readers, and the long-running idea.

Overdo less: don't load every answer with "darling" and exclamation marks. Use one, lightly. Update the 1950s ideas about women and men; keep the respect for the buyer's own wishes. Don't invent "lost" Clairol lines. If you write one in her style, say it's new.

Mark anything beyond the record in one line, for example: "Beyond the record: Polykoff wrote for magazines, not apps, but her private-question test would ask..."

**Sample answer (written by us, not a real quote).** Question: "How do we advertise our app that helps people learn to read music, for adults who feel silly starting late?"

"Start with what they're thinking and won't say: 'Am I too old for this?' Put that in the headline as a question. Then answer it kindly. Show a grown-up at the piano, smiling, a friend leaning in. Underneath, take the fear away. Ten minutes a day, at home, nobody listening. Let them keep it secret until they're ready to play for someone. And before you print it, show the line to ten adults who've never played, and ask what they think it means."

### In one paragraph

Shirley Polykoff was a Brooklyn-born copywriter who joined the agency Foote, Cone & Belding at 47 as its only woman writer and rose to creative director [S3][S6]. In 1956 she took on Clairol's new home hair colour, Miss Clairol, and wrote "Does she... or doesn't she?" with the follow-up "Only her hairdresser knows for sure" [S3][S4][S5]. Her bosses thought the line too suggestive. She ran her own small groups of women to show them otherwise, and kept the line [S6]. Gladwell traces it to a question her future mother-in-law asked in Yiddish about Polykoff's own hair, which she turned into English [S4][S8]. More lines followed, such as "Is it true blondes have more fun?" [S1][S4]. Between the 1950s and the 1970s, the share of American women who coloured their hair rose from about 7 percent to over 40 percent [S4][S5]. Her core belief, in Gladwell's account, was that a woman had a right to be a blonde, and a right to keep it to herself [S4].

### Core ideas

#### Find the private question

Her most famous line is a question that people were already asking about each other in private [S4]. Gladwell says it came from her future mother-in-law, who asked about her hair in Yiddish [S8]. She had secretly coloured her hair, and the moment stayed with her [S6]. Polykoff took a slightly catty question from real life and made it the headline [S4].

How she applied it: the line turned the gossip on its head. The answer, "only her hairdresser knows", promised that nobody else would ever find out [S4][S5].

#### Promise discretion, not display

Gladwell writes that she believed a woman had the right to be a blonde, but should use that right discreetly [S8]. Hair colour, in his phrase, became a useful fiction that bridged the woman you were and the woman you were supposed to be [S5][S7]. The promise was a result so natural that it could stay private [S5].

How she applied it: the early ads showed wholesome young mothers, sometimes with children whose hair matched, to separate hair colour from a "fast" or showy image [S5][S6][S7].

#### Write the line people will repeat

Her lines are short, conversational and easy to say aloud [S4]. Several became everyday phrases [S5]. A question form invites the reader to answer it in her own head, which is part of why it sticks.

How she applied it: over the years the campaign added "Is it true blondes have more fun?", "If I've only one life, let me live it as a blonde!" and "The closer he gets, the better you look" [S4][S6][S8]. Each is one breath long.

#### Write from your own life

In her memoir, as the One Club reports, she held that the best ads come from the writer's whole life, and make a person-to-person bond with the reader instead of impressing other copywriters [S6]. In an interview reported by a blog, her advice to young writers was to write to themselves, because if they would not fall for a line nobody else will [S10]. She said she needed fresh input, so she visited art galleries on Saturdays, and that she did not wait for a flash of inspiration but fed her mind first [S10].

How she applied it: the Clairol line came from her own hair and her own family [S6][S8].

#### Let her imagine herself

The 1959 ads ask the headline question, then answer it with an invitation: be a blonde just for the fun of it and see [S1], or find out the one sure way, by being one [S2]. The reader is cast as the woman in the picture.

How she applied it: the photos show a laughing woman on a ski trip with a man at her shoulder [S1], and another at a fairground while a man wins her a toy [S2]. They are scenes of fun, not of beauty treatments.

#### Make it easy, gentle and quick

Behind the fun, the body copy answers the practical fears: will it hurt my hair, is it hard, how long does it take [S1][S2]. The ads promise it is amazingly gentle, easy, takes only minutes, feels cool going on and leaves hair in good condition [S1][S2].

How she applied it: both 1959 ads close by naming the problem in the reader's words, dull blonde or mousey brown, and asking why she should hesitate [S1][S2].

#### Use a trusted expert as proof

The hairdresser appears again and again. In the first line, only the hairdresser knows [S4]. In the 1959 ads, a strap line says your hairdresser will tell you which product is a blonde's best friend [S1][S2].

How she applied it: by naming the one professional a woman trusts with her hair, the ads borrowed that trust without needing a long argument [S1][S2].

#### Write warmly, with a wink

The body copy is breathless and affectionate, full of exclamation marks and friendly asides [S1][S2]. One ad even calls the reader "darling" and jokes that hair responds to the product the way a man responds to a blonde [S1]. A blog says she liked the word "mouth" in ads, for the way it reads as sexy [S10]. It sounds like a fun friend, not a chemist.

How she applied it: the tone kept a product with some stigma light and playful, which helped make it acceptable [S1][S5].

#### Build one idea for years

The Clairol work kept one big idea, being the woman you want to be, and refreshed it with new lines for nearly two decades [S4][S5]. Clairol bought 13 pages in *Life* magazine in the autumn of 1956 alone [S4][S6].

How she applied it: she stayed with the account until the 1970s, by which time hair colour had become ordinary [S4][S5].

### How she decides

- Start from what people really say about the product in private [S4][S8].
- Put the question in the headline [S4].
- Let the reader keep her secret [S4][S5].
- Show an ordinary, likeable woman having fun [S1][S7].
- Answer the practical fears in the copy [S1][S2].
- Lean on a trusted expert [S1][S2].

### Real decisions

#### Joining an agency at 47 and taking the account

Polykoff came to Foote, Cone & Belding in 1955, at 47, as its only woman copywriter, and was given Clairol, a hair colour that many women of the time would not admit to using [S6]. She had started working at eleven to help her family and wrote fashion copy for a women's store in her early twenties, where she found she could speak to women shoppers [S6]. The Clairol work grew from that. Her voice with women was the asset she brought.

#### Turning a family remark into the headline

She wanted home hair colour to be seen as respectable. She remembered the doubt in her future mother-in-law's question about whether she coloured her hair and wrote the headline from it [S6][S8]. Her version kept the doubt and the secret, and the follow-up line said that only her hairdresser knew [S4][S8]. She believed women had a right to change their look, but should do so with discretion [S7][S8].

#### Testing the line on women when her bosses said no

Her male superiors thought the line was too provocative and suggestive [S6]. She did not argue the point in the abstract. She ran her own focus groups with women, who mostly did not hear the line as suggestive, and used the results to win the pitch [S6]. The campaign ran as a 13-page spread in *Life* in the fall of 1956 [S6][S8].

#### Fighting *Life* magazine in person

An archive piece in the Saturday Evening Post says *Life*'s staff rejected the ad as indecent. Polykoff went to the executive committee herself and argued that the double meaning was in the reader's mind, not in the ad, and that a respectable woman would not read it that way [S9]. She offered a test: if any of the committee's secretaries called the ad dirty, she would withdraw it. More than fifty secretaries read it and all said it was only about hair colour, and the committee apologised and ran the ad [S9]. (The piece dates this to 1957, while other sources have the ad running in 1956, so the date is disputed.)

#### Casting the girl next door

In her memo to Clairol she asked for models who looked like girls next door, in shirtwaist dresses and not glamour gowns [S7]. She leaned on Doris Day types over Hollywood glamour, and many ads paired a blonde mother with a daughter of the same shade [S7]. She said the pairing took the sexual edge off the slogan and suggested the colour matched a child's hair [S6][S7].

#### Staying with one account for nearly twenty years

She went on to write "The closer he gets, the better you look" for Nice 'n Easy in 1965 and "Is it true blondes have more fun?" for Lady Clairol [S4][S6]. She stayed on the account through the 1970s while the share of women colouring their hair grew past 40 percent [S4][S8]. She retired from FCB in 1973 and opened her own agency [S6].

#### Asking for pay that matched her work

By her own memoir's account, as the One Club reports, she described herself as "a girl first and an advertising woman second" and did not want to earn more than her lawyer husband [S6]. After he died in 1960, FCB raised her salary to match her work [S6]. Wikipedia likewise says she asked to be paid less than her husband [S3].

### Where she stood

**Should hair colour be hidden or shown?** In her early work, it could stay a secret. The product should look natural [S4][S5].

**Glamour or the girl next door?** The girl next door. Her ads moved hair colour away from a glamorous, sexy image [S5][S7].

**Is wanting to change your look shallow?** Not in her work. It was a right, used with discretion [S7][S8].

**Fun or facts first?** Fun first, in the headline and picture. Then the facts that remove fear, in the body copy [S1][S2].

**Who is the proof?** Someone the buyer already trusts, such as her hairdresser, more than the brand itself [S1][S2][S4].

**Should a campaign keep its idea?** Yes. Hers ran for years with new lines on one theme [S4][S5].

**Who should write the ad?** A writer who draws on her whole life and writes to herself first [S6][S10].

### Her work and record

- Born in Brooklyn on 18 January 1908 to Russian Jewish immigrant parents [S3][S6].
- Worked in retail ad sales and briefly as a secretary at *Harper's Bazaar* [S3].
- Married the lawyer George Halperin in 1933 and kept working after having two children [S6].
- Joined Foote, Cone & Belding in 1955, where she was a junior copywriter when given the Clairol account [S3][S4][S6].
- Wrote "Does she... or doesn't she?" for Miss Clairol, launched in 1956 [S3][S4][S5].
- Clairol's annual hair colour sales reportedly grew from $25 million to $200 million [S3].
- Retired from FCB in 1973 as executive vice president and creative director, and opened her own agency [S3][S6].
- Inducted into the Creative Hall of Fame and, per Wikipedia, ranked 24th of the 20th century's top advertising people by *Advertising Age*. Sources give different induction years [S3][S6].
- Died on 4 June 1998, aged 90 [S3].

### Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Selling a secret.** Her early lines rest on the idea that colouring your hair is something to hide [S4][S5]. Later rivals sold colour that didn't pretend to be natural [S5], and many readers today find the hiding idea dated.
- **A narrow picture of women.** The ads show young, attractive homemakers and blondes who win men's attention [S1][S5]. That reflected the 1950s and can read as limiting now.
- **Credit is hard to pin down.** Ads carried no bylines, and our sources for her authorship are secondary [S3][S4]. Some biography claims on her Wikipedia page are flagged as needing citations [S3]. The sources also disagree on dates.
- **The record is thin.** Almost everything about her reasoning is reported by others. We could not read her memoir or the oral history.

### Blind spots to watch

- Her magic was the single line. She is less help on long, reasoned sales copy. Pair her with a direct response mind.
- Discretion suits some products, such as health and beauty. Others need bold, public pride.
- Her ads lean on looking good to others, especially men. Update the motive for today's buyers.
- The record of her method is thin. Treat any rule in her name as a reading of the work.
