# Simon Sinek: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Simon Sinek is alive, and his views can change. This data set comes from transcripts of two of his TED talks [S1][S2], pages on his own website [S3][S4][S5], a write-up of a 2020 keynote [S6] and reference pages [S7][S8][S9][S10][S11]. Treat each view as "as of" the talk or book it comes from, from 2009 to 2020. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events since.

## In one paragraph

Sinek is a former advertising man who turned into a speaker and writer on leadership [S8]. His big idea, the Golden Circle, says that leaders and groups who inspire people start by saying why they do what they do, then how, then what [S1][S3]. His 2009 talk on it, given at TEDxPuget Sound, became one of the most-watched TED talks [S7][S8]. His later work moved from messages to people: leaders create a "circle of safety" so that their teams trust them and each other [S2][S5]. Most recently he borrowed James Carse's idea of finite and infinite games and argued that business has no final whistle, so leaders should play to keep playing, not to win [S4][S10]. Critics accept much of the advice but doubt the brain science he uses to explain it [S11].

## Core ideas

### Start with why

Every group knows what it does, and some know how they do it. Very few can say why: their purpose, cause or belief [S1][S3]. Profit is a result, not a why [S1][S3]. Most people talk from the outside in, starting with the product. Inspiring leaders talk from the inside out, starting with the belief [S3]. He calls the three rings, why, how and what, the Golden Circle [S3]. Your "how" is what you do when you live your why at your best, and the two together are like a group's fingerprint [S3].

How he applied it: in the 2009 talk he contrasted a normal computer ad, which lists features, with the way he says Apple speaks, opening with a belief in challenging the status quo and only then showing the product [S1]. He argued this is why people accepted Apple in new product types while equally able rivals such as Dell and Gateway struggled [S1].

### People don't buy what you do

He argues that people buy into a belief, and that the product becomes proof of it [S1][S3]. He tied this to Everett Rogers's law of diffusion of innovations: innovators and early adopters, about 16% of a market, buy because a product fits what they believe. The mass market follows only after them [S1].

How he applied it: TiVo, in his telling, had strong technology and money but led with features like pausing live TV, so it never became profitable. It should have led with a belief about control over your own viewing [S1].

### Leaders versus those who lead

A position gives you authority, he says, but people follow those who inspire them by choice, and they do it for themselves [S1]. His example is the 1963 March on Washington. About 250,000 people came without invitations, he says, because Martin Luther King Jr. told them what he believed, and they believed it too. King offered a dream, not a plan [S1].

How he applied it: he contrasted the Wright brothers with Samuel Pierpont Langley. Langley had money, contacts and the press. The Wrights had a bicycle shop and a belief that flight would change the world. Langley, he says, chased fame and quit the day the Wrights flew [S1].

### The circle of safety

In his second big TED talk, Sinek asks why people risk their lives for others. He tells the story of Captain William Swenson, awarded the Medal of Honor for rescuing wounded soldiers under fire in Afghanistan in 2009 [S2]. Those who do such things say others would have done the same for them [S2]. Sinek argues that heroism grows from the environment. Leaders can't control threats outside the group, such as the economy or rivals, but they do control whether people inside feel safe [S2]. Trust and cooperation are feelings, so a leader can't order them into being [S2].

How he applied it: he praised Bob Chapman of Barry-Wehmiller, who avoided layoffs in the 2008 recession by asking everyone, from the top down, to take four weeks of unpaid leave. Staff began trading leave to help colleagues who could least afford it [S2].

### Leaders eat last

The title of his 2014 book echoes a military custom he describes at the end of the talk: in the Marines, the officer eats last [S2][S8]. The book argues that a company does well when its leaders understand its true purpose and look after the people in their care [S5]. In the talk he says leadership is a choice, not a rank, and that we call them leaders because they go first: they take the risk before asking others to [S2].

How he applied it: he contrasted Southwest Airlines staff, who he says don't fear their leaders, with a gate agent whose rigid, fearful manner suggested she didn't trust hers [S2]. He also argued that public anger at some bank bosses came less from their pay than from breaking the deal that leaders protect their people [S2].

### The infinite game

Drawing on James Carse's 1987 book *Finite and Infinite Games*, Sinek says finite games, like football, have known players, fixed rules and an end [S4][S10]. Business, politics and life are infinite: players come and go, rules change, and there is no finish line [S4]. So no one "wins" business. You are only ahead or behind, and the aim is to keep playing [S4][S6].

How he applied it: in a 2020 keynote he warned against sports talk in business, because it suggests a final victory that doesn't exist [S6]. He argued that arbitrary targets, dates and numbers, and careless hiring and firing, drain energy and trust, and called them only a finite strategy [S6].

### Five practices for the long game

*The Infinite Game* (2019) names five practices: a just cause, trusting teams, worthy rivals, existential flexibility and the courage to lead [S10]. In the 2020 keynote he put them roughly like this: a cause strong enough that people will put their own interests aside for it; teams safe enough for people to be their best; rivals whose strengths show you your own gaps; readiness to change strategy sharply to serve the cause; and bold leadership [S6]. His example of courage was the drugstore chain CVS deciding to stop selling cigarettes [S6]. He admitted all five are very hard, and said the long-term results justify them [S6].

## How he decides

- Ask why before what or how [S3].
- Lead with the belief. Use features as proof [S1].
- Aim first at people who already share the belief [S1].
- Protect people before numbers, especially in a downturn [S2].
- Go first. Take the risk you ask others to take [S2].
- Judge a choice by whether it keeps you in the game, not whether it wins this round [S4][S6].
- Study rivals to learn, not to beat [S6].
- Be ready to change direction if the cause needs it [S6].

## Where he stood

**What should a group lead with?** Its why. Profit is a result of a clear why, not the reason for being (as of 2009) [S1][S3].

**What makes people give their best?** Feeling safe with their leaders and colleagues [S2].

**Are layoffs a fair tool in a downturn?** He praised a leader who found another way, and said great leaders don't give up people to save the numbers [S2].

**Can you win at business?** No. Business is infinite, so you play to keep playing (as of 2019 to 2020) [S4][S6].

**What one thing should leaders do?** When asked for one tip in 2020, he said to take better care of each other [S6].

## His work and record

- Born 9 October 1973 in Wimbledon, London [S8].
- Studied cultural anthropology at Brandeis University, then law at City, University of London [S8].
- Worked at the ad agencies Euro RSCG and Ogilvy & Mather, then started his own firm, Sinek Partners [S8].
- Gave "How Great Leaders Inspire Action" at TEDxPuget Sound in September 2009 [S7]. It is one of TED's most-watched talks [S8].
- Books: *Start with Why* (2009), *Leaders Eat Last* (2014), *Together Is Better* (2016), *Find Your Why* (2017), *The Infinite Game* (2019) [S8].
- *Start with Why* was the bestselling leadership book in one NPD BookScan year, mid-2016 to mid-2017, with 171,000 paperback copies [S9].
- Has taught strategic communication at Columbia University and been an adjunct at the RAND Corporation [S8].
- Started Optimism Press with Penguin Random House around 2018, and later The Optimism Company, a learning platform [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The brain science.** Sinek says the Golden Circle matches the brain: the neocortex handles reason and language (the what), while the limbic brain handles feelings and decisions but not language (the why) [S1]. *Start with Why* presents the limbic link as his own speculation [S9]. That split comes from Paul MacLean's "triune brain" model of the 1960s, which neuroscientists have largely dropped and now often call a myth [S11]. The advice may still help. The biology behind it is weak.
- **Why or who first.** Ken Krogue, writing on Forbes, argued that finding the right person matters more than starting with why, especially in sales, while agreeing that conversations should open with why [S9].
- **Training contract.** In 2018 *The Young Turks* reported a $98,000 no-bid contract from U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement for leadership training by Sinek, delivered through Ernst & Young [S8].
- **Hand-picked stories.** His proof is mostly stories (Apple, TiVo, the Wright brothers) rather than tests [S1]. A good story can fit almost any theory, so treat them as examples, not evidence.

## Blind spots to watch

- A strong why doesn't fix a weak product, a bad price or poor service. Pair him with someone who checks the offer and the numbers.
- His safety ideas assume leaders have room to protect people. A small team with no cash may face harder trade-offs.
- He tends to explain success after the fact through purpose. Ask whether the why was really the cause.
- The infinite game can become a reason to skip clear short-term goals. Some teams need a deadline and a number.
- He speaks to leaders. He says less about the people who do the work and what they want for themselves.
