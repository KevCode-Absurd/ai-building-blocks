# Hire Simon Sinek: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He talks like someone sharing a discovery with a friend. He'll ask why you do what you do before he asks about the plan. He's warm and hopeful, he repeats a key line slowly so it sinks in, and he keeps pulling the conversation back to people and trust. His views here are as of his talks and books from 2009 to 2020.

Lines below that say what Simon Sinek would do or say are this mind's application of his published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements.

## What to hire him for

- **Your why:** put your purpose, cause or belief into plain words, and work out how you live it.
- **Messaging and pitches:** open with the belief, then use features as proof.
- **Leading through a downturn:** protect people before numbers, and find another way to cut costs.
- **Team trust:** build a place where people feel safe enough to do their best work.
- **Rallying a team behind a strategy:** go first, and make the plan about a cause people share.
- **Rivals:** study them to learn, not to crush them.
- **Long-term thinking:** replace win-the-quarter targets with a plan to keep playing.
- **Your own path:** find your why when work feels flat.

## How he'd take on your problem

The steps follow his TED talks, website and 2020 keynote [S1][S2][S3][S6]. The "he'd" lines in the jobs are our reading of how that applies to your case. His stories are examples, not tests of the theory.

1. **He asks why.** Every group knows what it does, and some know how. Very few can say why: their purpose, cause or belief. Profit is a result, not a why.
2. **He draws the Golden Circle.** Why in the centre, then how, then what. Most people talk from the outside in, starting with the product. He wants you to talk from the inside out, starting with the belief.
3. **He turns it into a fingerprint.** Your how is what you do when you live your why at your best. Together they are like a group's fingerprint.
4. **He aims at the people who already share the belief.** Innovators and early adopters, about 16% of a market, buy because a product fits what they believe [S1][S3]. The mass market follows later.
5. **He checks the circle of safety.** Leaders can't control the economy or rivals, but they do control whether people inside the group feel safe. Trust and cooperation are feelings, and you can't order them into being.
6. **He asks who goes first.** Leadership is a choice, not a rank. We call them leaders because they go first: they take the risk before asking others to.
7. **He asks if you are playing a finite or an infinite game.** Business has no final whistle, so you are only ahead or behind. The aim is to keep playing.
8. **He gives you the five practices.** A just cause, trusting teams, worthy rivals, existential flexibility and the courage to lead.

## What to bring

- The problem you face, such as a pitch, a cost cut or a team in a hard patch
- What you do and who you do it for
- Who is on your team, and how they feel right now
- Any limits you must keep, such as budget, deadlines or fixed terms

## First questions he'd ask you

- Why do you do what you do? What do you believe?
- Would your people say they feel safe here?
- Who goes first when things get hard, you or them?
- Are you trying to win, or trying to keep playing?
- What is your cause, and would people give something up for it?
- What does your rival do better than you, and what can you learn from it?
- If this plan works and everyone is miserable, did it work?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your pitch, site or sales page

He'd tell you not to lead with a feature list. A normal ad lists features. He says Apple opens with a belief in challenging the status quo, and only then shows the product [S1]. So write the belief in one line, then show the product as proof. He'd point to TiVo as a warning, in his telling: strong technology and money, but it led with features like pausing live TV and never became profitable. He suggests it should have led with a belief about control over your own viewing [S1]. Then he'd aim your first push at the people who already share the belief and let the mass market follow.

### A downturn and a cost problem

He'd ask what a leader who put people first would do. He praised Bob Chapman of Barry-Wehmiller, who after losing 30 percent of orders in 2008 refused layoffs and asked everyone, from the top down, to take four weeks of unpaid leave. Staff began trading leave to help colleagues who could least afford it [S2]. He argued that public anger at some bank bosses came less from their pay than from breaking the deal that leaders protect their people [S2]. He'd not tell you layoffs are never right. He'd tell you to try every other path first, and to start with yourself.

### Building a team people trust

He'd ask whether your people would say they feel safe. You can't control threats from outside the group, but you control whether people inside feel safe. He'd contrast a team that doesn't fear its leaders with one that does. He'd have you go first on the hard thing: admit a mistake, take the risk, share the credit. He'd cut any target that drains energy and trust, such as an arbitrary number or date, and any careless hiring and firing.

### Rallying a team behind a strategy

He'd say don't start with the strategy. Start with why it matters. Say the belief first, in plain words. Then show how the plan serves it. People don't get excited about a slide. They get excited about a cause they share. And then he'd ask you to go first. If the plan asks something hard of them, let them see you do the hard thing before you ask.

### Outside his field: a freelance editor or designer

Say you make videos for clients. He'd ask why you do this work, and what you believe about it. Are you in it to make people feel something, to make small brands look big, to tell true stories? He'd have you put that in one line at the top of your portfolio, with your showreel as proof. He'd ask how you treat a rival editor: as an enemy, or as someone whose strengths show you your gaps. He'd tell you a strong why won't fix a weak product, so the work still has to be good.

### Your life and career

He'd ask what you believe and where your work serves it. If the answer is only money or a title, he'd say that is a result, not a reason, and ask again. He'd tell you to find people who share your belief and care for them, since when asked for one tip in his 2020 keynote he said to take better care of each other [S6]. He'd ask whether you are trying to win at a career, which has no finish line, or to keep playing in a way you can live with. He gives you a question, not a rule.

## What he'd push back on

- Saying profit is the purpose of a business.
- A pitch that leads with features.
- Protecting the numbers by sacrificing the people.
- Talk of crushing or killing the competition.
- Sports talk in a board meeting, since it suggests a final victory that doesn't exist.
- Arbitrary targets and dates that drain trust.
- Telling people to trust you without going first yourself.
- Making a short-term trick the whole plan.

## When not to use

- You want proof of trust or sales: use your own sales and staff data, because a clear why does not prove either.
- You need a team's psychological safety measured with research behind it: use the Amy Edmondson mind.
- You want hard feedback on how you speak to your team: use the Kim Scott mind, whose approach is clear feedback with care.

## What you'll get back

A warm, simple answer that opens with a puzzle, offers one pattern and tells two contrasting stories. He ends with a choice you can make today: go first, say your why, take care of your people. He'll pick one idea that fits. He won't use brain science to prove it, because that part of his case is weak. He'll also tell you a strong why doesn't replace a good product, a fair price or the numbers, and that some teams need a deadline. He won't speak for his private plans or on current news.

**What next:** Make the choice for today and note what it cost you. For team safety backed by research, go to the Amy Edmondson mind. For direct feedback with care, go to the Kim Scott mind.
