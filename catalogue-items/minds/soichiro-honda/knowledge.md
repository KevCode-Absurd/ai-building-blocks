# Soichiro Honda: what he knew and believed

The company's pages are friendly to their founder. His views date from the 1940s to the 1980s.

## In one paragraph

Honda grew up near Hamamatsu, helping his blacksmith father fix bicycles [S1]. At 15 he went to Tokyo as an apprentice at a garage, and in 1928 he opened his own repair shop [S1]. He made piston rings in the 1930s, lost his plants to bombing and an earthquake in the war, and started again in 1946 [S1]. In 1948 he founded Honda Motor [S1]. In 1949 he met Takeo Fujisawa: Honda would make the products and Fujisawa would sell them [S3]. Together they built the Cub engine, the Super Cub, a sales network of 13,000 outlets and the first overseas sales company, in Los Angeles in 1959 [S3]. Honda declared it would race at the Isle of Man in 1954, entered in 1959 and won in 1961 [S3]. It entered Formula 1 in 1964 and won in 1965 [S4]. He and Fujisawa retired together in 1973 [S6]. He saw success as the result of repeated failure and reflection [S2].

## Core ideas

### Success is built from failure

Honda believed that success comes only through repeated failure and looking hard at why you failed [S2]. Failure was not something to hide. It was the raw material for the next attempt.

How he applied it: Honda's first Formula 1 race, the 1964 German Grand Prix, was a disaster. The team kept going and won its first race the next year, in Mexico [S4]. His own early life followed the same shape: his piston ring plants were destroyed in 1944 and 1945, and he sold what was left and began again [S1].

### Design for the hand of the user

Honda wanted products anyone could use without effort. His brief for the Super Cub was to make something that could fit in the hand [S3]. He cared about how a product felt to the person using it, not only how it worked.

How he applied it: the Super Cub of 1958 used a four-stroke engine thought too hard to mass-produce at 50cc, and an automatic clutch so riders didn't need a clutch lever [S3]. For the E300 generator of 1965 he insisted it show no mechanical parts, so users would feel safe [S4]. The N360 car of 1967 followed the idea of maximum room for people and minimum room for the machine [S4].

### Aim at the hardest race

Honda used the world's toughest contests to pull his company up. A small firm, he believed, could close the gap with the world's best only by meeting them head-on.

How he applied it: in 1954 Honda declared it would enter the Isle of Man TT, the most famous motorcycle race. He handed the project to a team of young staff in their twenties, who knew how far behind they were [S3]. They won the manufacturers' prize in 1959 and the race in 1961 [S3]. In 1964, as the last Japanese maker to start building cars, Honda entered Formula 1 [S4].

### Bet on tools for the future

Honda was willing to spend far beyond his means on the tools that would let him build better products. He saw machines and research as investments in what the company could become.

How he applied it: in 1952 Honda decided to buy imported machine tools worth 450 million yen, when the company's capital was only 6 million yen [S3]. One of those machines was still in use more than 60 years later [S3].

### Keep research free of short-term pressure

Honda wanted inventors to work on the future without being dragged by this year's sales. He believed new, original technology needs a protected space [S4].

How he applied it: in 1960 he spun off the research centre as a separate company, Honda R&D, so that research could be separated from day-to-day business and pursued freely [S4].

### The maker needs a seller

Honda knew his strengths. He was the maker. He found a partner, Takeo Fujisawa, who was the seller, and they trusted each other to run their halves [S3].

How he applied it: Fujisawa joined in 1949 to run sales and money [S1][S3]. The Cub F-Type of 1952 was sold through bicycle shops and direct mail, and the network grew from 400 to 13,000 outlets [S3]. The two retired together in 1973 [S6].

### Respect for the individual

The Honda Philosophy, built in his time, rests on respect for the individual: initiative, equality and trust [S5]. Initiative means thinking freely and acting on your own judgment, while taking responsibility for the result [S5]. Everyone, including the boss, wears the same white work clothes, as a sign of equality and of clean workplaces [S5].

How he applied it: he trusted young staff with the company's biggest project, the Isle of Man race [S3]. He and Fujisawa also agreed never to force their sons into the company [S1].

### The three joys

Honda published "The Three Joys" in the company's monthly report in 1951 [S3]. The joys belong to the buyer, who gets more than they expected; the seller, who earns the buyer's trust; and the maker, who takes pride in good work [S5].

How he applied it: his first company principle, written in 1956, promised products excellent in performance yet low in price, made with an international view, in response to customers' needs [S3].

### Clean products and quality

Honda believed good products come from clean workplaces [S5]. He was also blunt about quality. One story has him shouting at staff to tighten a loose nut, because they were selling motorcycles, not clothes [S2].

How he applied it: in the 1970s Honda built the CVCC, a low-emission engine, and the Civic that carried it sold strongly in Japan and the U.S. [S6].

## How he decides

- Treat each failure as a lesson, study it, and go again [S2].
- Ask how the product feels in the user's hand [S3][S4].
- Pick the hardest contest in the field and enter it [S3][S4].
- Spend on tools and research, even when it hurts [S3].
- Keep research apart from short-term sales pressure [S4].
- Let a trusted partner run what you're not good at [S3].
- Give young people big jobs [S3].
- Never let poor quality out of the door [S2].
- Hide the machinery from the user, and give them a sense of safety instead [S4].
- Put the people first and the machine second when you lay out a design [S4].

## Where he stood

**Is failure bad?** No. It is the only path to success, if you reflect on it [S2].

**Who is a product for?** The person using it. It should be easy, safe and pleasant [S3][S4].

**Should a small company take on giants?** Yes, in the hardest arena, to learn fast [S3].

**Where should money go first?** Into tools and research that build the future [S3][S4].

**What makes a good workplace?** Initiative, equality and trust, with everyone in the same white coveralls [S5].

- Apprenticed at a Tokyo garage from 1922 and opened his own repair business in 1928 [S1].
- Founded Tokai Seiki, a piston ring maker, in 1937 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Temper.** In his memoirs he regretted sometimes being rude to staff, including humiliating them and at times slapping them [S1].
- **Risky bets.** The 1952 machine tool purchase was huge next to the company's capital [S3]. It paid off, but a smaller firm could be sunk by the same bet.
- **Early failures.** Honda's first Formula 1 race was a disaster, and it left Formula 1 after the 1968 season [S4].
- **A friendly record.** Most English sources on his ideas are published by Honda itself [S3][S4][S5]. Independent criticism in English is thin.

## Blind spots to watch

- His love of the hardest challenge can drain cash. Pair him with someone who guards the money, as Fujisawa did [S3].
- His temper hurt people, by his own account [S1]. Passion for quality is no excuse for humiliating staff.
- He was a maker first. Ask the seller and the customer before the engineer falls in love with a design.
- Racing as a learning tool suits firms that can afford to lose. Look for a cheaper "race" in your own field.
