### How she'd take on your problem

1. **Choose the positive activity and purpose:** Identify whether you want to express appreciation, offer kindness, reconnect or notice something welcome. Keep the purpose human and specific rather than set a compulsory happiness target.

2. **Check person-activity fit:** Ask about preference, resources and whether the form feels sincere. Include cultural and relationship context before selecting a familiar practice from a menu.

3. **Consider the other person:** For a favor, message or invitation, check what would be welcome and obtain consent where appropriate. A positive activity should not create an unwanted obligation.

4. **Make the gesture concrete:** Write the actual note, offer or invitation, in three or four sentences. Name one thing the person did or one need they told you about, so it does not read as a generic gratitude exercise.

5. **Notice engagement and burden:** A week later, answer three questions in a line each. Did it feel sincere? Did it take more from either of you than you expected? What happened that you did not plan? Do not treat the reply or a quick mood lift as the only score.

6. **Adapt without chasing happiness:** Write "keep", "change" or "drop" next to the practice, with one reason. If you change it, name the one thing you will do differently next time. If a real problem needs action, put that on your list instead. Do not check daily whether you are happy enough.

### Jobs, one by one

#### Choose an everyday kindness that fits

You want to do something kind but have little spare time. Think of someone you know and a need they have actually expressed. Choose a manageable action, such as sharing useful information or offering a specific piece of help. Ask before taking over. Afterwards consider whether the action respected the recipient and fitted your resources, rather than whether you felt instantly happier.

The deliverable: A concrete, consent-aware kindness plan with a realistic resource limit.

The pushback: Kindness is not a transaction that obliges gratitude. An unwanted favor can create work for the recipient, and a costly gesture can exceed the giver's resources.

#### Write a sincere thank-you message

Name something the person did and how it mattered. Write that plainly, without making the message a dramatic assessment of your entire relationship. Consider whether sending it is welcome and whether the recipient can receive it without pressure to respond. If the relationship is complicated, keep the note private or choose another person rather than forcing gratitude.

The deliverable: A specific thank-you note with no demand for a response.

The pushback: Gratitude should not excuse harm or create an obligation to maintain contact. Do not tell someone to thank a person who mistreated them as a route to wellbeing.

#### Make better use of something you already enjoy

You looked forward to a new hobby space but now barely notice it. List what the space actually makes possible. Choose one activity you would like to do there and invite participation if that would add meaning. Vary the use in a way that interests you. The purpose is to engage with the benefit again, not to force the original excitement to return.

The deliverable: A renewed-use plan grounded in what the improvement actually enables.

The pushback: Adaptation does not mean possessions or life improvements are worthless. A useful change may keep making life easier even when it no longer feels exciting.

#### Reconnect without making a grand plan

Choose a person you miss talking with. Send a short message that includes a real update and a question about something they care about. Offer a manageable way to catch up. Leave room for their schedule and interest. Review whether the exchange felt mutual rather than counting replies as evidence of your worth or the relationship's quality.

The deliverable: A warm reconnection message and an optional follow-up invitation.

The pushback: The quality and safety of relationships matter. More interaction is not always better, and a positive-activity framework should not pressure somebody to maintain an unsafe relationship.

#### Refresh a stale appreciation routine

Your notes of appreciation have become the same three items written mechanically. Change the prompt to something concrete, such as a person who made a task easier or a detail you would otherwise overlook. If writing feels forced, choose a different activity from the menu. Keep the alternative small enough to do sincerely instead of expanding the routine into another project.

The deliverable: A revised appreciation prompt and permission to choose another activity.

The pushback: Variety is not a demand to invent a new experience every day. An ordinary repeated pleasure can remain welcome; the user's experience matters more than a rigid rotation.

#### Choose a career experiment without promising happiness

You think a job title will finally make you happy. Identify what you expect it to provide: useful work, autonomy, money or connection. Design a modest way to investigate the daily reality, such as a conversation about the role's tasks. Compare the practical conditions with your expectations. Keep legitimate financial and safety needs visible rather than reducing everything to attitude.

The deliverable: A career-expectations table and a concrete question for a role conversation.

The pushback: A job experiment cannot predict permanent happiness. Positive activities do not remove structural problems or make a poor working environment acceptable.

### In one paragraph

Lyubomirsky is a psychology researcher at the University of California, Riverside. Her public work studies wellbeing and intentional positive activities. The positive-activity model she co-authored asks how features of the person and activity affect outcomes, rather than treating one practice as universally beneficial. The applications here use that conditional approach for ordinary kindness, gratitude, connection and everyday choices. [S1] [S2]
