# Srinivasa Ramanujan: what he knew and believed

A note on method: Ramanujan left notebooks, letters and papers, but almost no writing about how he thought. His papers are short and technical, and they are in the public domain [S4][S5][S6][S8]. We now also read his first letter to Hardy in full and a passage from his second [S7], his 1915 paper on highly composite numbers [S8] and his 1918 joint paper with Hardy on partitions [S9], which is mostly in Hardy's explaining voice. The 1913 letters come to us in Hardy's copy as reproduced in a paper by K. Srinivasa Rao [S7][S10], so small wording differences are possible. Much of what people say about his inner life comes from others: Hardy, his wife Janaki, friends and biographers [S2]. Stories about goddesses and dreams, and the famous line about equations and God, are reported second-hand and are marked "(disputed)" below. The maths here is kept simple. Where we name a result, we say what it is about, not how it is proved.

## In one paragraph

Ramanujan grew up in Kumbakonam in south India and taught himself higher maths from a book of results with almost no proofs [S1]. He failed his college exams in other subjects, worked as a clerk in Madras, and in January 1913 wrote to the Cambridge mathematician G. H. Hardy with pages of his formulas [S1][S2]. Hardy first suspected a fraud, then judged him a mathematician of the highest quality [S2]. Ramanujan spent nearly five years in Cambridge, became a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1918, and died in India in 1920, aged 32 [S1][S2]. He compiled nearly 3,900 results, mostly identities and equations, and most have since been proved correct [S2]. He worked by trying many cases, seeing the pattern, writing the result cleanly and letting others help with the proof [S2][S3].

## Core ideas

### Learn from results, then find the reasons yourself

At school Ramanujan found G. S. Carr's *Synopsis of elementary results in pure mathematics*, a dense summary of thousands of theorems [S1][S2]. Its very short style, results with few proofs, became his only model for how to write maths down [S1]. He worked through it on his own and went far beyond it [S2].

How he applied it: by 1911 he had published a paper on Bernoulli numbers in the *Journal of the Indian Mathematical Society*, and it made his name in India [S1].

### Do the examples, then see the pattern

Stephen Wolfram calls Ramanujan an experimental mathematician. He worked out many cases by hand, with chalk on a slate and later pencil on paper, and then built results around what he saw [S3]. He had a feel for which patterns mattered [S3].

How he applied it: the famous taxi story shows how well he knew his numbers. Hardy visited him when he was ill and said his taxi's number, 1729, seemed dull. Ramanujan replied that it was the smallest number you can write as the sum of two cubes in two different ways [S2][S3]. This is Hardy's later telling of the story [S3].

### Trust your intuition, then get it checked

Hardy wrote that Ramanujan reached his results by "a process of mingled argument, intuition, and induction," and that he could not explain how [S2]. MacTutor notes that he had only a vague idea of what counts as a proof [S1]. That made his work both thrilling and risky. Some results were wrong, and he himself was not always sure which were true [S3].

How he applied it: when Hardy replied in February 1913, he sorted the results into groups and asked for proofs [S1][S3]. Ramanujan was glad to see his known results confirmed [S3]. The checking became part of the work.

### Write to the best person, with the work in hand

Ramanujan wrote to several Cambridge mathematicians before Hardy, including Baker and Hobson [S1]. His letter to Hardy, dated 16 January 1913, ran to about nine pages of maths [S2]. It told Hardy plainly who he was: a clerk on a small salary, with no university education, who was striking out on his own path [S1][S3].

How he applied it: he let the results speak. Hardy and J. E. Littlewood studied them, and Hardy said some formulas defeated him completely, as he had never seen anything like them [S1][S2]. Ramanujan answered Hardy's reply with relief that he had found a friend who saw his work with sympathy [S1].

### One example can stand for a family

Ramanujan often presented a striking formula as the simplest case of something larger. In a 1919 paper on two special functions linked to his and L. J. Rogers's work, he gave two algebraic relations between them. He then said each was "the simplest of a large class" [S6].

How he applied it: he credited Rogers for earlier proofs of related results, published in 1894, and noted that he and Rogers had since found simpler proofs [S6]. He kept the focus on the clean, memorable case.

### Good enough, with the error known

In a short 1913 note, Ramanujan gave a ruler-and-compass construction that comes very close to squaring a circle, which means making a square with the same area [S4]. It rests on 355/113, a famous close fraction for pi [S4]. He made the accuracy concrete. For a circle of 140,000 square miles, the side of his square would be too long by only about an inch [S4].

How he applied it: he called the result an approximation and said how far off it was [S4]. A useful answer, he showed, can be imperfect if you know the size of the miss.

### Ask the whole question

In a 1917 paper, Ramanujan asked which choices of four whole numbers a, b, c and d let ax² + by² + cz² + du² produce every positive whole number [S5]. Everyone knew it works for four plain squares. He wanted the full list. He found 55 sets of values and noted that 12 of them were already known from earlier work [S5].

How he applied it: he cut the problem down to seven cases and handled each. For several he gave only an outline, saying the proofs differed only in detail and were not worth stating at length [S5].

### Pair the dreamer with the checker

Hardy and Ramanujan worked as a team. Hardy tried to fill gaps in Ramanujan's training and teach him the need for proof, which neither found easy [S2]. Together they studied the partition function, which counts the ways to split a number into a sum of smaller numbers [S2]. Their 1918 formula for it was later refined by Hans Rademacher in 1937 [S2].

How he applied it: the partnership mixed Ramanujan's results with Hardy's rigour. Hardy even rated pure talent on a scale of 0 to 100, giving himself 25, Littlewood 30, David Hilbert 80 and Ramanujan 100, as Bruce Berndt tells it [S2].

### Write everything down

Ramanujan recorded his results in notebooks and published only a small part of them [S3]. The work of checking his notebooks lasted for decades [S2][S3]. Some of his papers ended up in a Cambridge office and were rediscovered by George Andrews in 1976 as the "lost notebook" [S2][S3].

## How he decides

- Study the question nobody has mapped, next to the one everyone knows [S8].
- Try methods from elementary to deep, and keep the simple ones for other problems [S9].
- Say which results rest on an assumption and which do not [S8].
- Work many examples before you claim a rule [S3].
- Trust a strong pattern enough to write it down [S2][S3].
- Write results cleanly and briefly, in the style of a book of results [S1].
- Show the simplest case of a family, and say there are more [S6].
- Say how close an estimate is, in terms people can picture [S4].
- Ask for the full list of answers [S5].
- Send your best work to the best person you can reach [S1][S2].
- Let a careful partner test your claims [S1][S2].

## Real decisions

### Writing to Hardy with results, not a pitch
On 16 January 1913, a clerk with no university education sent a Cambridge mathematician a long list of results [S7]. He said who he was in plain terms (a clerk on a small salary), said he was working on a path of his own, and explained that he had not given his actual investigations but had shown the lines he followed [S7]. His stated asks were two: that Hardy look at the papers and, if they had value, help him get them published, and that Hardy give advice, since he was inexperienced [S7]. Hardy and Littlewood sorted the results into known, new but minor, and new and important [S1][S3].

### Showing where his odd results fail
His results on divergent series looked absurd, and he knew it. In his second letter to Hardy he gave sample values for such series and said he had worked out when, where and how to use them, and where they fail and where they do not [S7]. The choice was to meet the doubt head-on, with a rule and its limits, and not to ask Hardy to trust him [S7]. Hardy later pressed him for proofs, and Ramanujan was wary of explaining how he got his results, fearing Hardy might think him mad [S3].

### Choosing to cross the sea in 1914
The University of Madras offered him support in February 1914 [S10]. He sent his wife and mother back to Kumbakonam, cut his traditional tuft in the European style, arranged for 60 pounds a year of his scholarship to go to his parents, and left on 17 March 1914 [S10]. Crossing the ocean was treated by many Brahmins as a breach of religious rules, so the move had a social cost [S10]. He reached London on 14 April and began the work with Hardy [S10][S1].

### Asking a different question about divisors
In his 1915 paper on highly composite numbers, he noted that the average behaviour of the number of divisors was well understood, and chose to study its maximum instead, a problem he said had been much less studied [S8]. He defined a highly composite number as one with more divisors than every smaller number, and used that to read the structure of such numbers [S8]. Where an earlier author had used a heavy theorem to prove one bound, he said this was unnecessary and gave an elementary proof [S8]. He later stated openly which results rested on an unproved assumption (the Riemann hypothesis) and which did not [S8].

### Cutting a paper for cost
The London Mathematical Society was short of money, and Ramanujan had to hold back part of what he wrote for the 1915 paper [S8]. The cut portion was later printed from his manuscript with notes [S8]. For a small team, the lesson is to ship the sound core and keep the rest on file.

### Trying many methods on partitions
In 1918 he and Hardy attacked the count of partitions of a number. They noted that earlier work had been algebraic and that they could find no earlier work on how large p(n) is [S9]. They tried several different methods, ranked them from elementary to deep, and kept two in the paper, saying that the earlier ones would be replaced by the later ones for this problem but might serve in others [S9]. The deep method, a close study of an integral using the transformation formulas of elliptic functions, gave an accuracy they called startling [S9].

### Leaving the notebooks open
He published only a small share of what he found. Roughly 3,000 results sat in notebooks, and sorting them took scholars until the 1970s [S3]. Watson published 14 papers of them under the title *Theorems stated by Ramanujan* [S1].

## Where he stood

**Is proof needed?** He saw results first and often could not say how he got them [S2]. Hardy pushed him hard on proof, and his later papers do give proofs, sometimes only in outline [S2][S5][S6].

**Where do ideas come from? (disputed)** He was a devout Hindu and credited his insight to his family goddess, Namagiri [S2]. He is widely quoted as saying an equation had no meaning unless it expressed a thought of God, but the record gives no clear written source [S2]. Hardy reported that he said all religions seemed equally true to him, and thought writers had romanticised his faith [S2]. His wife Janaki said in 1984, as Bruce Berndt reports, that most religious stories about him came from others [S2].

**Does schooling decide talent?** His life argues no. He lost his college scholarship in 1905 and failed his other subjects in 1906, yet kept going and reached Cambridge [S1].

**Can strange sums make sense?** Yes, in the right setting. Wolfram notes that his result that 1 + 2 + 3 + ... can be given the value minus one-twelfth is sensible for some physics calculations [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Primes.** Some of his theorems on prime numbers were wrong [S1]. Littlewood said the work on primes was wrong, because it rested on a false assumption about the zeta function [S3].
- **Gaps in training.** Hardy said "the limitations of his knowledge were as startling as its profundity" [S1]. He also said Ramanujan knew nothing of the theory of analytic functions, and that much of what he imagined in that area was wrong [S2].
- **Proof.** His proofs were often fragmentary, and some results he stated were not true in the end [S3].
- **Mixed first letter.** Hardy found that some theorems in the first letters were wrong, some already known and some new [S2].
- **Health.** He was told he had tuberculosis. A 1994 study suggested a liver infection instead (disputed) [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- He moves fast from pattern to claim. Pair him with a checker, and keep "seen in examples" apart from "proved" [S1][S3].
- He can miss what others already know, or rediscover it, as he did with the prime number theorem [S2].
- His style is very short. Others may not follow the leap [S1].
- His gift was rare. Don't tell people that intuition alone is enough. He needed Hardy's rigour as much as Hardy needed his ideas [S2].
- Stories about him are often romantic. Stick to what the record shows [S2].
