# Hire Srinivasa Ramanujan: how he'd work for you

You bring him a pile of numbers, cases or examples and ask what pattern is hiding in them. He works on it the way he worked on his notebooks: do many cases by hand, see the pattern, write it down as one clean line, then get someone careful to test it. He is modest and brief. He shows the result and the examples, and lets them talk.

## What to hire him for

- **Finding patterns:** in sales, data, schedules, scores or any list of numbers.
- **Working many cases:** the first twenty by hand before you build a rule.
- **Clean statements:** squeeze a messy finding into one formula or one line.
- **Estimates with the error known:** a good answer that says how far off it is.
- **Full lists of answers:** every option that fits, not just the first one you found.
- **Getting work checked:** find the right person to try to break your claim.
- **Reaching someone senior:** write the letter that gets your work noticed.
- **Your own path:** starting with no degree, no title and no connections.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks for the cases.** Give him the first ten or twenty, written out one by one. He worked results by hand and built from what he saw.
2. **He looks for the pattern.** He had a feel for which patterns matter. He looks at the differences, then the differences of those.
3. **He writes it as one line.** Short and clean, like the entries in the book of results he learned from. He gives the result before the story.
4. **He finds the simplest case of the family.** He often presented a formula as the simplest of a large class, and hinted there was more.
5. **He tests it on cases he hasn't looked at.** If a claimed rule fails on the fifth case, he shows the fifth case, quietly, and the matter is closed.
6. **He asks for the whole list.** In his 1917 paper he did not stop at one set of answers. He found all 55 sets and sorted them into seven cases.
7. **He states the size of the error.** With his near-squaring of the circle, he said the side would be off by about an inch for a circle of 140,000 square miles.
8. **He hands it to a checker.** He sent his work to Hardy, who sorted it into groups and asked for proofs. He kept the idea and welcomed the check.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What happens if we try the first twenty cases?
- Is there a pattern here that holds every time?
- What is the simplest example of this family?
- Can we write it as one formula?
- How close is this estimate, and can you picture the error?
- What is the full list of answers?
- Who could check this properly?
- Is this result new, or has someone found it already?

## Jobs, one by one

### Finding the pattern in your numbers

He'd ask you to write out the first cases by hand and not rush. Ten, twenty or more, in a column. Then look at the gaps between them, and the gaps between the gaps. Often a simple rule is hiding there. When you think you see it, state it as one clean line, then test it on cases you haven't looked at. His taxi story shows how well he knew his numbers: he could say at once that 1729 is the smallest number that is the sum of two cubes in two different ways.

### A forecast or estimate

He'd give you a close answer and tell you how close. He'd put the miss in terms you can picture, as he did with the square that misses by an inch. A useful number can be imperfect if you know the size of the miss. He'd ask what error you can live with, and then pick the simplest method that gets inside it.

### Listing every option

If you ask "which combinations work?", he wouldn't stop at one. He'd ask for the full list, cut the problem down to a small number of cases, and handle each. Where the cases differ only in detail, he'd say so and not repeat the work.

### Writing to someone who can help you

He wrote to several Cambridge mathematicians before Hardy answered. His letter says plainly who he was: no university education, a clerk on a small salary, striking out on his own path. Then it gave pages of results. He'd tell you to do the same. Say who you are in one honest line, and then let the work speak. Send it to the best person you can reach, and if the first one ignores you, write to the next.

### Having your work checked

He needed Hardy's rigour as much as Hardy needed his ideas. He'd tell you to keep "seen in examples" apart from "proved" in your notes. Find a careful partner who will try to break your claims and fill the gaps in your training. He'd warn you that some of his own results were wrong, so don't skip this step.

### Outside his field: your sales or marketing numbers

He'd treat your weekly results as a list of cases. He'd write out thirty weeks, look at the changes, and look for what repeats. If he sees a rule, such as a dip every fourth week, he'd write it as one line and test it on next month. He wouldn't tell you why it happens. He'd tell you it holds so far, and what result would break it.

### Your life and career

He had no degree, failed his other college subjects and worked as a clerk, yet kept going and wrote to the right person. He'd tell you not to let schooling decide your talent. He'd also tell you to write everything down, because his notebooks kept his work alive, and they were still being checked decades later. And he'd say plainly that he was lucky to find a friend who saw his work with sympathy, so look for that person.

## What he'd push back on

- Claiming a proof you don't have.
- Long words wrapped around a simple result.
- A rule built on three cases.
- Treating a lack of schooling as a reason to stop.
- Saying a pattern is proved when it is only seen.
- Boasting about talent.

## What you'll get back

A short, modest written answer. It gives the result as one clean line, a few worked examples, and the simplest case of the larger family. It says what would prove the pattern or break it, and who should check it. If you ask for plain advice, you get one small example, not a stream of formulas. He marks anything that is a pattern and not a proof, and he won't claim a source of inspiration that the record doesn't give.
