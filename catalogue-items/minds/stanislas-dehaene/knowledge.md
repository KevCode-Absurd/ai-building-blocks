# Stanislas Dehaene: what he knew and believed

Public sources checked 2026-10-23. Source claims use the numbered references in mind.yaml. Exercises are editorial applications, not documented personal advice or quotations.

## In one paragraph

Dehaene helps you inspect learning as an interaction between attention, prediction, correction and consolidation. Bring a lesson or skill in which learners listen but do not reliably understand or remember. His four-pillar synthesis suggests looking for the missing learning activity before adding more explanation. The practical outcome is a lesson with a clear target, an active prediction and feedback that can change the learner’s model. [S1] [S3]

## Core ideas

### Select attention

His account of learning emphasizes selecting relevant information. [S3]

Attention needs a target. A crowded display may contain the answer while competing elements prevent learners from selecting it. Simplify what is visible around the comparison that matters.

**An application: direct attention to the relevant feature.** Take a worksheet with several instructions, colors and diagrams. Identify the single relationship the first task teaches. Place the needed quantities together, mark the comparison and postpone unrelated information. Ask the learner to point out the relevant feature before solving. Reintroduce complexity only when it serves another learning goal, and check whether the simpler presentation improved the explanation.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not confuse directing attention with making everything visually loud. If every element is highlighted, the learner still has to discover which signal matters.

**What the attempt should reveal:** Which information must be selected first? You receive a revised display and an attention cue.

### Generate a prediction

His lecture describes learning as comparing an internal prediction with incoming information. [S1]

An expectation gives feedback something to correct. The learner should make a reasoned prediction before seeing the outcome, with enough instruction to understand what is being asked.

**An application: make a learner’s prediction visible.** Before demonstrating a simple balance or measurement task, ask learners to predict the result and explain the relevant relation. Record answers privately. Run the demonstration, then compare each prediction with what happened. Ask learners to revise the explanation in a sentence. The final revision matters more than whether the first prediction happened to be correct.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not use surprise alone as evidence of understanding. A striking demonstration can be remembered while the learner retains the same mistaken explanation of why it happened.

**What the attempt should reveal:** What expectation did the outcome challenge? You receive a prediction-result-revision sequence.

### Correct the model

He presents error feedback as information that updates an internal model. [S1]

Feedback should identify a mismatch that the learner can act on. Merely marking an answer wrong leaves the governing rule unchanged. A changed case can inspect whether the correction took hold.

**An application: turn an error into a corrected model.** After an incorrect subtraction problem, ask the learner to explain the rule used. Show the exact step where it fails and contrast it with a valid case. Let the learner revise the rule, then try another problem with different numbers. Keep the corrected explanation beside the old one so the conceptual change is visible rather than hidden by rewriting the answer.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not infer a deep conceptual defect from one careless slip. First determine whether the learner’s explanation reflects a wrong rule, a missed instruction or a momentary execution error.

**What the attempt should reveal:** What rule changed after the correction? You receive an error analysis and a new diagnostic case.

### Engage without humiliation

His lecture distinguishes informative error from punishment that creates fear. [S1]

Informative errors and punishment perform different roles. Learners need room to make their current understanding visible. A lesson that makes wrong answers socially costly may suppress the evidence needed for correction.

**An application: encourage attempts without humiliation.** Ask learners to answer a challenging question privately before discussing it. Present two plausible explanations without naming who supplied them. Compare the evidence and invite revisions. Thank participants for reasoning that made a misconception inspectable. Keep the standard of correctness intact while removing the public ranking. A teacher can then respond to the model rather than a child’s willingness to speak in front of peers.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not replace correction with vague reassurance. A supportive climate should make accurate feedback easier to use, not make every explanation appear equally supported.

**What the attempt should reveal:** Can learners reveal a mistaken expectation safely? You receive a private-attempt and public-comparison routine.

### Consolidate over time

His learning synthesis includes consolidation through repeated practice and sleep. [S3]

A successful attempt during instruction does not guarantee later availability. Include a return after time has passed, and protect a realistic schedule rather than treating exhausted repetition as proof of commitment.

**An application: plan consolidation after a lesson.** After learning a new keyboard command, use it in a short task, write one recall prompt and return the next day. Ask for the command before showing the answer, then use it in a changed task. Keep an ordinary stopping time for the evening. If recall fails completely, provide another explained encounter before increasing the gap.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not prescribe sleep as a guaranteed substitute for learning the material. Consolidation depends on an initial learning experience; a schedule cannot preserve knowledge that was never understood or practiced.

**What the attempt should reveal:** What can be retrieved after the lesson is no longer fresh? You receive a later prompt and a practice return.

### Adapt the challenge

His lecture urges problems that sustain curiosity without becoming too easy or too hard. [S1]

A useful challenge gives learners something to anticipate without leaving them unable to form an expectation. Adjust the starting information and size of the step rather than choosing between total discovery and total explanation.

**An application: set a challenge that sustains curiosity.** For a new coding command show a minimal working example. Ask what a small change will do, let the learner predict and run it, then discuss the result. Add only one new feature at a time until the model is stable. Finish with a case the learner chooses. The activity should invite investigation because its consequences are understandable and worth checking.

**The distinction to preserve:** Do not treat being lost as evidence of active engagement. If no plausible prediction is possible, the learner may need a clearer example or prerequisite instruction before productive exploration can begin.

**What the attempt should reveal:** Can the learner make an informed prediction? You receive a ladder of examples and one independent exploration.

## How he decides

Move from an observable task to a proposed mechanism and back again. The learner should be able to say what prediction was made and what evidence changed it. Dehaene’s public explanations are enthusiastic about the brain’s learning capacity, but the portrait should not decorate routine advice with unsupported neurological certainty. Say attention selects relevant information, then identify the information in this lesson. Say feedback updates a model, then show which expectation was wrong. Preserve distinctions among an experiment, a synthesis and a classroom adaptation. Technical terms are useful when they explain why an activity is designed as it is. If they do not change the design, a plain account of the attempt and correction is usually stronger.

## Where he stood

The four pillars work together. Attention without a meaningful target can become mere compliance; engagement without feedback can preserve an error; correction without a later return can remain fragile. His lecture’s prediction cycle also clarifies why learner activity is not measured only by visible movement. A quiet learner making and checking an expectation may be cognitively active, while an elaborate activity can leave the relevant model untouched. The educational implication is to design the thinking, not simply add action to a lesson. These applications remain hypotheses about teaching practice and should be checked against actual learning. ## Their work and record

His Collège de France lecture on active engagement and error correction presents a cycle of prediction, comparison and model adjustment. An error signal matters because it supplies information about a mismatch. The lecture explicitly distinguishes that informative role from punishment, which can discourage exploration and learning. [S1]

The same lecture connects explicit teaching with opportunities to test knowledge and notes the risk of overestimating learning during rereading. It also argues for challenges that sustain curiosity without becoming inaccessible. A classroom application should therefore make the learner’s expectation visible and provide a usable correction, rather than merely create surprise. [S1]

His official scientific site and institutional biography place the work in reading, mathematics, reasoning and consciousness. The experimental-cognitive-psychology chair describes links among behavior, brain architecture and mental representations; the UNICOG laboratory page identifies a broader program of cognitive neuroimaging. These affiliations establish a research context, not the claim that a brain scan can prescribe a personalized study schedule. How We Learn brings the four-pillar account to a wider audience. ## Where they were wrong or criticised

Neural evidence cannot alone select a curriculum or diagnose an individual learner. His four-pillar account is a useful synthesis, not an exclusive list of everything that matters in a classroom. [S1] [S2] These are limits on applying the approach; the exercises above do not claim stronger evidence than the sources supply.

## Blind spots to watch

Return a four-part lesson card: the feature to attend to, the prediction to generate, the correction to inspect and the later opportunity to retrieve or practice. For an adult learning a tool, the card can describe one command and a safe example before broader use. Include a place to note a changed explanation. The approach should not diagnose a learner’s brain from a failed exercise. If access, language or prior knowledge prevents participation, repair those conditions before interpreting the result as an attention problem.
