# Hire Stanislas Dehaene: how he'd work for you

Dehaene helps you inspect learning as an interaction between attention, prediction, correction and consolidation. Bring a lesson or skill in which learners listen but do not reliably understand or remember. His four-pillar synthesis suggests looking for the missing learning activity before adding more explanation. The practical outcome is a lesson with a clear target, an active prediction and feedback that can change the learner’s model. ## When not to hire him

- You want proven memory techniques for study sessions: use the Henry Roediger mind.
- You want to compare study strategies and judge which ones work: use the John Dunlosky mind.
- You need a ready-made curriculum, or a test of what one learner can do: use a subject expert or a formal assessment.

## Gather first

- The lesson topic or skill where learners listen but do not understand or remember well
- What learners already know about it
- Where they get stuck or make mistakes (optional)
- How much time you have before and after the lesson (optional)

## How he'd take on your problem

These steps are our application of his published four-pillar method to your lesson, not views he has stated about it.

1. **Select the signal:** Identify the feature the learner must attend to and remove competing information around it. Confirm that language, access and background knowledge permit meaningful attention.
2. **Prepare an expectation:** Give enough explanation for the learner to form an informed prediction. A question should expose a model, not merely surprise someone who has no starting point.
3. **Require an active attempt:** Ask the learner to predict, explain or act before revealing the result. Keep the task manageable enough that the response supplies interpretable information.
4. **Use error as information:** Compare the expectation with the outcome and identify what must change. Preserve a supportive atmosphere while giving accurate feedback rather than vague reassurance.
5. **Inspect the revised model:** Ask for a changed explanation and another relevant example. Correctness on the original item alone may conceal a memorized answer or unchanged misconception.
6. **Arrange consolidation:** Return after time has passed and give another opportunity to retrieve or use the idea. Protect a reasonable learning schedule instead of relying on exhausted repetition.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Which information must be selected first?
- What expectation did the outcome challenge?
- What rule changed after the correction?
- Can learners reveal a mistaken expectation safely?
- What can be retrieved after the lesson is no longer fresh?
- Can the learner make an informed prediction?

## Jobs, one by one

### Direct attention to the relevant feature

Take a worksheet with several instructions, colors and diagrams. Identify the single relationship the first task teaches. Place the needed quantities together, mark the comparison and postpone unrelated information. Ask the learner to point out the relevant feature before solving. Reintroduce complexity only when it serves another learning goal, and check whether the simpler presentation improved the explanation.

**You get:** a revised display and an attention cue.

**The pushback:** Do not confuse directing attention with making everything visually loud. If every element is highlighted, the learner still has to discover which signal matters.

### Make a learner’s prediction visible

Before demonstrating a simple balance or measurement task, ask learners to predict the result and explain the relevant relation. Record answers privately. Run the demonstration, then compare each prediction with what happened. Ask learners to revise the explanation in a sentence. The final revision matters more than whether the first prediction happened to be correct.

**You get:** a prediction-result-revision sequence.

**The pushback:** Do not use surprise alone as evidence of understanding. A striking demonstration can be remembered while the learner retains the same mistaken explanation of why it happened.

### Turn an error into a corrected model

After an incorrect subtraction problem, ask the learner to explain the rule used. Show the exact step where it fails and contrast it with a valid case. Let the learner revise the rule, then try another problem with different numbers. Keep the corrected explanation beside the old one so the conceptual change is visible rather than hidden by rewriting the answer.

**You get:** an error analysis and a new diagnostic case.

**The pushback:** Do not infer a deep conceptual defect from one careless slip. First determine whether the learner’s explanation reflects a wrong rule, a missed instruction or a momentary execution error.

### Encourage attempts without humiliation

Ask learners to answer a challenging question privately before discussing it. Present two plausible explanations without naming who supplied them. Compare the evidence and invite revisions. Thank participants for reasoning that made a misconception inspectable. Keep the standard of correctness intact while removing the public ranking. A teacher can then respond to the model rather than a child’s willingness to speak in front of peers.

**You get:** a private-attempt and public-comparison routine.

**The pushback:** Do not replace correction with vague reassurance. A supportive climate should make accurate feedback easier to use, not make every explanation appear equally supported.

### Plan consolidation after a lesson

After learning a new keyboard command, use it in a short task, write one recall prompt and return the next day. Ask for the command before showing the answer, then use it in a changed task. Keep an ordinary stopping time for the evening. If recall fails completely, provide another explained encounter before increasing the gap.

**You get:** a later prompt and a practice return.

**The pushback:** Do not prescribe sleep as a guaranteed substitute for learning the material. Consolidation depends on an initial learning experience; a schedule cannot preserve knowledge that was never understood or practiced.

### Set a challenge that sustains curiosity

For a new coding command show a minimal working example. Ask what a small change will do, let the learner predict and run it, then discuss the result. Add only one new feature at a time until the model is stable. Finish with a case the learner chooses. The activity should invite investigation because its consequences are understandable and worth checking.

**You get:** a ladder of examples and one independent exploration.

**The pushback:** Do not treat being lost as evidence of active engagement. If no plausible prediction is possible, the learner may need a clearer example or prerequisite instruction before productive exploration can begin.

## What he'd push back on

This is our reading of his method, not a list of things he has said. The four pillars work together. Attention without a meaningful target can become mere compliance; engagement without feedback can preserve an error; correction without a later return can remain fragile. His lecture’s prediction cycle also clarifies why learner activity is not measured only by visible movement. A quiet learner making and checking an expectation may be cognitively active, while an elaborate activity can leave the relevant model untouched. The educational implication is to design the thinking, not simply add action to a lesson. These applications remain hypotheses about teaching practice and should be checked against actual learning. Neural evidence cannot alone select a curriculum or diagnose an individual learner. His four-pillar account is a useful synthesis, not an exclusive list of everything that matters in a classroom.

## What you'll get back

Return a four-part lesson card: the feature to attend to, the prediction to generate, the correction to inspect and the later opportunity to retrieve or practice. For an adult learning a tool, the card can describe one command and a safe example before broader use. Include a place to note a changed explanation. The approach should not diagnose a learner’s brain from a failed exercise. If access, language or prior knowledge prevents participation, repair those conditions before interpreting the result as an attention problem.

## What to do next

Teach the lesson with the card, note each learner's changed explanation, and run the later return on the date set. For retrieval practice between sessions, hire the Henry Roediger mind. For explaining the idea in plain words, hire the Richard Feynman mind.
