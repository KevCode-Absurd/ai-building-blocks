# Stefan Sagmeister: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Sagmeister is an Austrian designer based in New York, born in Bregenz in 1962 [S4]. He made album covers for Lou Reed, the Rolling Stones, Talking Heads and David Byrne, and won two Grammys for packaging [S4]. He started his own studio in 1993, and ran it with Jessica Walsh as Sagmeister & Walsh from 2011 to 2019 [S4]. He is known for closing the studio every seven years for a year of experiments with no client work [S1][S10]. His personal projects turn life lessons into type, test what makes people happy, argue that beauty is central to good design, and show long-term data on human progress [S4][S5][S7][S8]. He wants design to make an emotional connection with people, and he believes that taking form seriously makes work function better [S2][S3].

## Core ideas

### Make an emotional connection

Sagmeister says his studio wants to create an emotional connection with an audience, beyond passing on information [S2]. His simple test for good design is whether it either helps people or delights them [S1]. He opposes cold, impersonal communication of the kind he links with modernism, and prefers identities that feel human, varied and flexible [S1].

How he applied it: for an AIGA Detroit lecture poster, he had the text cut into his own skin and photographed. The aim was to show the pain that seems to come with most design projects [S6].

### Beauty is part of function

He once held the view that ideas come first and form is secondary. He changed his mind [S1]. His studio came to beauty through results: whenever it took form seriously, the work did better for clients [S3]. By 2017 he argued that beauty is a core part of being human, not a surface styling trick, and that designers, artists and architects had largely set it aside [S3].

How he applied it: he points to public housing from the 1950s and 1960s, built to house as many people as cheaply as possible. It proved unfit to live in and was pulled down about 20 years later. In his view, aiming only at function often produces work that doesn't function at all [S2]. Beautiful buildings like the Pantheon last, which he sees as an argument for beauty as sustainability [S1]. He and Walsh made the *Beauty* exhibition in 2019 [S4] and a 2018 Venice Biennale piece titled *Beauty = Function* [S9].

### Take a year off every seven years

Every seven years Sagmeister closes his studio to clients for a year and experiments [S1][S10]. He does it partly to fight boredom, and partly because it proved good for business: ideas from the year feed client work for years after [S1]. He doesn't switch off. He treats the year as working time for small experiments [S2]. He was nervous before the first one and called his clients to explain [S1].

How he applied it: his 2009 TED talk showed projects that came out of a sabbatical in Bali [S10]. A later one was split between Mexico City, Tokyo and Schwarzenberg in Austria, because he wanted new experiences [S2].

### Schedule experiments, or they won't happen

Outside the sabbaticals, Sagmeister values regular, scheduled experiments, so deadlines can't push them aside [S1]. His main creative blocks are boredom, dull content and fear, and time off helps with all three [S1]. For ideas he uses train trips, new hotel rooms, walks and research, plus Edward de Bono's random-object method and James Webb Young's process for producing ideas [S1].

How he applied it: he found that moving his home office from a basement to an upstairs room with high ceilings made him much more productive [S2].

### Stay small and choose your clients

He keeps the studio small so it can choose clients it respects, avoid unpaid pitches and keep the owners doing the work [S1]. He prefers clients whose products he uses himself, and believes good design can come as a by-product of loving the work [S1]. His money advice is plain: keep time sheets, spend less than you earn and don't work weekends [S1]. He thinks branding agencies overrate their power, because the product's quality matters most [S1].

How he applied it: early music work paid very little, but its packaging earned award nominations that opened doors [S1]. The studio usually has about a dozen projects at different stages [S2].

### Turn what you've learned into work

Sagmeister's book *Things I Have Learned In My Life So Far* (Abrams, 2008) took about twenty lessons and set them as type in places normally used for advertising: billboards, projections, light boxes, magazine spreads, annual report covers and giant inflatable monkeys [S5].

How he applied it: the book had 256 pages and 15 different covers [S5].

### Study happiness, then look outward

He spent about ten years exploring happiness, which became *The Happy Show* (2012) and *The Happy Film* (2016) [S4]. He tried meditation, cognitive therapy and mood-changing drugs to see if he could raise his own happiness [S7]. Training his mind helped in some ways, but chasing happiness directly didn't work [S1]. As of 2018, his main advice was to stop focusing on your own happiness and look after the happiness of others [S2].

How he applied it: he now focuses on good relationships with people, with his work and with something bigger than himself. He keeps small habits, like exercise and reading one email of praise each morning, and argues for low expectations [S1].

### Take the long view

Since 2019 his *Now Is Better* series has placed data graphics into 18th- and 19th-century paintings, to show long-term trends in democracy, war and peace, health and literacy [S4][S8]. He argues that the long view shows many things getting better, even when the daily news feels bleak, and that building on past success works better than despair [S1].

How he applied it: the series has toured to Tokyo and Seoul, and an expanded version opened in Shanghai in 2024 [S4][S8].

## How he decides

- Ask if the work helps people or delights them [S1].
- Aim for an emotional connection as well as clear information [S2].
- Take form as seriously as function [S3].
- Stay small, choose clients you respect and don't pitch for free [S1].
- Put experiments on the calendar [S1].
- When bored or afraid, change your setting or take time off [S1][S2].
- Look at long-term data before you despair [S1].

## Real decisions

### Cutting the talk into his own skin
For an AIGA Detroit lecture poster, he had the text cut into his skin and photographed it [S6]. His reason was to show the pain that seems to come with most design projects [S6]. The shock suited his own brand, and it would be a risk for a client [S6].

### Closing the studio for a year
He closed the studio to client work every seven years and used the year for experiments [S1][S10]. He was nervous before the first one and called his clients to explain [S1]. He says he often works more hours on a sabbatical than in a normal year, and that everyone he knows who took one rates it among their best choices [S2]. He chose three places for the third one (Mexico City, Tokyo and Schwarzenberg) so he would not repeat old experiences [S2].

### Changing his mind on beauty
Early on he agreed with his mentor Tibor Kalman, who found beauty uninteresting [S2]. After about thirty years he said beauty and form are central to function [S1][S2]. The proof came from the studio: when it took form seriously, client goals were met more often [S3]. He tells how a Memphis conference full of cheapest-and-fastest objects felt next to a Lisbon castle with patterned doorknobs, and asks what happened to us [S1].

### Running a critique salon
He copied artist Louise Bourgeois's Sunday living-room critiques and ran Monday evening salons for up to twelve designers [S1]. He critiques about one in ten Instagram submissions, picking work he can push further [S1]. He avoids snarky reviews because they hurt and teach nothing [S1].

### Moving the office upstairs
During his time in Mexico he moved his office from a basement to a high-ceilinged upstairs living room and became far more productive [S2]. He treats atmosphere as part of the method [S2].

## Where he stood

**Is beauty superficial?** No. As of 2017 he called it central to being human and a strong tool for communication [S2][S3].

**Is design art?** No. Design has to function and art doesn't. He calls himself a designer, not an artist [S1].

**Can you chase happiness directly?** Not well. Look after relationships and other people's happiness instead [S1][S2].

**Is the world getting worse?** By many long-term measures, no, and he wants his work to remind people of that [S1].

**Should designers call themselves storytellers?** In a 2014 video interview he argued, bluntly, that most people in design and advertising who use the label aren't telling stories [S11].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The storyteller rant.** His 2014 remarks spread widely because of their crude wording. A No Film School writer agreed the label had become a fad but thought the outrage overblown, and asked if Sagmeister's definition was too narrow [S11].
- **Beauty versus his mentor.** His own view changed. He once agreed with Tibor Kalman that beauty was uninteresting, and now argues the opposite [S2]. Users should know which period a view comes from.
- **Thin outside critique.** Wikipedia mentions a 2017 controversy only by citation, without detail [S4]. We found no full critical review to open, so we don't describe it.

## Blind spots to watch

- A year off every seven years assumes a studio with savings, loyal clients and a famous name. Most small teams can't do it as he does.
- His focus on beauty and feeling can underrate cost, speed and accessibility. Pair him with a usability or operations mind.
- His optimism about long-term data can feel cold to someone living through a bad year.
- Shock tactics, like the skin-cut poster, suited his own brand [S6]. They can backfire for a client.
