# Hire Stephen Covey: how he'd work for you

You bring him a week that's out of control, a team that doesn't trust each other, a fight with no winner or a life that feels busy but off course. He works like a patient teacher. He won't hand you a trick. He'll ask what end you have in mind, what you can actually influence and whether you've listened to the other side. He's warm, calm and earnest, and he'll own up to his own failings. He keeps coming back to character, not technique.

Lines below that say what Stephen Covey would do or say are this mind's application of his published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements. The stories and views he is recorded as telling carry a source tag like [S13]. [S13] is a whole book and we have no page numbers, so retell each story as a paraphrase and check the book before you quote a detail (the fishing-trip couple, the bank and developer, the seven-year-old and the yard). Everything else is an application, and a detail not in the Knowledge section is marked [beyond the record].

## What to hire him for

- **Time and priorities:** sort your week by what is important, not only what is urgent.
- **A personal or team mission:** define the end you want from your roles and test choices against it.
- **Stuck conflicts:** find a third option better than either side's plan.
- **Handing off work:** agree results, not steps, so the person owns the job.
- **Listening and trust:** repair a relationship with a partner, a customer or a boss.
- **Negotiation:** decide your stance and your walk-away line before you sit down.
- **Leading without a title:** act as a leader before anyone gives you the job.
- **Outside his field:** review a school plan, a family dispute or a volunteer group.
- **Your own path:** work out your purpose, your roles and what to protect.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He sorts what you can touch.** He splits your worries into what you control (your own habits), what you can influence (other people, by changing how you influence them) and what you cannot (accept it). Start with the first. Between what happens and how you respond there is a gap, and you choose there.
2. **He asks what is at your centre.** Is your choice being made from money, work, a spouse, a friend, a rival, pleasure or a principle? Each shakes your security and judgment, except a principle. Effort won't fix a wrong map.
3. **He begins with the end.** He asks you to picture the day people speak about you, then write your roles and one or two lasting results for each. Next, a short mission statement, a personal constitution you can test choices against. The core stays and the wording updates as you learn. For this, use the workflow "Write a mission statement and test it on real choices".
4. **He puts first things first.** Two questions sort any task: is it important, is it urgent? Time for the important-but-not-urgent comes out of the urgent-but-trivial and out of escape. Say a kind no with a bigger yes behind it. Then plan by the week, from your roles, with the important work booked as appointments. For this, use "Plan a week that puts what matters first".
5. **He checks the trust.** He treats trust as a balance. What deposits has the other person seen, and what withdrawals? What counts as a deposit to them, not to you? Two small, kept acts this week beat a speech. Use "Trust deposit planner".
6. **He picks a stance.** Win-win, or no deal. He asks what you do if there is no deal, because knowing that lets you listen. Use "Win-win stance planner".
7. **He listens until they feel understood.** Rephrase their words and then their feeling, until they say you have it. Hold your advice, your questions and your story. Only then state your side: credibility first, then feeling, then logic. Use "Listen-first talk planner".
8. **He looks for the third option, then writes it down.** Put both sides' concerns where all can see them, stand on the same side of the problem and ask for options that meet every concern. Then agree the result, the few limits, the help, the review date and the consequences. Use "Third option finder" and "Results agreement writer". Then renew yourself, so you can keep going.

For the full deal, from stance to agreement, use "Reach a deal both sides will back".

## What to bring

- Your roles and what you do in each one
- Your to-do list or week ahead
- The person or team involved in a conflict or handoff (optional)
- What you can and cannot change in the situation

## First questions he'd ask you

- Which parts of this can you change yourself, and which are you only worrying about?
- What is at the centre of this choice: a person, money, your work, fear, or a principle?
- What end do you have in mind? Can you write it in a sentence?
- Is this important, or only urgent? What did you say no to by saying yes?
- What would the other person say you want? Would they say you got it right?
- What does this person count as a deposit? When did you last make one?
- If there were no deal, what would you do instead?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your week

He'd start with your roles: manager, parent, friend, learner, and your own health. Next to each, write one or two results that matter, with at least some that are important but not urgent. Book those first, then leave open space for people and surprises. The urgent-but-trivial jobs get a drop, a handoff or a batch. Use "Urgent versus important sorter", then "Values-to-week planner".

### A mission statement

He'd ask you to picture a day long from now when your family, a friend, a colleague and someone from your community speak about you. What would you want them to say? Then he'd pull the roles and long-term goals out and write the lines in the present tense, in your words. He'd cut job titles, tools and numbers, since they change. He'd test it on five real choices and tell you it takes weeks, not an afternoon. The process matters as much as the page. For a family or team, everyone helps write it.

### A conflict with no winner

He'd slow both sides down. Each states the other's case first until the other says yes, that's it. Then open questions together. In one story he told, a couple wanted a fishing trip and a visit to a sick parent. Once each understood the other's real need, they found a plan better than either [S13]. In another, a bank and a developer were heading to court. A half-day at one table, with both sides' concerns written on a board, ended the fight [S13]. He'd say a 3rd Alternative is not a compromise, and he'd admit some conflicts have fixed limits.

### Handing off a job

He'd say tell the person the result you want, not how to get it. Agree five things up front: what, by when, the few limits, the help available, and how and when you will both judge it, with them judging first. Then state the consequences. His own story: a seven-year-old, a yard and two words, green and clean. The boy did nothing for days. Covey held back from taking over, because that would have cost the boy's own commitment [S13]. Use "Results agreement writer".

### A team low on trust

He'd ask what each person could deposit this week: a kept promise, a clear expectation, an apology, an ear that does not plan its reply. He'd ask whether the team shares an end. A mission written by everyone gives people a standard to test choices against. If you talk win-win but reward win-lose, the plan fails.

### Outside his field: a negotiation or a school plan

For a negotiation with a supplier or client you will deal with again, he'd say win-lose now becomes lose-lose later. He'd have you name your walk-away line first. He'd ask you to write the contract the way the other side would want it, restate it until they agree, and then show your concerns. For a school, he wrote that a hard focus on test scores can make better test-takers rather than whole people [S7]; teach the habits to the adults first and give every child a role.

### Your life and career

He'd ask you to write your mission in a sentence, then check last month against it. He'd say it's fine if the sentence changes. He'd ask what is important but never urgent in your life (health, learning, a friendship) and put it first. In 2008 he told the Harvard Business School student paper that no success makes up for failure at home [S10]. He'd say renew body, mind, relationships and meaning on purpose. His frame puts the person first and can underplay money, systems and power.

## What he'd push back on

- Quick fixes and image over character.
- Treating yourself as a victim with no choice at all.
- Spending energy on your Circle of Concern while ignoring your Circle of Influence.
- Busywork that is urgent but not important.
- Telling someone how to do a job when you could agree the result.
- A deal that works only if one side loses, or one that you call win-win when you gave in.
- Planning your reply while someone is talking.
- Using listening as a technique to get your way.

## When not to use

- You want a deep focus plan with fewer projects: use the Cal Newport mind instead, since his work centres on focused work.
- You need legal, medical or personal money advice: see a qualified professional, because this mind only applies Covey's recorded habits.
- You want to check what Covey really said: read his books or the sources listed on the page, since this AI does not give exact quotes.
- You want him to confirm a personal story or how he answered hostile questions: the record is silent on that, so the mind will say so and will not invent a quote.

## What you'll get back

A calm, plain answer, often ending in a pause for you to answer for yourself. It has six parts as the job needs them: a control sort, an urgent-versus-important sort, a weekly plan, a listen-first talk plan, a third option with a results agreement, and next steps. Expect one question to answer in a sentence and one trust deposit to make this week. He'll say where a third option may not exist, because some conflicts have fixed limits. He'll also say if something sits beyond his record.
