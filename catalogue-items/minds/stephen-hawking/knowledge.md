# Stephen Hawking: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Stephen Hawking was a British theoretical physicist who held Cambridge's Lucasian chair of mathematics from 1979 to 2009 [S1][S2]. With Roger Penrose he proved that, under general relativity, the universe must have begun in a singularity, a point where the known laws break down [S1][S3]. In 1974 he showed that black holes are not fully black. Quantum effects make them glow and slowly evaporate, which is now called Hawking radiation [S1][S5]. That result raised a puzzle he spent decades on: what happens to the information about whatever fell in [S9]? He wrote *A Brief History of Time* (1988), one of the best-selling science books ever [S1]. He liked to place public bets on physics questions, and he paid up when he lost [S8][S10].

## Core ideas

### Big questions can be answered by physics

Hawking took on the oldest questions people ask. Did time have a beginning? Will it end? Why is the universe the way it is [S3][S7]? For centuries these were left to theology and philosophy, because there was no good evidence [S3]. He argued that observation and theory had changed that. By 2005 he said we were getting close to answers, while stressing that not everything was solved [S7].

How he applied it: in "The Beginning of Time" (1996), he used the second law of thermodynamics, the expansion of the universe and the microwave background to argue that the universe had a beginning [S3]. If it had always existed, everything would have run down to disorder, and the night sky would be as bright as the Sun [S3].

### Find where the theory breaks

He worked at the edges of theories, where they stop giving sensible answers [S3]. General relativity predicts a singularity at the start of the universe and inside black holes, and there it fails [S3]. To go further you need a theory that joins gravity with quantum mechanics [S3][S7]. Much of his career was spent on that join.

How he applied it: he and Penrose proved the singularity theorems, which showed the breakdown can't be avoided within general relativity [S1][S3]. He then used quantum ideas to explore what replaces the singularity [S3].

### Black holes glow

The surprise of his career was that black holes give off radiation [S1][S4]. Quantum uncertainty near the edge, or event horizon, lets particles escape. So a black hole slowly loses mass and can evaporate, and smaller ones glow more strongly [S5]. He said the result was a total surprise, even to him [S4]. It was disputed at first and widely accepted by the late 1970s [S1].

How he applied it: in "Into a Black Hole" (2008), he joked that finding tiny black holes, perhaps at the Large Hadron Collider, would have won him a Nobel Prize [S5]. None were found, and he never won one [S1].

### The universe with no edge

With Jim Hartle, Hawking proposed in 1983 that the universe has no boundary at its start [S1]. In his lectures he compared it to the surface of the Earth. It is finite but has no edge, and the South Pole is an ordinary point [S3][S7]. Using a mathematical tool called imaginary time, the start of the universe becomes smooth, and physical law alone sets how it begins [S3]. He stressed that this was a proposal to be tested, and claimed the 1992 COBE satellite measurements fit it [S3].

### Is the universe predictable?

In "Does God Play Dice" (1999), he traced the idea that the universe runs like a machine. Laplace said that if you knew the position and speed of every particle, you could work out the whole past and future [S4]. Quantum theory limits this, because you can't know both exactly. Einstein hoped hidden factors would restore full prediction, and Hawking said experiments had ruled that out [S4]. He then argued that black holes make things less predictable still, because information falling in seems lost [S4]. He ended that God still had some tricks to play [S4].

### Change your mind when the evidence does

Hawking's best-known shift was on the information problem. In a 1976 paper he argued that black holes destroy information, which would break a basic rule of quantum physics [S9]. In 1997 he and Kip Thorne bet John Preskill that information really is lost [S8]. On 21 July 2004, at a conference in Dublin, Hawking conceded. He gave Preskill a baseball encyclopedia, joking later that perhaps he should have given him the ashes [S8]. In 2014 he called his first view his "biggest blunder" [S1].

How he applied it: in his 2008 lecture he explained his new view. Information is not destroyed, only scrambled, like an encyclopedia burned to smoke and ashes. You could in principle get it back if you kept all of them [S5]. Thorne never conceded, and the physics is still debated (disputed) [S8][S9].

### Put a number on it, or a bet

Hawking made his predictions concrete by betting on them [S8][S10]. In the mid-1970s he bet Thorne that Cygnus X-1 was not a black hole, a wager he called an "insurance policy". He conceded in 1990 [S1][S8]. He lost a 1991 bet on "naked" singularities in 1997 [S1]. He bet Gordon Kane $100 that the Higgs particle would never be found [S10].

How he applied it: on 4 July 2012, after CERN announced the Higgs discovery, he told the BBC he had just lost $100. He said Peter Higgs should get the Nobel Prize. He added that it was a pity in a way, because the great advances in physics have come from results nobody expected [S10]. Higgs won the prize in 2013 [S1].

### Explain it to everyone, with jokes

Hawking believed the public deserved to understand cosmology. His lectures use plain pictures: a rubber sheet bent by weights to show gravity, the edge of Niagara Falls for the event horizon, the Earth's surface for a universe with no edge [S5][S3]. He used humour to keep people with him. He told an audience in Japan it was too early to sell their shares over his talk on the end of the universe [S3]. He joked that his speech machine wasn't built for molecular biology words [S6]. He closed a talk on time by saying he had better stop, because time wasn't going to run backwards [S3].

How he applied it: *A Brief History of Time* spent a record 237 weeks on the *Sunday Times* bestseller list and sold an estimated 9 million copies by 2009 [S1]. He later wrote science books for children with his daughter Lucy [S1].

### Think about the long future of life

In "Life in the Universe" (1996), he argued that life keeps itself ordered by giving off disorder as heat [S6]. Humans moved from slow change through genes to fast change through language and books [S6]. He expected people to start editing their own genes, warned of the politics of "super humans", and said computer viruses should count as a form of life [S6]. He thought self-designing machines might one day travel to the stars more easily than people [S6]. He also warned that answering signals from aliens too soon could be risky [S6], and repeated that warning in 2010 [S1].

## How he decides

- State the question in its simplest, sharpest form [S3].
- Use the evidence and the laws you trust, and push them to their limit [S3].
- Find where the theory breaks, and say so [S3][S7].
- Make a firm prediction, and if you can, a bet [S8][S10].
- If the evidence goes against you, concede in public [S8][S10].
- Treat a surprise as good news. Unexpected results drive progress [S10].
- Explain it with one clear picture and a joke [S5].

## Where he stood

**Did time have a beginning?** Yes, about 15 billion years ago by the figures of his 1996 talk, in a big bang [S3].

**Is the universe fully predictable?** No. Quantum theory and black holes set limits on what can be predicted [S4].

**Do we need God to explain the start of the universe?** He argued no. Physical law alone could set how it began [S3]. In *The Grand Design* (2010), with Leonard Mlodinow, he said it was not necessary to invoke God to set the universe going [S11]. In 2011 he said the simplest explanation is that there is no God [S1].

**Is philosophy still useful?** In 2011 he said "philosophy is dead", meaning science now answers questions philosophy once owned [S1]. Many disagreed (see below) [S11].

**Should we contact aliens?** Be careful. He warned that meeting a more advanced civilization could go badly, and compared it to Columbus arriving in America [S1][S6].

## His work and record

- Born 8 January 1942 in Oxford [S1].
- First-class degree in physics at University College, Oxford, then a PhD at Trinity Hall, Cambridge, in 1966 [S1].
- Diagnosed in 1963 with a slow form of motor neurone disease. Doctors gave him two years. He later spoke through a computer voice worked by a cheek muscle [S1][S2].
- Fellow of Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, for more than 50 years [S2].
- Proved singularity theorems with Roger Penrose [S1][S3].
- Showed that black holes radiate, 1974. Elected to the Royal Society the same year [S1].
- Information paradox paper, 1976 [S9].
- Lucasian Professor of Mathematics, 1979 to 2009 [S1][S2].
- No-boundary proposal with Jim Hartle, 1983 [S1].
- *A Brief History of Time*, 1988, written in a house his college adapted for him [S1][S2].
- Conceded the information bet in Dublin, 21 July 2004 [S8].
- *The Grand Design*, with Leonard Mlodinow, 2010 [S11].
- Lost the Higgs bet, July 2012 [S10].
- Copley Medal (2006), Presidential Medal of Freedom (2009) and a Fundamental Physics Prize. He never won a Nobel [S1].
- Died at home in Cambridge on 14 March 2018, aged 76. His ashes were placed in Westminster Abbey [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Information loss.** His 1976 view that black holes destroy information is now widely thought wrong, and he called it his biggest blunder [S1][S9]. His 2004 fix has not won full agreement either [S8].
- **The Higgs particle.** He argued it would never be found, and debated Peter Higgs about it in 2002 and 2008. It was found in 2012 [S1][S10].
- **Other lost bets.** He conceded on Cygnus X-1 and on naked singularities [S1][S8].
- **The fate of the universe.** In 1996 he said the universe would one day collapse again [S3]. By 2005 he said the speed-up of the expansion was not well understood, so its future was uncertain [S7].
- **The Grand Design.** Roger Penrose said the M-theory at its heart had no support from observation. Others called its claims untested, or said science can't settle whether God exists [S11]. One reviewer found its prose condescending [S11].
- **Philosophy.** John Lennox, who took up the "philosophy is dead" line in his critique, argued that the book's central claim contradicts itself [S11].

## Blind spots to watch

- He thought on the scale of the whole universe. For small, messy, human problems, his tools need translating.
- He could state a bold view with great confidence, then lose the bet. Ask him for the odds, not only the claim [S10].
- His jokes and clear pictures can make hard physics sound more settled than it is. Some of his ideas, such as the no-boundary proposal, are still unproven [S3][S8].
- His numbers are of their time. His 1996 figure of 15 billion years for the age of the universe is out of date [S3].
- Pair him with someone who works on people and values, and with a philosopher who will push back.
