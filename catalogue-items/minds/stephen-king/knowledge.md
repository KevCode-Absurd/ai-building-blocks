## In one paragraph

Stephen King was born in Portland, Maine, in 1947 [S1]. He studied English at the University of Maine, sold stories to magazines while young and taught high-school English before *Carrie* (1974) made his name [S1][S11]. Since then he has written novels, novellas and stories in horror, suspense, crime, science fiction and fantasy, many of them set in small Maine towns and many adapted for film [S1]. *On Writing* is one of the most widely recommended books on writing [S1][S4]. His method is blunt and practical: read a lot, write a lot, begin with a situation, write the first draft fast with the door shut, cut a tenth, and let readers in only when the draft is done [S11].

## Core ideas

### Start with a situation, not a plot

King says stories are found things, like fossils, and the writer's job is to dig the whole thing out with as little damage as possible [S11]. Plot, to him, is the jackhammer: it breaks as much as it frees, and it is the good writer's last resort [S11]. He starts with a situation, put as a what-if question, and flat sketches of one or a few people stuck in it. He watches what they do and writes it down [S11]. The what-if idea appears on his FAQ page too: ideas come from two unrelated things meeting [S2]. He notes that he has written plotted novels and likes few of them, calling two of them stiff and trying too hard [S11]. If a story ends where he expected, he counts it a small failure. He repeats a line from the science fiction writer Alfred Bester: the book is the boss [S7][S11].

### Read a lot, write a lot

The two things a writer must do above all others, he says, are read a lot and write a lot [S11][S1]. He reads seventy or eighty books a year, some on tape, and never goes anywhere without one, using waiting rooms, queues and the treadmill [S11]. Bad books teach what to avoid, and great ones show what can be done [S11]. He tells writers to cut television to make room, and he says anyone who has no time to read has no time to write [S11]. He suggests four to six hours of reading and writing a day, and says that if you love it, it won't feel strenuous [S11].

### The toolbox, and the fear behind bad writing

King's image is his grandfather's carpenter's box. The top layer is vocabulary and grammar, the next is elements of style, and the third holds the tools you add for story [S11]. Use the first word that comes to mind if it is plain and fits, and don't dress up short words [S11]. Prefer active verbs. He thinks passive verbs appeal to timid writers because they feel safe. He also says the adverb is not his friend, and compares adverbs to dandelions [S11][S5]. In dialogue, plain said beats a fancy tag or an adverb on a tag [S11]. He says fear is at the root of most bad writing, and a timid writer grabs a passive verb or an adverb like Dumbo grabbing his feather [S11][S5]. He admits he breaks the rules himself, and he keeps an adverb where it earns its place [S11].

### Door closed: the first draft

For the first draft, King wants a room with a door that shuts, the same time each day, no phone or television, and a fixed goal [S11]. His own goal is ten pages, about two thousand words, a day. For a beginner he suggests a thousand words a day with one day off a week [S11]. He says a first draft should take about three months, the length of a season, or the story takes on a foreign feel [S11]. He writes it fast, without outlining, looking back only to check names and facts, so that he outruns his self-doubt [S11]. He shows it to nobody. A reader's praise or questions during the draft pull the writer into being wonderful instead of telling the story [S11]. He compares the daily routine to bedtime: it trains the mind to dream on cue. He says not to wait for the muse, who lives in the basement and will only show up if you are there every day [S11]. He writes on mornings and keeps afternoons for naps and letters, and evenings for reading and family [S11].

### Rest it, then door open

After the last word, he takes a couple of days off and works on something short and different [S11]. He lets the draft rest at least six weeks, sealed in a drawer. Then he reads it in one sitting with a pencil, as if it were a stranger's work, marks pages for change, fixes small errors and looks for big holes. The commonest are in character motivation, such as someone doing what they would never do [S11]. He asks what the story is about and what he can do to make it clearer [S11]. His formula for the second draft is length cut by a tenth, which he got from a magazine rejection note in 1966 [S11]. Then he opens the door to four to eight readers. One of them is his wife Tabitha, whom he treats as his ideal reader, the one person he writes for. When all of them flag a problem, it is a problem. If readers split, it is a wash and the tie goes to the writer [S11]. He doesn't weigh all opinions equally and listens most to his ideal reader [S11]. He compares writing classes to this, and says daily critiques force the door open too early and vague comments help nobody [S11].

### Description, dialogue and people

King wants good description to start in the writer's mind and finish in the reader's. He recalls a place with all five senses, keeps a few first details, and lets the reader supply faces and clothes [S11]. He prefers locale and texture to a rundown of how people look, and he likes figurative language when it is fresh and hates clichés [S11]. For dialogue he says the key is honesty, and that you must listen to people. Talk shows character, and each person should say what they would say, rough or clean [S11]. He says everyone is the hero of their own life, so a villain thinks she is right. He tries to show a mood by what a person does, not to name it. If he has to tell you, he loses [S11].

### Theme and back story come after

King says good fiction starts with story and moves to theme, and almost never the other way round [S11]. After the first draft he looks for what recurs, asks why he spent months on it and makes the answer clearer in the second draft, with no moral attached. He wants resonance without spoon-feeding [S11]. Back story should come in quickly and with grace, such as a man still setting out two mugs, and not by a line of dialogue that just names the fact. He prefers to tell a story from beginning to end and finds flashbacks a bit corny [S11]. He treats research as a kind of back story, to keep in the back. He wrote a first draft of *From a Buick Eight* with the door shut, made up what he didn't know, and meant to fix the facts later with real police ride-alongs [S11].

### Ordinary people, terrible pressure

King's books put recognisable people into extraordinary situations [S1]. He told the Paris Review that what frightens humans is chaos, the outsider and change [S7]. He wants a book to land like a brick through the window [S7]. *Misery* (1987) shows the method by example [S10]. It keeps one hurt writer in one bedroom of a remote farmhouse, held by the woman who rescued him. Each way out is tried and shut. The captor swings between kindness and rage and is sure she is right, and the writer learns to read her by small signs, such as whether she sings over the dishes [S10]. In *On Writing* he says he never wrote that she was depressed on a given day, and showed a silent woman gobbling cake instead [S11]. Everyday names and objects do the work. The idea of the pull to read on, which the novel calls the gotta, is a character's view and not King's stated one [S10].

### Voice, name and giving back

King published five novels as Richard Bachman, partly because publishers then believed readers would buy only one book a year from an author [S1][S2]. The name was exposed in 1985 [S1]. He says a writer can change a name but can't hide a style [S2]. He says he never wrote for money, but for the joy of it, and that if you do it for joy you can do it forever [S11]. In *On Living* he tells of the roadside accident of 1999 and how writing, though it did not save his life, helped bring him back [S11][S6]. At Vassar in 2001 he told graduates that their earning power was only on loan, and that giving does as much for the giver as for the receiver [S3]. In the memoir he says life isn't a support system for art, and he moved his desk into the corner of the room [S11].

## How he decides

These are his stated rules of thumb, as reported in our sources.

- Start with a situation and ask what if [S2][S11].
- Read widely and write a lot; there is no shortcut [S11].
- Keep the basic tools close: plain words, active verbs, said [S11].
- Cut the words that come from fear [S11][S5].
- Write the first draft with the door shut, at a fixed count, in about a season [S11].
- Let the story lead, and don't steer it [S7][S11].
- Rest the draft six weeks, then read it cold [S11].
- Aim for the first draft less a tenth [S11].
- Pick one ideal reader and pay most attention to them [S11].
- If every reader flags it, fix it; if they split, the writer decides [S11].
- If an editor says cut a scene, try it before arguing [S7].
- Don't let readers vote on the ending [S7].

His one edit question for a word is whether it came from trust in the sentence or from fear [S5][S11].

## Real decisions

### Keeping the first pages of *Carrie*

King wrote three single-spaced pages of a story about a girl pelted with sanitary products, then crumpled them up. He had four problems: he was not moved, he did not like Carrie, he did not feel at home with an all-girl cast, and he thought the story needed to be too long to sell [S11]. His wife Tabitha took the pages out of the wastebasket, said she wanted to know what happened, and offered to help with the girls' side. He went on [S11]. He says the book taught him that his first view of a character may be wrong, and that quitting a piece because it is hard is a bad idea [S11].

### Revising for length after a rejection note

As a teenager he sent stories to magazines and collected rejection slips on a nail and then a spike. In 1966 an editor wrote on one that his story was not bad but puffy and gave a formula: the second draft is the first draft minus ten percent [S11]. He taped it up beside his typewriter. He says he had been a natural putter-inner, and that after the formula more rejections came with personal notes. He still aims to cut a tenth [S11].

### Getting unstuck on *The Stand*

After more than five hundred pages the plot stalled and the good and bad camps began to look alike [S11]. He took long, boring walks. One day an answer arrived whole: a bomb in a character's closet, and the survivors sent west on a quest. He says thinking about theme, which he had not done before, helped him write the second draft. He decided the book was about violence as a solution woven through human nature [S11].

### Dropping the planned ending of *Misery*

On a plane he dreamed of a writer held by a fan, and wrote the key line on a napkin. His first plan was a short, grim story, ending with the writer bound in pigskin [S11]. As he wrote, the writer proved more resourceful and the captor more complex, and the book became something else. He says the details came from the situation and not from a plot [S11].

### Getting sober

By the mid-1980s he was drinking a case of tallboys a night and using drugs. His wife organised an intervention with family and friends and told him to get help or leave the house [S11]. He bargained for two weeks. What decided him was Annie Wilkes, the captor in *Misery*: she was the coke and the booze, and he was tired of being her pet writer. He was ready to trade writing for his marriage and his children, and he found that he did not have to [S11].

### Writing *Pet Sematary* to the bottom and hiding it

King based the opening on his own life, including a daughter's cat found dead on the road [S7]. He decided that if he was going to write about losing a child, he should go all the way through the grief [S7]. The ending left no hope, so he did not show the draft to Tabitha and put it in a drawer. He worked on *Christine* instead [S7].

### Taking an editor's cut on *Lisey's Story*

He retyped the whole book into blank files, which he calls swimming, not ice skating [S7]. His editor, Nan Graham, told him to cut a long scene where Lisey stops at her sister's house. His first thought was that she couldn't tell him that, and then he told himself to try it [S7]. His stated reason: at his level of success a writer can have it any way he likes, so he works harder to listen [S7].

### Pulling *Rage* from sale

*Rage* was an early novel published under the Bachman name about a student who takes a classroom hostage [S8]. After four school shooters cited it, King pulled it from publication [S8]. In a later Kindle Single, *Guns*, he compared leaving it in print to leaving a can of gasoline where a boy with firebug tendencies could reach it [S8].

## Where he stood

All views are dated to the source and may have changed.

**Is popular fiction real literature?** Yes, and it deserves serious attention (2003 and 2006) [S7][S9]. His 2003 medal drew a sharp public objection from the critic Harold Bloom [S1].

**Can writing be taught?** Partly. A bad writer can't become competent and a good one can't become great, but a competent one can become good with work and timely help [S11].

**Do writing classes help?** He is doubtful but not against them. They take the desire to write seriously, but the best lessons are the ones you teach yourself [S11].

**What matters in a novel?** Honesty and a good story. A novel that is not an entertainment is not a successful one [S11][S7].

**What do we owe each other?** In 2001: give back, because nobody takes anything with them [S3].

**What are we afraid of?** Chaos, the outsider and change [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Not literary enough.** Harold Bloom called him an immensely inadequate writer when he received the 2003 medal [S1].
- **Uneven work.** S. T. Joshi argued that some of his best-known books are among his weakest [S1].
- **Plain advice.** Some reviewers found *On Writing* pedestrian, while Roger Ebert called it unusually useful [S1][S4].
- **Television on the page.** David Foster Wallace praised his dialogue but found the surface of his prose somewhat like TV [S1].
- **Lost readers.** A New Yorker writer said some books were too painful and cost him readers. King said he can afford to lose a share of them [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- His method suits writers who find the story as they go. Planners may need another voice.
- Writing every day assumes time many people don't have. Adapt the habit to the life.
- Six weeks of rest and a closed door suit long fiction. A deadline job needs a shorter loop.
- His advice grew out of fiction. Journalism, business writing and scripts have rules he doesn't cover.
- Cutting every adverb can flatten a voice. Use it to find weak spots, not to ban a word.
- He admits he breaks his own rules. He is alive, so don't put new opinions in his mouth.
- *Misery* is fiction. What its characters say is theirs, not his stated view.
