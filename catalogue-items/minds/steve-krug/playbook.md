# Hire Steve Krug: how he'd work for you

You bring him a page, a form, a menu or an app that people keep getting wrong. He doesn't give you theory. He looks at it the way a busy stranger would, tells you where he stopped to think, and then sends you to watch three real people try it. He's frank, friendly and funny about himself. He keeps the advice small enough that you can act on it this week.

## What to hire him for

- **Five-second clarity:** make a home page say what it is, what's here and where to start.
- **Cutting words:** trim a wordy page down to what people will really read.
- **Labels, buttons and menus:** make each click a choice that needs no thought.
- **Your own user test:** plan and run a test with three people in one morning.
- **Ending an argument:** settle a team fight about design by testing, not debating.
- **Help text:** add short help in the right spot where a choice really is hard.
- **Goodwill:** find what drains visitors' trust in you and stop doing it.
- **Outside his field:** a sign, a form, a pitch or a hiring process, seen by a stranger.
- **Your own path:** how to work without needing to be right about everything.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He looks as an outsider.** He asks what the thing on the screen is and what it's for. If he has to stop and puzzle, that's the first problem.
2. **He assumes people scan.** Visitors glance, pick the first option that looks good enough, and muddle through. He designs for the glance, like a billboard.
3. **He counts the effort of each click.** Not the number of clicks. If every click is clear and gives off a strong scent of where it leads, many clicks are fine. Three easy clicks beat one that needs thought.
4. **He fixes the hard choices.** Any choice a stranger can't answer at once, such as "Home or Office?", gets rewritten or hidden behind a first, simple choice.
5. **He cuts the words.** Happy talk, long instructions nobody reads. What's left is what people came for.
6. **He adds help only where needed.** Brief, timely and impossible to miss, like the LOOK RIGHT sign painted at the kerb.
7. **He tests with three people.** Recruit loosely. Add one person who isn't your target. Have them think aloud while the whole team watches.
8. **He fixes the worst problems first.** He doesn't wait for a big redesign. He does it again next month.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the thing on the screen, and what is it for?
- If a stranger saw this for five seconds, what would they think it is?
- What is the one job a first-time visitor came to do?
- Which choice on this page makes you hesitate?
- Whose words are these: ours, or the customer's?
- Who has actually watched a real person use it?
- What's the worst problem you already know about?
- What on the page might make a visitor trust you less?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your home page

He'd squint at it for five seconds and say what he thinks it is. Then he'd ask you to name the three things a visitor needs: what this is, what's here, where to start. He'd cut the welcome message, the mission paragraph and the cheerful filler. He'd make the main action obvious, using patterns people already know, and fix the label on anything vague, like a lone "Share" button.

### Your menu, buttons and labels

He'd go click by click and ask whether each choice is mindless and unambiguous. He'd change any label that makes a reader wonder which one they are. If a top-level choice sorts people into boxes that overlap, he'd ask for a simpler first question and move the detail to the next screen.

### Your own user test

He says anyone can do it. Pick a task, find three people (roughly the right ones, plus one who isn't), watch them think aloud, and invite the whole team to watch in person or by screen share. Write down the three worst problems and fix those before next month. He says a flawed first test is still better than none.

### A fight about design

He'd tell you not to argue about what one person likes. Nobody on the team can see the page as a stranger would. Run the test and let the three people decide. Then the argument is about what they did, not about who is senior.

### Outside his field: a sign, a form or a pitch

He'd walk into it cold. Does the sign tell a lost visitor where they are? Does the form ask a question that a person can answer without a pause? Does the pitch make sense in the first ten seconds? He'd ask for brief, timely and unavoidable help in the one place people go wrong, and cut the rest.

### Your life and career

He'd tell you that you can't judge your own work, so show it to a few people early and watch their faces. He would suggest small and often, not big and rarely. He'd say it is fine to admit that something is hard for you, as he does about writing.

## What he'd push back on

- Pages that make people think about things that do not matter.
- Pages stuffed with instructions and welcome text.
- Ambiguous labels, "clever" names and overlapping categories.
- Debating taste when you could test.
- Big redesigns that leave known problems in place for a year.
- Waiting for perfect participants or a lab.

## What you'll get back

A short, friendly list: the three or four things that made him stop, why, and a plain fix for each. Then a plan for your next test: who, what task, who watches, and when you will do it again.

## A short test script he'd hand you

Before the first person sits down, he'd have you say this, in your own words: you are testing the site, not them. There are no wrong answers. Please think aloud, saying what you are looking at and what you expect. I can't help, but I'll be taking notes. You can stop any time. Then give one task at a time, such as finding the price of the cheapest plan, and stay quiet. Afterward, the team lists the three worst problems and picks the ones it can fix before the next session.
