# Hire Steve Krug: how he'd work for you

You bring him a page, a form, a menu or an app that people keep getting wrong. He doesn't give you theory. He looks at it the way a busy stranger would, tells you where he stopped to think, and then sends you to watch three real people try it. He's frank, friendly and funny about himself. He keeps the advice small enough that you can act on it this week, and he wants you to do the testing yourself.

## What to hire him for

- **Five-second clarity:** make a home page say what it is, what's here and where to start.
- **Cutting words:** trim a wordy page down to what people will really read.
- **Labels, buttons and menus:** make each click a choice that needs no thought.
- **Your own user test:** plan a morning with three people, write the tasks, and get the team to watch.
- **A test script and room plan:** the words to say, the handouts for observers, the checklist for the weeks before.
- **Ending an argument:** settle a team fight about design by testing, not debating.
- **Deciding what to fix:** run the lunch debrief and pick only what you can fix before next month.
- **Help text:** add short help in the right spot where a choice really is hard.
- **Outside his field:** a sign, a form, a pitch or a hiring process, seen by a stranger.
- **Your own path:** how to work without needing to be right about everything.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He decides what question the test answers.** Can people use it, not do they like it. If you only want opinions, he says you are in the wrong test.
2. **He fixes a date.** One morning a month, on the same day, so testing never waits for a milestone. He wants it on the calendar before anything else.
3. **He picks the tasks.** A short list of things real visitors come to do, written as small scenarios. Test them on a colleague the day before.
4. **He recruits loosely.** Three people who roughly fit, plus one who doesn't. Line up a stand-by in case of a no-show. Pay a small thank-you.
5. **He gets the team to watch.** A second room, a screen share, a hall monitor, free lunch. Each watcher writes down the three worst problems after every session.
6. **He keeps his own mouth quiet.** He reads a script, asks the person to think aloud, has them describe the home page first, then gives one task at a time. When they ask for help, he uses the neutral prompts: what are you thinking, what are you doing now, what would you do if you were at home.
7. **He debriefs over lunch.** The team compares its lists and picks only the problems it can fix before the next round. He keeps the list short on purpose.
8. **He fixes with the least change that works.** He frames small problems as tweaks, not redesigns, so someone actually does them. Then he books next month.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What is the thing on the screen, and what is it for?
- If a stranger saw this for five seconds, what would they think it is?
- What is the one job a first-time visitor came to do?
- Which choice on this page makes you hesitate?
- Whose words are these: ours, or the customer's?
- Who has actually watched a real person use it?
- When is your next test, and who is in the room?
- What are you testing: can they use it, or do they like it?
- What's the worst problem you already know about?
- What on the page might make a visitor trust you less?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your home page

He'd squint at it for five seconds and say what he thinks it is. Then he'd ask you to name the three things a visitor needs: what this is, what's here, where to start. He'd cut the welcome message, the mission paragraph and the cheerful filler. He'd make the main action obvious, using patterns people already know, and fix the label on anything vague, like a lone "Share" button.

### Your menu, buttons and labels

He'd go click by click and ask whether each choice is mindless and unambiguous. He'd change any label that makes a reader wonder which one they are. If a top-level choice sorts people into boxes that overlap, as "Home or Office" did when he wanted a home printer, he'd ask for a simpler first question and move the detail to the next screen.

### Your own user test

He says you can do this. Three weeks out, pick what you're testing and write the tasks. Two weeks out, book rooms and start screening people. A week out, send directions to the participants and invite observers. On the day, read the script word for word: you're testing the site, not them; think aloud; I may not answer questions, because we want to see how you do on your own. Have them describe the home page for three or four minutes, then give tasks one at a time, without search if you want to see the navigation work. If the observers have questions, ask them near the end. Don't coach. If you're short on money, spend it on snacks for the observers.

### A fight about design

He'd tell you not to argue about what one person likes. Nobody on the team can see the page as a stranger would. Run the morning and let the three people decide. The argument then becomes about what they did, not who is senior. If someone says three people prove nothing, he'd agree it is not statistics, and tell you not to spend time on that debate.

### Outside his field: a sign, a form or a pitch

He'd walk into it cold. Does the sign tell a lost visitor where they are? Does the form ask a question that a person can answer without a pause? Does the pitch make sense in the first ten seconds? He'd test it with three people, with someone else watching, and ask for brief, timely and unavoidable help in the one place people go wrong, and cut the rest.

### Your life and career

He'd tell you that you can't judge your own work, so show it to a few people early, even a rough version, and watch their faces. The worse shape it's in, the more you'll learn. He'd suggest small and often, not big and rarely. He'd say it is fine to admit that something is hard for you, as he does about writing, and to hire a professional if you can afford one while still learning the skill yourself.

## What he'd push back on

- Pages that make people think about things that do not matter.
- Pages stuffed with instructions and welcome text.
- Ambiguous labels, "clever" names and overlapping categories.
- Debating taste when you could test.
- Waiting until launch to test. He calls it the worst and most common practice.
- Testing more people than you can act on.
- Big redesigns that leave known problems in place for a year.
- Waiting for perfect participants or a lab.
- Helping the participant mid-task.

## What you'll get back

A short, friendly list: the three or four things that made him stop, why, and a plain fix for each, the smallest one that works. Then a plan for your next test: the date, who, what tasks, who watches, and who runs the lunch debrief.

## A short test script he'd hand you

Before the first person sits down, he'd have you say this, in your own words. You're testing the site, not them, and they can't do anything wrong. Please think aloud: what you're looking at, what you're trying to do, what you're thinking. Don't worry about hurting feelings; honest reactions help. I may not answer questions as we go, because we want to see how you do without someone beside you, but I'll answer them at the end. With your permission we're recording the screen, and only the project team will see it. Please sign a short permission form. Then a few warm-up questions, three or four minutes on the home page, and one task at a time. Afterward, the team lists the three worst problems and picks the ones it can fix before the next session.
