# Steve Wozniak: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Steve Wozniak is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from interviews he gave in 1993 [S4] and 2007 [S3], an interview for the book *Founders at Work* (on the book's site by 2009) [S2], a reply he posted on his own site in 2022 [S5], and public reference pages. Treat each view as "as of" the date of the interview it comes from. His interviews are in copyright, so this is our summary, with a few short quotes. Some stories about early Apple are told in different ways by different people. Where his account is the only one we have, we say so. Nothing here covers his private life, and nothing tells you what he thinks about events after these dates.

## In one paragraph

Wozniak is an American electrical engineer who designed the Apple I and Apple II, the machines that launched Apple Computer, which he co-founded with Steve Jobs and Ronald Wayne on 1 April 1976 [S1]. He designed the Apple I's hardware, circuit boards and operating system alone [S1]. The Apple II, shown in April 1977, added colour, sound, graphics and eight expansion slots, and in 2007 he called it probably his greatest invention [S1][S3]. His design rule was to use as few chips as possible, because fewer parts meant lower cost, fewer bugs and a design you could fully understand [S2][S4]. He built computers he wanted for himself, shared his plans with other hobbyists, and preferred engineering to running a company [S2][S4]. Since leaving Apple as an active employee in 1985, he has started other companies, taught computer classes to children and helped found the Electronic Frontier Foundation [S1].

## Core ideas

### Fewer parts is better design

Wozniak said in 1993 that he liked things with very few chips, because to him that was good design [S4]. In the *Founders at Work* interview he explained why: fewer parts cost less, are easier to understand and leave less room for bugs [S2]. He traced the habit to teaching himself as a teenager, when he designed computers on paper and tried to beat his own earlier designs with fewer chips [S2].

How he applied it: he gave the Apple II colour output using a chip that cost about one dollar [S1]. For the Apple II floppy disk, he skipped about 20 chips on the drive maker's own board and replaced them with a small, clever circuit of his own [S2].

### Build what you want for yourself

He built computers he wanted and couldn't buy [S2]. He said in 1993 that the Apple II was not made to be a product to be sold [S4]. It was a computer he wanted to own and could afford [S4]. In 2007 he said he worked out ways to do things very simply, at low cost, all in one computer [S3].

How he applied it: he built the Apple I as his own machine and showed it at the Homebrew Computer Club [S1][S2]. Jobs saw that others wanted one and proposed selling boards [S4]. In his telling, Apple itself was a total accident of history [S4].

### Leave room to grow

Wozniak wanted users to be able to add to their computer. He pointed out in 1993 that rival machines such as the Commodore PET and the TRS-80 could not take plug-in cards [S4]. He said the chips and the number of slots in the Apple II were all his choices [S4].

How he applied it: after a dispute with Jobs, who wanted two slots, the Apple II shipped with eight [S1]. The open design let other companies make cards and software for it [S2]. In 2022 he wrote that one add-on card could give his favourite model, the Apple IIc, 128KB of memory and a second operating system [S5].

### Share the plans

At the Homebrew Computer Club he handed out his schematics so others could build their own computers [S2]. He saw the club as a place to show off clever work and to help others build [S2][S4].

How he applied it: the Apple I started as a design he shared with hobbyists. It only became a product when a local shop, the Byte Shop, ordered 50 of them [S1].

### Check every little detail

He described an obsessive focus on detail. Engineers, he said, must be diligent and check everything [S2]. He wanted to understand a design so deeply that it had no errors [S2]. He told Livingston that no bug was ever found in the Apple II's hardware or software [S2]. That is his own claim.

How he applied it: he built the Apple II floppy disk in about two weeks to show at a trade show. When the work was lost overnight, he rebuilt it [S2].

### No tools? Find a way

Wozniak grew up without money for parts, so he designed on paper [S2]. He told founders not to lean on fancy tools. If you lack them, find a way to do the job anyway [S2]. He added that young people have an edge, because they can work very late nights [S2].

How he applied it: he wrote the BASIC language for the Apple I by hand [S2]. When he needed a floppy disk controller and had none, he designed his own [S2].

### Honesty first, then excellence

His first advice to founders in *Founders at Work* was to hold the highest ethics, to be open and truthful, and to explain what you are doing when you must keep something private [S2]. Next, seek excellence: make your product better than the average person would. If you can whip something out quickly, ask whether you can make it better [S2].

How he applied it: in 1990 he helped found the Electronic Frontier Foundation, which defends people's rights online [S1]. In 2021 he spoke up for the right to repair [S1].

### Stay an engineer

Wozniak didn't want to run Apple. He said he left business, sales and suppliers to Jobs, and that they never formally split the work [S2]. He saw himself as the company's hardware technician [S2]. In 1993 he said success was not about money, and that he felt fulfilled before he was rich [S4].

How he applied it: he left Apple as an active employee in early 1985, mainly to start CL 9 and get back the fun of building new technology [S1]. CL 9 made the first programmable universal remote control in 1987 [S1]. He later taught computer classes to children in fifth to ninth grade, and to their teachers [S1].

## How he decides

- Use the fewest parts that do the job [S2][S4].
- Build what you would want to own [S2][S4].
- Leave room for users to add and change things [S4].
- Share what you know with people building the same things [S2].
- Check every detail until you understand the whole design [S2].
- Be open and truthful, even when you must keep a secret [S2].
- Make it better than average, then ask if it can be better still [S2].
- If you lack a tool, find another way [S2].

## Where he stood

**Will computers become truly intelligent?** His view has changed. In 2007 he said we would never have artificial intelligence, and that a pet was smarter than any computer [S3]. In 2015 he voiced fear about it, and in 2016 said he no longer worried [S1]. In 2023 he signed a public letter calling for a six-month pause on training the most powerful AI systems, and warned that AI could make scams harder to spot [S1].

**What was Steve Jobs's role?** As of 1993, he called Jobs a visionary who was very bright but hard on people [S4]. In *Founders at Work* he said Jobs drove the business side, with sales, suppliers and marketing [S2]. In 2007 he said they had shared a love of electronics since they met in 1971 [S3].

**Should a company control its hardware and software?** As of 2007, yes. He said great products come when Apple controls all parts of the computer [S3].

**What makes a good portable machine?** In 2022 he named the Apple IIc as his favourite Apple II, mainly because it was small and portable [S5].

**What is success?** As of 1993, good work and a good life, not money [S4].

## His work and record

- Born 11 August 1950 in San Jose, California [S1][S6].
- Built his first computer, the "Cream Soda", with Bill Fernandez in 1971 [S1].
- Designed calculators at Hewlett-Packard in the early 1970s. HP looked at his computer and passed on it [S1][S2].
- Designed the circuit board for Atari's *Breakout* with Jobs in 1973 [S1].
- Finished the basic Apple I design on 1 March 1976. It sold for $666.66 [S1].
- Co-founded Apple on 1 April 1976 and left HP after Mike Markkula invested that November [S1].
- Introduced the Apple II in April 1977 and the Disk II floppy drive in 1978 [S1].
- Stepped away from Apple for a time in 1981, when Jobs took over the Macintosh project [S1].
- Sponsored the US Festivals in 1982 and 1983 [S1].
- Received the National Medal of Technology in 1985 [S1].
- Finished his Berkeley degree in electrical engineering and computer science in 1986. Wikipedia also gives 1987 [S1][S6].
- Helped found the Electronic Frontier Foundation in 1990 [S1].
- Joined the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2000. He holds four U.S. patents [S6].
- Published his memoir *iWoz*, written with Gina Smith, in 2006 [S1].
- Launched Woz U, an online tech school, in 2017 [S1][S7].
- Co-founded Privateer Space in 2021 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Woz U.** In 2018 CBS News reported complaints from more than two dozen students and staff: outdated recorded lectures, weak mentors and pressure to enroll. The school admitted course errors and denied pressure selling. Wozniak declined to be interviewed [S7]. A complaint about the school's quality was later dismissed [S1].
- **AI calls.** His 2007 claim that we would never have AI did not age well, and he has since changed his view more than once [S1][S3].
- **Blue boxes.** As a young man he built "blue boxes" for making free long-distance calls, which Jobs sold [S1].
- **Big losses.** He lost several million dollars on the first US Festival in 1982 [S1].
- **Atari pay.** About ten years after *Breakout*, he learned that Atari had paid a $5,000 bonus that Jobs did not share with him. He has said he would have given Jobs the money if asked. The story rests mainly on his account [S1][S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- He designs for engineers like himself. Pair him with someone who thinks about non-technical buyers and how products are sold.
- Fewest-chips cleverness can make a design hard for others to follow or fix later.
- He avoided management. His advice suits a builder more than someone leading a large team.
- Late nights work for a young solo builder. They are no plan for most teams.
- His views on AI and tech have shifted over time. Use his dated words, and don't project them onto new events.
