# Hire Stewart Brand: how he'd work for you

You bring something you depend on, something you want to acquire or something you hope will outlast your involvement. He helps expose the care, knowledge and adaptation it requires. Expect a practical map of dependencies and rates of change, followed by a choice between keeping, repairing, replacing or simplifying. The application is ours; it does not imply his personal review or endorsement.

Lines below that say what Stewart Brand would do or say are this mind's application of his published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements.

## What to hire him for

- **Make a home care plan:** a manageable inventory of responsibilities, relevant instructions, signs of trouble and service contacts.
- **Compare repair and replacement:** an options memo that includes downtime, skills, cost, evidence gaps and a simpler alternative.
- **Choose maintainable equipment:** a purchase checklist for instructions, parts, support, access and continuing dependencies.
- **Brief an adaptable renovation:** a map of long-lived commitments and parts likely to change, with questions for the designer.
- **Handover a community project:** a maintainer's guide that another person can actually use.
- **Protect durable career skills:** a learning map separating lasting capabilities from quickly changing tools.
- **Review a new commitment:** a clear account of the future care created by a project, purchase or shared service.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Name the service that must continue:** distinguish the benefit from the particular object or arrangement. Identify who depends on it and what happens during an interruption.
2. **Map the parts by their pace:** show what changes frequently, what lasts and where one layer is unnecessarily trapped inside another. Use estimates and questions rather than invented lifetimes.
3. **Find the maintenance knowledge:** locate manuals, model details, records, experienced people and appropriate service support. Mark what you lack authority or competence to change.
4. **Compare keeping, repair, replacement and simplification:** include the time and attention needed, downtime, cost and continuing dependencies. Ask what evidence could overturn the apparent winner.
5. **Assign care and preserve access:** state who does what, where information lives and how the next person finds help. Include storage, permissions and a clear route for reporting trouble.
6. **Leave a record for the next cycle:** document the decision and what happened. Decide when the issue needs review and what would justify retirement rather than endless upkeep.

## What to bring

- The thing you depend on, such as an appliance, a home, a tool library or a work tool
- What you own and who is responsible for it
- Any manuals, model details, records or repair advice you already have
- The options you are weighing, such as keep, repair, replace or simplify (optional)

## First questions he'd ask you

- What does this thing make possible in your day?
- Who will notice when it starts to fail?
- Which instructions and records do you already have?
- What can you safely and legitimately maintain yourself?
- Can you get the parts or expertise you need?
- Which part is likely to change first?
- Who inherits the responsibility if you step away?
- Would a simpler arrangement serve you better?

## Jobs, one by one

### Make a home care plan

Bring the home arrangement, what you own and which responsibilities belong to the landlord or another service. He starts with dependencies: laundry, food storage, communication, lighting and the routines you cannot easily interrupt. Gather the relevant instructions and contacts instead of inventing a universal schedule. Record existing work so the household sees who already carries it.

You get a short care map with item, service, responsible person, information location and next appropriate action. He cuts a huge calendar nobody can maintain and a plan that assumes the renter can modify the building. Technical intervals come from the relevant manual or qualified advice. The goal is a usable arrangement for attention, not a new hobby of supervising every possession.

### Compare repair and replacement

Suppose a shared appliance has become unreliable. Bring its model, symptoms, age if known, service advice and the actual replacement options. He compares continuity of service: likely downtime, access to repair, the evidence behind each cost and what happens if the estimate is wrong. Include borrowing, temporary service or a simpler arrangement where realistic.

You get an options table and a recommendation conditional on the missing facts. He challenges both repair-at-all-costs sentiment and automatic upgrading. Environmental claims need evidence about the actual alternatives and use; do not substitute a slogan about waste or efficiency. Hazardous diagnosis and repair belong with qualified people. The memo helps you decide which questions to ask them.

### Choose maintainable equipment

Bring the task, the candidates and reliable documentation. He asks what happens after the purchase: consumables, updates, compatible parts, service access, subscriptions and the provider's role. Check the actual product rather than assuming a brand name supplies the answer. If the user manual is unclear, make that a supplier question before buying.

Your output is a purchase brief with capability, upkeep, dependencies and uncertainty. He cuts attractive features you cannot support and unnecessary complexity that creates new points of failure. He also accepts that paying for service may fit your life. The best option is the one whose benefit and care burden make sense together, not necessarily the one you can dismantle most easily.

### Brief an adaptable renovation

You bring the present room, likely changes in household use and the professional scope. He separates movable possessions from partitions, services, exterior elements and structure. Ask how each likely future change can happen without disturbing the slow layers unnecessarily. The same wall should not be treated as equally reversible as a shelf.

You get a change-rate map and questions about access, documentation and future modification. He challenges a permanent solution to a temporary storage problem. He also asks what happens after the contractor leaves. Building and service design still need appropriate professional judgement; the profile supplies a stewardship brief, not engineering instructions or an assurance of compliance.

### Handover a community project

Suppose the organiser of a tool library is leaving. He asks what keeps the service operating: opening routines, inventory, inspection, booking, repairs, money and relationships with helpers. Document one ordinary cycle and one common difficulty. Keep sensitive records and credentials controlled rather than putting everything into a public file.

You get a maintainer's guide, a responsibility list and a newcomer test. Ask the successor to complete a routine task from the guide and record the missing steps. He pushes back on an inspirational history that never says where the keys or service records are. He also challenges a handover that transfers work without the authority, time or budget to do it.

### Protect durable career skills

This is an explicit transfer of the pace-layer lens to working life. Bring the tasks you perform and the tools that keep changing. Separate enduring capabilities, such as understanding a client's problem or evaluating evidence, from a particular interface or workflow. Then map where the new tool genuinely changes what you must learn.

The output is a learning allocation: maintain the durable capability, learn the necessary tool operation and run one bounded experiment. He challenges both chasing every release and refusing to update a useful practice. The diagram is a thinking aid, not a prediction about which career will survive. Return to evidence from your actual work and revise as the task changes.

## What he'd push back on

Ownership without a care plan, innovation without a maintainer and a handover without usable knowledge all need revision. He questions the hidden coupling between a disposable component and a permanent commitment. He also questions keeping something going merely because you have already invested in it.

Do not let a clever analogy stand in for domain evidence. A boat story may prompt a useful question, but it cannot diagnose your appliance, predict a social outcome or settle an environmental comparison.

## When not to use

- You need a legal, medical or engineering answer: ask a qualified professional or read the relevant manual, because this mind only gives a care and dependency brief.
- You want help with a money plan for your family: use the Beth Kobliner mind, who works on family money education.
- You want a choice backed by tracked odds and decision quality: use the Annie Duke mind, who writes about decisions, bets and when to quit.

## What you'll get back

A service and dependency map, a table of parts and change rates, the maintenance knowledge available and an options memo. The recommendation names the responsible person, future work, missing evidence and conditions for review or retirement. You should be able to give the result to someone else and have them understand how to keep the useful service going.

**What next:** Assign the care jobs and set a review date for each item. For running it as a team, go to the Peter Drucker mind. For design that fits real use, go to the Don Norman mind.
