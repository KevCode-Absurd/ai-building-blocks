## In one paragraph

Butterfield is a Canadian founder with a philosophy degree who twice set out to build an online game and twice ended up with something else [S2][S7]. Game Neverending failed to raise money in 2002, and its photo feature became Flickr, which Yahoo bought in 2005 [S2][S7]. Glitch, his second game, closed in 2012, and the chat tool his team had built for itself became Slack [S2][S8]. His core lesson is that people don't buy a product, they buy what it does for them. In his 2013 memo he told staff that Slack sold better teams, not group chat, the way a saddle maker should sell the joy of riding [S1]. He also pushed for extreme polish, a beginner's eye for every small irritation, and harsh self-review [S1][S3]. He later added ideas on product design, such as the "owner's delusion," and on big-company busywork [S4].

## Core ideas

### Sell the ride, not the saddle

Butterfield's best-known idea is that you should sell the change your product brings, not the product. In the memo he imagines a saddle company. If it sells saddles, it fights rivals on leather and style. If it sells horseback riding, it grows the whole market and leads it [S1]. Nobody was shopping for a group chat system, so Slack had to sell the result: lower costs of talking to each other, less information overload, and a searchable record of the team's work [S1].

How he applied it: the memo framed Slack as selling better organisations and better teams. It pointed to Harley-Davidson selling freedom and Lululemon selling yoga as brands that sell a way of life [S1]. Slack's later listing papers still described it as more than an email replacement [S5].

### Ask who your customers will become

Slack asked a lot of users. They had to spend hours learning a new tool and give up years of email habits [S1]. Butterfield argued that a big ask needs a big reward and near-perfect execution [S1]. So the memo described the person Slack wanted users to become: relaxed, productive, in control of their information, and less frustrated by not knowing what their team was doing [S1].

How he applied it: the team's job, in his words, was to learn what people think they want and translate Slack's value into those terms, from the website through daily use [S1].

### Polish away every petty irritation

People who don't yet want your product have little patience for its flaws [S1]. Butterfield asked staff to look at Slack with a beginner's mind, as a first-time user would, and hunt down small irritations: unclear buttons, slow pages, missing feedback [S1]. He asked for harsh self-review, and for rough edges to be polished until the product felt smooth [S1][S3].

How he applied it: the memo used a restaurant as the model. A great meal depends on the room, the timing and the staff noticing your needs, not just the food [S1]. Slack's 2016 open letter to Microsoft made the same claim, crediting its success to craft and small details rather than a feature list [S6].

**Quality is everyone's job**

The memo said that making sure all the pieces fit together is not someone else's job [S1]. Everyone, whatever their title, owned the whole experience. A Slack history post says the team had a house saying that meant every problem belonged to everyone [S3].

How he applied it: he told staff to aim their work at the larger purpose, not at shipping single features, and to listen to users, measure what they did, and keep improving [S1].

### Find what users can't stop doing, then cut the rest

Both his big successes came from pivots out of failure [S2]. Flickr began as a photo feature inside the game. He said the first version was a terrible idea that impressed people but wasn't very useful, yet within about three months it was clear Flickr had legs [S2]. The team split on whether to keep the game. After a tied vote and some persuading, they dropped it and focused on Flickr [S2].

How he applied it: with Glitch, 97% of people who signed up left within five minutes, despite a small, devoted fan base [S2]. After 18 months of experiments, he decided the sixteenth idea wouldn't work if the first fifteen hadn't, and shut it down with about $5 million still in the bank [S2][S14].

### Build for yourself first, with no ego

Slack began as a fix for the Glitch team's own problems. The founders lived in four cities and talked on IRC, a 1989 chat system that cannot deliver a message to someone who is offline. So they built a way to log the messages, and once there was a log, they wanted to search it [S14][S2]. Over three and a half years, as the team grew to about 45, each time a communication problem became unbearable they spent the fewest minutes possible fixing it and moved on [S14]. He said they designed Slack without knowing it, from real needs, and that this is the best way, because designers and founders are tempted to guess what an imaginary person wants [S14].

How he applied it: his first pitch deck for Slack already had the launch pricing, the product vision and the marketing plan, and in 2015 he said they were still executing the same deck, which is rare [S2][S14]. He said three and a half years of use by their own team was a kind of product-market fit for a market of one [S2].

**Games were never about games**

Looking back in 2015, he said the thread through GradFinder, the games, Flickr and Slack was people talking to each other. He saw games as a space for people to interact through play, like bridge or a dinner party, and the real use of computers as communication [S14]. A computer with no connection feels inert, he said [S14].

How he applied it: he told Stanford students that the early web showed him technology's power to connect people, from model-train fans to breast cancer survivors [S14].

**Nobody shops for a communication tool**

When Slack asked customers what they used before, about 80 percent said nothing, because teams pick a sales system or a code system on day one but never pick how they talk [S14]. His reason for the design: a tool that cannot hold everything from a company memo to "I'll be ten minutes late" becomes another thing to check, as Yammer did for his team after about six weeks [S14].

### Getting a group to switch is hard, and then it's a moat

He said the team underestimated how hard it would be to get people to switch, because no one thought they needed Slack [S2]. A whole group had to change at once. But once a team switched, that same group habit made it hard to leave [S2].

How he applied it: Slack went back again and again to friends' companies, such as Rdio and Cozy, to learn how to explain its value and earn the social proof it needed [S2].

### The CEO's job is the story

Looking back, he said the advice he'd give his younger self is to focus on storytelling and convincing people. Without people believing, the product and the market don't matter [S2]. He described the CEO's job as telling a story that enough investors, press, staff and customers believe [S2]. In 2015 he added the hard part: the first version of the story does not come true, so you change it, and then change it again, until you ask whether you are stupid, crazy or lying [S14].

How he applied it: when Glitch closed, he told the team in person, then helped them land new jobs with a public page, reference letters and coaching. Every person found a job, and some later came back to work at Slack [S2].

### Empathy, courtesy and a short list of values

In 2015 he said empathy is probably the most important skill a designer has, because you must step back from your own ego and stand in the user's shoes [S14]. An early hire wrote down three traits to look for: empathy, curiosity and diligence [S14]. Later, at a management offsite, the team settled on empathy shown as courtesy, craftsmanship tempered by playfulness, thriving and solidarity, and he used them as a check on business decisions [S14].

How he applied it: he cited a rule from investor John Doerr, that if a decision is very hard to make it probably does not matter, since the choices are close [S14]. He also said, at 42, that he was still learning how much alignment matters more than the exact direction in which a group is aligned [S14].

### Watch for the owner's delusion and busywork

In a 2025 podcast he described the "owner's delusion": people who know their product too well overestimate how much new users understand [S4]. He also talked about "hyper-realistic work-like activities," such as meetings and reviews that look like work but produce nothing of known value, which spread when real work shrinks in a big company [S4]. He framed the goal of design as sparing people from having to think, while noting that some friction helps when users must learn something new [S4].

## How he decides

- Name the outcome you sell before you name the product [S1].
- Judge the product through a first-time user's eyes [S1].
- Be harsh with your own work, in the name of excellence [S1].
- Treat every gap between pieces as your problem [S1][S3].
- Watch what users keep doing, and follow it [S2].
- After a big launch, spend a year on the basics: speed, reliability, launch time [S9].
- Keep the platform open to developers rather than taxing them [S9].
- When the best ideas have all failed, stop, and stop before the money runs out [S2].
- Build the thing your own team needs most, then use it and see [S2].
- Bring the team along on a big change, and look after people when you let them go [S2].

## Real decisions

### Drop the game, keep the photo feature
In 2002 Game Neverending could not raise money in the post-dot-com, post-Enron, post-9/11 market, so Flickr was a last-ditch use of technology already built [S14][S10]. A photo-sharing feature replaced the inventory items in the game client [S14]. The team split on whether to keep the game, and after a tied vote and some persuading they dropped it [S2]. He says the first version was a bad product that made a great demo, since you could only see photos of people online at the same moment, and within about three months it was clear Flickr had legs [S2][S14]. In early 2005 he chose between an Accel term sheet and a sale to Yahoo and picked Yahoo, which he later called the right decision [S14]. Because the team had little left to lose, they also opened an API so others could build on Flickr [S14].

### Leave Yahoo with a joke
After Yahoo bought Flickr, he found it hard to get things done in a company of about 12,000 people [S10]. On 13 June 2008 he sent a mock-formal resignation email, built on a metaphor of being a tinsmith in a company that had wandered from its first trade [S12]. It said his ability to contribute had shrunk to near nothing and that he wanted to go back to "working with tin" [S12]. The email leaked within days and was widely read [S12]. He left in July 2008 [S7].

### Shut Glitch while money was still in the bank
Glitch launched in September 2011 [S8]. He says 97% of sign-ups left within five minutes (in 2015 he said 98% left right away), and that after 18 months of tests he judged that if the first fifteen ideas had not worked, the sixteenth would not [S2][S14]. The team had raised $17.5 million and was in the worst spot, not quite failing and not quite succeeding, with strong revenue per user and a devoted community [S14]. On a sleepless night he decided he no longer believed it would work, and that if he stopped believing nobody else had a foundation to; he emailed the board, and two days later told the staff [S14]. He closed it in December 2012 with about $5 million still in the bank (he gave $5.5 million in 2017) [S2][S14][S8]. The company could not find a buyer for the game either [S11]. The announcement said a smaller core team would build new products from the chat technology [S11].

### Look after the team that was closing
About 30 people were laid off, and he compared the moment to the destruction of Alderaan in Star Wars [S10][S11]. He told the Glitch team in person, with no slide deck, and said he cried before he spoke, because he had talked one engineer into moving cities for the job [S14]. His investor Andrew Braccia says he first asked the board to approve generous severance and a month spent helping everyone find work [S14]. A public page, reference calls and coaching followed [S2][S14]. Players with remaining credit could take it back, donate it or leave it [S14]. Every person found a job, and some later returned to work at Slack [S2]. He said having money left let them do this, and that the goodwill helped later [S14].

### Turn their own chat log into a product
The chat tool Glitch's team used lost messages for people who were offline, so they built a bot to log them [S2]. Searching the log was easy, and new hires could read what came before them [S2]. Eight of the Glitch staff moved to the new work, and he said going through a hard time together is the best bonding there is [S14]. He says three and a half years of the team using it was a kind of product-market fit for a market of one [S2]. For the first three or four outside teams, getting a yes took dozens of tries, including visiting offices to demo in person [S10]. Slack opened to the public in February 2014 [S7].

### Tell staff what they sell
In 2013, about six months after work on Slack began and shortly before its preview release, he wrote the saddles memo to the small team [S13]. He said the team was still before product-market fit and should work from both the product end and the market end, and that defining your own market is the best route to fit [S13]. It said Slack sold a better way to work, not chat, and asked staff to see it as a first-time user and be harsh in review [S1][S13].

### Keep the year boring, keep the platform open
At the 2017 SaaStr conference, with about 1,000 staff, he said he expected a very boring year, focused on basics such as launch time, speed and reliability [S9]. He argued against charging developers a toll, because openness was essential to the platform's use [S9]. He called an IPO the most likely outcome, more than a year away, and said the company had chosen years earlier to run with public-company controls [S9]. His stated reason was that growing complexity and a richer product made team performance the most critical factor [S9].

### Raise the next round on numbers, not a story (2013 to 2014)
With about $5 million left from Glitch, the eight-person team built a private preview, then an official launch where people could sign up and pay [S14]. By the time they needed more money they had spent about $1.5 million of the $5 million, and the valuation was a quarter of a billion dollars and rising fast [S14]. He said the pitch was the numbers: with enough growth you need no other story, and teams that start using Slack do not stop [S14]. He added that the old Glitch investors chose not to take their money back and said they would rather he try something [S14].

## Where he stood

**What does a customer buy?** The outcome. People buy riding, not saddles; better teams, not chat [S1].

**How polished should a product be at launch?** Very. New users who don't yet want it will quit at the first irritation [S1].

**When should you pivot?** When users show you a better path and the old one is going nowhere. Don't hedge for long [S2].

**What is a leader's job in a bad stretch?** Keep believing so others can, and tell the story. If you stop believing, stop the project [S14].

**How should you end a failed project?** Early, honestly and kindly. He gave customers refunds or a choice to donate their money, and helped staff find work [S2].

## His work and record

- Born March 21, 1973, in Lund, British Columbia [S7].
- Earned a philosophy BA from the University of Victoria in 1996 and an MPhil from Cambridge in 1998 [S7].
- Co-founded Ludicorp in 2002, which made Game Neverending [S7].
- Launched Flickr in February 2004. Yahoo's purchase closed in March 2005 [S2].
- Left Yahoo in July 2008 [S7].
- Co-founded Tiny Speck in 2009. Its game Glitch launched in September 2011 and closed in December 2012 [S8].
- Spoke at Stanford with Andrew Braccia on 30 September 2015, when Slack was growing about 3% a week [S14].
- Wrote the saddles memo in July 2013, before Slack's preview release [S1][S3].
- Slack launched publicly in February 2014 [S7].
- By January 2019, Slack had more than 88,000 paying customers and more than 10 million daily active users [S5].
- Slack began trading on the New York Stock Exchange through a direct listing on June 20, 2019 [S8].
- Salesforce agreed to buy Slack in December 2020 and closed the deal in July 2021 [S8].
- Stepped down as CEO in December 2022 and left Salesforce in early January 2023 [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Overconfidence with Glitch.** He said the team thought it couldn't fail because it had money, connections and better technology. It failed anyway [S2].
- **Underestimating the switch.** He admitted the team badly underestimated how hard it would be to get people to change tools [S2].
- **Losses and competition.** Slack lost $138.9 million in the year to January 2019 [S8]. In 2020 it complained to the European Commission that Microsoft was bundling its rival, Teams, with Office [S8], a sign of how hard the fight had become.
- **Listing lawsuit.** Investors sued over Slack's direct listing. The U.S. Supreme Court ruled for Slack on a key point in 2023, and the case was dismissed in 2025 [S8].

## Blind spots to watch

- His ideas come from software with venture money behind it. A small shop can't always polish for years or shut down with millions left [S2].
- "Sell the outcome" can drift into vague promises. Pair it with someone who checks the claim is true.
- His teams built for themselves. That works when you are the customer, and can mislead when you aren't.
- He speaks less about pricing, sales teams and operations. A council needs someone for those.
