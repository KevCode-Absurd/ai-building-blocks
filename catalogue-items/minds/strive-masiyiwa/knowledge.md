## In one paragraph

Masiyiwa is a Zimbabwean engineer who wanted to start the country's first private mobile phone network in 1993 [S7][S8]. The government refused him a licence, and he spent about five years in court, nearly going broke, before the courts broke the state's monopoly [S4][S7]. He refused to pay bribes or take on politically connected partners along the way, and said his faith ruled that out [S4]. Econet grew into a group with operations across Africa and beyond, including the mobile money service EcoCash and the fibre network now under Cassava Technologies [S7][S8]. He argues that business and social good belong together, that Africa's private sector can transform farming and power the way it transformed phones, and that giving should start long before you are rich [S1][S2][S5][S6]. He left Zimbabwe for South Africa in 2000 and is now based in London [S7].

## Core ideas

### Stand up to corruption, even when it costs you

Masiyiwa's best-known stance is refusing to pay for favours. In 2000 he told a reporter that Africa won't end corruption until people stand up and refuse [S4]. A middleman for three ministers once told him a licence would cost $400,000, and even offered to let him pay in instalments. He said no [S4]. He also refused to bring members of the ruling elite into his company as partners [S4].

How he applied it: when he lost the public tender for the licence to a group backed by the president's nephew, his team later found papers showing an official had cut 20% from Econet's score. After more court fights, Econet got its licence [S4].

### Use the law, and make the case bigger than yourself

He fought the state through the courts, with a New York telecom lawyer advising his local team [S5]. His lawyers framed the case as one about freedom of expression, telling Zimbabwe's Constitutional Court that most people in the country had never heard a phone ring [S5]. The court ruled that the state monopoly broke free speech rights [S4].

How he applied it: when the president then used emergency powers to ban private mobile networks, with two years in jail for breaking the ban, Masiyiwa kept going in court instead of giving up or paying off officials [S4]. Sources differ on whether the licence came in late 1997 or in 1998 [S4][S8].

### Hold out longer than the obstacle

The licence fight nearly bankrupted him [S7]. He has said that if he had known what lay ahead, he might not have started, and he told business students that young people often don't see barriers, which can be a strength [S6]. His advice is to go ahead without assuming the obstacles will stop you [S6].

How he applied it: Econet had already spent millions on base stations and was days from launch when the government stopped it. He kept the company alive through the years of court cases [S4].

### Bring the product to people who were left out

He saw phones as both a business and a tool against poverty. In 2000 he said there is a direct link between telecoms and empowerment [S4]. In 1998, when Econet got its licence, about 70% of Zimbabweans had never heard a phone ring [S8].

How he applied it: EcoCash, launched in 2011, served people without bank accounts. Within 18 months, 31% of Zimbabwe's adults had signed up, and by 2017 it had about 6.7 million users [S8].

### Earn trust before you ask for money

Mobile money asked people to hand their cash to a phone company. Masiyiwa compared it to getting people to try a drink they'd never tasted [S5]. His team used cheap, clever tactics to build trust. One was a staged street fight that ended with actors showing the money was safe in the phone [S5].

How he applied it: Econet paid university students to ride city buses and chat about sending money to relatives, so other riders would overhear. The team worked out that about 1.5 million people needed to hear the message. He said that was the whole marketing plan [S5].

### Build giving into the business from day one

Masiyiwa rejects the idea of waiting to get rich before giving back. He told Stanford students to register a foundation at the same time as a company [S5], and told NYU students that giving has to be part of the day job [S6]. In 2014 he and his wife pledged to give at least half of the money from their family assets to charity [S3].

How he applied it: his scholarship work started as a promise to pay school fees for children of employees who had died of HIV/AIDS, and grew to support tens of thousands of students through the family foundation [S6].

### The private sector can fix what governments couldn't

He argues that the phone boom proved what good incentives, investment and sound rules can do in Africa, and that the same should be done for farming [S1]. He notes that starting a business in Africa is hard because of weak government systems and patchy access to basic resources, and that farmers feel this most [S1]. With Richard Branson, he made the case for mini-grids as a fast, cheap way to bring power to the many Africans who lack it, since extending national grids is slow and costly [S2].

How he applied it: he chaired AGRA, a body that works on African farming, after Kofi Annan, and co-founded the Carbon War Room with Branson [S7].

### Create jobs for a young continent

At Stanford in 2018 he said Africa has about 1.2 billion people with an average age of 19, so creating jobs is urgent [S5]. He said entrepreneurship is alive in Africa but needs support systems to grow, and he urged African students abroad to bring their skills home early [S5]. He also argued that starting a business beats working for a big company [S5].

How he applied it: he used his large social media following to celebrate African women entrepreneurs, so girls could see what they might achieve [S6].

### Link the long vision to short wins

He says a great vision depends on delivering wins day by day, and that consistency joins the two [S9]. When he first spoke of linking all of Africa by fibre, listeners' reactions led him to state a smaller goal, joining South Africa and Zimbabwe, and the network later reached Cairo [S9]. He accepts that investors think short-term, so visible milestones are needed [S9].

How he applied it: he also says a business that solves a real human need sells itself, because people come looking for it [S9].

## How he decides

- Ask whether the path is clean. If it needs a bribe, find another way [S4].
- Use the courts and the rules, and frame your case around the public good [S5].
- Expect a long fight, and plan to outlast it [S4][S7].
- Start with people who have never had the product, and earn their trust first [S5][S8].
- Prefer cheap, human marketing that spreads by word of mouth [S5].
- Build giving into the company from the start [S5][S6].
- Look for the next sector, such as farming or power, where the phone story can repeat [S1][S2].
- Keep the big vision, but state goals you can hit this year [S9].
- Respect the people you work with, and be curious about new places and people [S9].

## Real decisions

### Go ahead when the state phone company saw no future in it
In 1993 he first asked the state phone company to join him, and it turned him down, telling him it saw no future in cellular [S4]. It also called the state monopoly water-tight [S4]. He went ahead on his own, hired an American telecoms lawyer to advise his local team, and began the court fight that lasted about five years [S4][S5]. He estimated that three in four Zimbabweans had never heard a phone ring, so he saw a clear unmet need [S5].

### Argue free speech, not only business
When the case reached the Constitutional Court, with a nine-member bench, the argument moved from the monopoly itself to freedom of expression [S5]. He said most people in the country had never heard a ringing phone, so they were shut out of speaking [S5]. The court ruled that the monopoly broke free speech rights [S4]. He says the case made him "public enemy number one" [S5].

### Refuse a $400,000 price and the elite partners
A middleman for three ministers told him a licence would cost $400,000, then offered instalments [S4]. He refused, and also refused to bring members of the ruling elite in as partners [S4]. His stated reason was his faith: it was "just not the way that I do business" [S4]. When asked years later why he would not pay off officials, a practice he said was common, he framed it as a matter of integrity [S9].

### Keep going after the decree and the lost tender
Econet had spent millions on base stations and was days from launch when the president used emergency powers to ban private cellular networks, with up to two years in jail [S4]. A court-ordered public tender then went to a group linked to the president's nephew [S4]. Documents later showed an official had cut Econet's score by 20%, and after more court fights and a cabinet reshuffle Econet got its licence in 1997 [S4]. He kept suing rather than pay or give up [S4].

### Keep two things on the desk
He says he became a Christian on the eve of launch, and for five years his desk held only the country's constitution and his Bible [S9]. He had no money, so he took his case public and wrote openly about his frustrations [S9]. He drew on a verse about a hundredfold return for what is given up, and treated it as a 100% return worth waiting for [S9]. In 2000 he was forced to leave Zimbabwe [S7][S9].

### Build trust in mobile money with a street fight and bus talk
For EcoCash in 2011, Econet staged a street fight in which actors showed that money was safe because it was in the phone [S5]. He likened asking people without bank accounts to hand cash to a phone to asking them to try a drink they had never tasted [S5]. He also paid university students to ride city buses and chat about sending money to relatives, after working out that about 1.5 million people needed to hear it [S5]. He said there was no other marketing [S5]. About 31% of Zimbabwe's adults signed up within 18 months [S8].

### Shrink the public goal, keep the real one
About fifteen years before his 2018 talk, he told people he wanted to link all African countries by fibre, saw their reactions, and scaled his public goal down to joining South Africa and Zimbabwe [S9]. His reason was that a big vision needs visible wins along the way, since investors think short-term [S9]. About fifteen years later the network reached Cairo [S9].

### Register the foundation with the company
His school-fee scheme began as an informal promise to pay for the children of employees who had died of HIV/AIDS [S6]. It grew into a foundation that supported over 40,000 students by 2015 [S6]. He told Stanford students to register a foundation at the same time as the company [S5]. In 2014 he and his wife pledged at least half of the funds from their family assets to charity, citing a verse about giving [S3].

## Where he stood

**Should you pay to get a deal done?** No. He refused bribes and political partners, and said that was not how he did business [S4].

**Is business a force for good or just for profit?** Both. He calls himself a social entrepreneur and sees his company as a tool for causes like education and climate [S6].

**When should you start giving?** Now, not after you are rich [S5][S6].

**What will drive Africa's growth?** As of 2014 to 2018, he pointed to private investment with good rules, jobs for young people, and new sectors like farming and off-grid power [S1][S2][S5].

## His work and record

- Born 29 January 1961 in what was then Southern Rhodesia [S7].
- Earned an electrical engineering degree from the University of Wales in 1984 [S7].
- Worked briefly as an engineer for the state phone company, then started his own firm [S7].
- Founded Econet in 1993 and fought about five years in court for a licence [S5][S7].
- Won a Constitutional Court ruling that the state phone monopoly broke free speech rights [S4].
- Econet got its licence in 1997 or 1998 (sources differ) [S4][S8].
- Left Zimbabwe for South Africa in March 2000 [S7].

- Signed the Giving Pledge with his wife in 2014 [S3].

- Served as African Union special envoy during the COVID period [S7].
- In 2024 became the first Black billionaire on the Sunday Times Rich List [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

The sources we opened contain little direct criticism of him. Some points to weigh:

- **Hard times at the operator.** In 2015, after a regulator forced tariff cuts, Econet Wireless Zimbabwe laid off 100 staff and cut salaries by 20%, yet paid $15 million in dividends that June [S8]. Critics of such moves ask who bears the cost in a downturn.
- **Quick fixes.** Paying doctors' allowances shows his habit of filling a public gap with private money, which critics may say lets the state off the hook (our note, not in the sources) [S7].
- **Survivor's story.** His advice draws on a fight he won. Our note: founders who refuse to bend often lose, and the accounts we read do not dwell on them [S4][S6].

Treat any claim not in these sources, such as political allegations found on blogs, as unverified.

## Blind spots to watch

- Holding out for five years needs money, lawyers and nerve that most small firms lack. Ask what a smaller player can afford [S7].
- He trusts courts to work. In places where they don't, his method may fail.
- His faith-based ethics are clear, but he says little in these sources about trade-offs when staying clean means staff lose jobs.
- He speaks from telecoms and big platforms. Pair him with someone who knows craft, brand or retail.
