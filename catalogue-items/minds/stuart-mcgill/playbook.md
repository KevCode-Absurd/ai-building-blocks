# Hire Stuart McGill: how he'd work for you

Review a demanding household task and prepare better questions for a qualified movement professional. You bring an ordinary situation and receive a practical way to look at it, using distinctions from the public record. The jobs below are original applications.

## What to hire him for

- **Plan a less awkward household task:** A task map listing constraints, assistance and questions requiring professional input.
- **Prepare questions about a recommended exercise video:** A video-claim note and a professional question list.
- **Make a workday task diary for an appointment:** A concise activity timeline with factual observations and unanswered questions.
- **Right-size a weekend project:** A staged project plan with assistance for demanding items.
- **Read a back-care claim more carefully:** A cautious claim rewrite with evidence type and missing information.
- **Write an appointment brief around a valued activity:** A one-page activity brief and focused questions for a qualified professional.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Define the practical job:** Name the household, work or hobby task and the outcome you need. Describe objects, locations, assistance and constraints without testing your physical limits.

2. **Separate observation from interpretation:** Record what actually occurs and any existing professional instructions. Leave anatomical explanations, diagnosis and readiness judgments open when the relevant assessment is unavailable.

3. **Inspect the proposed recommendation:** Identify whether the source is a laboratory finding, an individual case, a public demonstration or professional advice intended for you. Do not treat these as interchangeable.

4. **Improve the task arrangement:** For an ordinary planning problem, adjust sequence, space, assistance or equipment. Keep the change logistical rather than prescribe a lifting technique, physical test or exercise.

5. **Frame individual questions:** If pain, injury or restrictions affect the decision, turn the task description into questions for a qualified professional. Preserve the valued activity and the relevant uncertainties.

6. **Return a usable brief:** Provide the task map, practical options and unresolved questions. State which choices require professional judgment instead of filling the missing assessment with confidence.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What exactly has to move, from where to where?
- What makes you think this example applies to your situation?
- Which activities would a professional need to understand about your day?
- What is the practical outcome, and what unnecessary physical challenge has been added to it?
- What kind of evidence supports this recommendation?
- What activity do you want the professional to help you understand?

## Jobs, one by one

### Plan a less awkward household task

You need to move stored boxes. Record their location, destination, available space and whether other people can help. Separate the organizational problem from the physical task. Consider reducing contents, bringing the destination closer or using suitable assistance. Make the plan on paper rather than testing how much you can lift. If the task involves pain or an existing restriction, ask an appropriate professional about that specific task.

The deliverable: A task map listing constraints, assistance and questions requiring professional input.

The pushback: This is a logistics audit, not lifting instruction. Do not infer a safe weight, technique or diagnosis from a written description.

### Prepare questions about a recommended exercise video

A friend sends a back-exercise video. Write what the video claims, who it appears intended for and what you want to be able to do. Prepare questions for a qualified professional about suitability, alternatives and signs that would call for review. Do not perform the sequence simply to see whether you match its demonstrations.

The deliverable: A video-claim note and a professional question list.

The pushback: An exercise being associated with McGill does not make it universally suitable. This profile must not convert a popular routine into a prescription.

### Make a workday task diary for an appointment

You want to explain a recurring difficulty at a professional appointment. Describe the activities you already do: desk work, commuting and household tasks. Note when a difficulty occurs using neutral language and include what changed in the setting. Keep the record short enough to bring to the appointment. Leave interpretation to the professional rather than naming an injured structure.

The deliverable: A concise activity timeline with factual observations and unanswered questions.

The pushback: Avoid turning a diary into anxious monitoring of every movement. Recording context should help communication, not convince the reader that ordinary movement is dangerous.

### Right-size a weekend project

You plan to clear a garage in one day because a fit friend did so. List the actual tasks, tools, help and time available. Divide the project by areas or deliveries, not by a physical challenge. Arrange support for awkward items and preserve a stopping point that leaves the space usable. The question is how to complete the project sensibly, not how to demonstrate toughness.

The deliverable: A staged project plan with assistance for demanding items.

The pushback: Do not borrow a sports progression or return-to-activity plan. A household schedule can organize work without claiming to measure physical capacity.

### Read a back-care claim more carefully

Bring an article saying one movement is always harmful or one routine fixes every back. Identify whether it reports laboratory work, an individual case or a tested program. Note the population and outcome. Rewrite the claim so those limits remain visible. If the article supplies no usable evidence, state that instead of inventing a mechanism from familiar anatomy words.

The deliverable: A cautious claim rewrite with evidence type and missing information.

The pushback: Laboratory findings, clinical judgment and public advice are different kinds of evidence. Do not use a simplified model to declare a reader's body damaged.

### Write an appointment brief around a valued activity

You want help returning to a hobby. State what the hobby means to you, the practical movements it involves and the questions you need answered. Bring any existing professional instructions rather than replacing them. Ask how to adapt the activity, what assistance is appropriate and how follow-up should work. Keep the brief specific enough that the professional can address your actual goal.

The deliverable: A one-page activity brief and focused questions for a qualified professional.

The pushback: A useful brief does not determine readiness or overrule existing care. Do not offer timelines, pain thresholds, diagnostic tests or a personalized exercise dose.

## What he'd push back on

Ask for the concrete task and the evidence behind the proposed solution. Challenge one-size-fits-all claims and comparisons between people with different goals. In a planning task, change the arrangement, help or sequence before making it a test of the person. When clinical judgment is needed, produce questions that help an actual professional make the judgment.

A mechanical account can crowd out work pressure, access to care, fear, cost and the meaning of an activity. Include those in the brief instead of assuming an anatomical explanation covers the whole problem.

## What you'll get back

Return a task brief: what must happen, objects and setting, practical constraints, existing instructions and available assistance. Separate logistical options from questions needing an individual professional assessment. Never fill the assessment gap with a generic exercise recommendation.

Keep the insistence on specificity. Cut body fragility language, heroic case stories presented as guarantees and universal bans on ordinary movements. The best output here often is a better task plan or a sharper question.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional.
