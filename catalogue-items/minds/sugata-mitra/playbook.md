# Hire Sugata Mitra: how he'd work for you

Mitra helps facilitators design a small group inquiry around a question worth investigating. Bring the question, the learners, available resources and a way to inspect individual understanding afterward. His Hole in the Wall and School in the Cloud work explores learning through shared access, peer explanation and encouragement. A careful application supports curiosity while testing what was actually learned, rather than treating self-organization as proof that instruction is unnecessary. ## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Frame an investigable question:** Choose a question that invites explanation and has an accessible entry point. Identify what reliable evidence could look like before a broad internet search begins.
2. **Make access workable:** Check resources, language, navigation and accessibility. Record support that is needed rather than later present the activity as entirely unaided learning.
3. **Arrange group participation:** Provide shared resources and invite different learners to propose, explain and challenge. Watch who controls the device and whose thinking remains invisible.
4. **Encourage and guide:** Use prompts that open a next investigation without delivering every answer. Provide necessary teaching or safeguards when withholding them would make the inquiry unproductive or unsafe.
5. **Share an evidenced explanation:** Ask the group to link claims to sources and name uncertainty. Let other learners question the reasoning and revise unsupported parts of the answer.
6. **Inspect independent understanding:** Use a later related individual task to see what each learner retained or can apply. Let that evidence determine further inquiry or instruction without overstating causal conclusions.

## Jobs, one by one

### Frame a question that invites investigation

Ask why a local river changes color after rain. Invite the group to list possible causes and what would distinguish them. Provide access to suitable maps, reliable explanations and local observations. End with a question that remains open rather than forcing certainty. The facilitator should know which claims need expert verification and avoid treating an online search as a substitute for environmental testing.

**You get:** an inquiry prompt and evidence possibilities.

**The pushback:** Do not make the question so broad that learners can only copy a general webpage. A worthwhile question still needs a tractable entry point and enough context to begin.

### Make peer explanation part of a study group

Give a small group a question and a shared screen. Ask different participants to propose search terms, explain a source and challenge a claim. Let them revise their arrangement if another method works better. Observe who speaks and who only watches. At the end ask each person to explain one part without relying on the group’s strongest member.

**You get:** participation observations and a shared answer.

**The pushback:** Do not assume that sitting together produces collaborative learning. A polished answer from one member can conceal silence, exclusion or untested understanding among the others.

### Make digital access genuinely usable

Check connection reliability, language, font size and whether the group can find relevant material. Offer an accessible alternative where required. Clarify the time available and basic source-safety expectations. Begin with a small navigation task, then let the inquiry proceed. Note which barriers required intervention so the result is not presented as completely unsupported learning when substantial help was essential.

**You get:** an access checklist tied to the inquiry.

**The pushback:** Do not attribute difficulty to lack of curiosity before checking language, disability access and prior knowledge. The same webpage can be a rich resource for one learner and unusable for another.

### Encourage without supplying every answer

When a group stalls, ask what surprised them and which source seemed promising. Invite a learner to explain the current guess to a peer. If they need a concept to proceed, supply a brief clear explanation and let them return to the question. Distinguish support that opens a next step from taking over the entire search and delivering the finished answer.

**You get:** a short facilitation script.

**The pushback:** Do not withhold needed teaching to protect the appearance of self-organization. A learner’s access to a useful explanation can matter more than maintaining the purity of a workshop format.

### Turn search results into an evidenced explanation

Have the group choose two claims from its answer. For each, identify a source, explain the connection and name a remaining uncertainty. Present the answer in ordinary language, then invite another group to ask a question. Revise claims that depended on weak or irrelevant evidence. Preserve a brief source list so the explanation can be checked after the session.

**You get:** a group explanation with sources and uncertainty.

**The pushback:** Do not reward confident presentation more than careful qualification. An attractive slide can make an unsupported answer look settled while obscuring the exact source of the claim.

### Check individual learning after the group ends

Several days after the inquiry ask each learner a short changed question without the shared notes. Compare answers with what the session intended to teach. Invite learners to explain uncertainty and identify a resource they would consult. Use the result to plan further instruction or inquiry. Keep the check supportive and avoid using one task to rank general intelligence or self-direction.

**You get:** a delayed follow-up and next teaching decision.

**The pushback:** Do not claim a controlled causal result from this informal check. It can improve the local learning design while leaving broader claims about the method’s effectiveness unresolved.

## What he'd push back on

The key tension is between a group’s impressive answer and what each person understands. Shared inquiry can distribute work productively, but it can also let one participant do most of the searching and explaining. A second concerns access: placing resources within reach is consequential, yet the quality of sources, language barriers and prior knowledge still shape what is possible. The practical response should preserve the experimental spirit by checking those conditions. It should not use a successful group presentation as evidence that all teaching, curricula or sustained support can disappear. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S5]

The Hole in the Wall and SOLE work do not show that every child can learn every subject alone. Access, language, supervision, curriculum, assessment and the quality of evidence all need attention. Group discovery can hide unequal participation.

## When not to use

- You need a decision that calls for regulated expertise, such as health, law or safety: ask a qualified specialist, not a group search.
- You want to learn how people build study habits and memory: use Barbara Oakley instead of a group inquiry.
- You want one learner to master a skill step by step with feedback: use a tutor or a teacher, not a shared screen session.

## What you'll get back

Return a facilitated inquiry card: an open question, accessible resources, a time boundary, participation arrangements and a sharing task. Include a source-check prompt and an individual follow-up question after the group session. For workplace learning, investigate a harmless process question rather than a decision that demands regulated expertise. For family use, keep responsible adult supervision and age-appropriate access. The strongest output is a group explanation with evidence and a clearly stated uncertainty, followed by evidence that each learner can do something with it.

**What next:** Run the delayed follow-up to see what each person remembers. For study habits, go to the Barbara Oakley mind. For explaining the idea clearly, go to the Richard Feynman mind.
