The book credited to him, *The Art of War*, was probably written down a century or more after the dates given for his life, perhaps by several hands [S3][S4]. ## In one paragraph

*The Art of War* is a short Chinese book on how to win conflicts, in 13 chapters [S1][S4]. It opens by calling war a matter of life and death for the state, so it must be studied with care [S2]. Its main ideas are simple to say and hard to do. Weigh the odds before you act. Keep wars short, because long ones ruin the country [S1]. The best win breaks the other side's will without a fight [S1]. Know the enemy and know yourself [S1]. Make yourself safe from defeat first, then wait for the enemy to give you an opening [S1]. Avoid strength and strike weakness, and stay as shapeless as water [S1]. Pay for good information, because it comes only from people [S1]. The book has shaped generals in China, Japan, Vietnam and the West, and business readers since [S3][S4].

## Core ideas

### Do the sums before the fight

The book begins with planning. It names five things to weigh: the Moral Law (whether people are united behind the leader), Heaven (weather and timing), Earth (the ground), the Commander, and Method and discipline [S1]. It then gives seven questions to compare both sides, such as which leader has the people behind them, which general is more able, which army is better trained and which is fairer with reward and punishment [S2]. The side that makes more of these sums before the battle tends to win [S4].

How he applied it: the first chapter is a checklist, not a battle plan. The general who follows this counsel should keep his command; the one who ignores it will be beaten and should be dismissed [S2]. The advice is to use the rules, then make use of any helpful circumstance beyond them [S2].

### War is costly, so keep it short

Chapter 2 is about money. Armies eat supplies, wear out weapons and drain the treasury, and the people pay [S1]. The book says no country has ever gained from a long war [S1]. The aim is victory, not a long campaign [S1]. It suggests feeding the army from the enemy's stores and rewarding soldiers who capture goods, to cut the cost [S1].

How he applied it: the same thinking runs into the last chapter. Spending a modest sum on spies is far cheaper than years of war, so refusing to spend it is called "the height of inhumanity" [S1].

### Win without fighting

The book ranks ways to win. Best is to break the other side's plans. Next is to stop their alliances. Next is to beat their army in the field. Worst is to besiege walled cities [S2]. The highest skill is to break the enemy's resistance without a battle at all [S1]. Fighting is a sign that the earlier, cheaper options failed [S1].

How he applied it: chapter 3 warns that sieges take months to prepare, and an angry general who throws men at the walls can lose a third of his men while the town still stands [S2].

### Know the enemy, know yourself

Chapter 3 ends with the book's most famous line. If you know both the enemy and yourself, you need not fear a hundred battles. If you know yourself but not the enemy, you'll lose as often as you win. If you know neither, you'll lose every time [S1]. The same chapter lists five keys to victory, starting with knowing when to fight and when not to [S2]. Others include using large and small forces well, keeping the whole army of one mind, being ready when the enemy isn't, and a general free from meddling by the ruler [S2].

How he applied it: Mao Zedong quoted the "know your enemy" idea and linked it to his own campaigns [S4].

### Make yourself unbeatable, then wait

Chapter 4 splits the job in two. Not being defeated is in your own hands. Whether the enemy can be defeated depends on them [S1]. So the good fighter first makes himself safe, then waits for the enemy to make a mistake [S1]. The winning side seeks battle only after the win is already secured by position and planning. The losing side fights first and hopes to win [S1].

How he applied it: the chapter ties victory to plain work: measuring, estimating, calculating and weighing chances [S1].

### Direct and indirect, and the power of momentum

Chapter 5 is about energy, or momentum. The direct method engages the enemy, but indirect moves win the battle [S1]. Mixing the two gives endless combinations, just as five notes, five colours and five tastes make more mixtures than anyone can count [S2]. Momentum comes from timing. The book likens energy to a drawn crossbow and the decision to the release of the trigger [S1].

How he applied it: the book says a large army can be run like a small one if it is split into units with clear signals [S1]. Organisation lets the commander time his strike.

### Avoid strength, strike weakness, and be like water

Chapter 6 says to arrive first and choose the ground, so the enemy comes to you tired [S1]. Attack where they are not ready, and keep them unsure where you'll strike, so they spread their forces thin [S1]. Water flows around high ground to the low places; an army should avoid what is strong and hit what is weak [S1]. Since water has no fixed shape, war has no fixed conditions. The one who changes tactics to fit the enemy and wins is called a heaven-born captain [S2].

How he applied it: chapter 8 adds that some roads must not be taken, some towns must not be attacked and some orders from the ruler must not be obeyed, if the situation calls for it [S1].

### Deception and appearances

The first chapter says all warfare is based on deception [S1]. When able, seem unable. When near, seem far [S2]. Attack where the enemy is unprepared and appear where you aren't expected [S1]. Chapter 9 turns the idea around and teaches how to read the other side's signs: humble words may hide a coming attack, and dust or birds can show where troops are moving [S1].

How he applied it: the book treats the enemy's view of you as something you can shape, and their signals as something you must read with suspicion [S1].

**Six ways a force courts defeat.**

Chapter X says an army can fall to six faults, and each is the commander's, not nature's: sending a force against one ten times its size so it flees; strong soldiers under weak officers, which breeds disobedience; angry officers who fight on their own before the commander can judge; a weak general with unclear orders; an inability to size up the enemy, so a small detachment meets a large one; and no picked troops at the front [S1]. The same chapter says that knowing your men can attack is only half the answer. You also need to know the enemy can be attacked, and that the ground allows it [S1]. It adds that a general who advances without craving fame and retreats without fearing blame is a treasure to the state, and that kindness without the power to enforce makes soldiers like spoilt children [S1].

How he applied it: the rule reads as a pre-launch check for any team: size of the task against your strength, who is in charge, whether orders are clear, and whether anyone is acting out of resentment [S1].

### Information comes from people

The last chapter is on spies. Foreknowledge can't come from spirits, from past cases or from calculation. It comes only from people who know the enemy [S1]. The book names five kinds of spy and says to use them together, to reward them well and to check their reports [S1]. It closes by saying an army's ability to move depends on them [S1].

How he applied it: the cost case for spies (cheap next to years of war) shows how the book links every idea back to the price of conflict [S1].

## How he decides

- Weigh the five factors and seven questions before committing [S1][S2].
- If you can win without a fight, do that [S1].
- If you must fight, fight briefly [S1].
- Make your own position safe first [S1].
- Fight only when the sums say you'll win; otherwise wait [S1][S2].
- Strike where the other side is weak or unready [S1].
- Change the plan as the ground changes [S1].
- Don't act from anger. Anger can turn to gladness, but a ruined state and the dead don't come back [S1].
- Pay for good information and test it [S1].
- Treat your people with care but keep firm discipline [S1].

## Real decisions (enriched minds)

The first five are rulings the book gives. The last three are acts told of the author in Giles's introduction, which quotes the historian Sima Qian [S1]. Scholars treat the drill story as legend [S5], so read all three as teaching tales more than certain history.

### Go only if you can win, even against the ruler
The book says if victory is sure, the general should fight even when the ruler forbids it, and if it is not, he should not fight even at the ruler's bidding [S1]. Its reason is that the general must serve the country, not his own fame or fear of blame [S1].

### Do not fight in anger
It warns that anger can turn to gladness, but a ruined state and the dead do not come back [S1]. A leader should move only when it serves the state.

### Make the siege the last choice
The ranking in the third chapter puts breaking the enemy's plans first, then his alliances, then his army, and attacking walled cities last [S1]. The stated reason is cost.

### Guard against the six faults of command
Chapter X lists six faults, all the commander's, such as sending a force against one ten times larger or leaving orders unclear [S1].

### Pay for spies instead of waiting
The final chapter argues that a small sum spent on spies is far cheaper than years of war, and that refusing to spend it is cruel [S1].

### Drilling the king's women: clear orders first
In the story Sima Qian tells, King Ho Lu of Wu asked to see Sun Tzu's method tested on 180 women from his palace [S1]. Sun Tzu split them into two companies under the king's two favourites and explained the commands. When they laughed, he said that if orders are not clear the general is to blame, and drilled them again [S1]. When they laughed a second time, he said that if orders are clear and the soldiers still disobey, the officers are at fault, and had the two leaders beheaded [S1]. When the king begged him to spare them, he answered that a general with the king's commission cannot accept certain commands of the king [S1]. The next two leaders drilled the women with perfect precision [S1].

### Waiting at Ying, 512 BC
In the history Giles quotes, Ho Lu had won a town and wanted to strike the Ch'u capital, Ying [S1]. Sun Wu said the army was exhausted, so it was not yet possible and they must wait [S1]. His reason was the state of the troops, not the ambition of the target [S1].

### Winning allies before the strike, 506 BC
Six years later Ho Lu asked if the time was now right [S1]. Sun Wu and Wu Zixu answered that the Ch'u general was greedy, and that the princes of T'ang and Ts'ai had a grudge against him [S1]. Their advice was to win over those two states first, then attack [S1]. Ho Lu followed it, won five battles and entered Ying [S1]. This matches the book's order of preference: spoil their alliances before you fight their army [S1].

## Where he stood

**When should you fight?** As a last resort, and only when the odds are already on your side [S1][S2].

**What makes a good leader?** The book names wisdom, sincerity, kindness, courage and strictness [S1]. It also lists five faults that wreck a general: recklessness, cowardice, a hot temper, being too touchy about honour and caring so much for the troops that he can't make hard calls [S1].

**How should you treat your people?** Like your own children, so they'll follow you anywhere, but not so softly that they become like spoiled children [S2]. Humane at first, then held by firm discipline [S1].

**Should the ruler run the battle?** No. A ruler who interferes with a capable general is one of the ways to lose [S2].

**Can you rely on a fixed plan?** No. Like water, tactics must take their shape from the ground and the enemy [S1].

## His work and record

- Traditional dates are 544 to 496 BC, in the Spring and Autumn period [S3]. Other sources give simply "about 500 BC" [S5].
- Sima Qian's history, written around 97 BC, is the earliest account of his life. It says he served King Helü of Wu [S3]. Some scholars call this account largely fiction [S3].
- The same history tells of the king's concubines: drilled as soldiers, they laughed at orders, so he had the two leaders put to death and the rest obeyed [S3]. The story has long been treated as legend [S5].
- He is credited with Wu's win over the larger state of Chu at the Battle of Boju in 506 BC [S3][S5].
- *The Art of War*, in 13 chapters, is generally dated to the early Warring States period or a little before [S3][S4].
- In 1972, bamboo slips found at Yinqueshan in Shandong, sealed between 134 and 118 BC, matched about a third of the modern text [S3].
- Cao Cao wrote the earliest known commentary in the early 3rd century [S3]. In 1080 it became the lead text of China's Seven Military Classics [S3].
- It reached Japan in 760 [S3]. Jean Joseph Marie Amiot made a French version in 1772, and Lionel Giles the first annotated English one in 1910 [S3][S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Did he exist?** Since the 12th century, scholars have pointed out that an earlier, detailed history of the period never mentions him, and that the book mentions things, such as crossbows, from a later age [S3]. Most now read the book as a later work tied to his name [S3][S4].
- **A text that changed.** Early versions varied a lot in size and division, and later editing changed the text in ways that aren't clear [S4].
- **Stretching the book.** Business and sports writers have drawn many lessons from it. Reference sources report these as claims by admirers rather than proven results [S3][S4]. A book about armies and states doesn't map neatly onto a sales team.
- **Harsh methods.** The concubine story, legend or not, shows a leader willing to kill to make a point [S3]. The book's praise of deception and its use of "doomed spies," fed false news and sacrificed, belong to war, not to ordinary work [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He sees every situation as a contest. Many problems in work and life have no enemy, and treating partners or customers as rivals can do harm.
- Deception wins battles. In business and friendships, it costs trust you need for years. Keep his attention to appearances and drop the lying.
- He says little about building something new. His focus is on position, cost and timing. Pair him with a maker or a designer.
- He writes for a ruler with an army. Small teams with no reserves may not be able to "wait for the enemy's mistake" for long.
- The text is short and open to many readings. It's easy to make him say whatever you already think.
