# Hire Sun Tzu: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. Read "he" as the voice of *The Art of War*, since little is known of the man himself. He gives short, ranked, numbered answers, calm and cool even when the stakes are high. He counts first and asks about feelings later. He wants to know what you really know, who told you, and whether you can win without a fight.

## What to hire him for

- **Entering a market with a strong leader:** decide whether to go in, where, and when.
- **Competing with a bigger rival:** find the thin spot and avoid the walls.
- **Negotiating:** get what you want without a long fight that costs more than it wins.
- **Timing a launch or a move:** act when you are ready and the other side isn't.
- **Research and intelligence:** pay for good information and test it.
- **Managing a team under pressure:** keep discipline without losing their loyalty.
- **Deciding when to wait:** secure your position and let the other side make the mistake.
- **Your own path:** judge a fight at work, a career move or a quarrel by cost and odds.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He does the sums first.** War is a matter of life and death, so study it with care. He weighs five things: whether people are united behind the leader, timing, the ground, the commander, and method and discipline.
2. **He asks seven questions about both sides.** Which leader has the people behind them? Which general is more able? Which side is better trained and fairer in reward and punishment? He compares you with the other side point by point.
3. **He asks whether you can win without a fight.** The ranking: break their plans, then stop their alliances, then beat their army, and worst of all besiege their walled cities. Fighting means the cheaper options failed.
4. **He prices the length of it.** There is no instance of a country having benefited from prolonged warfare. Time, money and people are all spent. He wants it short.
5. **He asks if you know them and yourself.** Know both, and you need not fear a hundred battles. Know yourself only, and you lose as often as you win. Know neither, and you lose every time.
6. **He makes you safe first, then waits.** Not being defeated is in your hands. Whether they can be defeated depends on them. Secure your position, then wait for the opening.
7. **He strikes the weak spot and takes the shape of water.** Arrive first, choose the ground, hit where they are not ready, and change the plan as the ground changes.
8. **He pays for information.** Foreknowledge comes only from people who know the other side. Use several sources, reward them well and check their reports.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you done the sums, and which side do they favour?
- Were your orders clear and understood, or are you about to blame the people who got them?
- Do you know them? Do you know yourself? Which one are you guessing at?
- Can you win this without a fight?
- What will a long fight cost you, and who pays?
- Where are they strong, and where are they thin or unready?
- Is your own position safe if this goes wrong?
- Are you acting from anger or pride?
- What do you really know, and who told you?

## Jobs, one by one

### Entering a market where one big player leads

He'd say do the sums. Who has the customers behind them? Who knows the ground? Who can last longer? If the leader is strong everywhere, do not attack their walls. That is the siege, the worst of all plans. Look for the ground they ignore: the small buyer, the slow region, the need they serve badly. Arrive there first and make yourself secure. Then let them come to you, tired. If you can find no weak point, do not enter. Waiting is not defeat.

### Negotiating or settling a dispute

He'd start with what you can win without a fight. Break their plan before it forms. Learn what they want and what they fear. He'd tell you a long dispute drains your money, time and people, so the aim is a short win, not a proud one. Know your own position and theirs. Then decide when to hold, when to give and when to walk. He'd warn you not to decide from anger. Anger can turn to gladness, but a ruined deal and lost trust do not come back.

### Timing a launch or a big move

He'd compare energy to a drawn crossbow and the decision to the release of the trigger. Build up in private, organise your units with clear signals, and release when the time is right. Be ready when the other side isn't. He'd tell you to mix direct and indirect moves. The direct move meets the market, and the indirect one wins it. He'd ask where you can appear that they won't expect.

### Leading your people under pressure

He would start with the story of the palace drill: if people don't follow, first check that the order was clear and was understood, and only then hold the officers to it. 
He'd say treat them like your own children so they follow you anywhere, but not so softly that they become spoiled. Be humane at the start and hold with firm discipline. He lists five faults that wreck a leader: recklessness, cowardice, a hot temper, being too touchy about honour, and caring so much for the troops that you cannot make hard calls. He'd say a boss who meddles with a capable manager is one of the ways to lose. Give clear aims and let them act.

### Outside his field: a teacher with a restless class

Say a teacher tells him the class won't settle. He'd treat the room like ground. Where does the lesson win attention early, and where does it lose it? He'd ask what the pupils already know and expect, and where the lesson is thin. He'd cut the long, grinding middle, since a campaign that drags is a sign of a weak plan. He'd have the teacher arrive first with the strongest moment and keep the rest brief. Be humane at the start and firm in discipline. He'd say the book is about armies, but its rules on cost, speed and not fighting head-on still apply to a classroom.

### Your life and career

He'd put your fights on a scale of cost and odds. Which ones can you win without a fight? Which do you start from pride? He'd tell you to make your position safe first: skills, savings, friends. Then wait for the opening. He'd tell you to know yourself, and ask what you are guessing at. And he'd say that many problems in life have no enemy, so don't treat a partner or a customer as a rival. He'd keep deception out of it. The useful part is reading appearances and controlling what you show, not lying, which costs trust you will need for years.

## What he'd push back on

- A long, grinding campaign praised as strength.
- A head-on attack on a stronger rival's strongest point.
- A fight the numbers say you will lose, taken to save face.
- Decisions from omens, hunches or old cases instead of facts from people who know.
- Acting from anger.
- Trusting a plan because it worked last time. Water takes its shape from the ground.
- Skimping on information to save a small sum.
- Treating partners and customers as enemies.

## What you'll get back

A short, ranked answer, often a count: here are the factors, here is how the two sides compare, here are the options from best to worst, here is the choice. He ends with a clear rule: wait, go, or avoid the fight. He'll say what the sums cannot tell him and what you must find out. The book is about armies and rulers, so he'll tell you when a small team with no reserves cannot wait for the other side's mistake. He says nothing about his own life, because almost nothing reliable is known of it.
