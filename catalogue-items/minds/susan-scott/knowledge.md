# Susan Scott: what she knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-23. Source tags identify the public record. The worked applications are original exercises written for this catalogue, rather than claims that Susan Scott advised those fictional people.

## In one paragraph
Susan Scott treats the quality of conversations as central to the quality of a relationship and the work it supports. Fierce means showing up candidly and making the conversation real; it can be compassionate as well as challenging. Her approach asks people to examine reality, bring the important issue into the room, provoke learning and enrich the relationship. The practical use is a conversation that has been avoided until it is becoming larger and harder. She also asks the speaker to examine the emotional effect of their own delivery. [S1][S2][S3]

## Core ideas
### Name the issue that is asking for a conversation
Scott’s relationship interview describes difficulties accumulating through conversations that never happened. Staying current means noticing a problem while it can still be addressed as a specific issue, rather than saving it until it becomes a large indictment. The useful beginning is to identify what needs discussion and why it matters. A vague sense that everything is wrong makes a conversation harder to enter and easier to derail. [S4]

**An original application:** A collaborator has changed the handoff time twice without checking with you. Raise the effect on your preparation now, using the actual changes. Avoid saving the incidents until the next unrelated disagreement, when they may become evidence that the person never respects you. The issue to discuss is how handoff changes are agreed and communicated, with room to learn why the changes occurred.

**Where this goes wrong:** A conversation with a hidden pile of grievances is difficult to answer. Do not use one incident as a doorway to prosecute everything you dislike.

### Interrogate your version of reality
The Fierce approach asks people to interrogate reality and provoke learning. A leader’s picture can be incomplete, and a comfortable shared story may hide the important information. Scott’s interview challenges a conversation that merely delivers a predetermined verdict. The practical stance is to state what you see and remain available to another perspective, particularly from people who experience a different part of the work. [S2]

**An original application:** A manager believes the team resists a new process because they dislike accountability. Before defending that story, ask where the process helps and where it creates duplicate work. A colleague reveals two competing instructions. The manager’s interpretation must now change. The conversation can still insist on reliable work, but it should address the actual conflict rather than demand enthusiasm for the original assumption.

**Where this goes wrong:** Curiosity is hollow if every answer is treated as an objection to defeat. Leave a real possibility that your first account is incomplete.

### Show up in the conversation
Scott defines a fierce conversation through the act of becoming real in it. This includes candor about what matters, rather than hiding behind a polished presentation or a management technique. Her examples include complex meetings and compassionate exchanges as well as confrontation. Presence gives the other person something clear to respond to, while requiring the speaker to remain engaged with the answer instead of retreating into a prepared performance. [S2]

**An original application:** You want to discuss a role change with your manager but keep describing general team needs. State that you want to explore a different responsibility and explain the contribution you hope to make. Admit the uncertainty you have about readiness. Then ask how the manager sees it. You are now in a real conversation about your ambition and the organization’s needs, rather than a vague policy discussion.

**Where this goes wrong:** Being real does not require saying every thought or disclosing every feeling. Choose what serves the purpose and respects the relationship’s actual boundaries.

### Take responsibility for the emotional wake
Scott describes the emotional wake as the effect left by how a message is delivered. In the SBN interview, she recalls feedback that her message could be accurate while her delivery harmed people. Examining intent and tone became part of her own learning. This gives a speaker a responsibility beyond being technically correct: consider the experience the words create and whether it helps the intended conversation remain possible. [S3]

**An original application:** A draft message says the team has once again failed to do the obvious. The underlying concern may be a missed check, but the sarcasm invites people to defend their worth. Rewrite it around the actual omission, its effect and the change you need to discuss. Keep the seriousness. Removing contempt does not require pretending the missed check was harmless or the standard optional.

**Where this goes wrong:** A gentler tone cannot make a concealed threat fair. Examine the actual request and power involved, not just the politeness of its wording.

### Make room for what the other person will say
The relationship interview emphasizes ongoing curiosity and the danger of assuming a familiar person is fully known. A candid opening must therefore be followed by listening. The speaker may anticipate the answer and prepare a defense before hearing it, turning the encounter into two competing scripts. Giving room for the response allows an unexpected fact, need or interpretation to enter the conversation and change what follows. [S4]

**An original application:** A partner says they feel excluded from plans. You believe you invited them last week and are ready to prove it. Ask which moments they mean and let the examples arrive. You may discover the issue concerns decisions made before the invitation. Then explain your own understanding and discuss a different planning point. The aim is a fuller picture rather than victory over the first sentence.

**Where this goes wrong:** Listening only to gather material for a rebuttal preserves the original argument. Be willing to learn something that changes your next move.

### Keep the relationship current through follow-through
The Fierce remote-work article describes misunderstanding and indirect complaint growing when people lack direct input from one another. Its concern with conversation chaos connects to Scott’s wider emphasis on the relationship as an ongoing conversation. A useful exchange changes what happens next, and the parties need a way to notice whether the issue is improving. This can require a direct follow-up rather than returning to third-party complaints. [S5]

**An original application:** Two remote colleagues keep misreading short messages as dismissive. Discuss a recent example directly, agree when a complex issue should move to a call and decide how to flag ambiguity. After a week, ask whether the new arrangement helped. Keep necessary written decisions available. The relationship improves through the repeated practice, not through one dramatic clearing-the-air call followed by the same old pattern.

**Where this goes wrong:** Directness is not a ban on seeking support or using formal reporting channels. Choose the route that fits the issue and its consequences.

## How she decides
- **Name the issue that is asking for a conversation:** Write the specific issue and its current effect in plain language. Choose the part that most needs discussion and identify the person who can address it. Keep the opening focused enough that the other person can respond.
- **Interrogate your version of reality:** Separate what you observed from the explanation you attached to it. Ask whose view is missing and invite it directly. Let new information alter the account before deciding what the problem requires.
- **Show up in the conversation:** State what matters to you and why, using concrete language you can stand behind. Be present for the response instead of reciting an entire prepared speech. Let the conversation develop from what is actually said.
- **Take responsibility for the emotional wake:** Read the proposed wording for sarcasm, exaggeration and contempt. Preserve the issue and its consequence while changing the delivery that attacks the person. If you have already caused harm, acknowledge that part directly.
- **Make room for what the other person will say:** After the opening, ask for the other person’s view and leave room for it. Follow the specific examples rather than the answer you expected. Check your understanding before making the next claim or proposing the resolution.
- **Keep the relationship current through follow-through:** Agree the next action and how you will return to the issue. Choose a channel suited to its complexity and keep an appropriate record of decisions. Speak directly to the relevant person where that is appropriate and safe.

## Where she stood
Scott’s interview answers connect directness with responsibility for the emotional wake left behind. She describes learning that an accurate message could still be delivered in a damaging way. Her public explanation of fierce conversation includes difficult feedback and shared exploration of complex issues, with curiosity rather than a guaranteed outcome. In the relationship interview, she emphasizes staying current instead of accumulating grievances until they erupt. The point is to address something while it is still discussable, with enough care to hear what the other person actually thinks. [S2][S3][S4]

## Their work and record
The Fierce account identifies Scott as its founder and traces its work to Fierce Conversations. The 2010 SBN interview discusses the book’s 2002 release and her leadership work. The Mind Tools interview addresses Fierce Leadership and her challenge to accepted management practices. Fierce Love extends the conversation focus into intimate relationships, as discussed in the interview with Jessica Higgins. The organization’s remote-work article examines how text channels and indirect complaints can intensify misunderstanding. These sources establish the public method across work and life; their promotional reach claims are not guarantees of personal results. [S1][S2][S3][S4][S5]

## Where she was wrong or criticised
A conversation cannot guarantee that the other person will cooperate, change or treat you fairly. The official account itself frames a single conversation as potentially consequential rather than certain to transform a situation. Our criticism of an overextended conversation-first approach is that some problems require formal authority, a process change or protection, not just more candor. Choose the setting and channel with the real power relationship in mind. A person need not expose themselves to retaliation or repeated mistreatment in order to perform courage. [S1][S2]

## Blind spots to watch
Candid speech can become self-indulgent if the speaker treats emotional intensity as proof of truth. Keep observations separate from interpretation and give the other account a real hearing. Conversely, excessive preparation can become another way to postpone the issue. Prepare the opening, the purpose and the material examples, then engage. In a written exchange, ask whether the channel can carry the complexity. A live conversation may help, but written records and formal channels can be necessary for clarity and accountability. [S2][S3][S5]
