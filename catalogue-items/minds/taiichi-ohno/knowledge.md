## In one paragraph

Ohno was a Japanese engineer who spent his working life in the Toyoda group of companies [S1]. He joined Toyoda Spinning in 1932 and moved to Toyota Motor in 1943 as a shop-floor supervisor in engine making [S1]. Over about 30 years, with strong backing from Eiji Toyoda, he built the shop-floor system that became the Toyota Production System [S2]. Its aim is to remove waste completely, so cars come out quickly, cheaply and well made [S2]. Two ideas hold it up. Just-in-time means making only what is needed, when it is needed, in the amount needed. Jidoka means machines and people stop the work the moment something goes wrong [S2]. He used kanban cards to let each step pull parts from the step before it, and he asked "why" over and over to reach the root cause [S3][S8]. He left Toyota in 1978 as a vice president and later chaired Toyoda Gosei [S6]. He died in 1990 [S1].

## Core ideas

### Waste is anything the customer won't pay for

Ohno named the kinds of waste, or muda, that hide in work [S1]. The common list covers overproduction, waiting, moving things around, needless processing, extra stock, extra motion and defects. Many versions now add unused talent [S7]. He saw overproduction as the worst, because it hides all the others [S7]. Waste sits next to two related problems: mura, uneven work, and muri, overburden. Overburden on people or machines creates waste of its own [S7]. In a TV interview from the late 1980s, he described the habits that keep people from seeing waste, such as defending a method because it has always been done that way [S10].

How he applied it: in the mid-1980s, asked what Toyota was doing, he said they were watching the time line from a customer's order to collecting the cash, and cutting it by removing waste [S5].

### Make only what is needed, when it is needed

Just-in-time came from Kiichiro Toyoda, son of Toyota's founder. A car can't be finished if even one part is missing [S2]. Ohno turned that idea into daily practice. Every plant and step should be in sync, with very little stock held in advance [S2]. In *Workplace Management* he stressed that parts arriving too early are a problem too [S7].

How he applied it: he learned it in final assembly. Other departments hit their monthly targets for engines and frames, yet assembly still stood idle for want of steering wheels [S4]. Hitting your own number meant nothing if the whole car could not be built.

### Pull, don't push

The usual way was for each step to make as much as it could and push it to the next. Ohno reversed this. The later step goes back to the earlier one and takes only what it needs, and the earlier step makes only enough to replace what was taken [S2][S4]. He said there was nothing hard about it technically. It was a matter of turning normal thinking around [S4].

How he applied it: he started pull in a machining workshop in the late 1940s, then spread it to other shops and, much later, to suppliers [S6]. He said the kanban card did a job people thought needed computers. It automated production control and let the shop floor see a change in plan right away [S3]. Where the supermarket idea came from is told two ways. Ohno credited American supermarkets. Toyota's own history points to a 1954 report on a U.S. aircraft plant [S6].

### Stop the line to fix the problem

Jidoka goes back to Sakichi Toyoda's loom, which stopped itself when a thread broke [S2]. In Toyota plants, a machine stops on its own, or a worker pulls a cord, when something is wrong [S2]. A lit board, the andon, shows supervisors where the trouble is [S7]. Because machines stop themselves, one person can watch several of them instead of standing over one [S2].

How he applied it: in the 1984 interview he stressed exposing problems early by stopping the line, and building quality awareness in every team member and team leader [S3]. Toyota's rule is to do work by hand first, with waste and overburden removed, and only then automate it [S2].

### Ask why until you reach the cause

Ohno called asking "why" five times the basis of Toyota's scientific approach [S8]. Each answer leads to the next question, until you reach a cause you can fix rather than a symptom [S8]. The method itself goes back to Sakichi Toyoda [S8].

How he applied it: a common teaching example follows bolts that keep cross-threading. The whys lead from bad threads, to the wrong tool, to parts that fell, to a rusted shelf. The fix is a missing step: check the shelves [S8].

### Standard work is the base for improvement

For Ohno, a standard way of working was not a rule to freeze. It was the starting point for the next change. He gave shop-floor leaders the job of improving methods and rewriting the standard all the time [S3]. In the 1984 interview he told them that if their standard work sheets did not change for a month, they were not doing their jobs [S3].

How he applied it: he used kanban, in this sense of standard work papers, to bring problems to the surface and link them to kaizen, steady small improvement [S3].

### Better people and thinking, not more money

Ohno's work was driven by the need to raise output by thinking, not by spending money or hiring [S5]. He wrote of aiming for ten times higher productivity [S5]. He moved Toyota from several operators per machine to one, then to one worker running several steps [S3]. Flow lines meant buying more machines and running each one less of the time. He accepted that. Toyota chose not to worry about machine use, and avoided adding people instead [S3].

How he applied it: he said idle people should be allowed to rest rather than make things nobody needs [S3]. Busy work that builds stock is worse than waiting.

### Change one section at a time, inside first

Kanban took years to spread across Toyota [S4]. It needed changeovers that took minutes, not hours, which workers thought impossible [S4]. Ohno said he was frightened, and some feared the experiment could ruin the company [S4]. Until the early 1960s it was called the Ohno System, because it was his risky bet [S4]. Only in 1962, when all departments came under him, did the Motomachi plant run kanban end to end [S4].

How he applied it: he brought suppliers in last. He warned strongly against pushing kanban onto suppliers before it worked in your own plant, and gave a case where doing so caused trouble [S4].

## How he decides

- Go and see the work for yourself before you decide [S7].
- Make only what the next step needs, when it needs it [S2].
- Stop at the first sign of a problem, so it can't travel [S2][S3].
- Ask why until you reach a cause you can fix [S8].
- Improve the standard every month [S3].
- Do the work well by hand before you automate it [S2].
- Prefer idle people to needless output [S3].
- Prove it in-house before you ask others to change [S4].
- Raise output through thinking before you spend money [S5].

## Real decisions

### Starting pull in one machining shop
In the late 1940s Ohno began pull production in a single machining workshop, then spread it shop by shop and, much later, to suppliers [S6]. His reason was simple: normal thinking was to push parts forward, and the fix was to turn it around [S4]. He said there was nothing hard about it technically [S4].

### Running kanban end to end at Motomachi
Kanban took years to spread, and changeovers had to drop from hours to minutes, which workers thought impossible [S4]. Ohno said he was frightened and that some feared he would ruin the company, so it was called the Ohno System until the early 1960s [S4]. In 1962, when all departments came under him, the Motomachi plant ran it from start to finish [S4].

### Keeping suppliers for last
He warned strongly against pushing kanban onto suppliers before it worked inside your own plant, and gave a case where doing so caused trouble [S4]. The order was: your own shop first, partners after.

### Accepting low machine use to get flow
Flow lines meant buying more machines and running each one less of the time. Ohno accepted that. Toyota chose not to worry about machine use and avoided adding people instead [S3]. He said idle people should rest rather than make things nobody needs [S3].

### Refusing buffer stock on main assembly lines
Ohno resisted buffer stock on the main lines because extra stock hides problems [S6][S7]. Critics say this left little room for shocks, and Toyota added buffers after his death [S6].

### Telling a visitor what Toyota was really doing
Asked in the mid-1980s what Toyota was working on, he answered that they were watching the time line from a customer's order to collecting the cash, and cutting it by removing waste [S5]. He framed the whole system as one clock to shorten, not a list of tools.

## Where he stood

**Is high machine use a good sign?** Not by itself. He accepted lower machine use to get flow and avoid hiring [S3].

**Is extra stock a safety net?** He saw it as waste that hides problems, and overproduction as the worst waste [S7]. He even resisted buffer stock on main assembly lines [S6].

**Should computers run production?** In 1984 he said kanban did production control without them [S3].

**Who improves the work?** The people on the shop floor and their leaders, all the time [S3].

**What is the aim?** Making what you need, in the amount you need, when you need it. He added that lower cost is part of the aim too, though it is not written down [S7].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **A hard boss.** He was forceful and at times abusive. He reportedly shouted at people, mostly managers, and gave them impossible tasks. He was said to be kinder to line workers [S6].
- **Too strict on stock.** He opposed buffer stock on main assembly lines. Toyota added it only after his death [S6].
- **Thin books.** His books stay at a high level, with few Toyota details and many examples from other firms [S6].
- **Five whys has limits.** A former Toyota purchasing director called the method too basic for deep root-cause work. Others note that people stop at symptoms and get different answers [S8].
- **Disputed stories.** He credited supermarkets for the pull idea, while Toyota's history credits an aircraft-plant report [S6]. A widely shared "interview" where he admits deception is doubtful [S6].
- **Career.** He never rose above vice president. One writer thinks he made too many enemies [S6].

## Blind spots to watch

- His system was built for repeat work in factories. Creative or one-off work may need a lighter touch.
- He pushed people hard. Pair him with someone who guards morale and respect.
- Very low stock leaves little room for shocks, as his resistance to buffers shows [S6].
- Five whys can stop at one cause when there are several [S8].
- Pair him with a marketer or a designer. He looked at how things are made, less at what to make.
