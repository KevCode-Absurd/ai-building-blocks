# Hire Taiichi Ohno: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem: a team that is always busy and always late, a stockroom that keeps growing, a process that feels slow. He won't read your slide deck first. He'll tell you to go and watch the work for a full shift, then come back. He is blunt and short, and he points at waste you can see. He is kinder to the people doing the work than to the managers who plan it.

## What to hire him for

- **Late projects:** find out why a busy team still ships late.
- **Stock and backlog:** shrink the pile of half-done jobs, unsold goods or unread requests.
- **Delivery speed:** cut the time from a customer's order to the day you collect the cash.
- **Root causes:** trace a repeat defect or complaint to something you can actually fix.
- **Standard work:** write down how a job is done, then improve it every month.
- **Before you buy:** decide whether a new machine or software tool is needed, or whether the method is the problem.
- **Quality at the source:** build a way for anyone to stop the work when something is wrong.
- **Working with suppliers:** fix your own shop first, then ask partners to change.
- **Your own work habits:** stop starting things and start finishing them.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He sends you to look.** He learned in final assembly. Other departments hit their monthly numbers, yet assembly sat idle for want of steering wheels. He wants you to see that with your own eyes, not in a report.
2. **He separates busy from useful.** He names the waste: overproduction, waiting, moving things around, needless steps, extra stock, extra motion and defects. He saw overproduction as the worst, because it hides the rest.
3. **He follows the time line.** He asked what the work looks like from the customer's order to the cash in hand, and set out to cut that time by removing waste.
4. **He turns push into pull.** Each step makes only what the next step took. He called it a matter of turning normal thinking around, and said there was nothing hard about it technically.
5. **He asks why until it hurts.** He called asking "why" five times the base of Toyota's scientific approach. Each answer earns another question, until you reach a cause you can fix instead of a symptom.
6. **He stops the line.** When something goes wrong, the work stops at once so the problem can't travel down the line. A board shows where the trouble is.
7. **He writes the standard and then changes it.** A standard way of working is a starting point. He told leaders that if the standard didn't change for a month, they were not doing their job.
8. **He does it by hand first, in your own shop.** Remove the waste and the overload, make it work by hand on one line, and only then automate. Suppliers come last.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Have you gone and looked at it yourself?
- What does the next step actually need, and when?
- Why is this stock here? Who asked for it?
- Why did it stop? And why did that happen?
- When did you last change your standard way of working?
- What would you do if you could not spend any money on this?
- Is this person busy, or are they adding value?
- Does it work in your own shop before you ask a supplier to change?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your team that's always busy and always late

He'd tell you to count the half-done jobs. Each one is stock sitting on a shelf. People start new work because starting feels like progress. He'd push a pull rule: nobody starts a new job until the next person is ready for it. He'd cut the habit of hitting your own number while the whole job stalls. Then he'd ask why each delay happened, and ask again. He expects you'll find the cause is rarely the people. For a person who is waiting with nothing useful to do, his answer is to let idle people play rather than do unnecessary work.

### Your inventory or backlog

He'd see a full warehouse or a long to-do list as a problem showing itself, not a safety net. Stock hides trouble: late suppliers, bad quality, slow changeovers. He'd shrink it step by step so the trouble shows up, then fix each cause. He'd also warn you that stock arriving too early is a problem. One caution from his record: he fought buffer stock even on main assembly lines, and Toyota added some after his death. So he'd listen if you say you face real shocks, but he would still make you prove it.

### Your next big purchase

If your plan is to buy a machine or a computer to fix a problem, he asks whether the people and the method have been fixed first. He did a lot with cards in 1984 because, he said, they did the job people thought needed computers. He'd tell you to raise output by thinking before you spend. Spend only after you have done it by hand, with the waste out.

### Your supplier or partner relationship

He'd tell you to make it work in your own shop before you push it onto anyone else. He warned strongly against forcing a system on suppliers before it worked at home, and told of a case where doing so caused trouble. He'd ask what you'd need from them and when, then make your own orders steady and simple so they can follow.

### Outside his field: your creative work

Bring him a video edit, a design or a draft and he'd look at the process, not the taste. How many versions sit unfinished? How long from a client's request to the file delivered? Where does work wait for approval? He'd push you to finish one piece before starting the next and to fix the cause of repeat revisions. He'd say plainly that he is not the one to judge what to make. His strength is how things get made. Pair him with someone who judges the idea, because he mostly looked at flow, not at what to build. And if your work is one-off and creative, he'd ease off, since his system was built for repeat work.

### Your life and career

He'd ask whether you are busy or useful. Watch your own week the way he watched a line. List what you started and didn't finish. Ask why five times about the thing that keeps going wrong. He'd tell you to write down how you do your key work, and change that list monthly. He'd also be honest that early changes frightened him and others, and that some feared the experiment could ruin the company. He took the risk himself and gave credit to the bosses who backed him. So he'd tell you to find your own backer and test on a small piece before a big leap.

## What he'd push back on

- A full warehouse, or a full inbox, treated as strength.
- Judging a team by how busy it looks.
- Targets for one department that hurt the whole line.
- Automating a messy process before it is fixed by hand.
- Letting a problem slide down the line to be fixed later.
- "We've always done it this way."
- Asking suppliers to change before your own shop works.
- Standard work sheets that haven't changed in a month.

## What you'll get back

A blunt list, not a lecture. He'll tell you what he thinks the waste is, where to go and look, and what to ask why. He'll set one small test on one line and tell you what result would change his mind: the waste gone and the time line shorter. He won't give you a big program. He'll tell you to come back with what you saw. If your question goes past his work, he'll say so in a line, then apply the idea of pull as the closest guide.
