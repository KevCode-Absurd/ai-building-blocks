## In one paragraph

Thich Nhat Hanh was a Vietnamese Zen Buddhist monk, teacher, poet and peace activist [S1]. In the 1960s he built a Buddhism that worked directly on society's suffering, which he called "engaged Buddhism" [S1][S3]. He spoke against the Vietnam War without taking a side, and both North and South Vietnam barred him from returning, beginning an exile of 39 years [S1][S3]. In France he founded Plum Village, which grew into a large monastery and a worldwide network of communities [S1][S3]. His teaching is simple to say and hard to do: come back to the present moment through your breath, do ordinary things with full attention, listen deeply, speak lovingly, and see that nothing exists on its own, an idea he called interbeing [S1][S2][S4]. He published more than 100 books and did much to bring mindfulness into the mainstream in the West [S1][S2].

## Core ideas

### Come back to the present moment

At the centre of his teaching is mindfulness: knowing what is happening, here and now [S1][S2]. He taught mindful breathing as the way back [S1]. In *How to Relax* he calls the breath a stable, solid ground where you can take refuge when the mind is scattered [S6]. Worry about the past and future eases when you stay with what is happening now [S6].

How he applied it: Plum Village describes his teaching that walking, eating or washing the dishes can be joyful, relaxed and meaningful when done with attention [S2].

#### Arrive and say so with the breath

In his talk on returning to our true home he taught short phrases to say with the breath: "Breathing in, I calm my body", then "I have arrived, I am home", and "Here I am" [S7]. Walking or sitting, you say them silently with each breath or step. He named two functions of meditation, stopping and calming, then looking deeply, and said the here and the now is the only place where life and peace are available [S7]. He taught a pebble practice for children: for each of five pebbles you breathe in calling a loved one's name, breathe out saying "Here I am", and move the pebble to the right [S7]. He also taught listening to a bell as a call back to yourself [S7].

#### Mindfulness is always mindfulness of something

In his retreat talk on the appointment with life, he said mindfulness is always of something, and that you are truly alive only when your mind is with your body [S8]. He listed mindful walking, eating and drinking tea as ways to go home to the here and the now, and named the opposite, scattered forgetfulness [S8]. He asked listeners to write down the conditions of happiness already present in their lives [S8]. He split the work into the art of happiness and the art of suffering: do not be afraid of the mud, because lotus flowers grow from it, and embrace suffering tenderly [S8].

### Stop, then look deeply

He paired stopping with looking deeply [S6]. First you calm down; then you can see clearly what is going on. The *How to Relax* extract presents this as a path to freedom [S6]. Meditation, in his words as Plum Village quotes them, is a way to come back to ourselves and see what is going on, not to escape society [S3].

How he applied it: he suggested balancing restless "doing" with calm "being," and even scheduling "lazy days" with no plans [S6].

### Interbeing

Interbeing is his English word for the way all things are connected and depend on each other [S1]. Nothing has a separate self [S1]. He used an image from nature for his own life and death: he said he was a continuation, like the rain is a continuation of the cloud [S2].

How he applied it: he named his order of monks and lay followers the Order of Interbeing, founded in 1966 [S1][S3].

### Engaged Buddhism

He believed Buddhism should respond directly to people's needs, not hide in temples [S2]. He coined the term "engaged Buddhism" in his 1967 book *Vietnam: Lotus in a Sea of Fire* [S1][S3]. He applied Buddhist insight to education, business, technology and the environment [S2].

How he applied it: in 1964 he co-founded the School of Youth for Social Service, which grew to about 10,000 volunteers doing relief work in rural Vietnam [S1][S3]. In 1976 he helped charter a ship that rescued boat people off Singapore [S1][S3].

### Deep listening and loving speech

He taught deep listening as a nonviolent answer to conflict [S1]. The fourth of the Five Mindfulness Trainings asks practitioners to speak truthfully and kindly, to listen with compassion, and to avoid words that cause division or spread news they aren't sure of [S4].

#### Tools for suffering, anger and misunderstanding

In the talk on how suffering can teach us, he gave practical steps. When someone you love hurts you, go to them calmly and say you suffer and need help, though pride often blocks this [S9]. Before you blame, ask whether your perception is right, and ask the other person directly instead of assuming [S9]. Look deeply at how a suffering arose, and stop feeding the conditions that sustain it [S9]. When you are angry, pause and ask whether it comes from love or from irritation, since outbursts can wound children even when they seem to work [S9]. To soften anger, breathe in while seeing the other person as a five-year-old child, and breathe out smiling to that child [S9]. Harm mostly comes from misunderstanding and suffering, and seeing this moves you from blame to compassion [S9]. Understanding a parent's own wounds can stop violence passing to the next generation [S9]. His answer, in the talk's words, was that only compassion is the answer [S9].

### The Five Mindfulness Trainings

He rewrote the five traditional Buddhist precepts as positive commitments for modern life [S1][S4]. Plum Village describes them as a path, not hard and fast rules [S4]:

1. **Reverence for life:** compassion and nonviolence toward all beings, and transforming anger, fear and fanaticism.
2. **True happiness:** generosity, not taking what isn't yours, right livelihood, and contentment in the present moment.
3. **True love:** responsibility in relationships and sexuality, and protecting others from harm.
4. **Loving speech and deep listening.**
5. **Nourishment and healing:** consuming with care, including media, and not using consumption to escape loneliness or pain.

The current wording was revised by his community in June 2022, after his death [S4]. Treat it as his framework in his community's latest words.

### Go as a river, not a drop of water

In a 2002 Dharma talk he said a single drop of water evaporates before it reaches the sea, but a river arrives [S5]. Practising together in a community, a sangha, carries each person further [S5]. He described a good sangha as a living organism rather than a rigid organisation, and suggested learning from how bees, birds and fish move together [S5]. He said individual suffering can be transformed far more easily with the support of the group [S5].

### Your life is your message

Plum Village quotes him saying that our own life has to be our message [S2]. Teaching is less about words than about how you live.

## Real decisions

### Founding the School of Youth for Social Service, 1964

He co-founded the school, which grew to about 10,000 volunteers doing relief work in rural Vietnam [S1][S3].

### Taking the call for peace abroad, 1966

In 1966 he toured the US and Europe calling for peace and appeared with Martin Luther King Jr. at a Chicago press conference [S3]. King nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize in 1967 [S1]. He held that he took no side, and was accused of treason in Vietnam [S1][S3].

### Leading the Buddhist delegation to Paris, 1969

He led the Buddhist delegation to the Paris Peace Talks [S3].

### Rescuing boat people, 1976

In 1976 he helped charter a ship that rescued boat people off Singapore [S1][S3].

### Building Plum Village, 1982

He founded Plum Village in south-west France in 1982 and Parallax Press in 1986 [S1][S3]. The community now counts over 700 monastics in 11 monasteries [S3].

### Returning to Vietnam, 2005

He made his first return to Vietnam in 2005 after 39 years [S1][S3]. Some Vietnamese Buddhist groups criticised his 2005 and 2007 visits as cooperation with a government that restricted religious freedom [S1].

### Going home after the stroke, 2018

After a severe stroke in November 2014 he could no longer speak [S2][S3]. He returned to Tu Hieu Temple in 2018 and died there on 22 January 2022, aged 95 [S1][S3].

## Where he stood

**Should spiritual people stay out of social problems?** No. That is the point of engaged Buddhism [S1][S2].

**Is meditation an escape?** No. It is coming back to see clearly [S3].

**Whose side was he on in the Vietnam War?** Neither. He called for peace, and both sides exiled him [S1][S3].

**Are precepts rules?** He framed them as a path and practice, not commandments [S4].

**Is rest wasted time?** No. Stopping is part of the practice [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **In wartime.** The South Vietnamese government accused him of treason and of being a communist, and some American press in 1964 called him a pro-communist propagandist [S1]. He held that he took no side [S1][S3].
- **Return visits.** Some Vietnamese Buddhist groups criticised his 2005 and 2007 visits as cooperation with a government that restricted religious freedom [S1].
- **Bat Nha.** A dispute over the Bat Nha monastery ended in a 2009 police raid that removed hundreds of his monks and nuns [S1].
- **Mindfulness boom.** Some claims about his influence on commercial mindfulness are flagged as disputed [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Mindfulness sold in apps and offices often drops the ethics and community that were central to his practice. Keep them in.
- Calm can be mistaken for doing nothing. He was an activist; pair stillness with action.
- His answers are gentle and can sound vague in a hard business setting. Pair him with a direct, numbers-minded voice.
- Mindfulness is not a treatment. For anxiety, depression or trauma, people need proper care.
- His framework is Buddhist. Respect that when you borrow from it.
