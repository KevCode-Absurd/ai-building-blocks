# Thomas Edison: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

A note on method: Edison's own words here come from a magazine article he wrote in 1888 [S4] and an interview printed in 1901 [S3]. Both are in the public domain. The 1910 biography by Frank Dyer and Thomas Martin quotes him often, but Dyer was general counsel for the Edison laboratory and the book calls itself an authorized record, so read its praise with care [S5]. Edison is the subject of many myths, and some need flags. He did not invent the first electric light bulb. Others made lamps before him, and his work was a long-lasting lamp and a full lighting system [S1][S2]. He had nothing to do with the 1903 killing of Topsy the elephant [S7]. The story that he offered Nikola Tesla a $50,000 bonus and broke his word is disputed. Tesla's own account blames a manager, not Edison [S9]. His famous line about genius being mostly perspiration was printed in several versions (disputed wording) [S8]. A popular line about finding thousands of ways that don't work is widely quoted, but we could not trace it to a source we opened, so it is left out.

## In one paragraph

Edison was an American inventor and businessman who turned invention into an organised, team-based job [S1][S2]. He had only a few months of school, worked as a telegraph operator, and became a full-time inventor in 1868 [S1][S2]. In 1876 he opened a lab at Menlo Park, New Jersey, often called the first industrial research lab [S1]. From there came a better telephone transmitter, the phonograph and a complete system of electric light [S2]. He held 1,093 U.S. patents when he died in 1931, though many were largely the work of people on his teams [S1][S2]. His method was to decide what the market needed, then test option after option until something worked [S3][S5]. He also fought hard and unfairly against alternating current, and later admitted he had been wrong [S6].

## Core ideas

### Invent what people will pay for

Edison's first patent, in 1869, was an electric vote recorder for law-makers [S1]. When he showed it in Washington, a committee chairman told him it was the one invention on earth they did not want [S5]. His biographers say he decided then to invent only things with a real demand [S5]. In a 1901 interview he said he had always kept to commercially useful inventions [S3].

How he applied it: he set out in 1878 to compete with gas lighting, a business that already had millions of customers [S1].

### Trial after trial

Edison's method was to decide that a result was worth getting, then keep testing until it came [S3]. He said he had never done anything worth doing by accident. He named the phonograph as the one exception [S3].

How he applied it: for the light bulb filament, his team carbonised paper, thread, wood, cork, coconut hair and much more. In the end they tested more than 6,000 kinds of plant fibre [S5]. Bamboo from a palm-leaf fan worked best, and a bamboo filament could last more than 1,200 hours [S1][S5].

### Treat a failed test as a useful fact

His biographers describe a man who saw a failed experiment as a step forward, because it ruled out one more path [S5]. A co-worker said failure never bothered him unless it came from careless work. Then he got disgusted [S5].

How he applied it: on his storage battery, he ran more than ten thousand experiments before he got a first useful result [S5].

### Invent the whole system

A lamp on its own was worth little. Edison spent about 30 months building a complete system: lamps, generators, wiring, meters and a way to share out current [S2][S5]. His biographers say nothing less than a full system could give the lamp real value [S5].

How he applied it: the Pearl Street station in Manhattan began supplying power on 4 September 1882. It grew to 508 customers with 10,164 lamps [S1].

### Build an invention factory

Edison combined a machine shop and labs in one place, so ideas could be built and tested fast [S2]. He widened invention to include research, development and selling the result [S2]. His West Orange lab, opened in 1887, was more than ten times the size of Menlo Park [S1].

How he applied it: he worked with a team that included Charles Batchelor and Francis Upton [S1][S5]. On mathematics he was blunt. He said he could always hire mathematicians, but they could not hire him [S5].

### Hard work over waiting for a spark

In the 1901 interview, Edison said he had worked about twenty hours a day for fifteen years. He said success came from the hardest mental and physical effort [S3]. He told interviewers that genius was a tiny share inspiration and the rest hard work. The ratio shifted between versions, from 2 and 98 in 1898 to 1 and 99 in 1901 [S8]. He also thought the knack for invention was born in a person, so he did not claim hard work was the whole story [S3].

How he applied it: by his own account, he spent more than a year, twenty hours a day, Sundays included, getting the phonograph to record and play back one hard word, "specie", clearly. Once that worked, he knew the rest could be done [S5]. As a young night telegraph operator, he had learned to nap in a chair for a few minutes at a time [S5]. He said other people worked hard on many things, while he worked hard on one [S3].

### Picture the uses before the market does

In "The Perfected Phonograph" (1888), Edison listed what his machine could do: take dictation, read books aloud to blind people, teach speaking, play music, keep family voices, power talking toys and clocks, and save the sound of languages [S4]. He said hearing your own voice would teach you to be careful what you say [S4].

How he applied it: he had done little to sell the phonograph after 1878. Rivals improved it with wax cylinders in the 1880s, and only then did he set up the Edison Phonograph Company, in 1887 [S1].

### Turn a failure into the next business

His 1890s plant to pull iron from low-grade ore was his best-known business failure [S2]. He reused the crushing and kiln machinery to make Portland cement, and his kiln became an industry standard [S2]. A partner recalled that he insisted creditors of the failed works be paid in full [S5].

How he applied it: the same partner said Edison treated his own loss lightly. He joked that he could always get a job as a telegraph operator for $75 a month [S5]. Within about three years the cement and battery work had paid off the old debts [S5].

## How he decides

- Start from a real need someone will pay for [S3][S5].
- Decide if the result is worth getting. If yes, keep going until it comes [S3].
- Test widely and write down every result [S5].
- Count a failed test as one more option ruled out [S5].
- Build the whole system around the product [S2][S5].
- Put a shop next to the lab so ideas get built fast [S2].
- Hire experts for what you lack [S5].
- Move to the next problem once one works. He said a finished success bored him [S3].

## Real decisions

### Dropping the vote recorder
His first patent, a vote recorder, was shown to a committee chairman who said it was the one invention they did not want. He decided from then on to invent only what could sell [S5]. In the 1901 interview he said he had kept strictly to commercially useful inventions and had no time for novelties that only catch the popular fancy [S3].

### Choosing one object and sticking to it
Asked about his eighteen-hour days, he answered that everyone is busy all day, but most people spread the hours over many things while he put them into one. He said people fail for want of one thing to which they stick, letting all else go [S3].

### Following up the pricked finger
The one invention he said came by accident was the phonograph: the point on a telephone mouthpiece pricked his finger as he sang, and he told his assistants to build a machine that would work accurately [S3]. He did not stop at the surprise. He turned it into a design job.

### Waiting ten years, then perfecting it
In 1888 he wrote that the phonograph was now ready for the public, with wax cylinders, a motor and separate recording and playing parts, and answered the rumour that he had dropped it for the electric light [S4]. He used the article to list uses, from dictation to books for the blind to mailed cylinders [S4].

### Reading Faraday by night
As a young telegrapher in Boston he bought Faraday's works and read them through the night. A friend said he mastered mechanics, chemistry and electricity from odd hours of reading [S3].

### Choosing electricity as the field to bet on
In 1901 he called electricity the field of fields, holding secrets that would reorganize life, while saying he had learned very little next to the possibilities [S3].

## Where he stood

**Is invention luck or work?** Work. He said he never did anything worth doing by accident [S3].

**Should an inventor care about sales?** Yes. He kept to inventions people would use and buy [S3][S5].

**Is genius learned?** Mostly effort, he said, though he thought the gift itself was born in a person [S3][S8].

**Is money the point?** Partly. He said the wish for money can push a person forward, but that he worked mainly because he enjoyed it [S3]. He spent early earnings on books and equipment rather than saving them [S3].

**Does an inventor need theory?** He relied on it less than most. He leaned on testing, and said he could always hire mathematicians when he needed them [S5]. Tesla thought this was his great weakness [S10].

**Was alternating current safe?** In the 1880s he said no and fought it hard [S6]. His biographers argue he only opposed high voltage inside homes, and backed it for sending power over long distances [S5]. In 1908 he reportedly told a rival's son that he had been wrong [S6].

## His work and record

- Born 11 February 1847 in Milan, Ohio. He went to school for only a few months and had serious hearing loss from about age 12 [S1].
- Worked as a telegraph operator from 1863 to 1869 [S1].
- Patented an electric vote recorder on 1 June 1869 [S1].
- Sold the quadruplex telegraph for $30,000 in 1874. It sent four messages at once on one wire [S1].
- Opened Menlo Park in 1876 [S1][S2].
- Noticed the "Edison effect" during lamp tests, later the basis of vacuum-tube electronics [S2].
- Made the phonograph in 1877 [S1][S2].
- Filed his carbon filament lamp patent on 4 November 1879, granted 27 January 1880 [S1].
- Opened the Pearl Street station on 4 September 1882 [S1].
- Opened the West Orange lab in 1887 [S1].
- Published "The Perfected Phonograph" in June 1888 [S4].
- Showed the kinetoscope, a peep-hole film viewer built with William Dickson, on 20 May 1891 [S1].
- Lost control of his electric company when it merged into General Electric in 1892 [S1][S6].
- Turned the failed ore machinery to Portland cement and worked on a storage battery for electric cars in the 1900s [S2].
- Headed the Naval Consulting Board from 1915 [S1][S2].
- Tested 17,000 plants for rubber in the 1920s [S1].
- Received the Congressional Gold Medal on 29 May 1928 [S1].
- Died 18 October 1931 in West Orange, New Jersey [S1][S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The war of the currents.** He called AC deadly and backed Harold Brown, who killed animals with AC in public tests. From 1887 he backed an electric chair run on Westinghouse AC, a move widely seen as a smear [S1]. He also testified for it before the first electric execution in 1890 [S6]. AC won, and he later admitted he was wrong [S6].
- **Credit.** Many of his patents were largely the work of his staff [S1]. His company sold the Vitascope projector as his, though others invented it [S1].
- **Method.** Tesla said in 1931 that a little theory would have saved Edison most of his labour [S10].
- **Business misses.** The ore-milling works failed [S2]. In 1916 navy officials blamed him for overselling a battery after a submarine explosion [S1].
- **Hard on people.** He drove his staff very hard [S1].
- **Myths in his favour, and against him.** He gets credit for "the" light bulb [S1], and blame for Topsy's death [S7]. Neither is accurate.

## Blind spots to watch

- Trial and error is slow and costly when theory could narrow the search. Pair him with a theorist like Tesla.
- He could dig in on a bad bet, as with direct current, because of pride and money [S5][S6].
- The "lone genius" image hides the team. Give credit to the people doing the work.
- He could leave a great idea idle until a rival forced his hand, as with the phonograph in the 1880s [S1].
- He drove people hard. Long hours are not a plan for most teams today.
