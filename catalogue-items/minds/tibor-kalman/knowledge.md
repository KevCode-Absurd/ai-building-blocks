## In one paragraph

Kalman was a Hungarian-born American designer who taught himself design at a bookstore that became Barnes & Noble [S3][S4]. In 1979 he co-founded M&Co in New York with his wife, Maira Kalman, and others. It worked for Talking Heads, the Whitney Museum and Restaurant Florent, and became known for wit and "non-design," borrowed from everyday signs and packaging [S3][S7]. He art-directed *Artforum* and *Interview*, then co-founded Benetton's *Colors* magazine with Oliviero Toscani in 1991, and edited it from Rome [S3]. Illness forced him to leave in 1995, and he died in 1999 [S3]. The design world called him its moral compass and its most fervent provocateur [S3][S5]. His core belief was that design is a language, and the real question is what you use it to say [S1].

## Core ideas

### Design is just a language

Kalman told Eye that design is just language, and the real issue is what you use it to do [S1]. He found debates about typefaces and legibility narrow next to that question [S1]. He wanted design to drive cultural and political change, and noted that designers had so far been best at economic change, such as selling more [S1].

How he applied it: a writer studying his art direction of *Interview* noted that he attacked the reader with language more than with letterforms, and summed up his rule: how you use a typeface matters more than which one you pick [S10].

### The designer is a citizen

Kalman argued that designers are responsible for what their work does in the world [S5]. Because graphic design is mass communication, he said it should raise awareness of social issues [S5]. He urged designers to question a client's product and its effects on the environment [S5]. He believed that award-winning design reflects a company's whole ethic, and warned that many bad companies have great design [S3][S5].

How he applied it: at the 1986 AIGA awards he made an anonymous protest about Esprit's labour practices [S5]. M&Co sent holiday mailings about homelessness, and he pushed clients such as Restaurant Florent to use their ads for social messages [S5].

### Inject art into commerce

His AGI statement said designers aren't there to help clients wipe out everything of visual interest. They are there to make clients think about design that is dangerous and unpredictable, and to inject art into commerce [S2]. He held that good design should be unexpected and untried [S5]. He saw mere professional competence as the same as being mediocre [S5].

How he applied it: M&Co began with conventional "design by the pound" for banks and department stores, then turned into a platform for his mission [S5]. Its own office design was playful and absurd [S4].

### Learn from the vernacular

Kalman loved everyday design: the signs, menus and packaging of ordinary commercial life [S5]. He used this plain style as a protest against the cool corporate International Style [S3]. The Salon obituary describes a 1985 menu for Restaurant Florent built from Yellow Pages images [S6].

How he applied it: Ellen Lupton described M&Co's "non-design" look as a deliberate choice, though she criticised it (see below) [S7].

### The editor decides the content

Kalman separated two jobs. The editor decides what the content will be. The designer gives it form and amplifies it [S1]. He took the title of editor-in-chief at *Colors*, not art director, because he wanted control of the content [S1]. He wanted designers to learn to write and read more deeply, and warned them against becoming production artists [S1].

How he applied it: he planned *Colors* as a series of researched, themed "textbooks" on subjects such as AIDS and consumerism, made by mixed teams of writers, designers, photographers and illustrators [S1]. He called it the hardest work of his life [S1].

### Seduce people toward the truth

He believed editors have a duty to seduce their audiences in order to spread the truth [S1]. He wanted designers to take on issues like ecology and racism, and to use commercial techniques to make ideas persuasive [S1]. Messages should be short and engaging [S1].

How he applied it: *Colors* used doctored images that showed famous figures, such as Queen Elizabeth and the Pope, as members of other races [S3]. It ran pages in several languages, and he tried a final issue with no words, to be understood across cultures [S1][S3].

### Turn things upside down

Kalman told a television interviewer in 1998 that he used contrariness in every part of his life, trying to turn things upside down [S6]. He was theatrical and confrontational. He once ran an AIGA event as a "cultural circus" and cut off speakers with barking-dog sounds [S5]. He was willing to criticise peers and friends in public [S5].

How he applied it: colleagues described him as charming, prickly and demanding, and his staff found him hard to work for, though many admired his sense of purpose [S6].

### Protect independence from sponsors

He worried that corporate owners of media would pressure editors [S1]. He argued that good editors who build an audience can keep their freedom, and that sponsors benefit from that freedom [S1]. He said Benetton and Toscani gave him room to work, and that advertisers had less sway at *Colors* than at other commercial magazines [S1].

How he applied it: he still blamed poor distribution and weak commitment from Benetton for the magazine's problems [S1].

### Make the product too

By the early 1990s Kalman felt he had done what a designer could do in commercial work [S1]. He had moved from brochures, logos, packaging and record covers toward industrial design, film, television and architecture [S1]. He wanted to make the products people buy or spend time with, not only their packaging [S1]. Looking ahead in 1993, he pictured a "media lab" funded by sponsors, working on projects in architecture, type and film, and responsible media [S1].

How he applied it: after his illness he rebuilt M&Co as a smaller studio focused on ideas. Knowing his time was short, he took only clients he found meaningful, and treated society as the ultimate client [S4].

## How he decides

- Choose the unexpected over the safe [S2][S5].
- Let the content lead and the design amplify it [S1].
- Borrow from everyday life, not from design annuals [S3][S5].
- Use commercial tools to sell good ideas [S1].

## Real decisions

### Starting without training
Kalman began by designing a bookstore's displays, ads and packaging when the regular designer did not turn up [S2]. The habit of doing the job first and learning on the way shaped M&Co [S1][S2].

### Founding M&Co and moving toward culture
He founded M&Co in 1979. It first took whatever commercial work it could get, then moved to cultural clients and wider fields such as products, film titles, TV, children's books and architecture [S1]. After about 15 years he said he had pretty much used up what a designer could do, and wanted to make the things people buy, not only their packaging [S1].

### Taking the editor's job at Colors
When Benetton's creative director recruited him in 1990, he wanted to be editor, not art director [S1]. He modelled the magazine on *Life* before 1965 and said the company's sponsorship pressed on him less than on some "independent" magazines [S1]. He closed M&Co in 1993 and moved to Rome to run it [S1][S2].

### The "What if" race issue
He used computer graphics to change the races of famous people in an issue of *Colors*. It brought the magazine worldwide attention [S6]. He left after repeated clashes with Benetton's creative director, and after his cancer symptoms appeared [S6].

### Working through illness
Back in New York in 1995 after his diagnosis, he kept working [S2]. He pressed the AIGA to make members do charity work, and oversaw two books in his last years, including *Perverse Optimist* [S6]. A colleague said his illness made him easier to work with [S6].

### Turning down work, and quitting the annual reports

In the 1991 Print debate Kalman said M&Co turned down nearly as much work as it accepted, and walked away from projects that looked like a disaster mid-process [S11]. In its early years the firm took weak corporate work to survive, while doing cultural, music and unpaid work for smaller clients [S11]. He said M&Co had done the Limited annual reports for several years and would probably not take the next one [S11]. Later he called annual reports and packaging tools for distorting content [S12]. His reason was that a designer should not polish a client's message just because it is paid for [S11].

### Making the AIDS card a statement

Asked to design a card for a charity event, M&Co made a stark political statement about AIDS, "THEM=US", not a pretty card [S11]. For a related cake project the team wrote President Reagan's remarks about AIDS on the cake, in an angry piece, not a cute one [S11]. Kalman said the firm's work was ethical, political and moral at once [S11]. The choice makes the client's cause the content.

### Walking out of Edible Architecture

When M&Co proposed an AIDS-themed piece for the Edible Architecture show, the sponsor refused it [S11]. M&Co then did not take part [S11]. Kalman gave this as an example of the firm pulling out when the message it cared about was refused [S11].

## Where he stood

**Is professional polish enough?** No. Mere competence is mediocrity [S5].

**Can a bad company have good design?** It can look great, which is the problem. He judged design by the company's whole ethic [S3][S5].

**Who should control a magazine's content?** The editor. Designers should learn to write if they want that power [S1].

**Was the internet the future in 1993?** He found it slow and overhyped and thought print still had untapped room [S1].

**What is design for?** Changing culture and politics as well as selling more [S1][S4].

## His work and record

- Born in Budapest in 1949. His family fled to Poughkeepsie, New York, in 1956 [S3][S4].
- 1967 to 1970: studied at New York University and joined Students for a Democratic Society [S3].
- 1971: began design work at the bookstore that became Barnes & Noble, later leading its design team [S3][S4].
- 1979: co-founded M&Co [S3][S4].
- 1980: *Remain in Light* album design for Talking Heads [S2].
- 1987 to 1988: art director of *Artforum*. 1989 to 1991: creative director of *Interview* [S3].
- 1991: co-founded *Colors* with Oliviero Toscani [S3].
- 1993: closed M&Co and moved to Rome to edit *Colors* [S2][S3].
- 1995: left *Colors* because of illness and returned to New York [S2][S3].
- 1997: reopened a smaller M&Co focused on ideas and meaningful clients [S3][S4].
- 1998: *Perverse Optimist*, a retrospective edited by Peter Hall and Michael Bierut [S6].
- Died May 2, 1999. Awarded the AIGA medal that year [S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Looking down on the vernacular.** Ellen Lupton argued in 1992 that M&Co's "non-design" studied everyday culture from above, and turned the low into something more refined while claiming to escape design's sins [S7].
- **Stunt or sincere?** Critics called his homelessness mailings a publicity ploy. Steven Heller judged him sincere [S5].
- **Harsh on peers.** He called Paula Scher and Charles Spencer Anderson "grave-robbers" for reviving historical styles [S5].
- **Jokes over substance.** Victor Margolin found his 1998 book on Vitra's chairman a worthy tribute that leaned too much on pictures and gags, some unfunny. His strength, Margolin said, was ideas more than images [S8].
- **Imitators.** Dmitri Siegel argued in 2004 that students copied Kalman's bluntness and stock-photo irony without his critical thinking, producing hollow work [S9].
- **Benetton.** Heller noted that Benetton's ads looked gratuitous without captions, which is part of why *Colors* existed [S5]. Working for a clothing brand's magazine while criticising corporate design was an open tension [S3][S5].

## Blind spots to watch

- He assumed you can afford to turn down clients. Most beginners can't.
- Bluntness can wound and close doors. His own staff found him hard [S6].
- His 1993 view of the internet aged badly [S1].
- Provocation can drown the message. Pair him with a calmer craft or strategy mind.
