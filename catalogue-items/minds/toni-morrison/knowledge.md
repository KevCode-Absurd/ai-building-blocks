## In one paragraph

Toni Morrison was born Chloe Ardelia Wofford in Lorain, Ohio, in 1931, and died in New York in 2019 [S1]. She studied at Howard and Cornell, taught English, and became the first Black woman senior fiction editor at Random House, where she brought new Black writers and histories into print [S1][S11]. While raising two children alone she wrote early in the morning, and her first novel, *The Bluest Eye*, came out in 1970 [S1][S9][S11]. *Song of Solomon* (1977) and *Beloved* (1987) made her one of the major American novelists, and in 1993 she became the first Black woman to win the Nobel Prize in Literature [S1]. Her novels deal with the costs of racism and with Black American life, often from the side of Black women, in a rich, musical style that mixes myth and history [S1].

## Core ideas

### Write for your own people, without the outside gaze

Morrison said she wrote without the "white gaze," meaning she did not shape her stories to explain Black life to white readers [S1]. She wrote with Black readers, and especially Black women, in mind [S1]. This freed her to use the language, music and memory of the communities she wrote about without translating them.

The lesson for any writer: decide who you are really writing for. Writing for everyone often means writing for the most powerful reader in the room.

### Carry memory into the present

Her novels treat the past as something that is still alive. *Beloved*, set after the American Civil War, follows Sethe, a formerly enslaved woman haunted by the daughter she killed rather than let her be taken back into slavery [S4]. Sethe calls the way the past returns "rememory" [S4]. Morrison found the real story behind it, that of Margaret Garner, in an 1856 newspaper article while editing *The Black Book* [S4].

She did not write a history. She departed from the real case in many ways and wrote a novel about what slavery did to mothers, children and the self [S4]. She later said there was no memorial to enslaved people, so the book had to stand in for one [S4].

### Let the form carry the argument

Morrison chose the shape of each book to fit what it was about. *The Bluest Eye* follows Pecola, a Black girl in 1941 Lorain who longs for blue eyes, and Morrison said she wanted readers to feel how hurtful racism is [S6]. The book is told mostly by Claudia, as a child and as an adult, and its chapter titles break up lines from a *Dick and Jane* school primer, so the cheerful white family runs into the lives of the children [S6]. *Jazz* (1992) was built to echo jazz rhythms [S1]. In her Nobel lecture she told a story, not an argument, then turned it inside out by letting the young people answer the old woman [S7].

### Read the whole canon again, including what it leaves out

In *Playing in the Dark* (1992), first given as lectures at Harvard in 1990, Morrison argued that Black people shaped the great works of white American writers, even when those writers barely seemed to notice them [S5]. She looked at Willa Cather, Edgar Allan Poe, Nathaniel Hawthorne and Ernest Hemingway, and showed how blackness gave their books structure [S5]. Scholars use her phrase "Africanist presence" for this idea [S1].

The lesson: when you read any story or plan, ask who is present only as a shadow, and what work that shadow is doing.

### Language is alive, and using it is an act with consequences

In her Nobel lecture, Morrison told of an old blind woman whom young people tried to trick by asking whether the bird in their hands was alive or dead [S7]. The woman answered that she did not know, but that the bird was in their hands [S7]. Morrison read the bird as language and the woman as a writer. Language can die of carelessness, disuse, indifference or decree, and all users and makers are answerable when it does [S7]. She said oppressive language does not merely describe violence. It is violence, and it limits knowledge, so it must be rejected, altered and exposed [S7]. Language lives when it helps people picture the actual, imagined and possible lives of its speakers [S7].

How she applied it: she praised Lincoln's Gettysburg words because they refused to monument the dead or claim the last word [S7]. She also gave the Tower of Babel a new reading: many languages might be a gain, since heaven could be found at your feet, as life, and not as a place after death [S7].

### Artists go to work when times are hard

In a 2004 essay in *The Nation*, Morrison describes a day when she felt too low to write. A friend told her that dark times are precisely when artists must work [S2]. She notes that many writers have made important work in prison, exile or under persecution, and that rulers who want control tend to silence art and thought first [S2]. Her conclusion is that despair is not an option, and that making art and language is how a society heals [S2].

### You are your own story

In her 2004 speech to Wellesley graduates, Morrison refused to offer easy optimism [S3]. She warned that being told college is the best time of your life can keep people stuck in a long adolescence, which sellers encourage [S3]. She asked graduates to refuse narrow labels, such as "consumer," and to see themselves as specific people who shape their own stories, tone and language [S3]. She ended by suggesting that a life can be made into art [S3].

### Know when you are at your best

Morrison tells her students that one of the most important things to learn is when you are your best creatively [S9]. For her it is the morning. She began writing before dawn because small children woke at five, and she kept the habit by choice once she saw she was clearer-headed and more confident before the sun was up [S9]. Her small ritual is making coffee while it is still dark and watching the light come [S9]. She asks students what their ideal room looks like: music or silence, chaos or calm [S9]. Her own ideal is nine uninterrupted days and a big table, but she makes do with a small square of desk [S9].

How she applied it: she writes a first draft by hand in pencil on yellow legal pads, straight through, fixing it later on a computer, and she will grab any scrap of paper when a sentence arrives [S9]. She says the straight-through draft is not a law [S9].

### Leave the meaning open

Morrison turned down the "feminist" label for her work, because she preferred to open up readings rather than close them [S1]. Critics still argue about whether the figure called Beloved is a ghost, a person or something else [S4]. She trusted readers to hold more than one meaning at once. In the Nobel parable she did the same, offering several readings of what the old woman and the children might mean [S7].

## How she decides

- Write for the reader you most care about, not for outsiders [S1].
- Start from a true story or image, then free the novel from the facts [S4].
- Let the past press on the present [S4].
- Pick a form that does part of the work: several narrators, broken text, musical rhythm, a parable that turns on its teller [S1][S6][S7].
- Ask who is missing from the story, and what their absence hides [S5].
- Keep working when the world feels dark [S2].
- Refuse labels that shrink people [S3].
- Treat words as acts with consequences, and refuse language that dominates [S7].
- Work at the hour when you are sharpest, and put the first draft down by hand [S9].
- Leave room for the reader [S1][S4].

## Real decisions

### Writing before dawn, first by need and then by choice

When her children were small, Morrison wrote before they woke at about five, because that was the time she had [S9]. After she left Random House she stayed home for a couple of years, and found she had never worked out her own rhythms, which work and children had set for her [S9]. While writing *Beloved* in 1983 she saw she was clearer and more confident in the morning, so the habit became a choice [S9]. She tells students to find their own best hour and conditions [S9].

### Keeping her first novel from her employer

In 1965 Morrison, newly divorced with two small sons, took a job editing school anthologies for a Random House textbook arm in Syracuse [S11]. She began a novel there, finished it in New York after her 1968 move, and told nobody at work, since they might doubt where her loyalty lay [S11]. She felt it would be a serious conflict to show it to Random House, so *The Bluest Eye* came out from Holt in 1970 [S11]. The choice kept her job and her writing apart.

### Moving to trade books and building a list

A senior Random House executive asked if she would like to become a trade editor, and she saw she could work on Muhammad Ali's autobiography if she did [S11]. Robert Gottlieb, who ran Knopf, told her he would rather be her editor than her boss, and that is how it went [S11]. She was one of ten editors and the first Black woman there, and her goal was to build a list of Black authors [S11]. She was proud of the list, though the pay never gave a living wage and she taught on the side [S11]. She also said she liked book-making and would sneak down to plan with the designers [S11].

### Compiling *The Black Book*

In 1974 she edited a scrapbook-style anthology of photographs, documents and other pieces of Black life, even though some at Random House gave it only lukewarm support [S11]. It became a major success [S11]. In compiling it she found the story of Margaret Garner, which became *Beloved* [S1][S4].

### Cutting her hours, then leaving the job

After *Song of Solomon* in 1977, her boss asked what she would want if she could have it as she liked, and she asked to come in one day a week [S11]. A half-dozen years passed that way, but she cared less about publishing, since she felt the work she liked was valued less and less [S11]. With Gottlieb's urging to just be a writer, she gave up the job, in 1983 by one account and 1984 by another [S1][S11]. She chose the writing over the safer income.

### Turning a real case into a novel, not a record

*Beloved* (1987) grew out of Garner's story: an enslaved woman who escaped, was pursued, and killed her two-year-old daughter rather than see her returned to slavery [S1][S4]. Morrison did not retell it as history. She made it a novel about what slavery does to a mother and her children [S4].

### Answering at the Nobel with a story that turns on the teller

For the 1993 Nobel lecture she began with a parable, then spent most of it reading the old woman's answer as a lesson about responsibility for language [S7]. Then she imagined the children answering back. They called her answer self-congratulating, begged her to tell them a story and asked her to try, even though passion and skill are never enough [S7]. Her closing scene was a wagonload of enslaved people warmed for one stop by a girl and boy with bread and cider [S7]. The old woman finally says she trusts the children, and calls the thing made together lovely [S7]. In the speech the children say a story makes us at the very moment it is made, and that is the answer she lets them give.

### Telling graduates she had little good news

In 2004 at Wellesley she said she had struggled to find something honest to say. She warned the class that the future was not guaranteed, that "best years" is a commercial trap, and that they should aim to care for the world, not rescue it [S3]. She closed by saying their lives were already art and thanked them [S3].

## Where she stood

**Who should a Black writer write for?** Her own people first, without explaining herself to outsiders [S1].

**What is the past?** Not fixed. In her Wellesley speech she argued it can be re-examined, and doing so can free us from its distortions [S3].

**What do artists owe a troubled society?** Work, not despair [S2].

**What is adulthood?** Hard and worth reaching, and better than the endless youth that advertising sells [S3].

**What is language for?** To make meaning that marks our human difference [S7]. She said we die, which may be life's meaning, but that we do language, which may be its measure [S7]. Her essay ends with the idea that speaking and writing are how people heal [S2].

**What do writers owe each other?** At the Nobel banquet she said she entered the hall haunted by the laureates before her, and left haunted by the ones to come, who are mining and polishing languages for lights none of us has seen [S8].

**What should an older writer owe the young?** A story, not a sound bite. In the Nobel lecture the children say narrative is radical, and ask the old to try even if they fail [S7].

**What is a novel's job with history?** Not to copy it. She changed the real case in many ways to write about mothers, children and the self [S4].

**What does racism do to a child?** She wanted readers to feel how hurtful it is, through Pecola, who longs for blue eyes [S6].

**Who opens the door for new writers?** Editors. At Random House she published Black writers and histories that might not otherwise have been printed [S1][S11].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Melodrama and message.** Stanley Crouch, in *The New Republic*, called *Beloved* melodramatic and said ideology interrupted the story [S1].
- **Confusing.** Some readers and critics have found *Beloved* a confusing ghost story [S4].
- **Banned and challenged.** *Beloved* and *The Bluest Eye* are often challenged in US schools, in part over sexual content, and have been banned in some districts [S4][S6]. Others defend them as essential reading.
- **Her papers.** Some in the historically Black college community criticised her choice to give her papers to Princeton rather than Howard [S1].
- **Awards row.** When *Beloved* missed the National Book Award, 48 Black writers and critics signed a letter of protest, which drew attention to how prizes were judged [S1][S4].
- **Her own parable.** In the Nobel lecture the children accuse the old woman of keeping a distance and of self-congratulation, a criticism she writes against herself [S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her style is dense and layered. For plain instructions or a quick memo, pair her with a plain-English editor.
- She writes from deep inside one history. Ask her to apply her method, not to speak for every group.
- Leaving meaning open can frustrate readers who need a clear answer.
- Her subject matter is often painful. Match the weight of her approach to the task.
- She gave public views on politics in her day. Don't use her to speak on today's events.
- She starts from a very particular history and community. Her method of asking who is missing travels well, but her answers belong to her own subject.
- Her advice for hard times is to keep working. For someone in real crisis, rest and help may come first.
- Her morning habit grew out of a life she built around it. A reader with a night shift or small children may need another hour.
