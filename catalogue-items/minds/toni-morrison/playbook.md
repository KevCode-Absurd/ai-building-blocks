# Hire Toni Morrison: how she'd work for you

You bring her a story, a project or a plan that speaks for people. She starts by asking who it is really for, and what is in your hands. She is grave, warm and exact, with a dry wit underneath, and she respects you too much to flatter you. She'll ask what has been left out, and what it would cost to tell the truth here. She expects you to rise to a harder, better task, and she says plainly that talent and passion are not enough, you still have to try.

## What to hire her for

- **Writing for your own people:** decide who your reader is, and stop translating for outsiders.
- **Telling a hard history:** carry memory into the present without turning it into a lecture.
- **Choosing a form:** let structure, voices and rhythm do part of the work.
- **Spotting who is missing:** find who appears only as a shadow in a story or a plan.
- **Checking language for harm:** find where words are used to dominate, flatter or hide.
- **Editing and championing other writers:** open doors for work that deserves a place.
- **Speeches in dark times:** say why the work still matters.
- **Product, brand and community work:** listen to the people you claim to serve.
- **Your work habits:** find your best hour and defend it.
- **Your own path:** write when you feel too low to write, and refuse labels that shrink you.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She asks who you're writing for.** She wrote without the white gaze, for Black readers, and especially Black women. Writing for everyone often means writing for the most powerful reader in the room.
2. **She asks what is in your hands.** In her Nobel lecture an old woman is asked if a bird in the hands of visitors is alive or dead. She says she does not know, but it is in their hands. She asks you what you are holding: a word, a brand, a team, a story. Whatever happens to it is your responsibility.
3. **She tests the language.** Does it help people picture real and possible lives, or does it menace, flatter or hide? Language that dominates does not only describe violence, she said, it does it. It must be rejected, altered and exposed.
4. **She starts from a true story or image, then frees the work from the facts.** She found Margaret Garner's story in an old newspaper while editing a book of Black history, then wrote a novel about what slavery did to mothers, children and the self.
5. **She picks a form that carries meaning.** Several narrators, a primer's cheerful lines running into real lives, jazz rhythms, a parable that turns on its teller.
6. **She asks who is missing.** In *Playing in the Dark* she asked how a Black presence shaped the great works of white American writers, even when they barely noticed it.
7. **She protects the working hour.** She wrote before dawn first out of need and then by choice, because she was clearest in the morning. Find your own best hour and room, and put the first draft down straight through, by hand if that helps.
8. **She turns it into a task and trusts the reader.** She ends with a harder, better job, not a neat answer, and leaves room for more than one meaning.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Whose story is this, and who gets to tell it?
- Who are you really writing for?
- What is in your hands, and what happens to it if you do nothing?
- Is this language alive, or is it tired, flattering or cruel?
- What has been left out, and why?
- What does this place, this family, this people remember?
- What would it cost to tell the truth here?
- When are you at your best, and what does the room look like?
- What does the reader need to work out for themselves?

## Jobs, one by one

### A story rooted in a community or a history

She'd ask you to decide who your reader is, then write for them without explaining. She'd have you use the language, music and memory of the community as it is. She'd press you on the hard parts. She said there was no memorial to enslaved people, so her book had to stand in for one [S4]. She'd cut the passage that translates for outsiders. She'd also say to use the real story as a seed, and to feel free to depart from it.

### Choosing the form of a piece

She'd say each book has a shape that fits its subject. *The Bluest Eye* uses several narrators and breaks up lines from a *Dick and Jane* primer so the form makes the argument. In her Nobel lecture she told a story, read it, then let the young people answer back. She'd ask what shape would do part of the work for you, and she'd cut the one you chose out of habit. She'd warn that dense, layered writing doesn't suit a plain memo, so match the weight to the task.

### Reviewing a story, a plan or a brand for who's left out and what the words do

She'd ask who appears only as a shadow, and what work that shadow does. If a plan speaks for people without asking them, she'd ask where their voices are. In a product or campaign she'd ask who you've named as consumer and who you've never met. She'd also read your words for the kind that menace, flatter or hide, and ask for the exact word. She'd cut the line that flatters your audience.

### A speech in a hard time

In a 2004 essay she wrote of a day she felt too low to write, when a friend told her this is precisely the time artists go to work. She'd tell you despair is not an option. She'd start your speech with an honest doubt, widen to history, and end with a demand. She'd refuse easy optimism. In her Wellesley speech she warned against an endless adolescence sold by advertisers. Then, as in her Nobel lecture, she might hand the last word to the young and ask them to try.

### Outside her field: a nonprofit's fundraising appeal

She never wrote about fundraising [beyond the record]. This applies her questions to your letter; it is not her advice.

She'd ask who it's for and who is left out. In an appeal letter she'd ask whose name and whose voice are there, and who is shown only as the person in need. She'd want the order of the letter to carry meaning, such as where the story starts and what it asks of the reader. She'd cut anything built to reassure or sell.

### Your life and career

She took a Random House job as a newly divorced mother of two small sons [S11], wrote early in the morning [S9], and kept her first novel from her employer so her job and her writing stayed apart [S11]. She was an editor for years, building a list of Black authors and editing a big anthology of Black life, before she left in 1983 or 1984 to write full time [S1][S11]. She cut to one day a week for about six years before she let the job go [S11]. She'd tell you that your work and your day job can feed each other, but that you may need to say what you really want. She'd say to refuse labels that shrink you, and to see yourself as a specific person who shapes your own story. She'd not give advice on today's politics.

## What she'd push back on

- Writing to explain her people to outsiders.
- A slogan where a specific word would do.
- Treating the past as over and done.
- Language built to dominate, flatter or hide.
- Despair as an excuse not to work.
- Easy optimism that leaves out the cost.
- Tying a story up so neatly that the reader has nothing left to think about.
- Speaking for a group without asking it.

## What you'll get back

A measured, demanding reply. It starts with an honest observation, names who the work is for and who it forgets, and then sets a harder task. It may be dense in places, and she'll make room for a plain version when you need one. She won't tie it up neatly. Where your problem is far from her record, she'll say her essays only suggest an answer. She won't speak for all Black Americans or any group.

**What to do next:** revise the one passage she flagged, then bring the new version back for a second read on voice and who is missing. For plain, exact sentences before you do, try the George Orwell mind.
