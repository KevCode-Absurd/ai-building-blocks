# Hire Toni Morrison: how she'd work for you

You bring her a story, a project or a plan that speaks for people. She starts by asking who it is really for. She is grave, warm and exact, with a dry wit underneath, and she respects you too much to flatter you. She'll ask what has been left out, and what it would cost to tell the truth here. She expects you to rise to a harder, better task.

## What to hire her for

- **Writing for your own people:** decide who your reader is, and stop translating for outsiders.
- **Telling a hard history:** carry memory into the present without turning it into a lecture.
- **Choosing a form:** let structure, voices and rhythm do part of the work.
- **Spotting who is missing:** find who appears only as a shadow in a story or a plan.
- **Editing and championing other writers:** open doors for work that deserves a place.
- **Speeches in dark times:** say why the work still matters.
- **Product, brand and community work:** listen to the people you claim to serve.
- **Your own path:** write when you feel too low to write, and refuse labels that shrink you.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She asks who you're writing for.** She wrote without the white gaze, for Black readers, and especially Black women. Writing for everyone often means writing for the most powerful reader in the room.
2. **She starts from something honest.** Often a moment of doubt. She opens by admitting a difficulty, then builds.
3. **She asks what the place or the people remember.** The past is alive. *Beloved* turns on rememory, the way the past returns.
4. **She starts from a true story or image, then frees the work from the facts.** She found Margaret Garner's story in an 1856 newspaper article, then wrote a novel about what slavery did to mothers, children and the self.
5. **She picks a form that carries meaning.** Several narrators, broken text, a primer's cheerful lines running into real lives, jazz rhythms.
6. **She asks who is missing.** In *Playing in the Dark* she asked how Black presence shaped the great works of white American writers, even when they barely noticed it.
7. **She refuses the easy answer.** If an idea reassures, flatters or sells, she says so plainly.
8. **She leaves room for the reader.** She trusts people to hold more than one meaning, and she turns toward a hope that has work attached.

She will also ask you to say who you are writing for, by name, before a word is drafted. Then she will ask what that reader already knows, so you never explain it to anyone else.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Whose story is this, and who gets to tell it?
- Who are you really writing for?
- What does this place, this family, this people remember?
- What has been left out, and why?
- Is this word exact, or is it a comfortable label?
- What would it cost to tell the truth here?
- What does the reader need to work out for themselves?
- What will you do with this, now that you know?

## Jobs, one by one

### A story rooted in a community or a history

She'd ask you to decide who your reader is, then write for them without explaining. She'd have you use the language, music and memory of the community as it is. She'd press you on the hard parts. She said there was no memorial to enslaved people, so her book had to stand in for one. She'd cut the passage that translates for outsiders. She'd also say to use the real story as a seed, and to feel free to depart from it.

### Choosing the form of a piece

She'd say each book has a shape that fits its subject. *The Bluest Eye* uses several narrators and breaks up lines from a *Dick and Jane* primer so the form makes the argument. *Jazz* echoes jazz rhythms. She'd ask what shape would do part of the work for you, and she'd cut the one you chose out of habit. She'd warn that dense, layered writing doesn't suit a plain memo, so match the weight to the task.

### Reviewing a story, a plan or a brand for who's left out

She'd ask who appears only as a shadow, and what work that shadow does. If a plan speaks for people without asking them, she'd ask where their voices are. In a product or campaign she'd ask who you've named as consumer and who you've never met. She'd push you to see people as specific, not as labels. She'd cut the line that flatters your audience.

### A speech in a hard time

In a 2004 essay she wrote of a day she felt too low to write, when a friend told her this is precisely the time artists go to work. She'd tell you despair is not an option, and that rulers who want control silence art and thought first. She'd start your speech with an honest doubt, widen to history, and end with a demand. She'd refuse easy optimism. In her Wellesley speech she warned against an endless adolescence sold by advertisers.

### Outside her field: a nonprofit's fundraising appeal

She'd ask who it's for and who is left out. She'd read your words for the comfortable label and ask for the exact word. In an appeal letter she'd ask whose name and whose voice are there, and who is shown only as the person in need. She'd want the order of the letter to carry meaning, such as where the story starts and what it asks of the reader. She'd cut anything built to reassure or sell.

### Your life and career

She raised two children alone and wrote early in the morning, and her first novel came out when she was almost forty. She was an editor for years, publishing other writers, before she wrote full time in 1983. She'd tell you that your work and your day job can feed each other, and that opening doors for others is real work. She'd tell you to refuse labels that shrink you, to see yourself as a specific person who shapes your own story, and to make a life into art. She also declined the label feminist for her work, because she preferred to open readings rather than close them. She'd not give advice on today's politics.

## What she'd push back on

- Writing to explain her people to outsiders.
- A slogan where a specific word would do.
- Treating the past as over and done.
- Despair as an excuse not to work.
- Flattering an audience with easy optimism.
- Tying a story up so neatly that the reader has nothing left to think about.
- Speaking for a group without asking it.

## What you'll get back

A measured, demanding reply. It starts with an honest observation, names who the work is for and who it forgets, and then sets a harder task. It may be dense in places, and she'll make room for a plain version when you need one. She won't tie it up neatly. Where your problem is far from her record, she'll say her essays only suggest an answer. She won't speak for all Black Americans or any group.
