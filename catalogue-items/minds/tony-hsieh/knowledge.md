## In one paragraph

Hsieh was an American internet founder who studied computer science at Harvard, co-founded the ad network LinkExchange and sold it to Microsoft in 1998 [S6]. That company's culture went sour as it grew, and he came to dread going to work there [S2]. At Zappos he set out to avoid that mistake from the start [S2]. He invested in an online shoe start-up, Zappos, joined it as chief executive and spent two decades running it on one idea: get the culture right, and great service and a strong brand will follow [S3][S6]. Zappos hired and fired on ten core values, paid new hires to quit if they didn't fit, gave call staff no time limits, and offered free shipping and 365-day returns [S1][S3][S5]. Sales passed $1 billion in 2008, and Amazon bought the company in 2009 for about $1.2 billion [S7]. He argued that happy staff and customers lead to profit in the long run [S1][S3]. Later he moved Zappos to a system with no traditional bosses, which many staff chose to leave [S4]. He stepped down as CEO in August 2020 and died that November [S6][S7].

## Core ideas

### Get the culture right first

Hsieh's central belief was that culture comes before everything else. At Stanford in 2010 he said that if you get the company culture right, most other things, including service and a lasting brand, happen on their own [S3]. He was willing to keep employees, customers and suppliers happy even when it didn't seem to make business sense, because he believed it paid off later [S3].

How he applied it: half of each employee's performance review was based on living the culture and inspiring it in others [S3]. He learned this the hard way at LinkExchange. Early hires were friends, but once the company ran out of friends to hire, newer staff didn't fit, and by 100 employees he dreaded going in [S2].

### Make values you can hire and fire by

He wanted core values that people could act on, not words on a wall [S1]. Zappos rolled out ten core values around 2005, and hired and fired by them, even when someone was doing their job well [S1][S3]. One value was being humble. He said Zappos would turn down a talented but self-important candidate without a second thought [S1][S3].

He also grew leaders from within, mostly hiring at entry level and training people to become senior leaders within five to seven years, so outsiders who did not share the culture were less of a risk [S1].

How he applied it: candidates went through two sets of interviews, one for skills and one for culture fit, and had to pass both [S1]. Recruiters even asked shuttle drivers how candidates had treated them [S1].

### Service is the brand

Hsieh put service at the centre of Zappos's brand. He said the company turned to service about four years in, when the dot-com crash left it unable to pay for marketing [S1]. He told Stanford that Zappos wasn't trying to be as efficient as possible, but to give the best customer experience [S3]. He saw the brand as being about service, not shoes, so it could stretch into other fields [S1].

How he applied it: Zappos offered free shipping and returns for a full year, and phone staff had no time limits on calls [S5]. Staff could lend replacement shoes before the originals came back, and one rep sent flowers to a grieving customer, whose story then spread through her friends [S1]. By 2010, 75% of daily sales came from repeat customers [S3].

### The phone call is a chance to build a relationship

Most online shops tried to keep customers off the phone. Hsieh saw it the other way. Zappos took thousands of calls a day, and he called each one the best chance to build a relationship [S2]. Reps were encouraged to build rapport rather than rush people off the line [S5].

How he applied it: some lonely callers rang just to talk, and the longest call lasted eight hours [S3]. Every new hire, whatever the job, worked in the call centre taking orders [S3].

### Pay people to leave if they don't fit

Zappos made new hires an offer during training: take money and quit if the job wasn't right for you [S3][S5]. The amount started at $400 and rose to about $2,000 to $3,000 [S3][S5]. Only a small share, about 1% to 3%, took it [S3][S5].

How he applied it: he said those who turned down the offer were more committed, and the ones who stayed were more passionate and engaged [S3].

### Happiness drives business

Hsieh studied what makes people happy and applied it at work. He named connectedness, meaning the number and depth of your relationships, as a major source of happiness and a predictor of how engaged and productive staff are [S1]. He also pointed to a sense of progress through constant learning [S1]. A textbook case quotes him saying happiness drives almost everything people do [S5].

How he applied it: he looked for hires he'd want to spend time with outside work, and managers spent 10% to 20% of their time socialising with their teams [S1]. He wanted people to be the same at work as at home, and to become real friends by choice [S2].

### Choose the table, then play the long game

In a 2010 essay he said he taught himself the maths of limit hold 'em in 1999 and found that skill beats luck over time [S9]. He carried poker rules into business: judge a decision by its long-run quality, not one result, avoid emotional play, and above all choose the right table, meaning the right market [S9]. He added that in business you can define or enlarge your own table [S9].

How he applied it: he treated Zappos as a bet that service, not price, could win a market, and said short-term profit was worth walking away from [S1][S9].

### Do the opposite of the industry

Hsieh liked strategies that ran against the grain. He said he tried to do the opposite of what the rest of his industry did, and that he got bored easily [S1]. He believed that with fast-spreading news online, it was possible for good companies to win [S1].

How he applied it: he put money other firms spent on ads into service, phone support and free returns [S1][S5]. In 2013 he moved Zappos to a system without job titles or traditional managers, called Holacracy [S4][S6].

## How he decides

- Ask whether a choice fits the core values, and act on the answer [S1].
- Hire for culture as well as skills, and don't hire talent with ego [S1][S3].
- Choose the best customer experience over efficiency [S3].
- Give up short-term profit that would hurt the culture, for example by keeping call centres and fulfilment in-house [S1][S8].
- Spend on service and let customers spread the word [S1].
- Let people who don't fit leave early, with money in hand [S3][S5].
- Promote from within [S1].
- Pick a way of doing things that others in the industry don't [S1].

## Real decisions

### Selling LinkExchange when work stopped being fun
Hsieh and his partner built LinkExchange and hired friends first, which worked until about 20 people, when they had to recruit through resumes and interviews [S2]. They had not prioritised culture, and not every hire fit it [S2]. By about 100 employees he dreaded going to work, and he sold the company to Microsoft in 1998 for $265 million [S2][S6]. He said they had "basically ran out of friends," and he took the lesson to Zappos, where culture was a priority from day one [S2].

### Leaving the investor's chair to run Zappos
After about 20 investments through Venture Frogs, he told Stanford he got bored on the sidelines and missed building something [S3]. Zappos was the most exciting company in the portfolio, so he took over as CEO [S3]. He himself called the choice a leap of faith [S1]. He had first been so doubtful about selling shoes online that he almost deleted the founder's voicemail, until a market figure changed his mind [S6].

### Moving to Las Vegas to staff the call centre
Writing for Harvard Business Review, he said one of the company's most significant decisions came in early 2004, over lunch at a San Francisco restaurant [S10]. The article's standfirst ties the move to Las Vegas to staffing the customer call centre with high-calibre service reps [S10]. Zappos moved in 2004 [S7]. The body of the article was behind a paywall, so his full reasoning is not in what we read.

### Betting on service when ads were out of reach
About four years in, the dot-com crash left Zappos unable to raise money or pay for marketing [S1]. He built the brand on customer service instead [S1]. Staff lent replacement shoes free before the original pair came back, and one rep sent flowers to a customer whose mother had just died [S1]. He said short-term profit was something Zappos was willing to walk away from [S1].

### Writing values the company could hire and fire by
Core values were drafted with company-wide input over about a year, and he wanted them specific enough to be "committable," meaning Zappos would hire or fire on them even for strong performers [S1][S8]. Candidates faced two interview tracks, one for skills and one for culture, and a shuttle driver's report on how a candidate behaved could end the process [S1]. Being humble was a dealbreaker: a talented but egotistical candidate was turned down [S3].

### Paying new hires to quit
Zappos offered new hires $2,000 to $3,000 to leave in their first weeks if the job did not suit them [S3]. Only about 2% to 3% took it [S3]. His reason was that those who turned it down had decided they believed in the company and became more engaged [S3].

### Selling to Amazon and keeping Zappos' identity
Amazon bought Zappos in 2009, a year after sales passed $1 billion, in a deal valued at about $1.2 billion [S3][S7]. Hsieh said he valued that Amazon left Zappos' identity alone [S3]. Zappos then began teaching other businesses how to build their cultures [S3].

### Moving to Holacracy and paying people to leave
In 2013 Zappos began moving to a system with no job titles and no traditional managers [S4][S6]. In March 2015 a long staff memo offered severance to anyone who did not want to work that way, writing that self-management "is not for everyone" [S4]. In the first round 14% of staff left, and about 18%, some 260 people, had gone by early 2016 [S4]. Critics said removing structure can do more harm than good in a mature company [S4].

## Where he stood

**What matters most in a company?** Culture. Service and brand follow from it [S3].

**Should a business chase efficiency?** Not at the cost of the customer experience [S3].

**Profit or happiness?** Both, in the long run. He believed happy staff and customers lead to profit, and would give up short-term gains to protect culture [S1][S3].

**What should graduates look for?** Work they'd enjoy for ten years even without pay [S1].

**Is Zappos a shoe company?** No. He called it a service company that happens to sell shoes and clothing [S8]. He also saw himself as the person who sets the conditions in which others do well, not a towering leader [S8].

**What counts as happiness?** Ongoing progress more than arriving somewhere [S8]. He praised a refrigeration repair firm that used Zappos-style culture work and reported happier staff and customers [S8].

**Should companies have bosses?** By 2015 he wanted Zappos to run by self-management, and accepted that it wasn't for everyone [S4].

## His work and record

- Born December 12, 1973, in Urbana, Illinois [S6].
- Graduated from Harvard in 1995 with a computer science degree [S6].
- Co-founded LinkExchange in 1996; Microsoft bought it in November 1998 for $265 million [S6].
- Co-founded Venture Frogs with Alfred Lin, which invested in Zappos in 1999 [S6][S7].
- Became co-CEO of Zappos in 2001 [S7].
- Moved Zappos to Henderson, Nevada, in 2004, the year Sequoia invested $35 million [S7].
- Zappos sales reached $1 billion in 2008 [S7].
- Amazon agreed to buy Zappos in July 2009; the deal closed in November at a reported $1.2 billion [S7].
- Published Delivering Happiness in 2010; it spent 27 weeks on the New York Times bestseller list [S6].
- Led the Downtown Project in Las Vegas, a redevelopment effort, from 2009 [S6].
- Moved Zappos headquarters to the former Las Vegas City Hall in 2013 [S7].
- Retired as CEO in August 2020 and died on November 27, 2020, from injuries in a house fire; the death was ruled accidental [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Holacracy.** After his March 2015 memo offering severance to anyone who didn't want to work without traditional bosses, 14% of staff left in the first round and about 18%, some 260 people, by early 2016 [S4]. Critics said removing structure can do more harm than good in a mature company [S4].
- **Data security.** A 2012 hack exposed the personal data of 24 million Zappos customers and led to a class action, settled in 2019 [S7].
- **Happiness at any cost.** His stated willingness to put happiness ahead of business sense [S3] worked for Zappos's service, but critics of Holacracy questioned whether his ideas scaled to how a company is run [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- His ideas grew in a company with deep investor backing and later Amazon's support. A tiny firm can't always afford free returns for a year.
- Hiring for fit can drift into hiring people just like you. His own value of diversity needs active care [S2].
- A big change from the top, like Holacracy, can drive good people out [S4].
- He said little in these sources about pricing and margins. Pair him with someone tough on numbers.
