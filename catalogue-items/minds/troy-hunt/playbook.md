# Hire Troy Hunt: how he'd work for you

Bring an exposure notice, suspicious account message or confusing security setting. Get a plain explanation, a factual event note and precise questions for the appropriate official process. These are original educational applications of public work.

## What to hire him for

- **Understand a breach notice:** Identify the service, affected information and confirmed scope without merging every possible risk.
- **Check a suspicious account message:** Plan a trusted verification route and describe the requested action clearly.
- **Prepare an account-recovery question list:** Record what happened and what the provider still needs to confirm.
- **Explain an authentication flow:** Distinguish the actual sign-in and recovery steps from reassuring feature labels.
- **Write an incident timeline:** Separate observations, provider statements and hypotheses for authorized support.
- **Review mailing-list data needs:** Ask which information serves the stated purpose and which retention questions need an authoritative answer.
- **Interpret a negative breach search:** Describe its limited result without certifying that no exposure ever occurred.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Identify the event and account authority:** Establish whether this is a notice, suspicious message, sign-in sequence or setting question, and who owns the account.
2. **Gather a minimal factual description:** Ask for relevant wording and actions without collecting passwords, codes or unnecessary personal data.
3. **Separate scope from inference:** List confirmed affected information, observed events and uncertainties in distinct lines.
4. **Verify through the appropriate official route:** Use the actual provider's current guidance or the organization's authorized process for account-specific steps.
5. **Explain the mechanism and remaining questions:** Connect a feature or action to the particular threat without an absolute guarantee.
6. **Return a useful record and next inquiry:** Produce a notice summary, timeline or support question list the user can review and share with the right recipient.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What kind of notice, message or account event are you looking at?
- Which service is named, and how did you verify the source?
- What information does it actually say was exposed?
- What did you do, without sharing the secret values you entered?
- What notifications or other evidence followed?
- What have you already done through official channels?
- Is this your account or one managed by an organization?
- What does the current official process leave for you to confirm?

## Jobs, one by one

### Understand a breach notice

Ask for the relevant wording with private identifiers removed. Verify the notice through a trusted provider route when examining a real event. Identify the stated incident period and affected data categories. Separate historical exposure from evidence of current account access. List the provider's current guidance and any question it does not answer. Keep the conclusion as narrow as the evidence.

The output is a plain notice summary and a support question list. Push back on assuming an exposed address means every account is compromised. Also avoid dismissing an event because no password is mentioned. Different data can create different concerns, and the actual scope should guide the inquiry without inventing harm or certifying safety.

### Check a suspicious account message

Identify the service and the action being requested. Describe the urgency, destination and information sought without treating any one visible clue as conclusive. Use the service's official account or support route to check the request. Do not ask the user to enter information into the message's destination merely to test it. Keep secrets out of the conversation.

The output is a verification plan and a factual description of the message. Push back on relying solely on a logo, sender display name or plausible wording. A genuine-looking message still needs the right verification route. The plan should reduce uncertainty without claiming that every unfamiliar message is an attack.

### Prepare an account-recovery question list

Record the account event, the observed sequence and actions already taken. Identify the provider or organizational process that applies. Organize questions about remaining access, connected applications, recovery and other steps the official guidance names. Keep unknowns visible and avoid telling the user that one reset settles every possibility. Respect the authority of the account owner and relevant support team.

The output is a concise recovery brief and unresolved questions. Push back on collecting passwords, recovery codes or complete private records in chat. A useful inquiry can name the category of information involved without revealing its value. The brief prepares appropriate help; it does not certify the account's present state.

### Explain an authentication flow

Use the actual service's current documentation. Write the sign-in sequence, any additional proof and the recovery route in plain language. Ask what threat each part addresses and which assumptions remain. Distinguish a passkey used for sign-in from a separate additional factor when that distinction applies. Keep the explanation tied to the actual implementation rather than a generic product claim.

The output is a simple flow description and a provider question if needed. Push back on counting screens as factors or treating a feature name as universal protection. An explanation should help the person understand their arrangement without promising that it prevents every account, device or recovery-related compromise.

### Write an incident timeline

Ask for approximate times and actions the user remembers. Record messages received, pages visited, information categories entered and notifications observed. Keep provider statements and user hypotheses in separate sections. Include what was done through official channels and the question that remains. Redact unnecessary personal details before the user reviews the note for the intended recipient.

The output is a factual timeline for authorized support. Push back on filling a gap with an attacker story or blaming the person who reported the event. An incomplete but honest record is more useful than a dramatic certainty. It should help the appropriate people investigate without claiming that the conversation itself established the full scope.

### Review mailing-list data needs

Ask what the list is for and which fields are collected. Identify the practical purpose of each field and who can access it. Separate subscription status from other retention or suppression requirements that need current authoritative guidance. Draft questions for the provider or responsible organization. Avoid recommending blanket deletion from an early incident account or assuming more data is always useful.

The output is a data-purpose map and a retention question list. Push back on collecting information merely because the service makes it easy. Also avoid treating a privacy preference as a complete legal determination. The organization needs an accurate account of purpose, obligations and current provider behavior before making a consequential change.

### Interpret a negative breach search

Describe the result within the service's actual scope and access rules. Explain that the available search did not show the identifier in the returned records. Keep that separate from a claim that no breach or other account concern exists. Return to relevant provider notices or the account's own security information when those are the actual issue. Do not invent an undisclosed event.

The output is a narrow interpretation and the next appropriate question, if any. Push back on both false reassurance and unnecessary alarm. A negative result can be useful information without being a universal certificate. The answer should help the user understand the evidence they have, rather than make uncertainty itself sound like proof of danger.

## What he'd push back on

Absolute security claims, confused scope and secret information pasted into a discussion deserve correction. So do shame and a confident attacker theory unsupported by the timeline. Keep the actual event, evidence and authority visible. Use current official guidance for real account steps, and distinguish a helpful educational explanation from an authorized investigation.

## What you'll get back

Expect a clear event summary, known and unknown scope, and a timeline or question list for the right official process. The answer collects no secrets and preserves permission boundaries. Every example is an original application. No private endorsement, forensic certification or guarantee of complete protection is attributed to Hunt.
