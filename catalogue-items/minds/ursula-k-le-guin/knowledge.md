## In one paragraph

Le Guin was an American novelist, poet and essayist who wrote fantasy and science fiction with the depth of literary fiction [S1]. The daughter of two anthropologists, she grew up around people who studied how cultures work, and her invented worlds show it [S1][S10]. *A Wizard of Earthsea* (1968) began a fantasy series. *The Left Hand of Darkness* (1969) imagined a people without fixed gender and made her the first woman to win both the Hugo and the Nebula for best novel [S1]. *The Dispossessed* (1974) explored an anarchist society [S1]. She drew on Taoism, anthropology, feminism and anarchist thought [S1][S6]. She treated writing as a craft to honour, and said the book decides what it wants to be [S3][S11]. In her talks she kept returning to one move: speak for the people and the way of living that the powerful would rather leave out [S2][S7][S8].

## Core ideas

### Think like an anthropologist

Le Guin's parents were the anthropologists Alfred and Theodora Kroeber, and cultural anthropology shaped her fiction [S1]. Her worlds are societies, not just settings. She asks how people live: what they build, how they work, how they treat each other [S1][S4]. She told The Millions that she and her father share a temperament: both are interested in artifacts, how things are made and how operations are carried out [S10]. In the Paris Review she said hard-science writers dismiss biology, sociology and anthropology as soft stuff, while she draws on them a great deal, and hints at the complexity of a society so it does not shrink to an "empire" [S6].

How she applied it: *Always Coming Home* (1985) is an experimental book about an imagined pacifist people, the Kesh [S1]. *The Left Hand of Darkness* used a single invented word, shifgrethor, which she built from a pool of sounds but did not need a whole vocabulary for [S10].

### Make every detail agree

She built worlds from practical details that fit together: local wood and rock, where animals are kept, how people make cheese and buy milk [S4]. She tracked contradictions across books and wanted the descriptions to match [S4]. In prose, she said, you can suggest the doorknob and let the reader imagine it, while film must design every object [S10].

How she applied it: when Charles Vess drew a wizard's house in stone, she pointed out that her text called it wooden. They talked through the roof too. She first imagined slate or tile, he argued thatch would blow away on an exposed cliff, and they settled on wood shingles for a volcanic, forested island [S4]. She allowed that a wizard's house might change shape, so magic could cover the odd slip [S4].

### Imagine other ways of living, because any power can be changed

In her 2014 speech, Le Guin said hard times were coming, and that people would need writers who can see other ways to live and imagine real grounds for hope [S2]. She called them realists of a larger reality [S2]. She granted that capitalism can seem impossible to escape, but compared it to the divine right of kings, which also once seemed beyond question. Any human power can be resisted and changed by people, she said, and change often starts in art, especially the art of words [S2]. In 2006 she said the habit of serious reading is the best defence against the half-truths of ideologues and the lies of demagogues, and that a public library is the toolshed of imagination [S9].

How she applied it: *The Dispossessed* imagines an anarchist society and its strains, and won both the Hugo and the Nebula [S1][S6].

### Art is not a commodity

Le Guin drew a line between making a product for the market and practising an art [S2]. Shaping books around sales plans, she said, is not the same as responsible publishing or writing [S2]. She attacked publishers who charged libraries far more for e-books than readers paid, and called on writers to demand a fair share [S2]. In 2006 she said governments dislike literature and try to police it, but it escapes their control [S9]. In 2013 she said she wished she could be called simply a novelist and poet, not shoved into a genre pigeonhole [S6].

### Speak from the dark, not from success

At Mills College in 1983 she said she would not wish the graduates success, since success is somebody else's failure, and spoke about failure instead [S7]. She said that they would meet disappointment, injustice and loss, and she hoped they could live in "the dark place" as natives, not as exiles [S7]. She called the culture of success a world of competition, aggression and power built by and for others, and argued that you can do your own work in your own way, neither serving that world nor fighting on its terms [S7]. She hoped for a life without the need to dominate or be dominated [S7].

### The book decides

Le Guin said she does not decide what a book will be. The book comes and wants to be written, and she facilitates it, like freeing a figure from a block of marble [S11]. She said she never talks about a book until it is finished, which is unlike her mother, who liked to talk her writing out as she wrote [S10]. She said experience sat in memory for years before it turned into fiction, and the imagination recombined it, a process she compared to composting [S3]. *The Dispossessed* began with a character she knew was real but could not get out of the story, a physicist in a prison camp, like a diamond inside a lump of concrete that took years to chip free [S6].

### Writing is a craft

Le Guin treated writing as serious work, and said a craft honours the person who practises it [S3]. She revised heavily, sometimes through many full rewrites, and found revising more fun than first drafts, though both were hard [S3]. Her short advice to writers was to write, revise and, if possible, publish [S3]. She said she never wanted to be a writer with the writer's glamorous life. She just wanted to do the job and do it really well [S6]. For models she named Dickens, Austen and Woolf, and said to shoot for the top always [S6].

How she applied it: she wrote a book on craft, *Steering the Craft* (1998, reissued 2015) [S5].

### Fantasy asks about right and wrong, not about armies

In her 2004 speech she attacked three habits of the genre: that characters are white, that the setting is the Middle Ages, and that the plot is a Battle Between Good and Evil [S8]. She said the setting is an alternate world with its own economy and justice, and that her own books are about people making mistakes and trying to fix them [S8]. Where good and evil are labelled by white hats, the fight is only an excuse for violence [S8]. She said imagination is the instrument of ethics, and that fantasy is good at showing choices other than war [S8].

How she applied it: she fought cover departments for years over white faces on the covers of Earthsea, and had letters from young readers of colour who felt included when they learned Ged was not white [S8].

### Try ideas on, and correct yourself

Le Guin said Taoism gave her a handle on life in adolescence and that she kept returning to Lao-tzu, and she made her own version of the *Tao Te Ching* [S6][S1]. She said she is not a searcher for one truth. Her impulse is playful: she likes trying on ideas and ways of life, and a novelist is always trying on other people [S6]. She was also willing to say when she had got something wrong. In 1976 she defended *The Left Hand of Darkness* in an essay, and by 1988 she said gender was more central to the book than she had admitted [S1].

How she applied it: *Tehanu* (1990) returned to Earthsea for adult readers and reframed the series. Critics had said the early books centred a world run by men [S1].

## How she decides

- Ask how this society actually works, day to day [S1][S4].
- Make every detail agree with every other [S4].
- Imagine a real alternative, not just a darker or brighter copy [S2].
- Put art ahead of the sales plan [S2].
- Let the book say what it wants to be, and do not talk about it until it is done [S10][S11].
- Revise, and keep revising [S3].
- Let experience settle for years before you use it [S3].
- Do not write the Battle Between Good and Evil. Write the mistake and the repair [S8].
- Read the writers and thinkers you disagree with, and learn the field before you invent a new one [S6].
- Be ready to correct your own earlier views [S1].
- Choose a quiet, small life that leaves room for work [S10].

## Real decisions

### Sending a strange first novel to Knopf

At about twenty-three, Le Guin finished a very ambitious novel covering generations in an invented Central European country, Orsinia [S6]. Her father knew the publisher Alfred Knopf, and she asked him whether sending it would presume on the friendship [S6]. He said to go ahead, and Knopf wrote a kind letter saying he could not take it but that she was going somewhere [S6]. That was all she needed, she said, since she did not need acceptance, and she hopes no scholar digs the book out [S6].

### Making Earthsea's people brown in 1968

When she wrote the first Earthsea book in 1968, she said, fantasy was a northern European white tradition [S11]. She made her few villains white and most of her people brown or black [S11]. She said very little about it, because she feared it would put off many white children at that time [S11]. She later fought cover departments over white faces on her covers, and mostly lost [S8]. She was angry when a television miniseries used her names and cast almost everyone as white [S11].

### Reading her way into *The Dispossessed*

The story began as a short story about a physicist in a prison camp that went nowhere [S6]. She knew the character was real, but getting into the lump of concrete took years [S6]. Her pacifist activism sent her to read Gandhi and pacifist writings, then utopian thinkers, then Kropotkin and the anarchists [S6]. She then saw that nobody had written an anarchist utopia, and that it would be fun. She read all the anarchist literature she could get, in back rooms of small Portland shops [S6].

### Talking about failure at Mills College

In 1983 Le Guin gave a commencement talk "in the language of women" and said it would sound terrible [S7]. She told graduates she did not wish them success, since it is someone else's failure, and spoke about failure and the dark [S7]. Her stated reason was that people are going to meet failure, and the culture of success calls that place uninhabitable when it is where life happens [S7]. She told them to do their own work, ask for equal pay, and to live without power over others [S7]. She released the text for free reprinting [S7].

### Rewriting the rules for fantasy in 2004

Asked to speak for ten minutes at a children's literature breakfast on how she makes her books, she said she could not talk about how or why, only what [S8]. She chose instead to name three assumptions that bother her about fantasy: white characters, a Middle Ages setting and a Battle Between Good and Evil [S8]. She backed each with evidence: letters from young readers, cover fights, a send-up from Monty Python, and a reminder that even the Tolkien film turned a moral story into battle scenes [S8]. This is our summary of that point; we have not re-checked her exact words. She ended by asking publishers to honour the real heroic fantasies [S8].

### Giving a silent character a voice in *Lavinia*

In *The Aeneid*, Lavinia is a princess who is promised to the hero and never speaks [S11]. Le Guin became interested in who she was and what it all looked like to her [S11]. She said Lavinia began to talk to her, telling the same story from her side, and the book wrote itself and was a pleasure [S11].

### Settling the Old Mage's house

For the Earthsea sketches she worked out the floor plan of the Old Mage's house, where each bed sat, and how an old byre wall had split the room [S4]. She argued for a wood roof on a windy, volcanic, forested island, and when an illustration showed stone she proposed that a wizard's house could change [S4]. The collaboration with Charles Vess depended on trading evidence for the choice, not on authority [S4].

### Speaking for fantasy and science fiction in 2014

At the medal ceremony she dedicated the award to the fantasy and science fiction writers who had watched prizes go to realists for half a century [S2]. She used the speech to attack the market's hold on publishing and to say writers deserve a fair share of the proceeds [S2]. She ended by reframing the prize as one about freedom [S2].

## Where she stood

**Is fantasy serious literature?** Yes. She accepted her 2014 medal on behalf of fantasy and science fiction writers, who she felt had long been passed over for realists [S2]. In 2004 she said fantasy is useful for examining the real difference between good and evil [S8]. The critic Harold Bloom said she raised fantasy into high literature [S1].

**Are books products?** Not just products. She saw them as art, and the profit motive as a threat to it [S2].

**Can systems change?** Yes. Any human power can be resisted and changed [S2].

**What is success?** In 1983: someone else's failure. She wished graduates a place to live in the dark, with enough to eat, a warm place, friends and work they liked [S7].

**What does literature do?** It sets minds free of any tyrant or demagogue, and the habit of serious reading defends against lies [S9].

**How should young writers live?** She advised having a practical source of income, valued writing communities and rejected treating art as a competition [S3].

**Adaptations?** She objected when the 2004 *Legend of Earthsea* miniseries cast white actors as characters she had written with darker skin, and said the makers kept the names and made another story [S1][S11].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Gender in *The Left Hand of Darkness*.** Her own later view was that her first defence of the book missed how central gender was, and she apologised for showing only heterosexual relationships [S1].
- **A male-centred Earthsea.** Critics said the early Earthsea books showed a world run by men, with women at the still centre [S1].
- **Preachy late work.** Some critics found her later books didactic [S1].
- **Adaptations.** Her complaints about adaptations were sharp. She praised the visuals of the Studio Ghibli film *Tales from Earthsea* but criticised its moral sense and violence [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Rich worldbuilding can slow a story. Some readers want plot first.
- Her stand against commodity can be hard to apply for someone who must earn a living from sales. She also advised having a day job [S3].
- Ideas-led fiction can tip into lecture, as some critics said of her later work [S1].
- She is warm to the quiet and the small, and may undervalue loud, fast stories.
- Pair her with a plot-driven storyteller or a commercial voice for balance.
