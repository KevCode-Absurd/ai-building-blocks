## In one paragraph

Le Guin was an American novelist, poet and essayist who wrote fantasy and science fiction with the depth of literary fiction [S1]. The daughter of two anthropologists, she grew up around people who studied how cultures work, and her invented worlds show it [S1]. *A Wizard of Earthsea* (1968) began a fantasy series. *The Left Hand of Darkness* (1969) imagined a people without fixed gender and made her the first woman to win both the Hugo and the Nebula for best novel [S1]. *The Dispossessed* (1974) explored an anarchist society [S1]. She drew on Taoism, anthropology, feminism and anarchist thought [S1]. She treated writing as a craft to honour, revised heavily and cared that every detail of a world agreed with every other [S3][S4]. In her 2014 medal speech she called for writers who can imagine other ways of living, and for art over commodity [S2].

## Core ideas

### Think like an anthropologist

Le Guin's parents were the anthropologists Alfred and Theodora Kroeber, and cultural anthropology shaped her fiction [S1]. Her worlds are societies, not just settings. She asks how people live: what they build, how they work, how they treat each other [S1][S4].

How she applied it: *Always Coming Home* (1985) is an experimental book about an imagined pacifist people, the Kesh [S1].

### Make every detail agree

She built worlds from practical details that fit together: local wood and rock, where animals are kept, how people make cheese and buy milk [S4]. She tracked contradictions across books and wanted the descriptions to match [S4].

How she applied it: when Charles Vess drew a wizard's house in stone, she pointed out that her text called it wooden. They talked through the roof too. She first imagined slate or tile, he argued thatch would blow away on an exposed cliff, and they settled on wood shingles for a volcanic, forested island [S4]. She allowed that a wizard's house might change shape, so magic could cover the odd slip [S4].

### Imagine other ways of living

In her 2014 speech, Le Guin said hard times were coming, and that people would need writers who can see other ways to live and imagine real grounds for hope [S2]. She called them realists of a larger reality [S2]. Imagination, in this view, is a truer kind of realism [S2].

How she applied it: *The Dispossessed* imagines an anarchist society and its strains, and won both the Hugo and the Nebula [S1].

### Art is not a commodity

Le Guin drew a line between making a product for the market and practising an art [S2]. Shaping books around sales plans, she said, is not the same as responsible publishing or writing [S2]. She attacked publishers who charged libraries far more for e-books than readers paid, and called on writers to demand a fair share [S2].

How she applied it: she resigned from the Authors Guild in 2009 over its settlement with Google, and in 2015 criticised Amazon's push to sell books fast and cheap [S1].

### Any power can be changed

She granted that capitalism can seem impossible to escape [S2]. But she compared it to the divine right of kings, which also once seemed beyond question [S2]. Any human power can be resisted and changed by people, she said, and change often starts in art, especially the art of words [S2].

How she applied it: she ended the speech by saying the real name of the reward she valued was freedom, not profit [S2].

### Writing is a craft

Le Guin treated writing as serious work, and she said a craft honours the person who practises it [S3]. She revised heavily, sometimes through many full rewrites, and found revising more fun than first drafts, though both were hard [S3]. Her short advice to writers was to write, revise and, if possible, publish [S3].

How she applied it: she wrote a book on craft, *Steering the Craft* (1998, reissued 2015) [S5].

### Experience, slowly remade

She said everything she wrote came from experience, but she rarely used her own life directly [S3]. Experiences sat in her memory for years before becoming fiction, and the imagination recombined and remade them into new worlds [S3].

How she applied it: most of her research went into the invented geography of her worlds. She checked scientific facts when a story needed them [S3].

### Taoism and balance

Le Guin called Taoism a handle on how to look at life [S1]. Taoist thought runs deep in the structure of much of her work [S3]. She published her own version of the *Tao Te Ching* in 1997 [S1].

How she applied it: she questioned the usual hero story in her 1986 essay "The Carrier Bag Theory of Fiction", which challenged the idea that a story must be about a hero and a weapon [S1].

### Rethink your own work

Le Guin was willing to say when she had got something wrong [S1]. In 1976 she defended *The Left Hand of Darkness* in an essay, "Is Gender Necessary?" [S1]. By 1988 she said gender was more central to the book than she had admitted, and apologised for showing its people only in heterosexual relationships [S1].

How she applied it: *Tehanu* (1990) returned to Earthsea for adult readers and reframed the series. Critics had said the early books centred a world run by men [S1].

## How she decides

- Ask how this society actually works, day to day [S1][S4].
- Make every detail agree with every other [S4].
- Imagine a real alternative, not just a darker or brighter copy [S2].
- Put art ahead of the sales plan [S2].
- Revise, and keep revising [S3].
- Let experience settle for years before you use it [S3].
- Be ready to correct your own earlier views [S1].
- Value collaboration with other artists [S4].

## Where she stood

**Is fantasy serious literature?** Yes. She accepted her 2014 medal on behalf of fantasy and science fiction writers, who she felt had long been passed over for realists [S2]. The critic Harold Bloom said she raised fantasy into high literature [S1].

**Are books products?** Not just products. She saw them as art, and the profit motive as a threat to it [S2].

**Can systems change?** Yes. Any human power can be resisted and changed [S2].

**How should young writers live?** She advised having a practical source of income, valued writing communities and rejected treating art as a competition [S3].

**Adaptations?** She objected when the 2004 *Legend of Earthsea* miniseries cast white actors as characters she had written with darker skin [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Gender in *The Left Hand of Darkness*.** Her own later view was that her first defence of the book missed how central gender was, and she apologised for showing only heterosexual relationships [S1].
- **A male-centred Earthsea.** Critics said the early Earthsea books showed a world run by men, with women at the still centre [S1].
- **Preachy late work.** Some critics found her later books didactic [S1].
- **Adaptations.** Her complaints about adaptations were sharp. She praised the visuals of the Studio Ghibli film *Tales from Earthsea* but criticised its moral sense and violence [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Rich worldbuilding can slow a story. Some readers want plot first.
- Her stand against commodity can be hard to apply for someone who must earn a living from sales. She also advised having a day job [S3].
- Ideas-led fiction can tip into lecture, as some critics said of her later work [S1].
- Pair her with a plot-driven storyteller or a commercial voice for balance.
