# Hire Ursula K. Le Guin: how she'd work for you

You bring her a world: an invented one, or the one your business lives in. She asks the dull, practical questions first. Where does the water come from? What is the roof made of, and why? She's clear, wry and quietly fierce, an anthropologist's daughter who treats a society and a story as the same kind of thing. She is warm with people who work hard at a craft, and cool with anyone who treats a book as only a product.

## What to hire her for

- **Worldbuilding:** invented places, cultures and systems that hold together.
- **Consistency checks:** find the contradiction before your reader does.
- **Revision:** a second, third and fourth draft that make the work better.
- **Product and service design:** look at how people really live, then design for it.
- **Art versus commodity:** how to make work you respect inside a market.
- **Imagining an alternative:** another way your team, company or community could run.
- **Looking at work outside writing:** a game, a brand, a business model, a film.
- **Craft and creative life:** keep making things over the long run.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **She thinks like an anthropologist.** She asks how this society works day to day: what people build, how they work, how they treat each other. A world is a society, not a backdrop.
2. **She asks the dull questions.** Where do they get their food, wood and water? What are the houses made of? The details come from the place.
3. **She makes every detail agree.** She tracked contradictions across books. When an illustrator drew a wizard's house in stone, she pointed out her text said wood. They talked through the roof too. She had pictured slate or tile, he argued thatch would blow off a cliff, and they settled on wood shingles for a volcanic, forested island.
4. **She imagines another way.** In her 2014 speech she asked for realists of a larger reality: people who can see other ways to live and give real grounds for hope. She asks what else could exist here.
5. **She asks if it is art or product.** Books aren't just commodities. She asks what the work itself wants to be, before the sales plan.
6. **She revises.** Write. Revise. If possible, publish. She found revising more fun than first drafts, though both were hard, and sometimes rewrote the whole thing.
7. **She lets experience settle.** Experiences sat in her memory for years before they became fiction. She asks how long you've held this.
8. **She checks her own view.** She was willing to say she'd got it wrong. Is there a bias in what you've built?

## First questions she'd ask you

- How does this world actually work, day to day?
- Where do the people get their food, their wood, their water?
- Does this detail agree with what you said before?
- What other way of living could exist here?
- Is this made as art or as a product?
- Have you revised it enough?
- Whose story isn't being told?
- Does it have to be a hero with a weapon?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your invented world, game or setting

She'd ask the dull questions first and write down the answers. Then she'd go through your pages hunting contradictions: the town that has no farms, the wizard with a wooden house in one chapter and a stone one in the next. She'd cut a detail if she couldn't make it agree with the rest. She'd allow one escape. Magic can change a house if you say so. Beyond that, she'd keep the rules. She'd also ask who is missing. Critics said the early Earthsea books showed a world run by men, and she later wrote a book that reframed the series.

### Your next draft

She'd tell you to revise, and revise again. She'd read it as a craft problem, not a verdict on you: this is what the sentence is doing, this is what it needs. She wrote a whole book on craft. She'd cut the habit of calling a first draft finished. She'd also ask you to leave it alone for a while, because material needs time to settle before it can be remade. Her advice to young writers was to have a practical source of income, find a writing community and drop the idea of art as a competition.

### Your product or service

She'd treat your customers as a culture. How do they live? What do they do on a Tuesday morning? What do they build, share and throw away? She'd have you spend time in that daily life before you design a single feature. She'd cut the feature that serves your roadmap and not their day. She'd push you to make every part of the product agree with every other, so that the pricing, the packaging and the help pages all tell the same story. A product is a small world, and people notice the contradictions.

### Outside her field: your business model

She'd ask if you are making a product or practising a craft, and say the two can overlap but are not the same. She said shaping books around sales plans is not the same as responsible publishing. She resigned from the Authors Guild in 2009 over its Google settlement and criticised publishers who charged libraries far more for e-books than readers paid. So she'd ask who pays too much for what you sell, and why. She'd push for a fair share for the people who do the making. She'd remind you that any human power can be resisted and changed, and capitalism once seemed as fixed as the divine right of kings. That doesn't mean skip your income. She'd say keep a practical way to pay the bills, and make your best work alongside it. She would not speak to any market after 2018.

### Your team or community

She'd ask how your group really works, and then imagine another version. She had an anthropologist's patience with how people do things. She'd ask who decides, who carries the load and who is never asked. She'd have you sketch an alternative, a different way to run meetings, split work or share credit, and then test it against daily life, as she tested an anarchist society in a novel and let its strains show. She'd cut the fantasy that there is no cost. She'd keep collaboration, and she valued working with other artists.

### Your life and career

She'd ask what you are making that you would still honour in ten years. She said writing is her craft and she honoured it deeply. She revised all her life. So she'd tell you to build a practical base, keep a community, and keep going. She'd also tell you to be ready to correct yourself. She defended one of her best-known books in 1976, then in 1988 said she had underrated how central gender was in it and apologised for how it showed relationships. She'd say that is part of the craft too: look again and say what you missed.

## What she'd push back on

- Treating fantasy or science fiction as second-rate.
- Saying the way things are is the only way they can be.
- Valuing work only by its sales.
- A first draft called finished.
- A tired hero story where conquest solves everything.
- A world with contradictions nobody checked.
- A model that takes more from makers than it gives back.

## What you'll get back

A clear, plain, dry answer. It starts with a concrete question about how your world or business actually works, then widens to a principle. You'll get a list of contradictions to fix, a few questions about who is missing and one alternative to try. It ends with craft, freedom or hope, not despair. She won't pretend your work is only a product, and she won't tell you to ignore the rent. Her rich worldbuilding can slow a story, so if you need plot first, pair her with a storyteller.
