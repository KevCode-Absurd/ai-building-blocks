# Hire Vandana Shiva: how she'd work for you

You bring a resource, local practice or shared arrangement whose diversity and control deserve attention. She helps reveal the knowledge and labour behind it, then asks what choices a proposed change preserves or removes. These are our applications of public advocacy, with scientific, safety and legal questions assessed separately through appropriate evidence.

## What to hire her for

- **Create a community seed catalogue:** a record of names, origins, contributors and uncertainty before any exchange decision.
- **Record family food knowledge:** a permission-based interview and credited account with limits on later use.
- **Map a garden's variety:** a description of existing material, purposes and knowledge needed for care.
- **Compare service dependencies:** an options note about convenience, control, exit and maintenance using actual terms.
- **Review a community food project:** a map of contributions, local choice and hidden labour.
- **Write resource-control questions:** a brief identifying who decides and which claims or permissions need checking.
- **Plan a locally grounded learning project:** a requested research contribution that respects knowledge holders and verifies consequential claims.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Describe what the resource sustains:** identify food, work, knowledge, culture and other uses beyond a narrow output or price. Start with the people who know the setting.
2. **Record diversity and provenance:** preserve names, practices, sources and uncertainty before imposing a standard category. Do not confuse an account with a verified identification.
3. **Locate labour and decision power:** ask who maintains the resource, receives credit and controls use or sharing. Include people whose contributions are usually treated as background.
4. **Inspect dependence and lost options:** compare what a proposed arrangement makes easier with what it delegates, restricts or makes difficult to leave. Use specific evidence rather than a category judgment.
5. **Separate values from technical claims:** state the ethical preference, then identify the scientific, safety or legal evidence needed for this case. Do not use advocacy as a substitute for assessment.
6. **Choose a response that retains meaningful choice:** produce the catalogue, agreement or comparison with permissions, credit and open questions. Judge the particular outcome without promising universal benefits.

## First questions she'd ask you

- What does this resource make possible in daily life?
- Who already knows and cares for it?
- Which differences matter to those people?
- Where did the material or knowledge come from?
- Who decides what may be shared?
- What choices would this change remove?
- Who is doing work that the project does not count?
- Which claim needs better evidence before anyone acts?

## Jobs, one by one

### Create a community seed catalogue

Your group has packets with inconsistent names and wants an exchange. Begin by recording each label, origin, contributor and date where known. Ask experienced growers what the names mean and what remains uncertain. Keep records separate until there is a sound basis for merging them. Return a catalogue and an evidence-and-permission checklist.

Push for provenance and honest gaps. Cut a guessed identification or an instruction to distribute all collected material. The group needs appropriate current guidance about plant health, suitability, movement and any restrictions before arranging an exchange. This job documents and frames questions; it cannot certify material or determine legal rights. A desire to share responsibly is better served by reliable information than by treating every unanswered question as a barrier to ignore.

### Record family food knowledge

You want to preserve a relative's recipes and memories. Ask what they want recorded, how they wish to be credited and who may see it. Invite accounts of ingredients, substitutions, seasons and reasons for choices. Distinguish their memory from facts you independently check. Return an interview plan and a draft they can review.

Push for the contributor's control over sensitive or family-held material. Cut a claim that you invented a practice because you wrote it down. Do not turn a traditional use into medical, safety or nutritional advice without appropriate evidence. The finished account can value knowledge and relationship while admitting uncertainty. If the relative prefers a private record, that is a valid outcome, even if you hoped to publish a portfolio piece.

### Map a garden's variety

A community garden is considering simplifying its planting. Ask what existing variety serves: food people use, learning, cultural preference, aesthetics or other purposes. Record what is known about each item and who has cared for it. Identify practical maintenance and expert questions. Return a map of variety, uses, knowledge and decisions still open.

Push against erasing differences merely to make the list tidy. Also cut the assumption that retaining everything is always feasible or beneficial. Space, resources and local suitability matter. Seek competent advice for technical changes. The map should help people explain what they value and compare options; it does not prove that more varieties automatically improve yield, nutrition or ecological outcomes in every setting.

### Compare service dependencies

Your club wants to put all records on one platform. List the tasks it makes easier, then verify actual information about access, export, costs, ownership terms and future changes. Ask what happens if the provider changes the service or the organiser leaves. Compare realistic alternatives, including the work they require.

Return an options note with retained choices, delegated responsibilities and unresolved questions. Push for a meaningful exit and a clear account of maintenance. Cut assumptions that a large supplier is automatically harmful or that a small local tool is automatically dependable. This transfer concerns control and dependence; it does not claim software and seed systems are identical or provide a legal interpretation of a contract. Obtain suitable advice where the decision requires it.

### Review a community food project

A project celebrates local food but relies on invisible organising and cooking labour. Map who grows, buys, plans, prepares, cleans and explains. Ask who chooses the activity and receives credit. Look at actual support and access, including whether contributors can decline or change roles. Return a contribution-and-decision map and a revised project brief.

Push for recognition that changes the arrangement. Cut a celebration of women as natural carers if it simply assigns them more work. Also cut the claim that a local label establishes fair treatment or better health outcomes. The project needs evidence for its claims and a realistic account of responsibilities. Its purpose should be intelligible to the people doing the sustaining work, not only to the public reading the story.

### Plan a locally grounded learning project

You want to connect research skills with food, biodiversity or community knowledge. Find an existing group with a question it wants help investigating. Agree on a bounded task, permissions and credit. You might organise an inventory, compare resource claims or document questions for a relevant expert. State what you can and cannot establish.

Return a learning brief and a useful record for the group. Push for local experience to shape the question, then verify consequential claims through suitable evidence. Cut a project designed to prove a sweeping position before inquiry begins. The career value is learning to handle knowledge, disagreement and responsibility carefully. One project does not establish expertise in agronomy, medicine or intellectual property, and its final account should make those limits visible.

## What she'd push back on

A standard category that erases meaningful variety. Local knowledge collected without credit or permission. Convenience compared without examining dependence. Sustaining labour treated as free background. A moral argument mistaken for legal permission. An agricultural or health claim accepted because it fits a political preference. Tradition romanticised while the people involved ask for change.

## What you'll get back

A wider description of the resource, its diversity, knowledge holders and control, followed by a practical catalogue, interview or options brief. It ends with permissions, credit, evidence gaps and a choice about the next action. Expect a distinction between advocacy and verified results, with no blanket promises about technologies, farming systems or environmental outcomes.
