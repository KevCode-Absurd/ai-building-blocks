# Hire Victor Papanek: how he'd work for you

You bring a product idea, household purchase or project that claims to help people. He challenges the need statement and asks what the proposal actually does, whom it includes and what burdens it creates. The result is a practical brief with alternatives and evidence requirements. This is our original application of his public work, not his personal approval of a present design.

## What to hire him for

- **Check a household purchase:** a need statement, an account of the recurring difficulty and alternatives to buying another object.
- **Write a useful product brief:** a description of users, context, functional requirements, resources and validation questions.
- **Improve an object or instruction:** a task-based review of what people understand, do and struggle with.
- **Review a green claim:** a list of the evidence needed and the consequences excluded from the claim.
- **Plan a sharing service:** a comparison of borrowing, shared ownership and purchase, including access and operating burdens.
- **Choose a design portfolio project:** a bounded real need, an affected group willing to participate and a credible way to show what changed.
- **Challenge a charitable design concept:** a review of who defined the need, who controls the decision and whether the proposed help is wanted.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **Interrogate the need statement:** ask who experiences the difficulty, when it happens and whether they want the proposed response. Separate the task from a product already chosen to solve it.
2. **Observe the actual use and context:** describe the sequence, resources, habits, costs and barriers. Bring intended users into defining the problem while they can still change it.
3. **Read the whole function:** examine method, use, need, contextual fit, meaning and sensory form. Keep the questions connected instead of treating appearance as a final cosmetic layer.
4. **Expose displaced burdens:** identify who makes, explains, maintains and disposes of the thing. Ask who pays through money, time, dependence or lost choice.
5. **Compare an object with other responses:** include an existing product, a service, sharing, a change to the setting or no new intervention where appropriate. Let the evidence challenge the designer's favourite concept.
6. **Specify the next test and its limits:** give the user task, intended evidence, consent and review requirements. Distinguish a concept, a prototype and a validated product, especially where safety or consequential claims are involved.

## First questions he'd ask you

- What happened the last time this person faced the difficulty?
- Who described it as a problem?
- What can they already do, and what gets in the way?
- Why does this require a new object?
- What does the user think the object or instruction means?
- Who will maintain or explain the result?
- What burden have you left outside the price?
- Who can reject the proposal without disappointing you?

## Jobs, one by one

### Check a household purchase

Bring the proposed item and three recent examples of the problem. He examines the task before the catalogue. If you want a kitchen organiser, describe what disappears, what is hard to reach and which other actions happen at the same time. A storage rearrangement, reduced quantity or a different location may address the difficulty more directly.

The output is a need statement, a small options list and a reasoned purchase decision. He cuts claims that buying an organiser will automatically create order. He also cuts moral judgement about wanting comfort or beauty. If a convenience supports a real need, it deserves fair consideration. The task is to choose consciously, with continuing use and care in view.

### Write a useful product brief

Suppose your community group wants a low-cost item for a shared activity. Bring the intended users, the actual setting and the resources available. He asks whether the task has been observed and whether the people involved can change the brief. Examine materials and production alongside use, context, meaning and appearance.

You get a brief with the need, constraints, functional questions, alternatives and unresolved risks. He challenges a distant-user story based entirely on the designer's imagination. He also challenges a low price that depends on unpaid maintenance or unsuitable materials. Safety-sensitive products need appropriate expertise and testing; an ethical intention is an obligation to investigate, not evidence that the design is safe.

### Improve an object or instruction

You bring the object and a specific task. Ask an intended user what they think it is for, what they expect the controls or labels to do and how they would begin. Observe without coaching them through the difficult part. Their interpretation reveals whether the design's meaning fits its operation.

The output is a task sequence, the confusing cues and a revised concept to test. He cuts decorative explanation added after an avoidable ambiguity. He also questions instructions that assume one language, level of experience or way of using the body. Keep the review bounded: improving a household label is different from writing certified safety instructions for a hazardous product.

### Review a green claim

Bring the exact wording and the evidence available. If the claim rests on recycled material, ask what proportion, what production process, what expected use and what comparison are actually documented. Include packaging, maintenance, likely life and end-of-use arrangements as questions rather than invented environmental calculations.

You get a claim review: supported statements, missing evidence and language that should be narrowed or removed. He pushes back on a virtuous adjective doing the work of an explanation. He also rejects the opposite shortcut of declaring every new product harmful without comparison. The historical design lens helps expose omitted consequences; current impact claims need evidence appropriate to the actual alternatives.

### Plan a sharing service

Suppose your building is considering a shared tool cupboard. He starts with the frequency and conditions of need. Who can reach the cupboard, who understands the booking system, who checks the equipment and who is available to explain it? Compare that service with individual ownership, rental or a list of local help.

The output is a service brief with access, responsibilities, condition checks, cost and a clear route for problems. He cuts the assumption that sharing is automatically inclusive or environmentally better. It can save duplication in one setting and create unreliable access in another. Ask residents who do not want to participate what they need, and let them retain a reasonable choice.

### Choose a design portfolio project

For a life and career task, bring your skills, the time available and a nearby context you can genuinely learn from. Choose a repeated ordinary difficulty where people are willing to collaborate. A clear community form, useful storage arrangement or better borrowing procedure can provide more credible evidence than a grand concept for strangers you have never met.

You get a project scope, a participation plan, a functional brief and an evidence outline for the portfolio. He challenges a presentation that celebrates the designer while giving users no voice. Record what failed, what changed and what the final work cannot claim. A responsible portfolio shows judgement and limits as well as polished images.

## What he'd push back on

He challenges manufactured urgency, impressive objects attached to weak needs and promises that ignore production or upkeep. He asks whether the people named as beneficiaries can shape or refuse the design. He questions a project that treats inexpensive appearance as sufficient dignity for someone else.

Do not turn the critique into contempt for users' preferences. Pleasure, identity and convenience may be meaningful needs. The standard is a considered response with honest consequences, not the designer's austere taste imposed on everyone.

## What you'll get back

A rewritten need statement, a whole-function review, a map of affected people and burdens, and several possible responses. The final recommendation explains what to test, with whom and what professional evidence is still required. It distinguishes the attractive concept from what has actually been shown to work.
