# Victor Schwab: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Victor Schwab was a mail-order copywriter who spent his career on ads that had to pay for themselves in replies [S1][S4]. He started as a secretary to Max Sackheim, a copy chief who went on to help found the Book-of-the-Month Club [S4][S5]. He became a copywriter, worked on ads for Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence People*, and with Robert Beatty built the agency Schwab and Beatty [S1][S4]. His agency's clients keyed every ad and kept records of what each one brought in [S1]. From those records he drew a simple view of headlines. A good headline picks out the readers who care about the subject and promises them a worthwhile reward for reading [S1]. The writer's job is to make the ad harder to pass up than to read [S1]. He then carried the same care into the first paragraph and the subheads, so the reader keeps going all the way to the order form [S1].

## Core ideas

### Two jobs for every headline

Schwab said good headlines do two things. They select, from all the readers of a paper or magazine, the ones who are interested (or can be made interested) in the subject. And they promise those readers a worthwhile reward for reading [S1]. Later writers still quote this as a first rule for headlines [S3].

How he applied it: his own 1958 headline essay opens by pointing out to you, the reader, that you must care about advertising and that the headline has promised the ad will be worth your time [S1].

### Harder to pass up than to read

He argued that the headline gets people into the copy, and the copy can't get them into the headline [S1]. Some of the biggest flops, he wrote, had strong body copy that nobody read because it wasn't summed up in a good headline [S1]. So a copywriter's aim should be to make the ad harder to skip than to read, and the headline is the first and biggest step [S1].

How he applied it: he refused to put a percentage on a headline's share of an ad's value. He compared it to asking how much better a car that runs is than one that won't start [S1].

### The flagman by the track

Schwab pictured the headline as a flag held up by a flagman next to a railway [S1]. The train is a family speeding through busy daily life. The flag has a split second to catch the driver's eye and give a message strong enough to beat every other distraction [S1]. It must catch attention and offer a "reward for reading" big enough to pull the reader past the headline [S1]. A warning headline, he added, is more like a red flag [S1].

How he applied it: he used the picture to explain why a headline must be persuasive on its own, not just noticeable [S1].

### Gain, or loss avoided

He sorted the rewards a headline can promise into two kinds [S1]. The positive kind tells the reader how they can save, gain or achieve something, and so increase their wellbeing, money, standing or security. The negative kind tells them how to avoid or reduce a risk, worry, loss, mistake, embarrassment or drudgery [S1]. The best headlines go one step further and say how quickly, easily or cheaply the promise comes true [S1].

How he applied it: he noted that the fear of loss is sometimes stronger than the hope of gain. One of his 100 examples, about a "little mistake" that cost a farmer $3,000 a year, sold well in farm papers on just that idea [S1]. Of his 100 chosen headlines, 21 were wholly negative and 10 more began negative and turned positive [S1].

### Be specific, and use words that pull

Schwab pointed to the attraction of the specific: exact amounts, times, numbers and days [S1]. He also picked out small words that did heavy work. "Which" invites the reader to choose rather than to say no. "Who else" suggests other people are already on board. "These" in "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?" makes the reader want to know which mistakes [S1].

How he applied it: he argued that the headline "How to Win Friends and Influence People" would be a stale motto without the words "How to" [S1]. He said the first headline on his list, "The Secret of Making People Like You", ran in keyed ads that cost almost $500,000 and still paid [S1].

### Curiosity must stay tied to the product

He included only one pure curiosity headline in his 100 [S1]. His worry was that curiosity headlines often raise ideas unrelated to the product, so the copy then has to turn idle interest into a wish to buy, which is hard [S1]. A reader may read a curiosity ad when they have time to spare, he said, but will make time for a headline that promises something they truly want [S1].

How he applied it: he ran a "So What?" list of poor headlines that had appeared in costly space, such as "Value Is a Feature" and "Youth Cries Unto Youth" [S1].

### Pay off the promise in the first paragraph

Schwab treated the first paragraph as the place where most readers decide to stay or leave [S1]. It should follow through on the headline's idea, use short sentences, and start at once to deliver the promised reward, packing in a few major advantages and no small claims [S1]. He admitted it is often the hardest part to write. Writers often find their first few paragraphs were only a warm-up, and the ad really starts later [S1].

How he applied it: he told writers that the longer the copy holds the most readers, the more of them will act [S1].

### Subheads are ladder rungs

He called subheads the rungs of a ladder that make it easier to keep going down the copy [S1]. Use them freely in long copy, bring in the first one after two short paragraphs, and make some of them questions [S1]. Avoid flat statements that answer themselves. Make each one hint at the news in the next paragraph [S1]. Plan them as part of the copy instead of sprinkling them on after it is written [S1].

How he applied it: he suggested a run of subheads that tell the sales story in order, so even readers who only glance get the main points [S1].

## How he decides

- Judge a headline by its record: keyed replies or readership scores [S1].
- Ask who the headline selects and what reward it promises [S1].
- Try both a gain and a loss version [S1].
- Add how fast, how easy or how cheap [S1].
- Swap vague words for a number, a time or a price [S1].
- Keep curiosity tied to the product [S1].
- Make the first paragraph pay off the headline [S1].
- Treat an ad as business, not art [S1].

## Where he stood

**Can you say what share of an ad's value is the headline?** No. A good headline can be the difference between an ad that works and one that is never read [S1].

**Is a negative headline a mistake?** Not always. Many of his proven headlines warned of a loss, though he saw both kinds as ways to promise a reward [S1].

**Should ads look artistic?** He cared more about results. Using many subheads might make an ad look spotty, and he didn't mind [S1].

**How should an agency prove its worth?** By keyed results its clients can check, ad after ad [S1].

**What do readers want?** In an early excerpt he listed what people of his time tended to prefer, such as spending over saving and luxury over simplicity (the scan is hard to read in places) [S2]. The lesson he drew elsewhere was to promise rewards people truly want [S1].

## His work and record

- Born March 13, 1898, in Hoboken, New Jersey [S4].
- As a night student at Columbia, met Max Sackheim of the mail-order agency Ruthrauff & Ryan, who hired him as a secretary [S4].
- Sackheim helped found the Book-of-the-Month Club in 1926 [S5].
- Became a copywriter and worked on mail-order ads for Dale Carnegie, Charles Atlas and Sherwin Cody's English course [S4].
- With Robert Beatty, ran Schwab and Beatty in New York. A 1958 ad says it was founded about 30 years earlier and that clients had spent over $77 million through it [S1]. Goodreads gives 1926 and a garbled account of how it began (disputed) [S4].
- *How to Write a Good Advertisement* was in print by the early 1940s [S2][S6]. A longer 1962 edition included the headline essay [S3].
- Published "100 Good Advertising Headlines" as a two-page ad in 1958 [S1][S3].
- Wrote a series on split-run ads, "What 92 Split-Run Ads Tell Us", cited in later mail-order books [S6].
- Died March 22, 1980, according to a biographical dictionary of advertising [S6].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Mail order isn't everything.** His proof came from keyed ads that sold by reply [S1]. It fits direct response well, and says less about ads meant to build a brand over years.
- **Fear as a tool.** He counted 21 wholly negative headlines among his best [S1]. Loss and embarrassment appeals work, but they can tip into manipulation if the fear is out of proportion.
- **Headline formulas age.** Phrases such as "Do You Make These Mistakes?" were copied for decades [S1][S3]. Formulas lose force when every rival uses them.
- **Thin biography.** The record of his early career is patchy and partly contradictory [S4][S6]. Don't build claims on those details.

## Blind spots to watch

- He thought in single ads and replies. Pair him with someone who plans whole brands and long campaigns.
- His examples come from print in the 1920s to 1950s. The rules carry over, but numbers and word choices need fresh tests.
- He says little about design beyond what helps reading. A strong picture can do work he didn't measure.
- He focuses on the reader's self-interest. Readers also respond to humour, beauty and shared values.
