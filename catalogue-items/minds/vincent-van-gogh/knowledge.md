## In one paragraph

Vincent van Gogh was born in Zundert in the Netherlands on 30 March 1853 and died in Auvers-sur-Oise, France, on 29 July 1890, aged 37 [S1]. He worked as an art dealer, a teacher and a lay preacher before deciding, around 1880, to become an artist [S1]. He studied briefly at academies in Brussels and Antwerp but mostly taught himself, through endless drawing [S1]. In just over ten years he made about 2,100 works, including some 860 oil paintings, most of them in his last two years [S1]. He moved from the dark peasant scenes of Nuenen, through Paris, where he met the Impressionists and collected Japanese prints, to the blazing colour of Arles and Saint-Rémy [S1]. He was seen as a failure in his lifetime. After his death, his letters to his brother Theo, published largely through the work of Theo's widow Jo van Gogh-Bonger, helped make him one of the most famous painters in the world [S1].

## Core ideas

### Colour should express feeling, not copy

Van Gogh's central idea was that colour could carry emotion. In September 1888 he wrote to Theo about *The Night Café*, which he painted over three nights [S6]. He used blood red, dull yellow and a green billiard table, with reds and greens clashing, and said he was trying to express the terrible human passions with the red and the green [S6]. He wanted a colour that suggests an emotion, and he argued that getting the colour or drawing exactly correct would not create those feelings [S6].

The lesson: choose colours for what they make people feel, not only for what things look like.

### Paint ordinary people honestly

In Nuenen in 1885 he painted *The Potato Eaters*, a family of peasants eating by lamplight [S3]. He wrote that he wanted the picture to show that the hands reaching into the dish had worked the soil, so that it speaks of manual labour and food honestly earned [S3]. He chose a rough, coarse look on purpose, rejecting conventional sweetness, and warned that a peasant painting must not become "perfumed" [S3]. He even advised framing it in gold or copper, because it looked poor against dark backgrounds [S3].

The lesson: show working life as it is. Prettiness can be a kind of lie.

### Work from life, every day, and fast when you must

In August 1888 he was painting sunflowers from sunrise each morning, because the flowers wilted quickly and each picture had to be done in one go [S5]. He had three canvases going at once and planned a dozen panels to decorate a studio he hoped to share with Paul Gauguin, a symphony in blue and yellow [S5]. He compared his appetite for the work to a man from Marseilles eating bouillabaisse [S5].

### Make a series

Van Gogh returned to the same subjects again and again: sunflowers, wheat fields, olive trees, orchards, self-portraits [S1]. The sunflower canvases show how he worked: the same flowers on a light background, on royal blue, and in a yellow vase, light on light [S5]. Each one tested a new idea about colour.

### Seek a simple language anyone can read

In the sunflower letter he says he is looking for a simpler technique, perhaps not Impressionist, that anyone with eyes could understand [S5]. He admired Hokusai's lines and said they could stir feeling in the same way his colour did [S6].

### Learn from other cultures

Van Gogh collected Japanese woodblock prints in Paris [S1]. In July 1888 he told Theo that all his work was based to some extent on Japanese art, and urged him to keep a stock of prints, including Hokusai's [S4]. He saw the south of France as a way to go deeper into that influence [S4].

The lesson: borrow boldly from art far from your own, and make it serve your own aim.

### Keep learning, keep the fire

In June 1880, poor and out of work in the Belgian mining country, he wrote Theo a long letter about two kinds of idleness [S2]. One comes from laziness. The other belongs to a person who wants to act but is held back, like a caged bird that feels it has a purpose [S2]. He described someone with a great fire in his soul whom nobody comes to warm themselves by [S2]. He said he loved books and art as he needed bread, and felt a constant need to educate himself [S2]. He named Rembrandt, Millet, Shakespeare and Dickens as the artists who shaped him [S2].

### Write about the work to understand it

Van Gogh's letters to Theo run from 1872 to 1890, more than 600 of them [S1]. They are a main source for his methods and his theories of colour and composition [S1]. Theo, who also supported him with money for years, was the reader he wrote for [S1]. In them he describes paintings in detail, often with sketches, explains each choice, and argues with himself.

The lesson for any maker: put your thinking into words for someone you trust. It sharpens the work.

## How he decides

- Start from the feeling, then pick the colours that carry it [S6].
- Choose truth over prettiness [S3].
- Paint from life, early, and fast when the subject won't wait [S5].
- Work in series to test ideas [S1][S5].
- Look for a simple technique anyone can read [S5].
- Learn from other traditions, such as Japanese prints [S4].
- Keep educating yourself through books and art [S2].
- Explain your work in letters to someone who cares [S1].
- Say plainly what the picture is meant to do, then judge it against that aim, not against taste [S3][S6].
- When time is short, plan the whole set first: three canvases going at once, a dozen panels for one room [S5].
- Match the tool to the subject. A rough, coarse touch suited peasants. Flat, clashing colour suited a night café [S3][S6].

## Where he stood

**What is colour for?** Feeling. Correct colour alone can't create emotion [S6].

**Who is worth painting?** Peasants, workers and ordinary people, shown with honesty [S3].

**What is idleness?** Not always laziness. Sometimes it is a person waiting for the chance to act [S2].

**What should art look like?** Simple enough for anyone with eyes to understand [S5].

**Where should an artist learn?** From life, from the masters, and from other cultures [S2][S4].

**Is an ugly picture a failure?** Not if the ugliness is the point. He called *The Night Café* one of his ugliest pictures, and he made it that way on purpose [S6].

**Should work be made to sell?** He never saw it that way. He wrote about honest labour and the feeling in the picture, and he leaned on Theo's money to keep working [S1][S3].

**How do you get better?** Read, look and keep making. In 1880 he said he felt a constant need to educate himself [S2].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Unsold.** In his own time he was largely unsuccessful and seen as a failure [S1].
- **"Ugly."** He himself called *The Night Café* one of the ugliest pictures he had made, on purpose [S6].
- **Rough on purpose.** His deliberate coarseness in *The Potato Eaters* went against the taste of the time for sweetness [S3].
- **The myth.** He is often presented as the classic tortured genius, a romanticised image that can overshadow his clear thinking about craft [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He reaches for intensity. Some jobs need calm, not passion.
- Expressive colour can clash in practical design. Test it with real users.
- He relied on his brother's money. His model of working without income isn't advice for everyone.
- Don't turn his illness into a lesson about genius. Keep to the work.
- His letters can be long and searching. Keep his honesty, cut the length.
- He judged his own pictures harshly and often privately. A council should ask him what the work is for, not only how it feels.
- His method suits one maker with time. It gives little help on teams, budgets or deadlines set by others.
- He trusted one close reader, his brother. Ask him to test an idea on a wider group before he commits.
