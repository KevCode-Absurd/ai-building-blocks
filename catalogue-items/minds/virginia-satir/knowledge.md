## In one paragraph

Satir was a family therapy pioneer. Her books include Conjoint Family Therapy, Peoplemaking and The New Peoplemaking. Her teaching emphasizes self-worth, congruence, family rules and growth. ## Core ideas

### Include self, other and context

Satir's congruence model attends to personal experience and relationships within a situation. [S4]

Original everyday application: State your fatigue, acknowledge another person's commitment and describe the time constraint in one scheduling conversation. Do not erase your need to seem kind or erase the other person to seem strong. A congruent statement can be brief and firm. Check that your tone and intended message point in the same direction without policing every gesture.

### Notice coping stances

The model describes placating, blaming, excessive reasonableness and distracting as stress responses. [S4]

Original everyday application: If you agree to everything and complain later, try one honest preference during planning. Use a stance as a description of a moment, not a personality type. Notice what you leave out when under pressure. The next useful move may be to include your own need, the other's experience or the real practical setting.

### Preserve self-worth

Satir's self-esteem writing preserves a person's worth alongside the ability to change behavior. [S2]

Original everyday application: Name and repair a work error without making it a global verdict about your value. You can own an action without agreeing that you are fundamentally defective. This distinction makes a specific correction possible. Ask which behavior needs changing and which resources you already have, rather than demanding a different identity.

### Reveal family rules

Her work examines communication rules and whether they can change. [S5]

Original everyday application: Ask whether an inherited ban on disagreement still serves a family of adults making separate plans. A rule may once have protected order while now preventing honest conversation. State the rule in plain words and ask whose needs it serves. Propose one change small enough for people to understand and review.

### Ask directly

The Five Freedoms includes speaking, feeling and asking more freely. [S1]

Original everyday application: Request an evening of quiet instead of waiting for others to infer your fatigue. Asking does not guarantee receiving. It brings a wish into the relationship where others can answer honestly. Practice hearing a refusal without treating the request itself as a mistake, and look for a workable next option.

### Expect disruption during change

The Satir change model describes familiar order, disturbance and integration. [S4]

Original everyday application: Name temporary confusion after a meeting routine changes and agree when to review it. A drop in comfort can signal adjustment, but it is not proof that the change is good. Gather practical observations and preserve the possibility of revising the experiment. Use the model as a discussion aid rather than a universal timetable.

### Make contact

Her teaching connects awareness and communication with respect for individual differences. [S5]

Original everyday application: Ask what a child was trying to do before correcting rushed work. Start with a person rather than a defect to fix. You can recognize an intention and still identify a real problem with the result. Invite a next attempt that uses an existing strength instead of repeating the child's least useful family role.

## An everyday worked application

In this original family example, everyone says the holiday plan is fine and later complains privately. Write the hidden rule: disagreeing aloud is treated as disloyal. Ask whether that rule still helps. Practice a statement containing your preference, recognition of another's wish and the practical limit. Invite each person to make the same distinction in their own words. Decide one change to the plan, then review how the family handles the first disagreement. Keep the exercise ordinary and voluntary instead of attempting family reconstruction from a chat.

Before trying the example, define what a useful result would look like for the people involved. Separate a change in your own behavior from an outcome requiring someone else's participation. An invitation can be declined; a boundary can be maintained without agreement; a practical obligation may need a direct decision. These are different tasks, so do not judge all of them by whether the conversation feels pleasant immediately.

## Where the method was criticised or needs caution

The sources above primarily present the person's own account and institutional record. They should not be mistaken for independent proof that every application works. The cautions here are editorial limits on this educational adaptation, rather than invented reports of named critics or a claim of scientific consensus.

Experiential language can invite insight and can also outrun evidence. Do not infer a person's childhood, self-worth or hidden family role from a gesture. Do not prescribe touch, group disclosure or specialist interventions. Congruence here is a useful check on a message, not a test that certifies someone as authentic.

## Blind spots to watch

For a team changing routines, make assumptions explicit before naming resistance. Ask which part of the old process provided clarity and which part blocked work. Use the change model to discuss uncertainty and support, not to predict a compulsory stage for every employee. A sensible transition needs practical resources, not only hopeful language.

For a council, pair this lens with someone who checks facts, options and practical constraints. Bring the relevant schedule, commitments or task itself into the discussion. A relationship interpretation can clarify how people speak while leaving a shortage of time, money, space or support untouched. Keep those limits visible and do not turn every material difficulty into a communication failure.
