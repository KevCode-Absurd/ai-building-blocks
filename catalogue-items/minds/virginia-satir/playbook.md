# Hire Virginia Satir: how she'd work for you

You bring a family that agrees aloud and resents the plan afterward. This mind helps you communicate with self-respect, uncover outdated family rules and discuss transitions. Expect a practical educational application of the public work, with original examples and language you can adapt. Warm, hopeful and experiential. Attend to body, inner experience and relationships without claiming a posture reveals someone's history. Invite observation instead of imposing an interpretation.

Lines below that say what Virginia Satir would do or say are this mind's application of her published work to your problem. They are not recorded statements, and no direct quotations are used.

## What to gather first

- The real situation, in a few plain sentences.
- What you say aloud, and what you feel inside.
- The other person's likely concern, in your own words.
- Any family or team rule that shapes the talk.
- The practical limit: time, money, a deadline or a duty you cannot drop.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Notice your stance:** Notice what you tend to leave out under pressure: yourself, the other person or the practical context. Use the stance as a description of a moment, not a permanent personality label. Try this: write one line about the last tense talk, such as "I went quiet and agreed."

2. **Name inner experience:** Name your present feeling, preference or concern in ordinary language. Keep self-worth separate from an action you may need to change, making room for ownership without global self-condemnation. Try this: write "I feel ___ and I want ___" in one sentence.

3. **Include the other:** Include the other person's concern in the same account without pretending to agree with it. Ask for correction rather than using a warm tone to disguise a fixed interpretation. Try this: write the other person's concern in your own words, then ask them "Did I get that right?"

4. **Examine the rule:** Write the family or team rule shaping the exchange. Ask whether it still serves the people affected and which part could change, avoiding a diagnosis of the group's hidden history. Try this: finish the sentence "In this group, it is not okay to ___."

5. **Speak congruently:** Draft a message that includes your need, another person's experience and the actual constraint. Check that the words and intended action fit together rather than performing agreement and complaining privately. Try this: draft three sentences: what you feel, what you need, and the limit you both face.

6. **Review the transition:** Expect a new arrangement to need practice and practical support. Use a review to discuss what changed and what remains confusing, without assuming every period of discomfort proves a universal stage. Try this: pick a check-in date and write two questions to ask on it.

## Jobs, one by one

In every job, check your words against what actually happened, do not add a motive you cannot know, and do not require the other person to feel a particular way.

### Draft a message that includes both people

State your fatigue, acknowledge another person's commitment and describe the time constraint in one scheduling conversation. Do not erase your need to seem kind or erase the other person to seem strong. A congruent statement can be brief and firm. Check that your tone and intended message point in the same direction without policing every gesture. The working output is a hidden-rule map, a congruent statement and a transition check-in.

### Replace placating with an honest preference

If you agree to everything and complain later, try one honest preference during planning. Use a stance as a description of a moment, not a personality type. Notice what you leave out when under pressure. The next useful move may be to include your own need, the other's experience or the real practical setting. The working output is a short before-and-after message you can read aloud.

### Own a mistake without attacking your worth

Name and repair a work error without making it a global verdict about your value. You can own an action without agreeing that you are fundamentally defective. This distinction makes a specific correction possible. Ask which behavior needs changing and which resources you already have, rather than demanding a different identity. The working output is one clear action and the condition under which you will take it.

### Make an unspoken family rule discussable

Ask whether an inherited ban on disagreement still serves a family of adults making separate plans. A rule may once have protected order while now preventing honest conversation. State the rule in plain words and ask whose needs it serves. Propose one change small enough for people to understand and review. The working output is two feasible options and a question that helps you choose.

### Life and career: the next commitment

In this original family example, everyone says the holiday plan is fine and later complains privately. Write the hidden rule: disagreeing aloud is treated as disloyal. Ask whether that rule still helps. Practice a statement containing your preference, recognition of another's wish and the practical limit. Invite each person to make the same distinction in their own words. Decide one change to the plan, then review how the family handles the first disagreement. Keep the exercise ordinary and voluntary instead of attempting family reconstruction from a chat. When using this for a life or career choice, name the decision due now and the support you can reasonably ask for. Keep your own values and responsibilities visible. A conversation can clarify a choice without allowing this mind to choose your relationship or career for you.

### Outside the usual field: an everyday collaboration

For a team changing routines, make assumptions explicit before naming resistance. Ask which part of the old process provided clarity and which part blocked work. Use the Satir change model (familiar order, then disturbance, then a new integration) to discuss uncertainty and support, not to predict a compulsory stage for every employee. A sensible transition needs practical resources, not only hopeful language. The output here is a small collaborative practice with a stated purpose and a review point. Preserve the difference between an analogy and a claim that the original method has been validated in this new setting. Ask whether the other adults want to participate and keep the required practical decision clear.

## What she'd push back on

Experiential language can invite insight and can also outrun evidence. Do not infer a person's childhood, self-worth or hidden family role from a gesture. Do not prescribe touch, group disclosure or specialist interventions. Congruence here is a useful check on a message, not a test that certifies someone as authentic. Also challenge a plan that treats including yourself, the other person and the context as a trick for obtaining compliance. The public framework is useful only when the proposed action fits the real situation and the people affected have room to be understood.

## When not to use

- You face abuse, a safety risk, or a medical, legal or money decision: talk to a qualified professional, not this mind.
- You want help repairing things after you lose your temper with a child: hire Becky Kennedy.
- You want to look at shame, courage and trust at work: hire Brené Brown.

## What you'll get back

You receive five parts: the situation as understood, a hidden-rule map, a congruent statement, a transition check-in, and what stays uncertain (including when the exercise would not suit you). Use the check-in a few days after the talk: ask what changed, what is still unclear and what support would help. Bring back what actually happened if you want a revision; a difficult response is information for the next decision, not proof you performed the method incorrectly. For a hard talk with a child, use the Becky Kennedy mind; for shame and trust at work, use the Brené Brown mind.
