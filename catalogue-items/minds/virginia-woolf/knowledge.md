## In one paragraph

Woolf was an English novelist, essayist and publisher, and one of the great experimental writers of the twentieth century [S1]. Raised in a London home full of books, she became a founding member of the Bloomsbury Group of writers and artists, and with her husband Leonard founded the Hogarth Press, which published much of her work [S1]. In "Modern Fiction" she argued that novels should try to catch life as it reaches the mind, a steady shower of impressions, and that any method is right if it expresses what the writer means [S3]. She put this into practice in *Mrs Dalloway* (1925), which follows a London hostess and a shell-shocked veteran through a single June day [S2]. *A Room of One's Own* (1929) argued that a woman needs money and a room of her own to write fiction, and asked why history had so few women writers [S4]. She was a feminist, a pacifist and an opponent of empire, and also held class and racial prejudices that critics have rightly named [S1].

## Core ideas

### Catch life as it falls on the mind

In "Modern Fiction", Woolf asked writers to look closely at an ordinary mind on an ordinary day [S3]. It receives a constant stream of impressions, trivial and sharp, from every side [S3]. Life, she wrote, is not a set of neatly arranged events but a luminous halo around us from first awareness to last [S3]. The writer's task is to record those impressions in the order they fall, even if the result looks disconnected [S3].

How she applied it: *Mrs Dalloway* moves between minds, mixing description, inner monologue and speech so that the reader drifts through people's thoughts [S2].

### Any method is right

Woolf argued that fiction has no fixed "proper stuff" [S3]. Any method is right if it expresses what the writer wants to express [S3]. The only real failure is falseness [S3].

How she applied it: she later denied she had a deliberate "method" for *Mrs Dalloway*, saying its structure came without conscious direction [S2].

### Against the materialists

She grouped three popular novelists of her time, H. G. Wells, Arnold Bennett and John Galsworthy, as "materialists" [S3]. They wrote with great skill about the body, houses and incomes, but missed the spirit [S3]. She asked whether life lived in their books at all [S3].

How she applied it: she set out her aims in a Cambridge talk later revised as "Mr. Bennett and Mrs. Brown", while she was writing *Mrs Dalloway* [S2].

### Learn freedom from others, not their tone

Woolf admired James Joyce for trying to get closer to life by recording impressions as they come, though she wondered if his method shut out the wider world [S3]. She admired Chekhov's stories for their odd details and open endings, and Russian fiction for its humanity [S3]. Her advice was to learn from their freedom of form without copying their sadness [S3].

How she applied it: critics often read *Mrs Dalloway* as a response to Joyce's *Ulysses* [S2].

### One day can hold a life

*Mrs Dalloway* takes place on one day in the middle of June 1923, with memories folding back into the past [S2]. Big Ben chimes the half-hours, so the reader feels time passing [S2]. A party is the frame, but the subject is memory, war, illness and mortality [S2].

How she applied it: the novel grew from a short story in which Clarissa Dalloway walks through London to buy gloves, and the small errand opens up her past and her view of others [S2][S5].

### Money and a room of one's own

The central claim of her 1929 essay is that a woman must have money and a room of her own to write fiction [S1][S4]. She built the argument as a story: a narrator is turned off the grass and out of the library at an old university, then compares a rich men's college lunch with a plain dinner at a women's college [S4]. The difference, she shows, comes from centuries of money flowing to men [S4].

How she applied it: her narrator says a legacy of five hundred pounds a year freed her from fear and bitterness, so she could judge books on their own terms [S4].

### Judith Shakespeare

To show why there were no female Shakespeares, Woolf imagined that Shakespeare had a sister, Judith, as gifted as he was [S4]. Denied school and freedom, she is thwarted at every turn and dies young [S4]. The point is that genius needs conditions, and women were denied them [S4].

How she applied it: she noted that women blaze through the poets' works as characters while being almost absent from history's records [S4].

### Write without anger, find your own sentence

Woolf argued that the common sentence of nineteenth-century prose was built by men and didn't fit women [S4]. Jane Austen made a natural sentence of her own and wrote without hate or fear, which Woolf counted her great achievement [S4]. In Charlotte Brontë she saw anger at confinement bending the book out of shape [S4]. She also praised a new novel's plain line that "Chloe liked Olivia", because fiction had rarely shown friendship between women outside their ties to men [S4].

How she applied it: she argued that a great mind is androgynous, joining male and female sides, and that it is fatal for a writer to think of their sex while writing [S4].

### Read as a common reader

Woolf built *The Common Reader* around a reader who reads for their own pleasure, not to teach or to correct the opinions of others [S3].

How she applied it: the book's essays range from Chaucer and Montaigne to Jane Austen and Conrad, written for general readers rather than scholars [S3].

**The writer needs a patron: know who you write for**

In "The Patron and the Crocus" she argued that the usual advice to young writers, to write as shortly and clearly as possible and think of nothing else, leaves out the one thing needed: choose your patron wisely [S3]. A book is always written for somebody. Her image was a crocus seen in a London park: it is "an imperfect crocus until it has been shared", so you cannot simply ignore readers [S3]. But writers who despise the public (she named Butler, Meredith and Henry James) grow awkward and obscure, and newspaper writing, however skilful, fades when it is put in a book [S3]. The ideal reader, she said, makes a single real crocus feel important and does not want to be lectured or improved [S3].

**Tell the truth about yourself (Montaigne)**

She admired Montaigne for drawing his own soul with a pen, with all its confusion and variety. She noted two hard things: the difficulty of expression, because thoughts escape before you can catch them, and the harder difficulty of being yourself, because the inner voice often says the opposite of what other people say [S3]. He refused to teach or preach, and kept saying he was like other people [S3].

**The essay's one duty is pleasure**

In "The Modern Essay" she said the essay should give pleasure, cast a spell from the first word, and never rouse the reader. Learning must be so fused into the writing that no fact juts out; she faulted writers who served up research raw, and those who scold [S3].

**Trust your own judgement on new work**

In "How It Strikes a Contemporary" she pointed out that two critics will give opposite verdicts on a book that is two months old, though they agree on Milton. Her answer was to follow your own instincts and check them by reading the masterpieces again and again [S3].

**The common reader**

Her opening essay defines the common reader as one who reads for pleasure, not to teach or to correct others, and who builds out of odds and ends some kind of whole: a portrait, a sketch of an age, a theory of writing [S3].

## Real decisions

### Choosing a story about gloves
She began *Mrs Dalloway* as a short story, "Mrs Dalloway in Bond Street" (1923), in which a woman sets out to buy gloves. Big Ben strikes eleven and the errand quickly fills with memory and the thoughts of strangers [S5]. She then grew it into the novel [S2].

### Founding her own press
With Leonard she founded the Hogarth Press, which published much of her work, so she did not have to answer to a publisher's taste [S1].

### Inventing a narrator
For *A Room of One's Own* she admitted at the start that she could not answer "women and fiction" outright. She offered a narrator, an invented college and an invented sister for Shakespeare, and said her "truth" would mix with fiction and prejudice, so listeners should judge for themselves [S4].

### Treating anger as evidence
In the British Museum she read books by men about women, found they contradicted each other, and decided they had been written in the heat of emotion. She read the men's anger as a clue and moved from resentment to pity [S4].

### Taking the legacy
Her narrator's five hundred pounds a year from an aunt took away fear and bitterness and let her judge books on their own terms [S4].

## How she decides

- Look at an ordinary mind on an ordinary day [S3].
- Record impressions as they fall, not as plot demands [S3].
- Use any method, as long as it's true to what you mean [S3].
- Ask what the work leaves out of life, not only what it puts in [S3].
- Ask who had the money, time and room to write, and who didn't [S4].
- Write without letting anger bend the work [S4].
- Let a small errand or a single day carry the weight [S2][S5].
- Choose your reader before you write, and write for a patron who treats your one crocus as important [S3].
- Fuse the research so no fact juts out [S3].
- Check your instincts against the old masterpieces, not the latest critic [S3].

## Where she stood

**What should fiction do?** Get closer to life as it is felt, even at the cost of tidy plots [S3].

**Why so few great women writers?** Lack of money, education, privacy and freedom, not lack of talent [S4].

**War and empire?** She was described as anti-colonialist, anti-imperialist, anti-militarist and pacifist [S1]. *Three Guineas* (1938) looked at the roots of fascism in society [S1].

**Was she a snob?** By her own account, yes. In a 1936 talk titled "Am I a Snob?" she examined her privilege and concluded that she was [S1].

## Her work and record

- Moved to Bloomsbury after her father's death in 1904. A founder of the Bloomsbury Group [S1].
- Married Leonard Woolf in 1912. Together they founded the Hogarth Press [S1].

## Where she was wrong or criticised

- **Class contempt.** Her biographer Hermione Lee cites diary entries that many readers find offensive, including a 1920 remark that the lower classes were detestable [S1].
- **Antisemitism.** She often gave Jewish characters negative stereotypes, and wrote disparaging remarks about Jewish people in her diary. A magazine asked her to soften a story it found hateful toward Jews, and she reluctantly did [S1].
- **Race and disability.** Her writing includes racist language and prejudice against disabled people [S1].
- **Elitism.** Some postcolonial feminist critics have dismissed her and other modernists as privileged and elitist [S1].
- **Both things are true.** She was also a feminist and a pacifist who published work against colonialism and fascism. Scholars debate how to weigh these sides [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- Her case for money and a room assumes a middle-class writer. She says less about women with no inheritance at all [S1][S4].
- Inner-life writing can lose plot and pace. Some readers need a clearer story.
- Her private views on class, race and Jewish people were prejudiced. Don't reproduce them [S1].
- Her style is for literary prose. It can blur plain business writing.
- Pair her with a plain-style editor, or a storyteller who loves plot.
