## In one paragraph

Disney grew up in Missouri, moved to Hollywood in 1923 and started a studio with his brother Roy [S2]. In 1928 he and Ub Iwerks created Mickey Mouse, and *Steamboat Willie* used synchronised sound [S2]. He pushed animation forward with colour, a separate story department, art classes for his animators and the multiplane camera for depth [S2]. He bet the studio on *Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs*, which insiders mocked as "Disney's Folly" and which became the top film of 1938 [S5]. In the 1950s he moved into television and theme parks, opening Disneyland in July 1955 [S2][S4]. He spent his last years planning EPCOT, a city of the future in Florida [S6]. He won 22 Academy Awards from 59 nominations, both records [S2]. He was also hard on his staff, fought a union in 1941, and his studio's films contain racial stereotypes that critics have long pointed out [S2][S3].

## Core ideas

### Story first, and characters with personality

Disney believed a character only becomes believable once it has a personality [S1]. He wanted his stories to carry a point of view and a worthwhile theme, without an obvious moral [S1]. He set up a story department, with storyboard artists separate from the animators, so story got its own craft [S2].

How he applied it: on *Snow White*, story meetings began in October 1934, and he reportedly acted out the whole story for his staff in about three hours [S5]. The team gave each dwarf a name and a personality, added Dopey later, and cut dwarf scenes that slowed the story down [S5].

### Train people to a higher craft

Disney had high standards and high expectations of the people around him [S2]. He sent his animators to art classes, starting in 1932, and had them study live models and animals to learn how things really move [S2][S5].

How he applied it: that training gave the studio the skills to make its first feature film [S5].

**Study real movement, then exaggerate it.** In a 1935 memo on training young animators, Disney wrote that the studio should study real movement closely and use it as the base for fantasy and exaggeration, not copy it [S10]. He wanted animators to learn walking, lifting and bending through a comic, caricatured lens, and to let the whole body carry the emotion, with face and costume adding to a sound underlying action [S10]. He wrote that comedy has to make contact with the audience, and that it should rest on things the audience already knows [S10]. He called the result a caricature of life and action [S10]. How he applied it: he planned a training course so animators learn together, and gave a four-question way to review a scene: what idea was meant, how it was shown, what came of it, and what could be better [S10].

### Use new technology to tell the story better

Disney kept adopting new tools when they made the audience feel more. *Steamboat Willie* in 1928 was an early cartoon with synchronised sound [S2]. *Flowers and Trees* in 1932 won the first Academy Award for a cartoon short [S2]. The studio developed the multiplane camera to give flat drawings depth [S2].

How he applied it: each step, from sound to colour to depth, was used to make the story more real, not to show off [S2].

**Do what the medium does best.** Asked about his rule for picking a picture, Disney told DeMille not to do what another medium can do better, so he avoids stories that work better in live action [S11]. He joked that the studio would never do Hamlet, and he did not take a bet when DeMille pressed him on drawing human figures [S11]. He steered the talk back to what animation can and cannot do, and he tried to keep daydreams about the far future of the medium from crowding out the problems in front of him [S11].

### Bet big on what you believe in

Disney's lines on money stress doing good work first. A biography quotes him telling people to do a good job and let the money take care of itself [S1]. Another collection quotes his complaint that dreams make poor collateral for a loan [S1].

How he applied it: *Snow White* was budgeted at $250,000 in 1934 and cost about $1.5 million. Disney mortgaged his house, and halfway through he needed a $250,000 loan. A Bank of America executive approved it after watching a rough cut [S5]. The film opened in December 1937 and became the highest-earning film of 1938 [S5]. The next films, *Pinocchio* and *Fantasia* in 1940, lost money and left the studio in debt [S2].

### Make a place for the whole family

Disney got the idea for Disneyland while watching his daughters on a merry-go-round. He wanted a place where parents and children could have fun together [S4]. He is quoted as saying that all he wanted was to give the public good entertainment [S1], and that Disneyland was a work of love, not just a way to make money [S1].

How he applied it: the Disneyland dedication on 17 July 1955 welcomed everyone who came to "this happy place" and called the park their land [S4].

### Never finished

Disney is quoted as saying Disneyland would never be completed, and would keep growing as long as there was imagination left in the world [S1]. He used the same idea for EPCOT, which he said would never be completed and would keep testing new materials and systems [S6].

How he applied it: he described EPCOT as "a living blueprint of the future" in a film shot on 27 October 1966, two months before his death [S6].

### Find a new way to fund the dream

When bankers doubted, Disney found other routes. To pay for Disneyland, he created the *Disneyland* TV show for the young ABC network, which agreed to help finance the park [S4]. His team, WED Enterprises, later became Walt Disney Imagineering [S4].

In 1965 he told his cast that he wished he had bought more land around Disneyland at the start, because it would have given the company more control [S7].

How he applied it: for the Florida project, he bought land quietly through shell companies to keep prices down, about 27,433 acres by June 1965 [S6].

### Learn from the setbacks

A 1957 book quotes Disney saying that a kick in the teeth may turn out to be the best thing for you [S1]. He described Mickey Mouse as a symbol of independence, born when the studio needed a new character and freeing it from immediate worry [S1].

In 1965 he joked to the cast about scrambling to finish the landscaping after opening, and called the park's early stage a rehearsal, not a finished product [S7].

How he applied it: Disneyland's first public day was hot, crowded and full of breakdowns, and was nicknamed "Black Sunday" [S4]. The park kept changing and growing after it [S4].

### Show people the dream before it exists

Disney sold his biggest projects by letting people see them first. The weekly *Disneyland* TV show on ABC let viewers watch the park take shape and helped pay for it [S4]. For EPCOT, he filmed a 25-minute presentation that walked lawmakers and companies through the planned city, with its central core, green belts, and monorail and PeopleMover links instead of cars [S6].

How he applied it: the EPCOT film was shown to Florida legislators in February 1967, after his death, to win their support [S6]. The city itself was never built. The idea returned as a theme park in 1982 [S6].

## Real decisions

### Pushing for a park when the networks wanted a show

In 1965 he recalled that the networks wanted only a TV show, not a park, and that he pushed for the park anyway. When bankers pushed back, he found other sources of money [S7]. The ABC deal in the record above is the best-known answer [S4].

### Betting the studio on one feature

He mortgaged his house and borrowed again partway through *Snow White* rather than cut the film down [S5]. The bet paid off in 1938, and the next two films nearly undid it [S2][S5].

### Money as a constant squeeze

In 1965 he described steady money pressure at the park and the need to keep expanding to cope with attendance, with a pipeline of new areas always ahead [S7]. He did not treat the first version as the last [S7].

### Worrying about a cheap look

He worried the park might look cheap or generic. He told the cast the park was still a rehearsal [S7]. The same worry sits behind his habit of training staff to a higher craft [S2][S5].

### Refusing to sign over his artists without a vote

In 1947 he told Congress the studio never had labor trouble of its own, and that his artists did not want to join the union led by Herbert Sorrell. He said they asked for an election that never happened. He said he kept producing during the strike and would not hand his artists to a union without a vote, which he framed as a matter of principle [S8]. Critics, and the strikers' record of unfair pay and credit, tell another side [S3].

### Thanking the cast by name

At the 1965 party he thanked people by name, credited the whole team and thanked the band, so employees felt like insiders in a shared project [S7]. Staff accounts say he rarely gave direct praise [S2].

## Where he stood

**What is entertainment for?** To give the public a good time, with a worthwhile theme [S1].

**Who is the audience?** Parents and children together [S4].

**Money or the work?** The work. He believed money follows good work [S1].

**What comes first in a film?** Story and personality [S1][S5].

**How should a park or a city grow?** It should never be finished [S1][S6].

**What did he think of unions?** He opposed the 1941 strike and blamed it on communist agitation [S2][S3].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **The 1941 strike.** Animators struck over uneven pay and the lack of screen credit, and after Disney fired union members. He refused to recognise the union and later never forgave the strikers [S3]. The studio settled with equal pay and a grievance process [S3].
- **HUAC.** In 1947 he named union organisers as communist agitators before Congress [S2].
- **Race.** Critics, including the NAACP, faulted *Song of the South* (1946) for Black stereotypes. Critics also cite the crows in *Dumbo* and the depiction of American Indians in *Peter Pan* [S2].
- **Antisemitism claims.** Some early cartoons used Jewish stereotypes, and he gave Leni Riefenstahl a studio tour in 1938 [S2]. Many who worked with him denied he was antisemitic, and a Jewish group named him Man of the Year in 1955. His biographer Neal Gabler concluded he wasn't antisemitic himself but allied with people who were [S2].
- **Praise.** Staff said he rarely gave direct praise [S2].

## Blind spots to watch

- His belief in the vision could flatten the people doing the work. Pair him with a voice on fair pay and credit [S3].
- A big bet like *Snow White* can sink you. *Pinocchio* and *Fantasia* nearly did [S2].
- "Family" in his films often meant one narrow picture of America. Check who is left out or stereotyped [S2].
- He took credit easily. Make sure the team's names are on the work [S3].
