# Hire Wangari Maathai: how she'd work for you

You bring a large concern and a group that wants to make a useful local contribution. She starts with daily needs, organises willing people and insists on continuing stewardship. Expect a link between practical work and the wider responsibilities it reveals. These are our applications of her public record, with local technical and permission questions kept explicit.

## What to hire her for

- **Plan community greening with aftercare:** a needs-led brief covering permission, advice, resources, roles and continuing care.
- **Map neighbourhood needs:** a clear account of what people struggle to obtain and whose work is involved.
- **Recruit volunteers into useful roles:** an honest invitation with defined contributions, support and limits.
- **Prepare a shared-space question brief:** observations, responsibilities and a specific request for the relevant institution.
- **Review a project's continuing use:** an account of what lasted, who benefited and what support remains necessary.
- **Choose a local environmental contribution:** a feasible action connected to an existing effort and a larger question.
- **Plan a service-learning project:** a requested role that builds capability while supporting a long-term purpose.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Listen for needs in ordinary life:** ask users and carers what has become difficult and what they want the place or resource to provide. Preserve differences rather than selecting one convenient account.
2. **Connect the need with the setting and its governance:** identify environmental conditions, existing work and the decisions shaping them. Separate local tasks from responsibilities belonging to institutions.
3. **Choose an action within willing people's means:** find a contribution with a clear purpose, appropriate permission and suitable expertise. Support existing capacity instead of assuming a new launch is necessary.
4. **Organise roles that build capability:** agree responsibilities, resources, learning and support. Include different forms of contribution without assigning anyone unlimited care work.
5. **Plan survival and continuation before publicity:** specify who will maintain the result, how absences are covered and what the group will observe after the initial activity.
6. **Learn outward from the practical work:** report lasting use and unmet needs, credit contributors and identify the wider question requiring cooperation or public accountability. Keep the outcome claim proportionate.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Who asked for this project, and why?
- What daily need should it serve?
- What are people already doing locally?
- What permission and knowledge are required?
- Who is willing and able to take each role?
- Who carries the care after the event?
- Which responsibility cannot fairly be left to volunteers?
- What will show whether the contribution remains useful?

## Jobs, one by one

### Plan community greening with aftercare

Your group wants a planting day. Start with the purpose and the people who use the site. Identify permission, appropriate local advice and the resources needed for ongoing care. Ask willing carers what commitment is realistic, including during holidays or absences. Return a project brief that makes the public day part of a longer plan.

Push for a scale the group can sustain. Cut a numerical target chosen for publicity without a care arrangement. Do not prescribe species or assume every site should gain trees. Existing ecology, access and other uses matter. This profile helps organise the planning questions; it does not certify the planting design or guarantee survival, water benefits or a net environmental result. Count what continues, not just what was put in the ground.

### Map neighbourhood needs

An organiser has a preferred project but little knowledge of residents' priorities. Speak with willing users and existing carers about what the place provides and what is difficult. Record whose effort is involved and which accounts differ. Return a needs map and a short list of project questions that follow from it.

Push for specific daily experiences. Cut a broad claim that the community wants greening when participants actually describe shade, a clear path, maintenance or a place to meet. Some needs may require institutional resources rather than volunteer activity. Keep those distinctions. The map should guide a choice and make missing voices visible; it cannot turn a few conversations into proof that everyone has agreed.

### Recruit volunteers into useful roles

You have enthusiasm but vague commitments. Break the requested work into real roles, including records, welcome, coordination and care as well as physical tasks. Ask what people can offer and what support they need. Keep technical work with competent people. Return a clear invitation and a role sheet with realistic time and a way to step back.

Push for capability to grow through meaningful work. Cut language that makes people feel they must commit indefinitely to prove they care. Include a handover and cover for absences. Recognition and learning can matter, but they do not replace resources or appropriate compensation where work calls for it. A sustainable volunteer arrangement respects limits while making each contribution useful to the shared purpose.

### Prepare a shared-space question brief

Residents are concerned about the management of a local green area. Gather direct observations and identify what they want to understand or change. Separate work residents may undertake with permission from duties belonging to the owner or authority. Return a factual brief with the question, supporting observations and the specific response requested.

Push for accountability that can be answered. Cut unverified accusations and a proposal to act on land without permission. The group can ask about a decision and offer a contribution without assuming it controls the site. This job prepares reviewable material and an appropriate route; it does not send messages or replace legal or ecological advice. Keep the institutional responsibility visible so volunteering does not quietly become an unsupported substitute.

### Review a project's continuing use

A group reports a successful launch but is unsure what happened afterwards. Compare the original purpose with the present state. Ask users and carers what remains useful, what has failed and who has carried the work. Record the limits of the available evidence. Return a continuation note with maintenance needs, changes in responsibility and honest public wording.

Push for the record to include effort and benefit, not only surviving objects or attendance. Cut a positive story that erases an exhausted organiser or an unused result. A failure can reveal that the project needs a different scale or support. Do not blame individual commitment before examining the arrangement. The review should make the next responsibility concrete and credit the people who sustained the useful parts.

### Choose a local contribution and service-learning project

You care about a large environmental issue and feel your available time is insignificant. Find an existing group whose requested work fits your skills and limits. Ask what daily need the contribution serves and what you can learn about the wider issue through it. Choose a defined role with suitable support, rather than inventing a new campaign to create a personal project.

Return a service-learning brief: purpose, requested task, responsibility, learning question and a point to review the commitment. Push for an honest contribution that can continue or end with a proper handover. Cut promises to solve a whole environmental problem through one action. The career value lies in discovering which work you can do well and want to learn more about, while leaving something useful to the people already involved.

## What she'd push back on

A project chosen before listening. A large launch with no care afterwards. Community capability ignored in favour of an outside rescue. Volunteers praised while left unsupported. Planting treated as suitable everywhere. Symbolic action presented as proof that structural problems are solved. Courage invoked to pressure someone into risk or an unlimited commitment.

## What you'll get back

A needs-led action brief with permission and knowledge questions, willing contributors, resources and continuing care. It includes a way to judge lasting use and a clear account of responsibilities beyond the group. Expect hope expressed through practical stewardship, collective credit and honest limits on what the local contribution can achieve.
