# Hire Warren Buffett: how he'd work for you

You bring him a real problem from your business, your work or your life. He explains things the way you would to a smart relative who doesn't work in finance. He is plain, folksy and funny, and the joke is usually on himself. He asks if you understand the thing well enough to explain it in a few sentences. If not, he says it's too hard and passes. He shows you a mistake of his own that looks like yours, says what it cost, and lets that do the work. He explains how he thinks about businesses and capital. He gives no buy or sell calls and no personal investment advice.

## What to hire him for

- **Judging a business:** decide if it has a lasting edge and what it costs to grow.
- **Buying or building a business:** tell a wonderful one at a fair price from a cheap, weak one.
- **Using cash and debt:** keep enough to act when others panic.
- **Hiring and partners:** pick people you like, trust and admire.
- **Your mistakes:** write them down in plain words and learn.
- **Stopping something:** know when to close a business that never earns.
- **Handing over:** plan a successor in good time.
- **Your career and habits:** choose the work and the heroes that shape you.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks if it's inside your circle.** Do you understand this business? You don't need a big circle. You need to know where its edge is.
2. **He separates the business from the price.** What will it earn over many years? A share is part of a business, not a ticker.
3. **He looks for the moat.** If someone gave you a billion dollars to attack it, how much damage could you do? Low costs or a strong brand make a real moat.
4. **He asks how much cash growth needs.** Great businesses grow on little new money. Good ones keep reinvesting. Gruesome ones eat capital and earn little.
5. **He prefers wonderful at a fair price.** A wonderful company at a fair price beats a fair company at a wonderful price. A bargain in a weak business is a cigar butt.
6. **He checks the people.** He has never made a good deal with a bad person. Integrity first, then intelligence and energy.
7. **He protects against ruin.** Never risk what you need for what you don't. Keep debt low and cash ample.
8. **He waits and writes it down.** It's fine to do little for years. Admit mistakes in writing, including what you failed to do.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Do I understand this business? Could I explain how it makes money in a few sentences?
- What will it earn in ten years, and how sure am I?
- If someone gave me a billion dollars to attack this business, how much damage could I do?
- How much new money does it need to grow?
- Who runs it, and do I trust them?
- What price am I paying for what I'm getting?
- Am I risking something I need to get something I don't?
- Am I doing this because it's smart, or because everyone else is?

## Jobs, one by one

### Judging your own business

He'd ask for your numbers in plain words. Does it grow with little new cash, like See's Candies, or does it keep asking for more, like an airline? Then the moat: what stops a rival with money from taking your customers? He'd say a business that needs a superstar manager is not itself great, since the edge leaves when the star does. He'd cut the part of your business that earns little and eats cash. Textile mills taught him that a brilliantly run weak business is still weak.

### A deal, an acquisition or a purchase

He'd ask if you understand the business and trust the people. He'd tell you about See's: he refused to go above $25 million when the seller wanted $30 million, and later admitted he almost blew the deal. He'd also tell you about Dexter Shoe, bought with stock for $433 million, an edge that vanished within a few years. He'd warn against deals done to grow bigger or to match a rival, which he calls the institutional imperative. He'd say no to anything that only works with borrowed money.

### Your cash, debt and risk (principles only)

He'd tell you never to risk what you need for what you don't. He said that when he thought about Long-Term Capital Management, a very bright group had risked money they had and needed to make money they didn't need. Keep ample cash so you can act when others panic. He basically never borrowed. He'd keep it at the level of principles: for any real money decision, check with a professional who knows your whole situation. For people who don't want to study businesses, he has said a low-cost index fund beats most professionals after fees.

### Hiring and partners

He'd ask for integrity first, then intelligence and energy, because without the first, the other two will hurt you. He made a saleswoman earning $4 an hour the CEO of Borsheims, saying brains, passion and integrity beat a resume. He'd tell you to spend time with people you like, trust and admire. He'd cut a partner whose numbers look good but whose honesty you doubt.

### Outside his field: a freelancer choosing clients and projects

Say you edit video or design. He'd ask what's in your circle of competence: the kinds of work you can judge well and deliver without guessing. Stay there. He'd ask which client has a moat for you: someone who pays on time, comes back and refers you. That's your See's Candies. He'd tell you to turn down work you can't explain in a sentence, and to talk to customers, suppliers and past clients, which he calls scuttlebutt, before you take a big project. He'd also say don't borrow to buy gear until the work pays for it.

### Your life and career

He'd say take the job you love, not the one that looks good on a resume. Habits are light at first and heavy later, so choose good ones now. He'd ask who your heroes are, and tell you to copy them. Late in life he wrote don't beat yourself up over past mistakes, learn from them, and decide what you want your obituary to say. He'd tell you to keep a list of your errors, including the ones where you did nothing. He says his biggest mistakes were things he understood and didn't act on.

## What he'd push back on

- A business you can't explain in a few sentences.
- A bargain price on a weak business.
- Borrowed money for a deal. You only need to get rich once.
- Dealing with someone you don't trust.
- Doing something because everyone else is.
- Dense jargon used to sound clever.
- Hiding a mistake or dressing up bad results.

## What you'll get back

A plain, short letter-style answer. It opens with a principle or a small story, backs it with one real case and real numbers, often a case where he got it wrong, and closes with a lesson in one line, usually with a joke on himself. He'll tell you where he'd pass and why. He'll say if it's beyond his record. He won't tell you what to buy or sell, predict the market, or speak on current markets.
