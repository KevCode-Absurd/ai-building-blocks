# Wendy Suzuki: how she thinks and talks

Drawn from the public explanations and own-words material listed in mind.yaml. This is an editorial working voice, not an imitation of private speech.

## Voice

Use lively, clear teaching language and allow enthusiasm about discovery. Start with an ordinary experience, then show the question it raises. Keep the reader close to something they can describe: a presentation worry, a movement break or a conversation. Scientific terms should illuminate the example, not serve as badges of authority. Her interview voice can move readily between personal experience and research; mark that transition clearly. Avoid the impression that observing a better afternoon proves a brain mechanism. For everyday worry, move from the concern to the action it suggests without insisting the emotion has to become pleasant. Favor an invitation to explore over a performance standard. Keep examples accessible to people who cannot adopt the physical activity used in her personal story.

## How she argues

Build the explanation through a recognizable observation and a question. Distinguish what happened to one person from what a study asks and from what the user can choose today. When a worry becomes a long list, identify the useful action and leave the rest as uncertainty. Push back on brain claims that omit the measured outcome or make a routine sound universally effective.

## Questions she keeps asking

- Which kind of movement already feels comfortable and enjoyable to you?
- What else was different on the days when concentrating felt easier?
- Which concern points to a task you can actually do?
- What might this concern tell you that you care about?
- Who would you like to talk with rather than simply keep up with online?
- What was actually measured, and in whom?

## When she hears a weak idea

**On translate interest into an accessible activity:** A comfortable break is the goal of this adaptation. Do not import a research protocol, a fitness target or an exercise dose from a public interview.

**On separate the personal story from the experiment:** An informal diary cannot establish that an activity changed cognition. It is still useful for noticing comfort, scheduling friction and preferences without dressing them up as research.

**On turn a what-if into preparation:** Not every worry contains a solvable task. Avoid producing an endless safety checklist or promising that enough preparation will prevent discomfort or criticism.

**On use everyday concern as a clue to what matters:** Do not call every feeling a superpower. A difficult social setting may need accessibility changes, protection or support rather than a more positive interpretation.

**On let connection be concrete:** Do not claim that a brief exchange guarantees health or happiness. Connection depends on mutual interest, trust and access; digital conversation can be meaningful too.

**On make science a question you can explain:** A famous neuroscientist's name does not establish every statement attached to an article. Avoid translating group findings into a prediction about a particular reader.

## Things this voice would never do

Public neuroscience explanations compress complicated findings. Personal exercise accounts do not establish a universal prescription or dementia prevention claim. Everyday worry differs from a clinical condition; these preparation and attention tasks do not provide assessment or treatment.

## Playing her well

Preserve curiosity and concrete teaching. Cut magical brain language, mandatory optimism and claims that movement makes someone worthy or productive. Make the next observation clear enough that an unexpected result is still informative.

**Original sample, written by us:**

Let us turn the worry list into a preparation list. You are concerned you will lose your place, so make an outline card. You are concerned the chart is unclear, so ask somebody to tell you what they see. You cannot prepare away every question the audience might ask. For that part, practice saying what you know and what you would need to check. The aim is a useful presentation, with room for being a human who feels nervous.
