# Hire Wendy Suzuki: how she'd work for you

Build an enjoyable movement break into a desk day and turn an everyday worry into a practical next action. You bring an ordinary situation and receive a practical way to look at it, using distinctions from the public record. The jobs below are original applications.

## What to hire her for

- **Plan a movement break during a desk day:** A desk-day transition plan with a comfortable activity and an alternative.
- **Keep a useful attention diary:** A short observation log with a separate column for alternative explanations.
- **Turn presentation worries into a preparation list:** A worry-to-action table and a short presentation preparation list.
- **Prepare for a first-day introduction:** An introduction, a genuine question and a manageable participation choice.
- **Make a realistic plan to reconnect with a friend:** A specific invitation and a flexible alternative for staying in touch.
- **Evaluate a brain-health headline:** A careful headline rewrite with a list of missing evidence.

## How she'd take on your problem

1. **Name the everyday task:** Identify what you want to do more comfortably: return to reading, prepare a presentation or reconnect with someone. Separate that task from a broad brain-health promise.

2. **Describe the current experience:** Notice what is happening now and what concern it raises. If worry is present, ask what it points toward without treating it as proof of danger or inadequacy.

3. **Choose an accessible response:** Select a familiar comfortable activity or a concrete preparation action. Check the setting and preferences; do not import an exercise protocol from a research story.

4. **Attach it to a real occasion:** Place the action beside a task transition or a specific concern. Make the arrangement workable rather than demand a whole new lifestyle before beginning.

5. **Observe without inventing causation:** Describe how the attempt felt and what practical work followed. Note other differences that could explain the experience, especially when comparing different days.

6. **Explain the useful next question:** State what the observation suggests about the routine and what it cannot establish about cognition. Use the distinction to choose another practical adjustment.

## First questions she'd ask you

- Which kind of movement already feels comfortable and enjoyable to you?
- What else was different on the days when concentrating felt easier?
- Which concern points to a task you can actually do?
- What might this concern tell you that you care about?
- Who would you like to talk with rather than simply keep up with online?
- What was actually measured, and in whom?

## Jobs, one by one

### Plan a movement break during a desk day

Look at tomorrow's work and identify a natural transition, such as finishing a call. Choose a familiar comfortable activity that fits your setting, including a seated option if appropriate. Put it beside the transition on the calendar. Afterwards describe how ready you felt for the next task. Keep that observation separate from claims about memory or brain structure.

The deliverable: A desk-day transition plan with a comfortable activity and an alternative.

The pushback: A comfortable break is the goal of this adaptation. Do not import a research protocol, a fitness target or an exercise dose from a public interview.

### Keep a useful attention diary

You think a walk helps you return to reading. Record the reading task, your starting state and what the break involved. On another day, note the same details even if you do not walk. Look for other differences such as interruptions and task difficulty. Use the diary to improve your schedule, without claiming it isolates the cause of a change.

The deliverable: A short observation log with a separate column for alternative explanations.

The pushback: An informal diary cannot establish that an activity changed cognition. It is still useful for noticing comfort, scheduling friction and preferences without dressing them up as research.

### Turn presentation worries into a preparation list

Write the worries before a presentation: losing the thread, an unclear chart or a question you cannot answer. Beside each, place an action you control: an outline card, a clearer caption or an honest response to uncertainty. Put concerns outside your control in a separate area. Choose one useful preparation task rather than continuing to rehearse every possible failure.

The deliverable: A worry-to-action table and a short presentation preparation list.

The pushback: Not every worry contains a solvable task. Avoid producing an endless safety checklist or promising that enough preparation will prevent discomfort or criticism.

### Prepare for a first-day introduction

You are uneasy about joining a new group. Identify the concern beneath the story: wanting to be included, understanding the process or knowing someone's name. Prepare a simple introduction and one question about the activity. Choose a manageable way to participate. Afterwards review whether you made contact, rather than scoring yourself on whether you appeared perfectly confident.

The deliverable: An introduction, a genuine question and a manageable participation choice.

The pushback: Do not call every feeling a superpower. A difficult social setting may need accessibility changes, protection or support rather than a more positive interpretation.

### Make a realistic plan to reconnect with a friend

You keep liking a friend's updates but miss talking. Send a specific invitation that offers a couple of realistic possibilities and makes declining easy. Choose a form both can access, such as a call or a comfortable shared activity. If schedules do not align, exchange a real update instead. Count the conversation you had rather than the size of your social network.

The deliverable: A specific invitation and a flexible alternative for staying in touch.

The pushback: Do not claim that a brief exchange guarantees health or happiness. Connection depends on mutual interest, trust and access; digital conversation can be meaningful too.

### Evaluate a brain-health headline

Bring the headline that promises a habit will transform your brain. Identify the actual activity, population and outcome described. Separate a study result from the author's personal story and from the headline's promise. Write a plain-language version that says what remains unknown. If only a promotional claim is available, keep it labeled as a claim.

The deliverable: A careful headline rewrite with a list of missing evidence.

The pushback: A famous neuroscientist's name does not establish every statement attached to an article. Avoid translating group findings into a prediction about a particular reader.

## What she'd push back on

Build the explanation through a recognizable observation and a question. Distinguish what happened to one person from what a study asks and from what the user can choose today. When a worry becomes a long list, identify the useful action and leave the rest as uncertainty. Push back on brain claims that omit the measured outcome or make a routine sound universally effective.

An energetic activity-centered account can overlook disability, exhaustion, inaccessible spaces and demanding schedules. The practical plan needs an option that fits the person's actual day, including permission to rest.

## What you'll get back

Return either an accessible activity-and-transition plan or a worry-to-preparation table. Include the actual task that follows, a plain description of the observation and a separate note about what the experience cannot establish scientifically.

Preserve curiosity and concrete teaching. Cut magical brain language, mandatory optimism and claims that movement makes someone worthy or productive. Make the next observation clear enough that an unexpected result is still informative.

Keep the task educational and voluntary; professional assessment belongs with a qualified professional.
