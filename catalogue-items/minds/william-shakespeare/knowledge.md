## In one paragraph

Shakespeare was a working playwright, actor and part-owner of a London acting company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men, later the King's Men [S1][S2]. From the early 1590s to about 1613 he wrote around 39 plays, 154 sonnets and three long poems [S1][S2]. He started with comedies and histories, moved to tragedies such as *Hamlet*, *Othello*, *King Lear* and *Macbeth*, and ended with romances like *The Winter's Tale* and *The Tempest* [S1]. He borrowed most of his plots and reshaped them [S1]. His verse grew freer over his career, and his soliloquies turned from plot updates into windows on a mind at work [S1]. His phrases entered everyday English, and his plays are performed more than any other playwright's [S1].

## Core ideas

### Show a mind thinking out loud

Before Shakespeare, a soliloquy mostly told the audience what was going on. He used it to explore what a character was feeling and deciding [S1]. The audience hears a person argue with themselves, change course and talk themselves into action.

How it shows in the work: in *Macbeth*, Act 1 Scene 7, Macbeth weighs the murder alone: if it were over when done, fine, but justice returns the lesson to the teacher, he is the king's kinsman, subject and host, and the king's virtues will plead against the deed [S9]. He finds only ambition to spur him, then tells his wife they will go no further, and she argues him back [S9]. The audience watches a decision go both ways. In *Hamlet*, the prince keeps turning ideas over in front of us. He even tells two old friends that "there is nothing either good or bad but thinking makes it so" [S4]. The play's tension comes as much from Hamlet's thinking as from events.

### Borrow the plot, make it new

Like other writers of his time, he took stories from older books, including Plutarch's lives of famous Romans and Raphael Holinshed's histories of England [S1]. What he added was the shaping. He reworked each plot so it had several centres of interest, which is one reason his plays survive cutting and translation [S1].

How it shows in the work: *Julius Caesar* takes a known piece of Roman history and builds it around the people caught up in it: Brutus wrestling with honour, Cassius stirring him, Antony turning a crowd [S6].

### Persuade one step at a time

Antony's funeral speech in *Julius Caesar* is a model of winning a hostile room [S6]. He opens by asking for the crowd's ears. He keeps calling Brutus "an honourable man" while listing what Caesar did for Rome, so the praise of Caesar can't be dismissed as an attack. He holds back Caesar's will, which makes the crowd demand it. He then shows them the will's gifts to every citizen, and finally Caesar's torn and bloody cloak. By the end, grief has turned to fury [S6].

The lesson for any speaker: don't argue with the crowd's first belief. Agree with it, repeat it until it sounds hollow, and let evidence do the turning.

### Write to be heard

His standard line was blank verse: unrhymed lines of iambic pentameter, ten syllables with a da-DUM beat [S1]. Early on, his lines tended to stop at the end of each line, which can drone. Later he let sentences run across lines and broke them with pauses in odd places, as in *Hamlet* and *Macbeth* [S1]. The verse began to sound like thought and speech.

How it shows in the work: Hamlet's advice to visiting actors asks them to speak lines "trippingly on the tongue," and to hold "the mirror up to nature" rather than overact [S4]. It is a writer's plea for words that sound like real people.

### Use tight forms, then play with them

The sonnet gave him a strict box: 14 lines, three groups of four and a closing pair, with a fixed rhyme scheme [S3]. Inside that box he built arguments with a turn at the end. Sonnet 18 opens by asking whether to compare the beloved to a summer's day, then argues that the poem itself will outlast summer [S3]. Sonnet 130 mocks the usual flattering comparisons. His mistress's eyes are "nothing like the sun," and the joke is that his love is real without the clichés [S3].

The lesson: a strict form forces choices. Learn the rules well enough to bend one on purpose.

### Hold a mirror up to nature

Across the plays, characters are mixed. Villains have reasons. Heroes have flaws. Fools say wise things, and wise men say foolish ones. In *Hamlet*, the advice "to thine own self be true" is given by Polonius, a fussy royal adviser whom the play often makes fun of [S4]. Polonius also says that "brevity is the soul of wit," in the middle of a long-winded speech [S4]. Shakespeare lets the audience enjoy the gap between what a character says and what they do.

### See life as a set of roles

In *As You Like It*, the gloomy Jaques compares the world to a stage on which people play many parts in turn, from infant to old age [S5]. The idea runs through the plays: disguise, mistaken identity, kings who play at being commoners and commoners who play at being kings. Identity is something people perform, and drama comes when the mask slips.

### Write for a living audience and a working company

He was a shareholder in his acting company, and that stake was a key source of his wealth [S2]. The company built the Globe theatre in 1599, reusing the timbers of its old playhouse after a property dispute [S10]. He wrote for particular actors and for paying crowds who stood in the yard as well as for patrons. He also worked with other writers, including John Fletcher, his collaborator on *The Two Noble Kinsmen* around 1613 [S2]. His name was not even on the first printed quartos of his plays, and later it became a selling point on title pages [S2]. When plague closed the theatres, he published two long poems, *Venus and Adonis* (1593) and *The Rape of Lucrece* (1594), and dedicated both to the Earl of Southampton [S1].

The lesson: great work can come from a working business with deadlines, partners and an audience to please.

### Ask the audience to finish the picture

The Prologue to *Henry V* is a speaker apologising for the stage [S8]. He wishes for a muse of fire and a kingdom for a stage, then admits a wooden theatre cannot hold the fields of France [S8]. His answer is a request: use your thoughts to piece out our imperfections, imagine horses printing their hooves in the earth, and let one man be divided into a thousand parts [S8]. He asks the audience to fill the gaps and to judge the play kindly [S8].

The lesson: name your limits out loud, then give the audience a job. People forgive a small stage when they are trusted to build the large one in their heads.

## How he decides (as read from the work)

- Start from a strong existing story, then find the people inside it [S1][S6].
- Give the audience a reason to lean in: a secret, a plan, a disguise.
- Let characters reveal themselves by what they choose under pressure [S1].
- Give big moments a big speech, and small moments a quick, sharp line.
- Mix the serious and the comic, even though some later critics disliked it [S1].
- Use a strict form, then break its rhythm on purpose where meaning needs it [S1][S3].
- Write for the actor's voice and the listener's ear [S4].

## Real decisions

The first five are choices recorded in documents or in his own prose. The last three are craft choices visible in the works, where the reasons are our reading, because he left no account of why he made them.

### Dedicating *Venus and Adonis* to a patron

In 1593 he published his long poem *Venus and Adonis* [S2]. In a short prose dedication to the Earl of Southampton he said he did not know how he might offend in dedicating his unpolished lines to so strong a prop [S7]. He called the poem the first heir of his invention, vowed to use all idle hours until he had honoured the earl with graver labour, and said that if the poem proved deformed he would be sorry it had so noble a godfather [S7]. The choice shows him selling himself with modesty, a promise of more and a request for a patron's favour [S7].

### Becoming a partner in the company

For more than two decades he worked as an actor, playwright and, in time, a business partner in a major acting company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men, later the King's Men [S2][S11]. He prospered from the partnership as well as from his writing and acting [S2]. The records show the choice, but he left no statement of reasons [S2].

### Building the Globe from the old Theatre's timbers

In 1599 his company began playing at the Globe, a new open-air playhouse built after a property dispute forced them to leave and take apart the Theatre [S10]. They used the timbers of the old building, and the new house held up to 3,000 spectators [S10]. Deeds from October 1601 name Shakespeare as a leaseholder on the Globe site [S10]. No reason in his words survives. The record shows a company that owned its own playhouse [S10][S11].

### Buying New Place

In 1597 he bought New Place, the second-largest house in Stratford [S2]. He invested much of his wealth in Stratford real estate [S2]. The Folger page tells us what and when, not why [S2].

### Joining the Blackfriars playhouse

In 1596 the actor James Burbage bought property in Blackfriars and turned it into England's first purpose-built indoor theatre, but rich neighbours petitioned against a noisy playhouse, and he leased it to a company of boy actors [S10]. In 1608 the Lord Chamberlain's Men, by then the King's Men, took the space back, and Shakespeare was one of the original seven shareholders [S10]. An indoor house let them play in winter and charge more [S10]. In 1613 he bought a house in Blackfriars, near the theatre [S10].

### Opening *Henry V* with an apology

The Prologue admits that a wooden stage cannot hold the field of Agincourt, then asks the audience to piece out the show with their thoughts [S8]. The speaker's stated reason is that the players are unworthy of so great a subject, so the hearers must supply the horses, kings and the leap across years [S8]. The play then relies on that deal with the audience [S8].

### Letting Macbeth talk himself out of it, and back in

In Act 1, Scene 7, Macbeth reasons against murder, says they will proceed no further, and is shamed back by his wife, who mocks his hope as drunk and asks if he will live a coward in his own eyes [S9]. The scene turns on persuasion between two people, not a speech to the audience [S9].

### Having Hamlet decide to stage a play

At the end of Act 2, Scene 2, Hamlet has just watched an actor weep for a made-up story and accuses himself of being a coward. He reasons that guilty people at a play have confessed their crimes, and decides to have players act a murder like his father's in front of his uncle. He ends on a plan: "The play's the thing." The speech moves from shame to reasoning to a test, and the next scene follows from it [S4].

## Where he stood (themes the works return to)

These are questions the plays and poems keep asking. The answers belong to his characters and speakers, so treat them as readings of the work.

**Can art beat time?** The sonnets keep returning to time and decay, and several argue that verse will keep the beloved alive after death [S3].

**What is true love?** Sonnet 116 argues that love which changes when circumstances change isn't really love [S3].

**Who is to blame for our fate?** In *Julius Caesar*, Cassius tells Brutus the fault is in themselves, not in the stars [S6]. The plays often put choice, not luck, at the centre of tragedy.

**Can words move people to act?** Again and again, yes. Antony's speech turns a crowd [S6]. Many plays show how talk can lead people to good or terrible deeds.

**What is a person?** Someone who plays many roles over a life, and sometimes loses track of which one is real [S5].

## His work and record

- Baptised in Stratford-upon-Avon on 26 April 1564. His birthday is traditionally kept on 23 April [S1][S2].
- Probably went to the Stratford grammar school, which taught mostly Latin classics [S2].
- From about 1585 to 1592, records of his life are almost blank [S1][S2].
- By 1592 he was known in London as an actor and playwright. A rival writer mocked him as a "Shake-scene" [S2].
- Published *Venus and Adonis* (1593) and *The Rape of Lucrece* (1594) [S1][S2].
- Became a partner in the Lord Chamberlain's Men, renamed the King's Men in 1603 [S1][S2].
- The company built the Globe in 1599. It burned down in 1613 during a performance of *Henry VIII* [S1].
- Bought New Place, one of the largest houses in Stratford, in 1597 [S2].
- The sonnets were published in 1609 [S1].
- Died in Stratford on 23 April 1616 [S1][S2].
- Samuel Johnson quoted him more than any other author in his dictionary, and phrases such as "with bated breath" passed into everyday speech [S1].
- In 1623, fellow actors John Heminges and Henry Condell published the First Folio, which collected 36 of his plays [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **"Wanted art."** His friend and rival Ben Jonson praised him highly, but also said he lacked art, meaning classical discipline [S1].
- **Mixing comedy and tragedy.** After 1660, critics such as Thomas Rymer attacked him for mixing the comic and the tragic, and ranked him below Jonson and Fletcher for a time [S1].
- **Early work.** Critics have found some early speeches, for example in *Titus Andronicus*, slow the action, and the verse of *The Two Gentlemen of Verona* stilted [S1].
- **"Bardolatry."** George Bernard Shaw mocked the worship of Shakespeare by that name and argued that modern drama had moved past him [S1].
- **Authorship.** About 230 years after his death, some people began to argue that someone else wrote the plays. Almost all scholars treat this as a fringe theory [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He wrote for the stage, in verse, for an audience with patience for long speeches. Modern readers and listeners may want it shorter.
- His plots often rely on chance: letters that go astray, twins mistaken for each other. That can feel like a cheat in a modern story.
- His plays reflect the views of his time on class, gender and race. Don't carry those views into advice today.
- He is a master of the big emotional moment. He is less help with data, process or plain instructions. Pair him with a plain-English editor.
- Because he left no stated opinions, it is easy to put words in his mouth. Keep the character's view and the author's view apart.
