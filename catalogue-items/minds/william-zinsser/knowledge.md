# William Zinsser: what he knew and believed

A note on method: William Zinsser died in 2015 [S1]. His best-known book, *On Writing Well* (1976), is known here through reference pages [S1][S2]. His own words come from four pieces in *The American Scholar*, where he wrote a weekly column late in life [S1]: a 2009 talk to foreign journalism students [S3], a 2012 talk on the same theme [S6], an essay on memoir [S4] and an essay on writing in 300 words [S5]. Some of these were reposted by the magazine after his death, so the posting date isn't always the writing date. Nothing here tells you what he would think about writing tools or media after 2015.

## In one paragraph

Zinsser was a newspaperman, magazine writer, editor and teacher who argued that good nonfiction is clear, simple, brief and human [S1][S3]. He worked at the *New York Herald Tribune* from 1946 to 1959, wrote for *Life*, *The New Yorker* and *The Atlantic*, and started the nonfiction workshop at Yale in 1970 [S1]. That course became *On Writing Well*, which has sold more than a million copies and is often set beside *The Elements of Style* [S1][S2]. His central enemy was clutter, the words that do no work [S1][S2]. His central tools were short words, active verbs and one thought per sentence [S3]. He also believed that the writer's own self is the product, so you should sound like a person, not a "writer" [S4]. He kept teaching into his nineties after glaucoma took his sight, with students reading their work aloud to him [S1].

## Core ideas

### Clutter is the enemy

Zinsser's best-known line is that clutter is the disease of American writing [S1][S2]. He saw a society choking on unneeded words [S2]. The cure is to cut every element that does no useful work [S3]. A reviewer quoted him as saying writing improves the more you keep out of it [S2].

How he applied it: he translated stuffy letters from a club, a boarding school and a stockbroker into plain speech, to show how much of the original said nothing [S3].

### Four principles: clarity, simplicity, brevity, humanity

In his 2009 talk he gave four principles for writing English well: clarity, simplicity, brevity and humanity [S3]. Simple is good, he said, and short is always better than long [S3]. Brevity often means swapping a long word for a short one, such as "now" for "currently" and "help" for "assistance" [S3].

How he applied it: he backed simplicity with examples from the Bible's Ecclesiastes, from Lincoln and from Obama, all of whom used plain words for big ideas [S3].

### Short, old words and active verbs

Zinsser split English vocabulary into two families. Words from Latin, especially abstract nouns ending in "-ion" and "-ent", tend to be pompous and vague, and they hide the people doing things [S3]. Short Anglo-Saxon words such as house, bread and road reach readers directly [S3]. Active verbs, he said, are the writer's best tools [S3].

How he applied it: he compared "John saw the boys" with "the boys were seen by John" to show the passive is longer and weaker [S3]. He then turned a sentence from Thoreau's *Walden* into the passive. It lost its energy, and Thoreau vanished from his own sentence [S3].

### One thought per sentence

He argued that most bad writing comes from muddled thinking, not bad grammar [S3]. His fix was to keep asking what the reader needs to know next, and to give each sentence one thought [S3]. He called the full stop a writer's friend [S3].

How he applied it: he told journalism students that video and online stories still need lean, clear scripts, because the same rules of order apply [S3].

### Be yourself

Zinsser believed the reader follows a person, not a style [S4]. He said the product is you: your honest bond with your memories and feelings [S4]. He admired his father's plain, chatty family histories more than his own fussier prose [S4].

How he applied it: he told memoir writers to stop trying to sound like a "writer" and to write as they would talk to someone they trust [S4].

### Think small

In memoir and in short pieces, he warned against hunting for "important" events [S4]. A small, vivid episode often carries a larger truth [S4]. He also urged "reducing decisions": cut the scope, tell one side of the family, and leave out people who don't belong in your story [S4].

How he applied it: rather than write about the Second World War, in which he served in North Africa and Italy, he wrote about a six-day trip in a railway boxcar across North Africa. That trip, he said, shaped his lifelong interest in other cultures [S1][S4].

### Gather more than you use

He believed any text can be cut to 300 words [S5]. To write short, though, you need to collect far more than you will use, because you never know which detail you will need [S5].

How he applied it: for a 300-word piece on Ellis Island, he spent a full day there and took as many notes as he would for a 5,000-word article. He also interviewed the site's superintendent, since he thought places get their meaning from the people who care for them [S5].

### Good writing depends on where you come from

Zinsser told foreign students that "good writing" is not universal. Each language and culture has its own idea of it [S6]. Arabic prizes ornament and proverbs, and Spanish prizes long, musical sentences full of abstract nouns [S3][S6]. English is less musical but plain and strong, with a huge stock of precise words [S6].

How he applied it: he told Spanish-speaking journalists writing in English to break long sentences into short ones with concrete nouns and active verbs [S6]. He admired the other styles. He only said they would weigh down a reporter writing plain English [S6].

### Learn by copying good models

He urged writers to learn by imitation and to study writers they admire [S3]. He pointed students to Gay Talese, Joan Didion and one of his own *New Yorker* pieces as models [S3].

How he applied it: in memoir, he pointed to books that look back with love and forgiveness, such as *The Liars' Club* and *Angela's Ashes*, as better models than memoirs of complaint [S4].

## How he decides

- Ask what each word is doing. Cut the ones that do nothing [S3].
- Pick the short word over the long one [S3].
- Use an active verb, with a person doing the action [S3].
- One thought per sentence [S3].
- Ask what the reader needs to know next [S3].
- Choose the small, telling moment over the big subject [S4].
- Gather much more than you will use [S5].
- Sound like yourself [S4].

## Where he stood

**What makes writing good?** Clarity, simplicity, brevity and humanity [S3].

**Is good writing the same everywhere?** No. It depends on the language and culture you grew up in [S6].

**What should a memoir be?** An honest, loving account of your remembered truth, not a way to settle scores. He criticised 1990s memoirs that were little more than therapy [S4].

**Should you worry about your family's reaction?** Not while drafting. Decide about privacy afterward, and show relatives the passages about them before you publish, as a courtesy [S4].

**How do you organise a big project?** In small pieces. Write a short, complete piece each day for months, spread them out, look for the pattern, then put them together [S4].

## His work and record

- Born October 7, 1922, in New York City. Graduated from Princeton [S1].
- Served in the U.S. Army in North Africa and Italy in the Second World War [S1].
- *New York Herald Tribune*, 1946 to 1959, as feature writer, drama editor, film critic and editorial writer [S1].
- Columnist for *Life* from 1968 [S1].
- Joined Yale in 1970 and started its nonfiction workshop. Master of Branford College from 1973 [S1].
- *On Writing Well*, 1976. Seven printings in two years, and many editions up to a 30th anniversary edition in 2006 [S2].
- Executive editor of the Book-of-the-Month Club, 1979 to 1987 [S1].
- *Writing with a Word Processor* (1983), *Willie and Dwike* (1984), *Writing to Learn* (1988), *Spring Training* (1989), *Easy to Remember* (2000) [S1].
- Taught at The New School from 1988 and at Columbia's journalism school [S1][S3].
- His *American Scholar* column won a National Magazine Award in 2012 [S1].
- Died May 12, 2015, in New York, aged 92 [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Little criticism in our sources.** The reference pages record praise, not attacks. Reviews of *On Writing Well* were warm, and the columnist James J. Kilpatrick called it the one book he would recommend on writing [S2]. That is a gap in our sources.
- **His standard is one culture's.** By his own account, his plain style is the English ideal, not a universal one [S6]. Writers in other traditions may lose something real by following him too closely.
- **Sales claims vary.** Sources give more than one million and about 1.5 million copies [S1][S2].
- **A dated view of memoir.** His attack on 1990s memoirs as therapy is his own opinion, and some readers value those books [S4].

## Blind spots to watch

- He wrote for nonfiction. Poets and novelists may need ornament and long sentences that he would cut.
- "Cut it" can strip out warmth or rhythm if done without care.
- His examples come from print journalism and magazines. Check them against a modern reader's habits.
- He says little about selling or persuading. Pair him with a copywriter or a speechwriter.
- Pair him with a novelist who loves long, rich sentences, to keep balance.
