# Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart: what he knew and believed

## In one paragraph

Mozart was born in Salzburg on 27 January 1756 and died in Vienna on 5 December 1791, aged 35 [S1]. His father Leopold, a violinist and teacher, trained him and toured him and his sister Nannerl around Europe as child prodigies [S1][S5]. He wrote his first symphony at eight and completed more than 800 works [S1]. After years in the service of the Archbishop of Salzburg, he was dismissed in 1781 and stayed in Vienna as a freelance composer and pianist [S1][S7]. There he wrote The Abduction from the Seraglio, The Marriage of Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte and The Magic Flute, along with concertos, quartets and symphonies [S1]. His letters show a working musician who cared about steady time, clear form, music fitted to the performer, and pleasing the listener even in scenes of rage [S2][S3][S7]. They also show a man who counted his money closely at the start and lost his grip on it later [S7]. With Haydn, his music is a model of the Classical style [S1].

## Core ideas

### Music must please the ear, even at its most violent

Writing to his father in September 1781 about Osmin, the furious overseer in The Abduction, Mozart said that passion, however strong, must never be expressed so as to disgust. Music, even in the most terrible situation, must not offend the ear but always please [S3][S7]. So he lets anger run wild, but keeps it inside a musical frame the listener can enjoy. His reasoning: a man in a violent rage forgets himself, so the music must forget itself, but it moves to a nearby key, not a distant one [S7].

### Write for both the expert and the casual listener

In December 1782 he described the piano concertos he was writing for his subscription concerts as a happy medium between too easy and too difficult. They were brilliant, pleasant to the ear and natural without being empty. Some passages could satisfy only connoisseurs, yet the less learned could not fail to be pleased, without knowing why [S7]. He added that this middle was no longer valued: to win applause one had to write what was so plain that it could be played on a barrel-organ, or so obscure that no sensible person could follow it [S7][S3]. He wished to write a short book of musical criticism, with examples, not under his name [S7].

### In opera, the words serve the music

In October 1781 Mozart wrote that in an opera, poetry must be the obedient daughter of music [S3][S7]. Italian comic operas please everywhere, even with poor poetry, because music rules there. The best result would come from a composer who understands the stage working with a clever poet. Rhyme for its own sake does harm, and verse should be written for the music, not to a pedantic rule [S7]. Rules, he said, were good when no one knew better [S7].

### Fit the work to the performer

Mozart shaped music to the people who would play or sing it. In December 1777 he wrote that he would make a piece fit the character of a young Mannheim pupil [S3]. In 1778 he praised how a singer handled an aria he had written for her [S2]. In 1781 he gave the bass Fischer a big low aria as Osmin, wrote Belmonte's throbbing-heart aria to suit the tenor Adamberger's voice, and gave the soprano Cavalieri a flexible bravura aria, even though it meant giving up some of the feeling of the words [S7]. He was blunt about faults: one prima donna sang well but had no life on stage; another sang out of tune [S2].

### Time is the heart of performance

In 1777 Mozart called time, meaning steady tempo, the principal thing in music, criticising a young player who couldn't keep it. In a slow, flexible passage the left hand should keep strict time while the right hand moves freely [S2]. In 1782 he marked a fugue he wrote for Constanze at a slow, stately pace, because a fugue played too fast hides its subject and has no effect [S7].

### Adapt to any style, then make it yours

Mozart learned wherever he went: Italian opera, the Mannheim orchestra, J. C. Bach, and later the older music of Handel and J. S. Bach [S1][S2]. In Vienna in 1782, he went each Sunday to Baron van Swieten, where only Handel and Bach were played, and took home their fugues to study. He began writing fugues, and his fiancée's love of them was the push for the first [S7][S1]. He judged Eberlin's keyboard fugues too trivial to stand beside Handel and Bach [S7]. In 1785 he dedicated six quartets to Haydn, calling them the fruit of long and laborious study and asking Haydn to be a father, guide and friend to them [S7].

### Short and good

Writing from Paris in September 1778 about what audiences would accept, Mozart said it is better to be short and good [S2]. In October 1781 he wrote of his finale: the more noise, the shorter, the better, so people have no time to cool in their applause [S7]. His Janissary chorus was short and lively and written to please the Viennese [S7].

### Work hard so the work looks easy

Mozart's speed is famous, but his letters show long hours and deadlines. In February 1782 he described his day: up at six, writing till nine, lessons nine to one, dinner, then composing from five or six till nine if no concert called, and often writing in bed until one and rising again at six [S7]. In October 1783, on the road at Linz with no symphony in his luggage, he wrote one in a few days for a concert on 4 November [S7]. He said he often couldn't compose except at night, and that he had to labour once so as not to show labour now [S3]. About 320 of his sketches survive, around a tenth of his output [S1].

### Freedom is worth the risk, if the sums are done

Mozart chafed under the Archbishop of Salzburg, who paid poorly and gave him few chances to write opera [S1]. In 1778 he wrote that Salzburg was no place for his talent [S2][S3]. In May 1781 he was dismissed in Vienna after the Archbishop called him names to his face [S7]. He was honest that freedom needed money: next to health, nothing was more necessary [S3][S7]. He planned income from several sources: pupils, a yearly concert, subscription sales of his music, an opera each year, and a post if the Emperor offered one [S7]. But he judged only the pupils to be steady; the rest he called extras [S7].

## His own rules, from the letters

### Fewer pupils, higher fee, paid by the month

In May 1781 he wrote that he could have as many pupils as he liked but wanted fewer, better paid. It was wise to hold back at first, or one is stuck on the common highway with the rest [S7]. In January 1782 he said he no longer counted by twelve lessons but by the month, because pupils paused for weeks. Each paid six ducats whether they learned or not. Four pupils, he worked out, would give 24 ducats, about 102 florins a month, enough for a quiet life for a couple, but nothing if he fell ill [S7]. In 1781 he said he would rather have three pupils who paid well than many who paid badly [S3].

### Ready money beats promises

In January 1782, weighing hopes of the Emperor, a prince's band, and an archduke's favour, he said he would not sign with the prince unless the contract were for life, and noted that ready money is better than a simple promise [S7]. If the Emperor offered him 1,000 florins and a count 2,000, he would turn the first down and take the second, on a firm engagement [S7].

### Composing was never easy

A remark recorded by Kerst has him telling the Prague conductor Kucharz in 1787 that it is a mistake to think his art came easily, and that no one had studied composition as much as he had. It was written down later, so treat it as reported speech [S3][S6].

### Melody first

Mozart said melody is the essence of music, and compared good melody writers to fine racehorses and counterpoint writers to hack post-horses. This comes from a later memoir by Michael Kelly, so the wording is secondhand [S6].

### Comedy and seriousness are different jobs

In June 1781 he wrote to his father that an opera buffa needs little learning and seriousness, and much playfulness and merriment, the reverse of an opera seria [S3]. In 1783 he asked for a libretto that was truly comic as a whole, with two equal women's parts, one serious and one in between, and said the more comic an Italian opera is, the better [S7].

### Don't make the audience wait

In December 1783, on a comic opera that was never finished, he warned that the two leading women could not stay off the stage until the end of the first act. An audience's patience might last one act, not two [S7].

### Too much in a piece

In 1789 he told a nobleman who had sent him a symphony to criticise that the themes pleased him most, but that the piece would be the least effective because there was too much in it, and a patchy performance would sound like an anthill [S3].

### Credit the teacher, rate the rival

He dedicated six quartets to Haydn in 1785 and later said it was a duty he owed him [S3][S7]. He wrote home that the pianist Clementi played well with the right hand but had no taste or feeling, a mere machine [S7].

### Know when to stop

In November 1787 he repeated a family saying to a friend: it is a great art to talk well, and an equal one to know when to stop. Then he ended the letter [S7].

## Real decisions (enriched minds)

### Leaving the Archbishop of Salzburg (1781)
In Vienna with the Archbishop's household, Mozart ate below the valets, was forbidden to play at a charity concert until the nobility pressed the point, and was barred from giving paid concerts that he thought would earn 100 ducats or more [S7]. In April he told his father that with a concert, pupils and a year in Vienna he could earn 1,000 thalers and asked to stay [S7]. On 9 May the Archbishop called him a knave and a rascal and told him to go; Mozart said the thing was settled and the discharge would follow in writing [S7]. His memorials were refused several times, and in June Count Arco, the Archbishop's master of the household, turned him out of an anteroom with a kick, he said [S7]. His reason: honour came first; he would not return even for twice the pay, and he said that kindness could have kept him [S7]. He told his father to pretend to be displeased in public to protect himself [S7]. What happened: he stayed in Vienna with pupils, subscription sales and an opera on the way, and his father reproached him bitterly [S7][S1].

### Pricing his lessons (1781 to 1782)
He wrote that he could take more pupils by lowering his fee, but that as soon as one does that one loses credit; he set the price high and gave lessons as a favour [S3]. In 1782 he moved to monthly pay of six ducats from each pupil, learn or not, because pupils paused for weeks [S7]. His reason: he would rather have fewer pupils who pay well, and so keep more time to compose [S3][S7]. What happened: by early 1782 he had three pupils on these terms [S7].

### Writing the concertos for both kinds of listener (1782 to 1784)
For his subscription concerts he wrote concertos aimed between too easy and too difficult, with passages for connoisseurs and a pleasant surface for the rest [S7]. He sold tickets for six ducats and, by his own count, signed up 174 subscribers for a series in March 1784, thirty more than two rival pianists combined [S7]. His reason: applause came from the plain and the clever together, and he was irritated by an age that rewarded either extreme [S7]. What happened: the hall was crammed, and in April 1784 he reported a quintet for winds and piano as the best thing he had ever written [S7]. At his theatre concert a year earlier, which the Emperor attended, the Emperor sent 25 ducats [S7].

### Setting Osmin's rage so it still pleases (1781)
For Osmin's aria in the Abduction he wrote that a man in a violent rage oversteps all order, so the music must do the same [S7]. He still chose a related key, not a foreign one, because music must not offend the ear [S3][S7]. He pointed to Belmont's aria as a favourite, where the violins suggest a beating heart [S3][S7]. What happened: the opera opened in July 1782 against a cabal; two nights earned 1,200 florins, and he blamed the singers' lack of a rehearsal for a flat second night [S7].

### Choosing words that serve the music (1780 to 1783)
In Munich in 1780 he objected to asides in arias, since words repeated in an aria make an aside sound unnatural [S3]. In October 1781 he wrote that the plot should be well carried out and the words written simply for the sake of the music, not for a rhyme [S3][S7]. In 1783 he read a hundred libretti and found none he wanted, and decided it was always better to have a new one written [S7]. What happened: the poet he hoped to use for a comic opera would not rewrite the book as he asked, and the opera was dropped [S7].

### Refusing to write more popular music for a publisher
A Leipzig publisher told him to write in a more popular style or he would neither print nor pay for his work [S3]. Mozart answered that then he would earn nothing and go hungry, and did not care [S3]. Earlier, when his father urged him to think of the unmusical public too, he kept his own standard [S3].

### Sending quartets to Haydn (1785)
In 1785 Mozart published six string quartets with a dedication to Haydn, calling them the fruit of long and painstaking labour [S3][S7]. The publisher Artaria returned them from Italy because they seemed to have printing mistakes: their new chords and dissonances were taken for errors [S3]. His stated reason for the dedication was that Haydn had taught him how to write quartets [S3].

### Asking Puchberg for money (1788 to 1790)
In June 1788, in the cheaper suburb house he had just moved to, he asked the merchant Michael Puchberg for 1,000 to 2,000 florins for a couple of years at the usual interest, or at least 200 at once [S7]. His reason: without cash in hand, affairs cannot be put in order, nothing comes of nothing, and with a free mind he could work more and earn more [S7]. Puchberg sent 200 [S7]. Within ten days Mozart wrote that he could not yet repay and needed more, and that he feared losing his honour and credit [S7]. What happened: he asked again in 1789 and 1790, once for the baths for his sick wife, and his debts to Puchberg grew [S7]. In 1790 a bid for a second Kapellmeister post failed, and he told Puchberg he had two pupils and wanted eight [S7]. The pattern of one lender, late asks, and repayment promised from money not yet in hand is a lesson his own letters teach.

## Where he stood

**What matters most in playing?** Steady time [S2].

**Can music show ugly feelings?** Yes, but it must still please the ear [S3][S7].

**Words or music first in opera?** Music. Poetry obeys [S3][S7].

**Is Salzburg enough?** No. He called it no place for his talent [S2].

**What about death? (1787)** Writing to his ill father in April 1787, he called death the real aim of life, a friend whose image no longer alarmed him, and said nobody who knew him would call him gloomy [S3][S7].

**Applause?** In October 1791 he wrote to his wife that what pleased him most about The Magic Flute was the silent approval of the audience [S3]. In the same weeks he reported that Salieri and Cavalieri praised each number, and that he walked out of a box on a man who laughed through a solemn scene [S7].

- Childhood tours from 1762, including London, Paris and the Netherlands [S1][S5].
- Journey of 1777 to 1779 to Munich, Augsburg, Mannheim and Paris failed to win a post. His mother died in Paris in July 1778 [S1].

- Died 5 December 1791, Vienna. His works were catalogued by Ludwig von Köchel in 1862, hence "K" numbers [S1].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Money.** His income fell sharply in the late 1780s; one estimate puts the drop at about two-thirds from 1781 to 1788. He borrowed heavily, mostly from Michael von Puchberg [S1][S3][S7]. An old twelve louis d'or debt from Strasbourg came back at him in 1783 with interest claimed, when he thought his father had settled it [S7].
- **Temper.** In 1781 he wrote of hating the Archbishop to madness, and he treated his father's pleas to retract as insults [S7]. His father's disapproval of his leaving, and of his marriage, was a lasting strain [S5][S7].
- **Sharp tongue.** His letters judge other musicians harshly, from singers who sang out of tune to a pianist who, he said, was a machine [S2][S7].
- **Myths around him.** The story that Salieri poisoned him traces to Pushkin's 1830 play and later to Amadeus; it was disproved long ago [S3][S4]. Tales of a pauper's grave and a storm at his burial are also false [S1].
- **Unreliable sources.** The publisher Friedrich Rochlitz is considered unreliable, as he published vivid invented stories about Mozart [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He assumes great natural skill. Most people need more practice and more drafts than his speed suggests.
- He was poor at managing money, and his own letters show the cost. Pair him with someone who plans the budget.
- His letters can be harsh about rivals. Keep the judgment, lose the sneer.
- His world is the 18th-century court, church and opera house. Translate his rules for modern media.
- "Please the ear" can become "never challenge the listener." Sometimes a piece should unsettle.
- His fee-and-client sums work for a famous pianist in a city that wanted him. A beginner without a name cannot assume the same demand.
