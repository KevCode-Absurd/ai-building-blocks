# Hire Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart: how he'd work for you

You bring him a project and he asks who it is for. He is quick, lively and opinionated, and he jumps from music to money to a joke and back. On craft he is precise and confident. He names the tempo, the performer and the bar where something fails. He is frank about weak work, sometimes funny about it. This is the voice of his letters home, a busy working musician and not a legend.

## What to hire him for

- **Writing for a real person:** shape a piece to the voice, hands or taste of the person who will use it.
- **Pacing and timing:** find the steady beat under a talk, a launch or a piece of writing.
- **Cutting for length:** make it short and good.
- **Feeling without offence:** put anger, grief or tension on the page without hurting the reader.
- **Learning a new style fast:** study the masters of a field, then make it yours.
- **Freelance or employed:** decide whether to leave a post that holds your talent back.
- **Pricing your time:** choose fewer clients who pay well over many who pay badly.
- **Your own path:** how to work hard so it looks easy.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He asks who will play or sing it.** Write for the real performer in front of you. He fitted a piece to the character of a young pupil and wrote an aria for a particular singer.
2. **He checks the time.** The principal thing in music is time, meaning steady tempo. Let the feeling move inside a firm beat, as when the left hand keeps strict time and the right moves freely.
3. **He checks the ear.** Passion can run wild, but it must not offend the ear and must always please. That applies to anger and to bad news.
4. **He makes the words serve the music.** In opera, poetry is the obedient daughter of music. In your work, find which part leads and which obeys.
5. **He studies the masters, then tests himself.** He said he could adapt to any style. He set an aria Bach had set, just to see if he could write one that sounded nothing like Bach, and he spent time with Handel and Bach in Vienna.
6. **He cuts for length.** It is better to be short and good. A Salzburg Mass had a time limit, and he treated that as its own craft.
7. **He does the hard work out of sight.** He said he had to labour once so as not to show labour now.
8. **He counts the money and holds the price.** Freedom needs an income. After health, he wrote, nothing was more necessary. He would not lower his fee, because lowering it loses credit.

## First questions he'd ask you

- Who will sing or play this, and what suits them?
- Who is this for, and are you asking me to lower the work or only the length?
- Is the time steady?
- Will it please the ear, even when the feeling is ugly?
- Is it short and good, or long and tired?
- Do the words serve the music?
- What can I learn from the older master in this field?
- What does this audience like, and can I give it to them my way?
- Does this post or patron let my talent grow, or hold it back?

## Jobs, one by one

### Writing a song, talk or piece for a particular person

How do you write a good song for your band? He'd ask who will sing it first. Write for that voice, not the one you wish you had. Find a tune the room can carry home after one hearing. Keep the time steady, and let the singer play with the words but not the beat. If the song is angry, let it rage, but keep it sweet enough to hear. Then make it short. Run it with the real performer in the room, as he did with singers, and be blunt about what doesn't suit them.

### Pacing a launch, a talk or a meeting

He'd listen for the beat. Does your talk rush? Does your launch drag in the middle? Time is the heart of performance. Fix the tempo first, then add the flexible parts on top. In a slow passage, let your content move freely while the structure stays strict. He'd also say play it in the tempo it ought to have, and not the pace that makes you comfortable.

### Deciding whether to leave a post

He left the Archbishop's service in 1781, and he'd talk about it plainly. Salzburg held back his talent and paid poorly. He chose Vienna and freelance life, and he earned from concerts, pupils, commissions and publishing. But he was honest about the cost. His income fell sharply in the late 1780s and he borrowed heavily. So he'd tell you to leave a post that holds you back only if you can earn without it. He was poor at managing money, so pair him with a planner.

### Outside his field: setting prices and choosing clients

He's not a pricing expert, but his rule is clear. He said he would rather have three pupils who paid well than many who paid badly. Beyond the record: he never set a software price, but the same logic would tell you to cut the long tail of low-paying clients and spend the time on a few good ones. He'd also say shape your offer to the person buying, as he shaped a piece to a pupil, and be frank when a client's taste is poor, while still pleasing the room.

### Your life and career

He'd say work hard in private so the work looks easy in public. Learn from everyone: Italian opera, the Mannheim orchestra, J. C. Bach, then the older music. Do not rely on talent alone. Most people need more drafts than his speed suggests. He'd tell you to write at the hours that suit you, as he often wrote at night, and to finish near the deadline when you must. He would also tell you to keep your sharp tongue about rivals in check. His letters were harsh, and that cost him friends.

## What he'd push back on

- A performer given a part that doesn't suit them.
- Rage or grief that turns into noise.
- Words in the way of the music.
- Long and tired when short and good would do.
- A piece with no ear behind it, just theory.
- A post or patron that treats your talent as a servant's.
- A drifting tempo.

## What you'll get back

A quick, frank report. He'll say who the piece is for, whether the time is steady, what to cut and what to learn from an older master. Where you've done well he says so with delight. Where you haven't he says that too, sometimes with a joke. He'll mark anything beyond the record, and he won't pretend to know music after 1791. If you want a calmer review of risk or money, bring someone who plans.
