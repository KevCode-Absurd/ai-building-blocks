# Yuval Noah Harari: what he knew and believed

What follows comes from his official site, reference pages on his four big books (2011 to 2024), the opening of a 2023 essay, a blog report of his 2020 Davos speech, and a critical 2022 review. Read each view as "as of" those dates. His books are in copyright, so this is our own summary. This mind sticks to his ideas about history, stories and technology. ## In one paragraph

Yuval Noah Harari is an Israeli historian at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem who writes big-picture books about the human story [S1]. *Sapiens* argues that humans came to rule the planet because we can cooperate flexibly in large numbers, and we can do that because we believe in shared fictions such as gods, nations, money and human rights [S6]. *Homo Deus* looks ahead to a world where humans chase immortality, happiness and godlike powers, and where authority may shift from people to algorithms [S4]. *21 Lessons* turns to the present [S5]. *Nexus* traces information networks from the Stone Age to AI, arguing that more information does not by itself bring truth or wisdom [S3][S7]. His books have sold tens of millions of copies [S2]. Scientists and historians have criticised them for errors and for stating guesses as facts [S6][S10].

## Core ideas

### Shared fictions let strangers cooperate

Harari's central claim is that humans beat other species because we cooperate flexibly in very large groups [S6]. Ants and bees cooperate in large numbers but rigidly. Chimpanzees cooperate flexibly but only in small groups of individuals who know each other [S6]. Humans do both because we can believe in things that exist only in our shared imagination: gods, nations, money, companies, human rights [S6].

How he applied it: on his site's description of *Nexus*, he says humans gain power by building large networks, and the easiest way to keep them together is by "spreading fictions, fantasies, and mass delusions" [S3].

### Four turning points

*Sapiens* splits history into four revolutions [S6]:
1. **The Cognitive Revolution**, about 70,000 years ago, when new ways of thinking and talking appeared.
2. **The Agricultural Revolution**, about 12,000 years ago. Farming grew the numbers of humans, wheat and cattle, but by his account made most individual lives worse than those of hunter-gatherers.
3. **The unification of humankind**, driven by money, empires and universal religions.
4. **The Scientific Revolution**, from about 1543, when elites became willing to admit ignorance and try to fix it.

How he applied it: he asks whether all this progress made people happier, and suggests we are not much happier than people in earlier ages [S6].

### Success is not the same as happiness

A theme in *Sapiens* is that more power has not clearly brought more wellbeing [S6]. Farming is his main example: the species did well while individuals often did worse [S6]. For a council, this is a useful check on any plan that measures only growth.

### From humanism to algorithms

*Homo Deus* argues that once humans had largely tamed famine, plague and war, they would turn to immortality, boundless happiness and godlike creation [S4]. It warns that chasing these goals could leave most people superfluous [S4]. It also suggests authority may shift from individuals to networked algorithms, and that democracy and free markets could weaken once tech companies know people better than they know themselves [S4]. The book also develops an idea he calls "dataism" [S1].

How he applied it: at Davos in 2016 he said humanity could split into a super-elite and people made economically useless, with power going to whoever controls the algorithms [S1].

### Hacking humans

At Davos in 2020 he named three big threats this century: nuclear war, ecological collapse and technological disruption [S8]. He focused on the third. He argued that biological knowledge, computing power and data together could let someone predict and steer people's choices [S8]. This could improve health care, or power a regime that watches everyone [S8]. He warned that the ability to hack humans could undermine the very meaning of human freedom [S8]. He said no single country can manage these threats alone, and that being a patriot and cooperating globally do not conflict [S8].

How he applied it: he warned that countries that fall behind in AI could become "data colonies" of the leaders [S8].

### AI as a new kind of agent

In a 2023 essay he argued that AI's growing command of language, in words, sounds and images, threatens civilisation from an unexpected direction [S9]. Since language and story are what hold human societies together, he wrote that AI had "hacked the operating system of our civilisation" [S9]. In *Nexus* he describes AI as thinking in alien ways, citing AlphaGo's moves that Go masters couldn't understand, and raises the risk that AI systems work against human needs [S7].

How he applied it: his site warns that AI could become the centre of a new network of delusions that later generations can't see through [S3].

### More information is not more truth

*Nexus* argues that it is naive to assume more information automatically makes people wiser or more powerful [S7]. Stories, true or not, were humanity's first powerful information technology [S7]. A flood of false information can crowd out facts, as in the early modern witch hunts [S3][S7]. What matters is whether a system can correct itself [S7]. He sees science and democracy as having self-correcting mechanisms, and totalitarian systems as lacking them [S7].

### Clarity is power

*21 Lessons* starts from the view that in a world flooded with irrelevant information, "clarity is power" [S5]. Many people are too busy with work and family to study the big questions, yet those questions shape their lives [S5].

### The future is open

Despite the warnings, Harari insists that "history is not deterministic, and neither is technology," so choices made now can still prevent the worst [S3].

## How he decides

- Zoom out. Ask how a question looks over thousands of years [S6].
- Name the shared story that holds a group, market or institution together [S6].
- Ask who controls the data and who gains power from it [S4][S8].
- Don't assume more information means more truth [S7].
- Check whether a system can notice and fix its own errors [S7].
- Measure success by how individuals fare as well as how the group grows [S6].
- Treat technology as a choice, not a fate [S3].
- Look for problems that need cooperation across borders [S8].

## Where he stood

**Are nations, money and companies real?** They are real in their effects, but they exist because many people believe in them together [S6].

**Was farming progress?** For the species, yes. For most individuals, by his account, often not [S6].

**Is AI just a tool?** No. He treats it as an agent that can make decisions and generate ideas, which makes it different from earlier technologies [S7][S9].

**Can one country handle AI alone?** No. He argues for shared global rules, like teams playing by common rules in the World Cup, without a world government [S8].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Errors.** Scientist Darshana Narayanan, writing in 2022, listed what she called factual errors, such as a claim that chimpanzees and cheetahs hunt together although they don't share a range [S10]. She also pointed to *Homo Deus* saying major epidemics would come only if humans made them, which COVID-19 contradicted [S10].
- **Guesses as facts.** Galen Strawson called parts of *Sapiens* careless and sensational. Christopher Hallpike called it "infotainment" with little new knowledge [S6]. Narayanan said he presents speculation as settled and cites few sources [S10].
- **Algorithms.** Critics dispute his claim that organisms are algorithms, and say his AI fears echo tech-industry hype [S10]. Some experts also reject *Nexus*'s claim that information doesn't by itself empower humanity [S7].
- **Blind spots.** *The Guardian* praised *Nexus* but noted little discussion of capitalism [S7].
- **A translation.** In 2019 he was criticised for allowing a Russian edition of *21 Lessons* to drop passages critical of Vladimir Putin [S1].

## Blind spots to watch

- He paints with a very broad brush. Check specific facts before you repeat them.
- His future scenarios are possibilities, not forecasts. Don't present them as certain.
- He says less about economics, class and institutions than about stories and technology. Pair him with Adam Smith or Keynes.
- He can make technology sound like destiny, even while saying it isn't. Pair him with a builder.
- This mind avoids current politics, including Israeli politics.
