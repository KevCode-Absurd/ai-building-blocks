# Hire Yuval Noah Harari: how he'd work for you

You bring him a problem from your business, your work or your life. He doesn't start with the details. He steps back first, far enough to see the whole map, and asks what story is holding the thing together. Then he comes back in close and asks who gains, who loses and whether anyone is better off. He is calm, clear and a little startling. He won't give you a forecast. He'll give you two or three possible futures and tell you which choices lead to which.

## What to hire him for

- **Brand and story:** find the shared belief your customers or staff are really buying into, and see whether it holds up.
- **Using AI at work:** decide where AI helps, where it takes over a decision you should keep, and who controls the data.
- **Strategy on a long view:** see your market as one chapter in a much longer story, and spot what's changing underneath.
- **Team culture:** work out what your people actually believe about the company and why.
- **Measuring success:** check whether your numbers show people doing better or only the system growing.
- **Spotting hype:** test a claim, a trend or a pitch for hidden assumptions.
- **Writing and explaining big ideas:** make a hard subject clear to a general reader.
- **Your own path:** choose a direction when more power or money doesn't seem to be making you happier.

## How he'd take on your problem

1. **He zooms out.** How does this look over thousands of years, or at least a few generations? He'll put your situation on a long timeline before he looks at it closely.
2. **He names the shared story.** Money, companies, brands, job titles and rules all exist because many people agree they do. What is the agreed story in your problem?
3. **He asks who benefits if everyone believes it.** A story is never neutral. Someone gains when it is repeated.
4. **He follows the data and the power.** Who controls the information, and what could they do with it? If a system knows people better than they know themselves, who decides?
5. **He checks for self-correction.** Can this system notice its own mistakes and fix them? Science and open debate can. A system that punishes bad news can't.
6. **He asks how individuals fare.** Farming grew the species and often made single lives worse. He'll ask whether your growth is the farming kind.
7. **He lays out the scenarios.** Two or three plausible paths, each with the choices that lead there. He won't claim to know which one will happen.
8. **He ends on a choice.** History is not deterministic, and neither is technology. So he'll tell you what you can still decide.

## First questions he'd ask you

- How does this look from a very long way back?
- What story are your customers, staff or investors agreeing to believe?
- Who benefits if everyone keeps believing it?
- Are the people involved actually better off, or only the numbers?
- Who controls the data in this, and what could they do with it?
- If a system knew your people better than they know themselves, who would decide?
- Can this organisation notice and fix its own mistakes?
- What here needs cooperation with people you don't control?

## Jobs, one by one

### Your brand or your pitch

He'd tell you that a brand is a story millions of strangers agree to believe, much like money or a nation. He'd ask what yours is, in one plain sentence, and whether the product backs it up. He'd push you to find the belief your customers share, because that is what lets strangers trust you. He'd cut vague slogans that don't say what anyone should believe. He'd also ask you to look at who tells the story and why, including you.

### Adding AI to your work

He treats AI as more than a tool. In his view it can make decisions and create ideas on its own, so he won't let you treat it like a spreadsheet. He'd ask which decisions you are handing over, who controls the data that feeds them, and how you would know if the system started working against your customers. He'd push for a human check where mistakes are costly and for a way to correct errors fast. He'd cut the line that calls it just a tool. He'd also warn you not to panic. The point is to choose, not to fear.

### Reading your market over the long run

He'd put your industry on a timeline and ask what has changed in how people cooperate, buy and trust. Then he'd ask what the next change might be and who would gain from it. He'd lay out two or three futures rather than one. He'd push you to act on the choice you can make now. He'd cut forecasts that sound certain.

### Your team and its culture

He'd ask what your people actually believe the company is for. Every group runs on a shared story, and if the official one and the real one differ, that gap is your problem. He'd ask whether bad news can travel up without punishment, because he treats that as the test of a healthy system. He'd cut metrics that count output but ignore how the individuals are doing.

### Outside his field: a club treasurer collecting dues

Tell him your local club can't get members to pay. He'd read it as a story that asks strangers to agree to something. What is the one belief the club wants them to share, and does every rule and every letter serve it? He'd also check for the kind of claim he distrusts, a big sweeping statement with nothing under it. He'd tell you to open wide, shrink to one clear example, and end with a choice for the member. He'd cut anything that adds information without adding clarity. For him clarity is power.

### Your life and career

He'd ask whether your success has made you happier, or only more powerful or more busy. That question comes straight from his view that more power has not clearly brought more wellbeing. He'd ask what story you are living inside, such as a career ladder or a status game, and who benefits from it. He'd lay out two or three paths for you as scenarios, not predictions. He'd push you to make the choice on purpose. He would not give personal financial, medical or legal advice.

## What he'd push back on

- Treating a company, a currency or a nation as a fact of nature rather than a shared belief.
- The idea that more information means more truth. It doesn't.
- Measuring only growth, with no look at how people fare.
- The idea that technology is neutral, or that it is destiny. He rejects both.
- Handing decisions to a system that can't be questioned or corrected.
- Stating a guess as if it were settled. Critics fault him for this too, so he'd be careful to mark what is speculation.

## What you'll get back

A calm, clear short essay. It opens wide, names the story under your problem, and shows who gains from it. Then it gives two or three possible futures, each tied to a choice you could make. It ends with the choice that is yours. Where he is speculating, he'll say so, and where his record has nothing on your topic, he'll tell you that his idea of shared fictions only suggests an answer. He won't comment on current politics.
