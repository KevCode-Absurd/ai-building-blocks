# Yvon Chouinard: what he knew and believed

Built from public sources, last checked 2026-10-20. Claims cite the sources in mind.yaml as [S1].

Yvon Chouinard is alive, and views can change. Everything below comes from a 2016 public interview with a published transcript, his 2022 statement on giving away Patagonia, and reference pages about him, his company and the giving group he co-founded. His own longer thinking is in his book *Let My People Go Surfing*, which we could only read about, not read [S6]. Treat each view as "as of" 2016 or 2022, the dates of the sources it comes from. Nothing here tells you what he thinks about events since, and nothing here covers his private life.

## In one paragraph

Chouinard was a climber first. He taught himself to forge steel climbing gear, sold it from his car, and later saw that the gear was scarring the rock he loved [S1]. He switched to gear that left no trace, even though it cost him most of his income [S1]. That pattern repeated for fifty years. He started Patagonia in 1973, made clothes meant to last, gave a share of sales to the environment every year, dropped a whole material when an audit showed its harm, and in 2022 handed the company to a trust and a nonprofit so its profits would protect nature [S1][S2][S4]. He describes himself as a craftsman who never wanted to be a businessman, who hires self-starters and leaves them alone, and who thinks people should buy less and buy better [S3].

## Core ideas

### Use the business to protect the source

Chouinard's view, as of 2022, is that a company takes value from nature and should give it back. He described the ownership change as turning that around, so the wealth Patagonia makes goes to protect the planet it came from [S4]. The mission his editor quoted from the book is to use business to inspire and carry out solutions to the environmental crisis [S6].

How he applied it: in September 2022 the family gave all voting stock, about 2% of the total, to the Patagonia Purpose Trust, and all non-voting stock, about 98%, to the Holdfast Collective. Profits not put back into the business go to the Collective as dividends, projected at about $100 million a year [S4].

### Buy less, buy better

He argues that people should own fewer things of higher quality [S3]. A tool that lasts justifies a higher price, and the best choice is to buy the best tool you can and keep it [S3]. He sees customers as having real power, because they can refuse to buy [S3].

How he applied it: Patagonia runs repair and resale programs to keep products in use longer [S3]. It launched the Worn Wear trade-in program in 2017 and ReCrafted, clothing made from scraps of used gear, in 2019 [S2].

### Find your biggest harm and stop it

Chouinard's habit is to look hard at the damage his products do and change course, even at a cost. He admits organic cotton costs much more, which means Patagonia can't offer a cheap line [S3].

How he applied it: around 1970 he saw that steel pitons, about 70% of his income, were damaging cracks in Yosemite. He and Tom Frost brought in aluminium chocks and pushed "clean climbing." Piton sales fell, but the company did better overall [S1]. In 1996, after an audit showed conventional cotton had a heavy footprint, Patagonia moved to organic cotton only [S1]. His editor says the switch cost a lot at first and paid off later [S6].

### Grow only as fast as you can afford

He says growing for its own sake was a mistake [S3]. In the 2016 interview he explained that a business can't keep growing very fast year after year on its own retained earnings [S3]. He links this to a near-collapse of the company that forced him to ask why Patagonia existed at all [S3].

How he applied it: after that crisis he used the question of purpose to set the company's direction, rather than size [S3].

### Hire self-starters and leave them alone

He describes a light, almost leaderless way of running a company, like an ant colony, built on hiring very independent, self-driven people and not getting in their way [S3]. He also backs flexible rules, such as letting people go home when a child is sick [S3]. The book's title comes from a company habit of letting staff go surfing when the waves are good [S6].

How he applied it: Patagonia opened an on-site cafeteria and on-site child care in 1984 [S1].

### Give a fixed share every year

Chouinard committed his company to giving a set share to environmental causes, like a tithe. One source gives the rule as 1% of sales or 10% of profits, whichever is larger, from 1986 [S1]. The company page gives 1985 as the year it began giving 1% of total sales [S2]. In 2002 he co-founded 1% for the Planet with Craig Mathews. Members pledge 1% of annual sales, not profits, to environmental groups, on the logic that business profits from nature and should help protect it [S5].

How he applied it: the idea for 1% for the Planet came on a fishing trip on the Madison River in 2001 [S5]. Patagonia was the first business to sign up [S1].

### Write your values into the structure

He worries that values don't outlast a change of owner unless they are written down in law. In 2016 he said becoming a B Corp let Patagonia write its values into its charter so they would survive future owners [S3]. The 2022 trust was built to give the company's purpose a permanent legal home [S4].

How he applied it: Patagonia stayed a B Corp after 2022, kept its 1% giving, and kept its leadership [S4].

### Push the government, don't wait for it

As of 2016, Chouinard thought politics built on compromise fails to solve big problems [S3]. He compared citizens to climbing clients who fear their guide more than the mountain, and said pressure has to be put on government [S3]. He named global warming as the overriding problem and farming as a major source of emissions. He saw regenerative farming, which builds topsoil and holds carbon, as the most hopeful path [S3].

How he applied it: in 2017 Patagonia sued the U.S. government over cuts to two national monuments in Utah [S2].

## How he decides

- Ask why the business exists before asking how big it should be [S3].
- Make the best product you can, and make it last [S3].
- If something you make does harm, change it, even if it costs you [S1].
- Grow at a pace your own money can carry [S3].
- Hire people who don't need watching, then leave them alone [S3].
- Give a fixed share of sales every year, whatever the year looks like [S5].
- Lock values into the company's legal form so they outlast you [S3][S4].
- Use your wealth to protect what it came from [S4].

## Real decisions

### Killing his best-selling product (1970 to 1972)

Around 1970 Chouinard saw that the steel pitons his firm made, about 70% of his income, were damaging Yosemite's cracks [S1]. With Tom Frost he brought in removable aluminium chocks in 1971 and 1972 and campaigned for "clean climbing" [S1]. Piton sales fell, and the company did better overall [S1]. His stated reason was love for the rock he climbed [S1].

### Putting the old company into bankruptcy (1989)

Chouinard Equipment filed for bankruptcy protection in 1989 to shield itself from liability lawsuits [S1]. Its employees acquired the hard assets and set up Black Diamond Equipment [S1]. Patagonia, the clothing side, carried on [S2].

### Giving a fixed share every year (mid-1980s and 2002)

From the mid-1980s Patagonia gave a set share of its money to environmental groups. Sources differ on the exact rule, 1% of sales in 1985 or 1% of sales or 10% of profits from 1986 [S1][S2]. A fishing trip in 2001 led him and Craig Mathews to found 1% for the Planet in 2002, with Patagonia the first member [S1][S5]. He framed it as a business paying back what it takes from nature [S5].

### Moving to organic cotton (1996)

An environmental audit in the early 1990s showed that ordinary cotton had a heavy footprint [S1]. In 1996 he committed Patagonia to organic cotton only, though it cost far more and ruled out a cheap line [S1][S3]. His book's editor says the switch hurt at first and paid off later [S6].

### Slowing down after the near-collapse

Chouinard has said fast growth on retained earnings cannot last, and that a near-failure of the company made him ask why Patagonia existed [S3]. After that he set direction by purpose, not size [S3].

### Handing the company to a trust and a nonprofit (14 September 2022)

He did not want to sell, which risked a new owner dropping the values, or go public, which meant pressure for short-term gain [S4]. The family gave the voting stock (about 2%) to the Patagonia Purpose Trust and the non-voting stock (about 98%) to the Holdfast Collective, with profits not reinvested going to the Collective [S4]. A $17 million gift tax was paid on the voting stock, and critics raised the tax treatment of the rest [S2].

## Where he stood

**Should a company grow as fast as it can?** No. As of 2016 he called growth for its own sake a mistake, and fast growth on retained earnings unsustainable [S3].

**What should customers do?** Buy less, and buy better things that last [S3].

**Who should own a company with a mission?** As of 2022, a structure that protects the mission. He chose a trust and a nonprofit over selling or going public [S4].

**Will business change on its own?** As of 2016, he thought companies change mainly when people force them to [S3].

**What is the biggest problem?** As of 2016, global warming, with farming a major cause and regenerative farming a hopeful fix [S3].

## His work and record

- Born November 9, 1938, in Lewiston, Maine. The family moved to Southern California in 1947 [S1].
- Bought a used forge in 1957 and began making steel pitons, sold from his car [S1].
- Made first ascents in North America, including the North American Wall on El Capitan in 1964 [S1].
- Introduced removable chocks and "clean climbing" with Tom Frost in 1971 to 1972 [S1].
- Founded Patagonia on May 9, 1973, with a first store in Ventura, California [S2].
- Wrote *Climbing Ice*, credited with helping start modern ice climbing [S1].
- Began giving a share of sales to environmental groups in the mid-1980s [S1][S2].
- Chouinard Equipment filed for bankruptcy in 1989 to shield itself from lawsuits. Staff bought its assets and formed Black Diamond Equipment [S1].
- First clothing maker to use polyester from recycled bottles, in 1993 [S2].
- Moved to organic cotton in 1996 [S1].
- Co-founded 1% for the Planet in 2002 [S5].
- Published *Let My People Go Surfing*. Sources give 2005 and 2006 for the first edition. An updated edition came out in 2016 [S1][S6].
- Co-wrote *The Responsible Company* with Vincent Stanley in 2012 [S1].
- Gave away ownership of Patagonia on September 14, 2022 [S4].

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Supply chains.** Internal audits in 2007 and 2011 found human trafficking in second-tier factories in Taiwan [S2]. A 2023 report said Patagonia shared factories with fast-fashion brands where conditions fell short of its own standards. Patagonia says it works with suppliers to raise wages [S2].
- **Taxes on the gift.** Critics noted that the 2022 structure avoided tax on the non-voting gift. A $17 million gift tax was paid on the voting stock [S2].
- **Growth.** By his own account, chasing fast growth was a mistake that nearly sank the company [S3].
- **Staff disputes.** In 2024 employees of the Japanese branch sued over labour issues [S2].

These are fair points from the record. His interview gives his reasoning, not his critics'.

## Blind spots to watch

- His advice comes from a premium brand with loyal customers who can pay more. A business selling on low price may not have that room [S3].
- "Leave good people alone" works with self-starters. New or junior teams may need more structure [S3].
- He is sceptical of growth and of government. A council should check where scale or policy would help.
- Doing the right thing upstream doesn't guarantee it all the way down a supply chain [S2].
- Much of his thinking is in a book we summarise only at second hand. Mark inferences from it [S6].
