## In one paragraph

Ziglar was born Hilary Hinton Ziglar in 1926 and grew up poor in Yazoo City, Mississippi, the tenth of twelve children [S1][S7]. His father died when he was five, and he was helping support the family by 12 [S7]. After a Navy college programme in the Second World War, he sold waterless cookware door to door [S1][S7]. He struggled for two and a half years, then, after P. C. Merrell told him he could be a champion, became the number two salesman in a company of more than 7,000 [S7]. At one stretch he went through 17 different deals in five years, was often broke, and never declared bankruptcy [S7]. He dreamed of speaking from 1952, went full time in 1970, and his career took off after his Christian commitment in 1972 [S7]. His first book, self-published in 1974 as *Biscuits, Fleas, and Pump Handles*, became *See You at the Top* [S7][S10]. Its core line, which he repeated for almost 40 years, is that you can have everything in life you want if you help enough other people get what they want [S8][S10].

## Core ideas

### Help enough other people get what they want

This sentence runs through his whole career. It appears in his first book in 1974, in *See You at the Top*, in *Ziglar on Selling* (1991) and *Top Performance*, and in his last book, *Born to Win*, written with Tom [S10]. He said it on radio in 1978 and again to C-SPAN in 2002 [S7][S8]. For a salesperson it flips the job: find out what the buyer wants and help them get it, and your own success follows [S2].

How he applied it: in 2011 he said the best way to close a sale is to help the customer become a better person [S9]. He also trained salespeople for Mary Kay Cosmetics [S1].

### Self-image drives action

Ziglar believed people can't keep acting in a way that clashes with how they see themselves [S5]. He told his own story as proof. Before Merrell, he saw himself as a little guy from a little town; after Merrell's belief and example, he jumped from outside the top 3,000 to second place [S7][S4]. He loved a line from the singer Ethel Waters: God doesn't make junk [S9].

How he applied it: on radio he taught that you have to like and respect yourself before you can do much for others [S8]. One exercise he used on stage: stand in front of a mirror, look yourself in the eye and say why you love your job [S12].

### Hardening of the attitudes and stinkin' thinkin'

*See You at the Top* (1977 edition) calls "hardening of the attitudes" the world's most deadly disease, and its cover promised to cure "stinkin' thinkin'" [S11]. He even wrote a children's story called *The Stinkin' Thinkin' Vaccine* (1988) [S11]. For him, attitude was something you feed. In 1978 he told listeners to feed their minds good things all the time, and called happiness an inside job [S8].

How he applied it: in 2011 he said he had read about three hours a day for some 40 years, and followed a rule: if he couldn't teach it, he wouldn't read it [S9].

### Failure is an event

In *See You at the Top* he separated what happens to you from who you are. Failure is something that happened, not a label on the person [S2]. He also wrote that others can stop you for a while, but only you can stop yourself for good [S2]. As a boy, he said, he learned you can complain about a setback or do something about it [S9].

How he applied it: after a fall around 2007 left him with short-term memory loss, he said it taught him to embrace the struggle, and he kept writing and speaking with his family's help [S9].

### Promise only with a plan

Ziglar tied integrity to follow-through. In 2002 he said he never makes a promise without giving a plan to fulfil it, and that ability without honour has no value [S7]. In 2011 he said he had lived by one rule: if you say you will do something, go do it, with no excuses [S9]. He added that people won't listen to you if they don't trust you [S9]. A line credited to him calls integrity the most important persuasion tool you have [S2].

### Explain the why

He believed most people won't act without a reason. When you ask someone to do something, at work or at home, tell them why, and they are far more likely to remember and do it [S9]. In 2011 he said young leaders should always know their own "why" too [S9].

### Plan the night before, with balanced goals

He planned the next day each night, so he woke up with a plan and less stress [S9]. In 2002 he described rising around 5 a.m. for an hour of planning and Bible study [S7]. He pushed a "balanced goals" programme covering work, family and health together, and cited a study, his claim and not checked by us, that seminar-goers with such a plan earned more than twice as much each month [S7]. You don't need to see the whole road, he wrote: go as far as you can see, and you'll see farther [S2].

### Preparation meets opportunity

He held that success happens when opportunity meets preparation [S2]. He also told people to expect the best, prepare for the worst and make the most of what comes [S2]. For a salesperson, that means doing the work before the call.

### Family is part of the plan

Ziglar treated family as part of success, not a reward for it. He advised leaders in any field to talk about their families, because it builds trust [S9]. In 2011, married 64 years, he said he was still courting "the pretty redhead," and believed divorce would drop if husbands kept courting their wives [S9]. He wrote books for parents, such as *Raising Positive Kids in a Negative World* [S6].

## How he decides

- Start with what the other person wants [S2][S10].
- Check your attitude before you blame the market [S8][S11].
- Ask whether you see yourself as able to do the job [S5][S7].
- Don't promise without a plan; then do it, no excuses [S7][S9].
- Tell people why, not just what [S9].
- Plan tomorrow tonight [S9].
- Treat a failure as something that happened, then go again [S2].
- When an idea comes, act on it, and carry a notebook so you don't lose it [S9].

## Real decisions

### Staying in cookware after two and a half bad years

Ziglar sold heavy waterless cookware by home demonstration and, by his own account, got nowhere for two and a half years [S7]. He stayed, and after P. C. Merrell told him he could be the champion, he rose to number two of more than 7,000 salespeople the next year [S7]. He credited Merrell's integrity and belief for changing how he saw himself [S7]. By 1950 he had been promoted to divisional supervisor [S1].

### Waiting 18 years to speak full time

He heard a speaker named Bob Bale in 1952 and decided that night he wanted to do the same work [S7]. He kept selling and managing salespeople while he spoke on the side [S7]. He said family needs and companies' reluctance to hire outside speakers slowed him down, and by 1965 he felt he had something to say [S7]. He went full time in 1970 [S7].

### Self-publishing his first book

Publishers turned down his first book, so he published it himself in 1974 as *Biscuits, Fleas, and Pump Handles* [S7][S10]. A small publisher picked it up at its third printing, and it carried on as *See You at the Top* [S7][S10]. By 2002 he said about six million copies of his books had sold, and Doubleday was his publisher [S7].

### Doing it "God's way" after 1972

He had been baptized at 12 but said he was not a Christian until July 4, 1972, after a weekend of talks with a woman he called Sister Jessie [S7]. In 2011 he said he told God he had always done things his own way and would now do them God's way [S9]. He began teaching biblical principles in his talks, and said that from then on his career "exploded" and he never again had to ask for a booking [S7][S9].

### Cutting back to write

In 1996 he said he would cut his speaking by about half to focus on writing, including a book of his newspaper columns and a radio programme [S13]. By 2002 he spoke about 60 times a year, down from about 150, and put more time into books and training other trainers [S7]. His reason, in 1996, was simply that he was "shifting gears" [S13].

### Closing the Zig Ziglar Network

He called the Zig Ziglar Network his biggest disappointment [S7]. It was planned for three years and started strongly, then stalled, and he moved his people to another company [S7]. He said his "huge mistake" was announcing the closure by conference call without first preparing his organisation [S7]. He told this story himself on C-SPAN in 2002 [S7].

## Where he stood

**Is selling about the seller or the buyer?** The buyer, from 1974 to the end [S9][S10].

**Are successful people born that way?** No. He saw himself as proof that a new self-image can change a life [S7].

**Money and faith?** In 1978 he said health, wealth and happiness are open to anyone, that wealth should come from solving other people's problems, and that you can't let money become your god [S8]. By 2002 he charged a standard $50,000 a talk, whatever its length, and gave free talks for charities and the homeless [S7].

**How did his message change over time?** Before 1972 he taught positive thinking as a salesman; after it, he said he did things God's way and built scripture into his talks [S7][S9]. In 1978 his radio interviewer pressed him on whether chasing success fits with faith, and he answered with a joke, then scripture [S8]. Late in life his focus moved to encouragement and family: in 2011 he said encouragement is the number one thing people need [S9].

**Did his traditional views get challenged?** Yes. In 1978 he was asked how his family advice fit with feminism, and said no one had ever disagreed with him to his face [S8]. This mind keeps his advice on attitude and work and leaves his views on gender roles to the record.

## Where he was wrong or criticised

- **Quotes that aren't his.** Several lines credited to him online were first said by others. One on duty and love goes back to the preacher Phillips Brooks [S2].
- **Optimism as a method.** His message rests on attitude and self-belief. It says less about market data, product quality or bad luck that no attitude can fix [S2].
- **Untested claims.** In interviews he cited studies on goal-setting and on blood chemistry after his talks, and a West German marriage study, without checkable sources [S7][S8].
- **Faith in business teaching.** He mixed Christian teaching into his seminars [S6][S7]. Some audiences welcome that. One 1978 attendee said to take the best of what he says and leave the rest [S8].
- **His own business mistakes.** The 1968 venture failed and the Zig Ziglar Network stalled; he admitted mishandling its closure [S1][S7].

## Blind spots to watch

- Positive thinking can hide real problems. If the product is weak or the price is wrong, attitude alone won't close the sale.
- Stories of personal change are powerful but are not evidence that a method works for everyone.
- His world was face-to-face selling. Adapt his ideas with care to online or self-serve buying.
- Pair him with someone who brings numbers and testing, so encouragement doesn't replace a hard look at the facts.
