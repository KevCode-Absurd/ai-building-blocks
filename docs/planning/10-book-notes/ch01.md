## 1. The chapter in brief

The Preface sets out the book's method: focus on fundamentals rather than tools, start with the simplest solution and add complexity only when a problem demands it, and judge what will last by asking whether a problem is a basic limit of how models work or something better models will remove. Chapter 1 then makes four arguments that matter to us:

1. **Scale created "model as a service".** Only a few labs can train foundation models, so everyone else builds on top of them through APIs. That is exactly our position (API-only, four labs).
2. **Most of the action is in the application layer**: evaluation, prompts and context, and the interface. Model training and infrastructure matter much less for a team like ours.
3. **Before building, ask whether you should**: why build it, whether AI is needed, whether you can buy it instead, what role AI and humans play, what "useful" means in numbers, how hard the last mile is, and how you will keep it working while models and prices keep changing.
4. **Product first, data and models later.** With ready-made models you can ship the product first and only invest in data and model work once it shows promise. This is our ladder, and our "Make it yours" order (prompt → context → data → train last), described in other words.

Why it matters: the chapter confirms our API-only, evaluation-first, ladder-shaped plan. It also exposes three gaps. We have no "should you use AI for this / is it already built in" check on listings. We have no plan for a feedback loop when we store no user data. And we have no risk entry for a lab or platform giving away the same library free.

## 4. What it changes in what we already wrote

**Agreements (the book confirms us):**
- **05 §14 / 09 step 10 "finetune almost never":** the book's prompt-vs-finetune trade-off, and its advice to start at the application layer and move down only as needed, both back this. Finetuning is for new tasks or strict latency and cost needs, and only after the product works.
- **05 §16 and the 12 steps:** the book's central claim is that evaluation is the hardest and most important part of the application layer. Our factory is mostly an evaluation pipeline, which is right.
- **FINAL_PLAN §12 "no router" and class-not-model pages:** the Maintenance section's warnings (the best option today becomes the worst tomorrow; vendors fold) support keeping few third-party dependencies and owning our small tools.
- **The ladder and 09's order** are the book's product-first workflow.
- **No user data stored** fits the book's points on regulation (GDPR cost) and enterprise caution about data privacy.
- **Affiliate rule "ranking from our tests only"** is backed by the benchmark-prompting example.

**Corrections and sharpenings:**
- **FINAL_PLAN §12 steps 5–8:** add a *baseline run* (original + plain ask) before rewriting, and a *stop rule* on the fix loop. As written, step 8 has no measured start and no end.
- **06 §3.1 `run-test`:** log time to first token, total time, output tokens and sampling settings separately, not just "time". Enforce the fair-comparison rule across labs.
- **06 §10 re-tests:** add price-change and deprecation triggers. Put `as_of` and prices in `_models.yaml`, shared with the AI run cost mini tool.
- **05 §21 checklist:** routines get a higher quality bar than prompts (proactive logic). Today the routine column has the same gate as the others.
- **05 §1 / §6 agents:** add the Crawl–Walk–Run graduation rule for *non-risky* actions, measured by acceptance rate. Restate that risky actions never graduate.
- **05 §11 debug order:** put "generation settings → instructions → context → model" in front as the first-level triage.

**Gaps:**
- **No "should you use AI for this / is it built in already?" check** anywhere: not in the 12 steps, the intake engine's Decide stage (08 §4) or listing pages. Fix with `built_in_alternatives`, the plain-ask control, and a planner skill (section 6).
- **No feedback loop design** that works with "no user data stored". Fix with the triage routine that recreates failures on public test brands.
- **FINAL_PLAN §15 has no "platform absorbs us" risk.** Add it, with the response above.
- **FINAL_PLAN §9 scorecard** tracks volume (returning users) but not satisfaction. Add "it worked" rate per listing next to the north star.
- **09** has no lesson on *deciding whether to build* or on *setting a usefulness threshold before building*. Both belong before step 1 (see section 6, lesson 0).
- **05 §18 consulting intake** asks for a success number but not latency or cost ceilings or the AI/human role. Extend it with the `plan` and `usefulness` questions.

## 5. What we should not adopt (and why)

- **Pre-training, our own models, GPU clusters, serving, inference engines (model development and infrastructure layers).** We are API-only, one founder plus Claude, and free-first. The book itself says API users get inference optimisation from the provider. Never.
- **Dynamic, per-user features (per-user finetunes, a memory we hold).** They need user data on our side, which breaks the decided no-data rule. Personalisation lives in the user's own brand brain and AI account. Never on our side.
- **A data moat from usage data.** The book's startup moat assumes you collect usage data. We won't. Our substitute is our own test evidence plus public, voluntary signals. Don't let analytics creep into storing inputs or outputs.
- **Hosted conversational bots on our site** (support bot, "ask the library" chat that keeps transcripts). These need transcript storage and add moderation load. Later at most, and only stateless.
- **Companion, NPC, 3D and voice interfaces.** Off-mission for business building blocks. Never.
- **Browser extension and chat-app bots as delivery formats.** Interesting interfaces, but each is a product to maintain and secure. Later, only if comments ask for it repeatedly.
- **"Business continuity" or fear-of-missing-out framing in marketing.** It conflicts with our plain, honest tone and with "nothing promises a result". Never.
- **Repeating GitHub stars or lab benchmark numbers as quality signals** in tool guides. The book shows benchmarks swing with prompting method. Use only our own tests.
- **Mass content generation (SEO farms, fake reviews, auto-written guidebooks)**, either as listings or for our own site. It breaks our zero-invented-claims rule and the trust our pages depend on. Never.
- **Fully automated external actions ("Run" stage) for risky actions**, even with high acceptance rates. Our hard rule wins over the book's graduation logic. Never.
- **Embedding-based image search for references** at launch. It's useful later (05 §11 reranking), but it isn't needed for v0 and costs build time. Later.

## 6. What we teach users (Learn / Make it yours)

Ordered beginner → expert, plain words.

0. **"Should I use AI for this?" (new, before 09 step 1, Beginner).** Five questions on one card: why (save time, save money, grow sales); is it already built into a tool you use; does the job still work if the AI part fails (complementary) or not (critical, so check more); will you ask for it or should it come to you (reactive vs proactive); who checks it before anyone else sees it. Delivered as a **planner skill** ("Should I build this?") that also feeds our consulting intake (05 §18) and our own intake Decide stage. Launch.
1. **Where AI saves you time this week (Beginner).** The exposure idea: list your tasks, mark the ones AI might halve, and get the matching listing and ladder step. Launch.
2. **Start inside, then go outside (Beginner).** Use AI first on things only you read (summaries, analysis, drafts), then on things customers see, with the extra checks. Launch.
3. **Why the same prompt gives different answers (Beginner).** Models predict likely next words; they don't look up the truth. So check facts, and do sums and prices in a calculator. Links to the mini tools. Launch.
4. **Spot the mistakes (Beginner, extends 09 step 1).** An AI output with planted errors (a wrong price, an invented review, an off-brand line) to find with the checklist. Launch.
5. **What "good enough" means before you start (Intermediate).** Write down: how long it takes you by hand today, how good it must be, how fast, how much per run. That's your usefulness threshold, and it decides whether to keep going. Launch.
6. **Demos are quick, products are slow (Intermediate).** The 80% → 95% story in our own words, with our real hours from Gate 0. Budget for the last mile, and stop after a few fix rounds if it isn't getting there. Launch (after Gate 0 data).
7. **"Training" vs giving context (Intermediate, opens 09 step 7).** Projects, Gems, custom GPTs and brand brains give the model context; they don't train it. Training changes the model and comes last. Answers the "train ChatGPT on my writing" search. Feb–Apr 2027.
8. **The context ladder, shown on one product description (Intermediate).** Plain prompt → with examples → with your brand voice → with your review digest; finetuning only as a last resort. Launch (first three stages).
9. **Climb one step at a time (all levels).** Product first: use it by asking; once it's proven, connect the data; then schedule it; then build an app. Never start with the app. Launch, on every ladder page.
10. **Scheduled reports need a higher bar (Intermediate–Advanced).** Nobody asked for the 9 am report, so a bad one gets ignored. Use cheaper batch runs, and switch it off if you stop acting on it. Apr 2027.
11. **Fair model comparisons (Advanced, extends 09 step 3).** Same prompt, same examples, same settings for every model, or the test is rigged. The benchmark story makes the point. Feb–Apr 2027.
12. **Letting an agent do more, safely (Expert).** Start with suggestions only; log what you approve unchanged; let it act alone only on low-risk actions after a long clean run; never for money, publishing or sending. May 2027.
13. **Is your AI feature defensible? (Expert, for the /saas board).** Technology, distribution and data moats, and the risk that the model maker absorbs your feature. Apr 2027 with /saas.

## 7. Questions for Kevin

1. **Plain-ask control and built-in alternatives.** Should every listing be tested against "just ask the model in one sentence" (mid class only, roughly +1 run per golden input, a small credit cost) and be withheld if it doesn't clearly beat that? And should listing pages say openly when Shopify, Meta or Gmail already does a version of the job, even though it sometimes sends people elsewhere? I recommend yes to both: they are the honest answer to "is AI / is this listing needed?".
2. **Fix-loop stop rule and Gate B.** If Gate 0 shows the last mile (the fix loop) takes most of the hours per listing, as the book predicts, which gives way: the Gate B counts (50+ general, 25+ /ecommerce) or the 19 January date? I recommend cutting counts and keeping the date, with a 3-round stop rule per listing.
3. **Feedback without user data.** The book's improvement loop runs on production data. I propose we close it by recreating reported failures on public test brands, storing nothing. Do you also want an **opt-in** "share this failure" form where users paste a cleaned example? It would be the first user content we keep beyond comments, so it's your call against the no-data rule. I recommend no for v0, and revisiting with site v1.
4. **New planner listing at launch.** Add the "Should I build this?" planner skill (lesson 0) to the launch set and reuse it in the consulting intake and our intake engine's Decide stage? It's low effort and high value for beginners. I recommend yes.
