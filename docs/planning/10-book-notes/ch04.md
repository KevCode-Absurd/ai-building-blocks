# Chapter 4: Evaluate AI Systems: notes and how we use it

> Our mapping of one chapter of Chip Huyen's *AI Engineering* (O'Reilly, 2025) onto our stack, written 2026-10-18.
> The reader's section-by-section summary of the book (section 2) is left out on purpose, so sections run 1, 3, 4…; these notes keep only how the ideas apply to us.
> Book section names are given so the original can be found. The master is `../10_AI_ENGINEERING_FRAMEWORK.md`; where they differ, it wins.

## 1. The chapter in brief

Chapter 3 covered *how* to score outputs (exact checks, similarity, AI judges, comparisons). Chapter 4 turns that into practice in three parts. **First**, the criteria worth scoring: domain capability, generation quality (mainly factual consistency and safety), instruction following, and cost and latency. **Second**, how to choose a model. Filter on hard constraints, use public benchmarks only to shortlist (they are often contaminated and rarely match your task), then decide on your own tests. This part also covers whether to host a model or use an API. **Third**, how to build an evaluation pipeline you can trust. Evaluate each component as well as the end result. Write a guideline and a rubric with examples. Tie scores to business outcomes. Pick methods per criterion. Build sliced test sets that are big enough. Then check the pipeline itself for reliability.

Why it matters to us: our whole value claim ("tested ✓", "recommended model", "works on any business") depends on this pipeline. The chapter mostly confirms what `05` §16 and `06` §8–10 already say. It also exposes one serious gap. **Our test sets are far too small to support "cheapest within 5 points of the best" or "same score ±5 on 3 kinds of business."** The book's own sample-size table says a 5-point difference needs roughly 400 scored examples per comparison. We plan 5–20.

## 3. How we apply it: by building-block level

### The sample-size numbers, worked for our sets (used throughout)
The book's table follows *needed examples ≈ 1 / gap²*. Applied to our planned sets, the smallest gap we can reliably detect between two models (95% confidence, rough):

| Scored inputs per model | Smallest detectable gap | Where we use this size |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | ~45 points | `06` §10 prompts and skills ("5 golden inputs × 3 models") |
| 10 | ~30 points | intake engine test set (`08` §9), 10 sources |
| 15–20 | ~22–26 points | `09` §6 and `05` §16 golden sets |
| 5–7 per business | ~40 points | "same score ±5 on 3 kinds of business" (FINAL_PLAN §9) |
| 60 | ~13 points | 20 inputs × 3 businesses, pooled |
| 100 | ~10 points | flagship listings (proposed) |
| ~400 | ~5 points | what "within 5 points" actually needs |

Two more points, both standard statistics rather than the book:
- **Zero failures in n runs doesn't mean zero.** With 0 costliest mistakes in 20 runs, the true rate could still be up to about 15% (the "rule of three": about 3/n). Zero wrong prices is enforced by **code checks on every output at run time**, not proven by the test set. `05` §8 already puts hard rules in code; this is the statistical reason.
- **Paired comparisons help.** Running every model on the *same* inputs and comparing per input usually needs fewer examples than the book's table. It doesn't turn 20 inputs into 400, though, and repeat runs of the same input measure the model's randomness, not variety across inputs.

## 4. What it changes in what we already wrote

**Agreements (the book confirms):**
- `05` §16: quality is a rate; code checks first, then a judge; categories rather than 1–10; versioned rubrics; costliest mistake tracked on its own; golden sets never leaking into examples. All supported (Step 2 rubrics, Iterate, Data contamination).
- `05` §16 "grade the parts too" equals Step 1.
- `06` §10's "one repeat run per model checks consistency" is the book's "run the pipeline twice" reliability check, though the book's version concerns the *judge*, not just the model.
- API-only (FINAL_PLAN §12) is well supported by the seven axes for a one-person, free-first team.
- `05` §14's caution about the provider's terms when distilling is backed by the licence section and footnote 12.

**Corrections:**
1. **"Recommended = cheapest within 5 points of the best" is not measurable at our sizes** (FINAL_PLAN §12, `06` §10). With 5 inputs we can only see ~45-point gaps; with 20, ~22. Proposed rewrite:
   - *Recommended* = the cheapest model that (a) has zero costliest mistakes, (b) meets `hard` on every business slice, and (c) is **not clearly worse** than the best, meaning the bootstrap interval for the gap doesn't sit entirely above 5 points.
   - The page states the test size honestly: "on 20 inputs; gaps under ~20 points can't be told apart."
   - Flagship listings (the brand brain builder, the static ad generator, the BFCM pack) get 100 inputs, which detect ~10 points.
2. **"Same score ±5 on 3 kinds of business"** (FINAL_PLAN §9, six things to prove) has the same problem: at 5–7 inputs per business, we can only detect ~40-point differences. Restate it as "meets `hard` on each business, and no business is clearly worse (interval) than the others". Alternatively, accept ±15 at 20 inputs per business.
3. **`04` §6 step 1 says "the same example input"** (singular). This conflicts with `06` §10 (5 inputs) and `05` §16 (10–30). Align on the golden set.
4. **`06` §10 "the guessed class plus one below and one above"** can leave the top model out, so "best" isn't known. Always include top.
5. **`09` step 2 "every listing's own test set as a download"** creates contamination risk. Publish a sample; keep a hold-out.
6. **`05` §16 "spot-check the judge against human grades"** is too vague. Set it: about 20 outputs per section, judge temperature 0, agreement target, and a re-check whenever the judge model or rubric version changes.

**Gaps the chapter fills:**
- No **usefulness threshold** per listing (the `hard` line), and no link from score to user value. Add "usable without edits".
- No **out-of-scope** or **messy-input** slices in any golden set.
- No **per-slice reporting** (Simpson's paradox risk when pooling 3 businesses).
- No **experiment tracking fields** for the judge (model, prompt version, temperature).
- No **criterion correlation check** on the inherited 8-criteria judge.
- No **P90 latency** or must-have/nice-to-have latency flag.
- `08` intake targets ("8 in 10") rest on a 10-source set too small to tell 70% from 90%.

## 5. What we should not adopt (and why)

- **Self-hosting or open-weight fine-tuning.** Six of the seven axes favour APIs for us. The one that would favour self-hosting, on-device privacy, is already handled by "runs on the user's machine with their key". Never at this stage.
- **Specialised NLI or toxicity classifier models** (the DeBERTa scorer, Perspective-style models). They need hosting or extra services. Our claims are checked against a register by code plus a judge from another lab. Later at most, and only if judge cost becomes a problem.
- **Full SelfCheckGPT** (N extra generations per output). Too expensive under $2/run; use the free cross-model version instead.
- **SAFE with live web search.** Our rule is local grounding only, and global fact-checking invites scraping and unreliable sources. Items shouldn't make world claims at all.
- **Public benchmark scores or leaderboard ranks on listing pages.** They're contaminated and not about our tasks; they would mislead the people we're trying to help.
- **Running public benchmark suites ourselves.** HELM-scale costs are out of reach and the results wouldn't be relevant.
- **Logprob-based confidence as a core method.** Claude's API doesn't expose logprobs (verify), and the others vary. It's fine as an optional extra in OpenAI/Gemini judge runs, but not something the pipeline depends on.
- **Production human review at LinkedIn scale.** We store no user data and have no hosted runs at launch. Our production signal is votes, comments and voluntary shared results.
- **Engagement or stickiness metrics as the goal.** The book's own warning about addictive design fits our free, evergreen stance. "Weekly returning users who take a listing" is fine as a north star only alongside "it worked" votes.
- **400-input sets for every listing.** Unaffordable across hundreds of listings and against the $25 round cap. Use sized tiers (prompt 5, skill 20, flagship 100) and honest labels instead.

## 6. What we teach users (Learn / Make it yours)

**Beginner**
- *"Decide what good looks like before you start."* Write 3–5 yes/no questions for your output before you write the prompt (evaluation-driven development; INFOBench style). The download is a yes/no checklist template.
- *"Correct isn't the same as useful."* The LinkedIn job-fit example in our words: an answer can be true and still not help. Add "does it tell me what to do next?" to every checklist.
- *"Your AI is most likely to make things up when…"* you ask about something obscure or something that doesn't exist. Test it on purpose: ask for a quote the founder never gave.

**Intermediate**
- *"Let code check what code can check."* Character limits, counts, banned words, format, with a one-page list of checkable instructions drawn from the IFEval idea in our own words.
- *"Your test set needs a mix":* normal cases, hard cases, out-of-scope requests, and inputs with typos. And: if you care about it, give it a test.
- *"Two kinds of made-up":* contradicting your facts (a wrong price) versus adding facts you never gave (an invented benefit). Check both.
- *"Don't trust the average"*, with Simpson's paradox shown on a three-business example, and the habit of looking at each slice.

**Advanced**
- *"How many test cases do you need?"* The 1/gap² rule in plain words: 10 cases spot huge differences, 100 spot a 10-point difference, about 400 spot 5. Our pages say how many we used. Pair it with a **bootstrap mini tool** in the eval kit: paste scores, get a range.
- *"Pick a model like a pro":* list your must-haves, drop models that fail them, try the top model first to see what's possible, then step down until quality drops, and split the task before giving up on a cheap model.
- *"Check your checker":* run the judge twice, grade 20 yourself, and compare. Set the judge's temperature to 0 and write down its version.

**Expert**
- *"Leaderboards and why they lie a little"*: contamination, saturation, averaging. Use them to shortlist, never to decide.
- *"API or your own model?"*: the seven questions in our own words, including where your data goes with each lab and why silent model updates mean re-testing.
- *"Training on another model's outputs"*: check the lab's terms first (feeds `09` step 10).
