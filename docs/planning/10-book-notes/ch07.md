> The reader's section-by-section summary of the book (section 2) is left out on purpose, so sections run 1, 3, 4…; these notes keep only how the ideas apply to us.
> Book section names are given so the original can be found. The master is `../10_AI_ENGINEERING_FRAMEWORK.md`; where they differ, it wins.

## 1. The chapter in brief

Finetuning changes a model's weights. Prompting, context and tools (chapters 5 and 6) leave the weights alone. The chapter makes four points. (1) Finetuning comes after prompting has been pushed hard and measured, never first. Most "prompting doesn't work" stories turn out to be badly run prompt experiments. (2) Pick between retrieval and finetuning by the kind of failure: missing or stale facts need retrieval, wrong format, relevance or style needs finetuning. If you have both kinds, start with retrieval. (3) Memory is what makes finetuning hard. Gradients and optimizer states multiply the memory each trainable parameter needs, so most techniques (PEFT, LoRA, quantization, QLoRA) exist to shrink the number of trainable parameters or the bits per value. (4) The training run itself is the easy part. The hard parts are the data, the evals, serving the model, and keeping it ahead of new base models that keep getting better.

Why it matters to us: we are API-only, so about two-thirds of the chapter (memory math, number formats, LoRA internals, merging) is background we will never run ourselves. The remaining third is directly useful. It confirms 05 §14 ("almost never"), gives us a failure-type test to wire into `grade` and the step 8 fix loop, supplies the inputs for the "fine-tune or prompt?" break-even calculator, and gives Make it yours step 10 a clear structure: should you, build pairs, train via API, distil, check before and after, retire. It also says plainly why distilling for our own listings doesn't pay: users run on their own key, so we have no volume to save on.

**The break-even calculator, concretely** (one formula file for `build-mini-tool`, all inputs editable, example prices labelled):

Inputs:
- `V` runs per month; `H` horizon in months.
- **Route A, prompted model:** fixed prompt tokens `Pf` (instructions + examples), variable tokens `Pv`, output tokens `O`, cache hit rate `h`, prices `in_A`, `cached_A`, `out_A`.
- **Route B, finetuned model:** shorter fixed prompt `Pf_B`, the same `Pv` and `O`, prices `in_B`, `cached_B`, `out_B`. The user enters whatever the lab charges for a finetuned model; we don't assume it equals the base price.
- **One-off cost F:**
  - pair generation (`n_pairs` × top-model cost per pair);
  - human review of pairs (`hours_review` × hourly rate);
  - training (training tokens × `epochs` × training price);
  - before/after evals (golden-set runs on both routes + judge);
  - set-up time (`hours_setup` × rate).
- **Recurring cost R:** a retrain plus re-eval every `k` months (new base model, drifted data, a retired base).

Per-run cost:
- `A = Pf·(h·cached_A + (1−h)·in_A) + Pv·in_A + O·out_A`
- `B = Pf_B·(h·cached_B + (1−h)·in_B) + Pv·in_B + O·out_B`

Monthly saving: `S = V·(A − B) − R/k`. Break-even month: `F / S`. Break-even volume over the horizon: `V* = (F/H + R/k) / (A − B)`.

Outputs:
- "Pays back in X months" or, stated plainly, "never pays at this volume";
- the share of the saving that comes from the shorter prompt vs the cheaper model;
- a **quality gate line:** "only valid if the trained model passes the same test set: within 5 points of the prompted version and zero costliest mistakes."

Worked example with **made-up example prices**, review tagging:
- Route A costs $0.01/run on a top model; Route B costs $0.001/run on a trained cheap model.
- F: 500 pairs at $0.01 = $5; 4 h review at $40 = $160; training $20; evals $10; 6 h set-up at $40 = $240. Total ≈ $435.
- R: $100 every 6 months ≈ $17/month.
- At 20,000 runs/month: S ≈ $180 − $17 = $163, so it pays back in about 2.7 months.
- At 1,000 runs/month: S ≈ $9 − $17 < 0, so it **never pays**.

Teaching point: human time dominates F, and volume decides everything.

## 4. What it changes in what we already wrote

**05_PRODUCTION_PRINCIPLES**
- §14 *agrees*. ## 5. What we should not adopt (and why)
- **Any finetuning in our own factory or for our own listings.** We have no inference volume (users run on their own key), and our budget is tens of dollars. Every listing must also work on any of four labs, and a finetuned model ties a listing to one lab and one account.
- **A "house model" or industry model** (an ecommerce model). General models plus brand-brain context beat domain models (BloombergGPT), and a multi-task finetune risks the alignment tax across our many tasks.
- **Memory math, number formats, quantization, QAT/AMP, gradient checkpointing, CPU offloading.** These only matter if you hold the weights. We call APIs. Learn background at most.
- **LoRA configuration, rank and α choices, multi-LoRA serving, QLoRA, soft prompts, IA3 and the like.** Hosted APIs decide these, and the book itself says APIs may not expose all knobs. LoRA's "small adapter on a shared base" is a nice analogy for our skill families (shared core + industry pack), but only an analogy.
- **Model merging, task arithmetic, frankenmerging, upscaling, MoE upcycling, federated learning.** They need open weights and GPUs. They are experimental by the author's own account.
- **Self-run frameworks** (LLaMA-Factory, unsloth, Axolotl, LitGPT, DeepSpeed). We'd need GPUs and open-weight hosting, which breaks API-only and adds ops a two-person team can't carry.
- **Long-context, infilling and continued pre-training.** Not offered to us, and not needed.
- **Bias-mitigation finetuning.** Real, but not our job. Our guardrails are the claims register and banned patterns, enforced in code.
- **A "can I run this model?" mini tool at launch.** The audience doesn't need it. Keep it on the by-demand list.

## 6. What we teach users (Learn / Make it yours)

**Beginner**
- *"Your AI doesn't need training, it needs context."* What finetuning is in one paragraph. Form vs facts. Why the brand brain (facts) and examples (form) come first. The BloombergGPT story in two lines.
- *"Is it a fact problem or a form problem?"* A 2-minute diagnosis. Wrong price, old offer or a made-up claim: fix the brain. Wrong length, format or voice: fix the instructions and examples.

**Intermediate**
- *"Before you even think about training"*: the book's list of why prompt tests fail (vague instructions, examples that don't look like real inputs, no defined score) as a self-check tied to steps 2–4.
- *"How many examples?"* Start with 3–10, go up to about 50. If quality still plateaus on form, and only then, read step 10.

**Advanced (step 9)**
- *"Fine-tune or prompt? Do the sums."* The calculator. Caching already makes long prompts cheap, so the saving comes from moving to a cheaper model. Volume and your own hours decide it. Show the "never pays" case first.
- *"Why training costs more than using a model"* (optional explainer): one training step stores extra numbers for every weight it changes, which is why labs offer small trainable add-ons. Plain words, no formulas.

**Expert (step 10, by demand)**
1. **Should you?** Prerequisites done; failures left are form; high volume; a hundreds-of-pairs source exists; the calculator says yes.
2. **Build pairs.** Start from your own graded passes (code checks + a judge from another lab). Strip personal data. Replace prices, dates and claims with slots. Keep your test set out of the pairs.
3. **Train on your lab's API.** Pick a supported base model and start with the defaults. Read the training and validation loss: both falling means more data or epochs could help; validation rising means fewer epochs. Change one knob at a time, whichever ones the API exposes.
4. **Distil (same lab).** The top model with your best prompt makes outputs. Grade them, keep only the passes, and train that lab's small model on them. The book's progression path in miniature: debug the run on the cheapest model, then go up.
5. **Check before and after.** Run the same test set and the same judge on both. Check the trained model's other jobs too, because training one task can hurt others.
6. **Keep it or retire it.** Re-test monthly and when a new model ships. Retire it when the prompted base model is within 5 points with zero costliest mistakes.
- Worked example: the review tagger, measured honestly, including "the prompted cheap model already passed, so we didn't train".
- A "needed or wanted?" sidebar: training to learn the skill is fine, but call it practice, not a business case.
