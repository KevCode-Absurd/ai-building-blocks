How things are recommended

The stage and the persona are yours to choose: nothing is recommended for them. Everything else follows from them, and changes the moment you change a choice.

  • Product: what the persona buys. Where none is named on the persona, the brand's main product.
  • Angle: the angles written for the persona, and among them the ones that suit the stage, where an angle names a stage.
  • Offer: for an unaware buyer, no offer. For everyone else, the offers for the product, written for the persona and suited to the stage.
  • Format: the formats that suit the stage.

Nothing is blocked: anything can be chosen with anything. To change what an item is recommended for, open it with the pencil and tick the personas and stages it suits.

The lie I told my doctor for fifteen years

Edit

Script

I lied to my doctor for fifteen years. Every year at my checkup she'd ask how often I went to the bathroom. I'd say, 'Every day,' and I'd say it fast so she'd move on to the next question. The real answer was not every day, and it wasn't close, and I'd told that lie so many times it stopped feeling like one. I thought this was just my body, like how some people get headaches. Here's what fifteen years of that looks like. I knew every bathroom on my commute, and I knew which ones nobody ever used so I could take my time. By the end of the workday my pants felt tight and my stomach felt heavy, and I'd blame the sandwich I ate at lunch. So I stopped buying anything with a waistband that sat right there. My husband would ask if I was okay every time I came out of the bathroom, and I'd say, 'I'm fine,' because what was I going to say. I planned dinners out around what I'd already eaten that day. And I'd sit in the car outside my own house for a minute before I went in, because I needed the minute. Of course I tried things. I tried the coffee first thing every morning, which worked for about a week and then quit on me. I tried a green powder that tasted like a lawn, mixed with water in a shaker I still have. I tried prunes, and I can't look at a prune anymore. There was a jar of fiber gummies from the drugstore that I finished completely and got nothing from. There was a bacteria pill that had to live in the fridge and cost more than my phone bill. I tried walking after dinner too, which I still do most nights. Most of them seemed to work for a few days, and then it was Monday again and nothing had moved. So I decided my body was just built this way. The part I never say out loud is that you start making deals with yourself. If I don't go today, I can't eat anything tomorrow. I sat through a whole wedding reception thinking about my stomach instead of the bride. So when my friend Dana said it on the phone, I nearly missed it. She was in the middle of talking about her kitchen. Then she said she goes every single day now, and I said, 'Yeah, well, you've always been lucky.' 'No,' she said. 'I used to be exactly like you.' That got me, because she and I have the same kind of stomach and we've been joking about it for years. She told me about Grüns. Grüns is a little daily pack of gummies, a small handful in each one. It's tested by an outside lab for 70 pesticides, which mattered to me once I was eating it every morning. I didn't believe her. I've spent so much money on this exact hope that a gummy was the least likely thing on my list. But it was one pack a day and I already eat breakfast, so I got some. The first couple of weeks, nothing. I remember standing at the counter thinking, great, another one for the shelf. And then one morning I realized I'd gone the day before, and then the day after that too. I stood there trying to remember the last time that happened. It was a while ago now. I don't know where the bathrooms are on my commute anymore, because I don't need to know. It was the first time in fifteen years that I felt normal, and the strangest part was how boring it was. I just went, and then I went to live my day. So if you've been telling yourself your stomach is just built this way, and there's a drawer of things that didn't work, take a look at Grüns. One pack a day is the whole thing.

I lied to my doctor for 15 years

Primary text

My doctor has asked me the same question at every checkup for fifteen years, and for fifteen years I've given her the same answer. I say, "Every day," and I say it fast, and then I ask her about my blood pressure or the weather or anything else so she'll move on down her list. She writes something on her clipboard and we both let it go for another year. The real answer wasn't every day. It wasn't close, and I'd told that lie so many times that it had stopped feeling like one. I thought this was just my body, the way some people get headaches or bad knees, and I figured everybody around me had their own private thing they didn't talk about either. Here's what those fifteen years were like. I knew every bathroom on my commute, and I knew which ones nobody ever used, so I could take my time and not hear somebody jiggling the handle outside the door. I knew the one on the third floor of my office building that stayed quiet after lunch, and the gas station on the way home that was clean enough if I got desperate. That's a strange thing to be good at. By the end of the workday my pants felt tight and my stomach felt heavy, and I'd blame the sandwich I ate at lunch, or the pasta from the night before, or the stairs. So I stopped buying anything with a waistband that sat right there, and I started buying the kind of pants that forgive you. It worked, in the sense that I stopped noticing it as much. My husband would ask if I was okay every single time I came out of the bathroom, and I'd say, "I'm fine," because what was I going to say. He wasn't being nosy. He could tell something was off with me, and he was right, and I didn't want to get into it at nine at night. I planned dinners out around what I'd already eaten that day. If we were going somewhere small with one bathroom and a long wait, I'd think about that first and pick my food second. And sometimes when I got home I'd sit in the car outside my own house for a minute before I went in, because I needed the minute. My keys were still in my hand and the radio was still on, and nobody was waiting on me yet. Of course I tried things. Everybody tries things. I tried coffee first thing in the morning, which worked for about a week and then quit on me, so I blamed the coffee and went back to drinking it for fun. I tried a green powder that tasted like a lawn, mixed with water in a shaker I still have in the back of a cabinet. I tried prunes until I couldn't look at a prune anymore. There was a jar of gummies from the drugstore that I finished all the way to the bottom and got nothing from, and there was a pill that had to live in the fridge and cost more than my phone bill. I walked after dinner most nights, which I still do. Most of them seemed to be working for a few days. Then it was Monday again and nothing had moved, and I'd put the bottle back in the cabinet with the others and stop thinking about it for a while. The part that wore me down was the hoping and then the not hoping. So after enough of those, I decided my body was just built this way. I stopped calling it a problem, because if it was just how I was made, then there was nothing to fix and nothing to be disappointed about. That was easier than trying. The part I never say out loud is that you start making deals with yourself. If I don't go today, then tomorrow I can't eat anything, and I'll drink a lot of water, and I'll make up for it. I sat through a whole wedding reception thinking about my stomach instead of the bride, and I can remember the centerpieces and almost nothing else from that night. Then my friend Dana said something on the phone and I almost missed it. She was in the middle of telling me about her kitchen, the counters and the drawers and some shelf she was having put in, and then she said she goes every single day now. I said, "Yeah, well, you've always been lucky," and I meant it as a joke, and she said, "No. I used to be exactly like you." That got me, because Dana and I've the same kind of stomach and we've been joking about it for years. We're the two people in our group who know where the bathroom is in every restaurant we walk into. So when she said that, I listened, and she told me about Grüns. Grüns is a little daily pack of gummies, a small handful in each one, and it's tested by an outside lab for 70 pesticides, which mattered to me once I was eating it every morning. I didn't believe her. I've spent so much money on this exact hope that a gummy was the last thing on my list I'd have bet on. But it was one pack a day and I already eat breakfast, so I got some. The first couple of weeks, nothing happened. I remember standing at my counter one morning thinking, great, another one for the shelf with the rest of them. And then one morning I realized I'd gone the day before, and then the day after that too. I stood there trying to remember the last time that had happened, and I couldn't, because it had been years. That was a while ago now. I don't know where the bathrooms are on my commute anymore, because I don't need to know. It was the first time in fifteen years I felt normal, and the strangest part was how boring it was. I just went, and then I went and lived my day like it was nothing. So if you've been telling yourself your stomach is just built this way, and you've got a drawer or a shelf of things that didn't work, you could find out what Grüns is, a little daily pack of gummies that you eat with breakfast. It's one pack a day with breakfast, and for me it's what finally got me going every day.
