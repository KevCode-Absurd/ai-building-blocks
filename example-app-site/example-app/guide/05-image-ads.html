Image ads from winning layouts

Guide, chapter 5 of 20

The fastest route to a set of static ads: pick a product, pick layouts that have worked for other brands, and the app writes each ad for your brand and renders it on your real product.

4 ways to start an ad

Go to Create, then Ads, then From a winning layout. (The other 3 ways start from a script: see chapters 4 and 8.)

Step 1: set up the set

Pick the product, its photos, the layouts, the sizes

  1. Which product. From the brand brain.
  2. Product photos. The screen says how many of the product's photos go with every picture ("2 of 9") and marks them Sent. The app picks 1 for each view it finds: as it is sold from the front, 1 single unit (1 sachet, 1 bar) out of the pack it is sold in, out of its pack, in a hand, in use, close up. Press a photo to add it or take it out, up to 4. These photos tell the image model what the product is: its shape, its colours and its label. They do not set how it is posed, so the pictures of a set do not all show the same pack the same way. An image with words designed onto it, with a child in it or with a licensed character in it is never offered as the product.
  3. Your brand's own images. Below the product photos: the images the brand designed itself (they show how it dresses a picture) and its own photographs (its people, places and light). The app picks up to 6 of the first and 4 of the second, this product's first, and never a photograph that shows another product. They are shared out across the pictures: each picture is sent 1 or 2, a different one each time. Press Choose from all to change the pick. This list appears once the brand's images have been read, which the first set of ads does. The pictures pulled from the site's own pages are here too (chapter 2): the ones from this product's page come first, the ones kept for the whole brand are offered for every product, and a picture from a product's page that shows the product clearly is offered above, as a product photo. A picture nobody has looked at yet is not offered; the screen says how many are waiting.
  4. Which ad layouts. The app suggests 1 layout per ad type that suits the product's category and pack. For each you can:
    • swap it for another from the library (the two-arrows icon),
    • move it up or down, or remove it,
    • write a direction: what this ad should argue, who it is for, anything to stress or avoid.
  5. Sizes. 4:5 for the feed, 1:1, 9:16 for stories. Each size is composed for its own shape, not cropped.
  6. Notes for the whole set. A campaign, a season, an offer to lead with.

Press Write the plan. Nothing is rendered yet.

The direction box is the most useful control on this screen. "Write this one for the parent who is tired of the dinner fight, problem aware, no price" gets a sharper ad than no direction.

Step 2: what the app does

flowchart TD
  P[The writer writes each ad: buyer, argument, picture, words, feed copy] --> E[Copy pass sharpens the lines that do not sell]
  E --> V[Voice pass fixes wording]
  V --> PR[Each plan becomes an image prompt]
  PR --> S[Read before rendering: would this picture make sense?]
  S --> Y[The plan waits for you]
  Y -. you ask .-> C[Check claims]
  Y -. you ask .-> R[Review these ads]

This takes about 3 minutes for 6 ads and costs about 2 cents an ad.

Who are these for? On the new-set screen you can pick 1 or more personas, and every ad is written for them. Pick none and the writer chooses a buyer for each ad.

Check claims and Ad review are switches on the same screen, both off unless your brand has them on in Settings. With claim checks on, the writer cites a source for every fact, a second model reads every word against the brand's claims, and anything that fails is written again: about 1.5 cents an ad more.

Words that differ, and figures that stay whole

In a test of 50 ads, the texts told 1 story in the same moment and place, and 1 phrase was in 28 of them. 4 things now stand against that.

Each long text starts from its own story. An ad for an unaware or problem aware buyer has a long primary text: the story of the problem. It is written as its own step, 1 ad at a time, after the headlines. Before a word is written, each of these texts in a set is handed where its story starts: a moment, a place, who else is there and a way in, and from the research a theme no other text of the set was handed. It is told the moments the other texts have, so it does not tell them. Everything after the start is the writer's. The moments and places are ordinary life, with the places, people and props the brand brain records mixed in. Sets made one after another carry on through the same list, so 10 sets of 1 ad do not all start in the same kitchen. The versions of a script's primary text (chapter 4) and copy asked for in the chat are handed a start the same way.

Phrases already used are passed as spent. The app reads the brand's 60 newest texts. A run of 3 to 6 words that is in 3 or more of them, or in 2 texts of the set being written, goes to the writer of those same texts as "said already in recent ads, so say it another way". What an ad has to say is never spent: the offer, the guarantee, the product's facts, the claims, the brand's and the products' names, and any figure.

A headline is written for the buyer's stage. Every headline writer (an image ad, its variants, a script's headlines, the chat) is told where the buyer is and what a headline does there.

The buyer isThe feed headline
UnawareTheir own moment or thought, in their words. No product, brand, pack, ingredient, price or offer
Problem awareThe problem as they live it, or what could be different. No product, brand, price or offer
Solution awareThe kind of answer this is, and what is different about it
Product aware, most awareThe product, its proof, its price and its offer are welcome

A figure stays with its unit and with who was counted. A figure about the product, its results, its price, a study or a survey is said as the brand's facts give it. A share of people keeps who was asked: "67% of surveyed customers" is not "67% of customers". A figure keeps its unit: a number given for the pack is not said of 1 piece inside it. The writer is sent the brand's own words for how the product is counted. An age, a time of day or a small count in a story is the writer's to use.

These are checks that flag, not editors. A headline that names the thing for sale to an unaware or problem aware buyer, and a figure that is not in the facts, lost who was asked or changed its unit, are flagged by rule. A line that is flagged is said again, twice at most. A line that passed is never touched. What still fails is a warning on the ad for you to read. Nothing here stops an ad.

Step 3: review the plan

The plan: checks on the words at the top, then each ad with who it is for, the review, the words and the feed copy

Read the plan before you spend on images. For each ad:

  • Title and argument. The 1 thing this ad says.
  • Written for. The persona and where that buyer is in the decision.
  • Ad review. Shown when you asked for it: points from a second model that read the ad as a stranger would. Write again with these sends the points back to the writer.
  • Words on the image. Edit them directly. With claim checks on, the small tag beside a line is the source of its fact: a claim id, an offer id or a theme id.
  • Copy for the feed. The primary text, headline, description and button.
  • The picture. What the photograph shows, and what was changed from the reference and why.
  • Read before rendering. Whether the prompt describes a picture that could exist, and what was fixed.
  • Prompt for the image model. Open it to read or edit the exact prompt.

At the top, Checks on the words. It says whether the set was written freely or is held to the brand's claims, and has 2 buttons: Check claims and Review these ads. Each reads the words as they stand and costs about half a cent an ad.

  • A red line is a failed check, and appears only on a set held to the claims: a forbidden claim, a broken guardrail, a number that differs from the claim it cites. The set cannot be rendered until it is fixed. Edit the words and save.
  • An amber line is a warning: a result no claim covers, filler, a headline that is too long, a quote that is not in the research. You decide.

The ad review is strict. It rarely calls a draft ready, and its points are advice, not a gate. Fix what you agree with.

Step 4: render

Press Render. The button shows the count and the cost (about 15 cents an image at high quality). Each image is rendered with these attached, by role:

  1. the product photo that leads this picture: the view nearest to how the picture shows the product (a picture of a hand leads with the photo in a hand, where there is one). It says what the product is, and that must match exactly. Where the picture shows the product clearly and the ad is about 1 single unit of a product sold as a pack of them (its words say "1 pack a day", or its scene is 1 sachet being opened, held or used), a clean photo of 1 unit on its own leads, where such a photo is on file, and the picture is checked against that photo and not the outer pack. With no such photo the pack leads as before, and the unit stays small beside it,
  2. up to 3 more real photos of the same product, each named for what it shows,
  3. the brand's type and colour specimen,
  4. 1 or 2 of the brand's own images, a different one for each picture of the set,
  5. the library reference, for composition only, where the look lever sends it.

Each finished image is then looked at twice: once for the pack, the words and the brand look, and once for whether the picture makes sense (how the pack is opened, the hands, the scene). An image that fails is rendered once more with the problems as corrections.

How the pictures of a set are kept different

A set is looked at side by side, and 2 pictures taken the same way read as 1. So before the plan is written, each ad is dealt its own camera, its own light and its own way of showing the product (standing, held in a hand, lying flat seen from above, turned a little, among everyday things), and its own place where the brand brain records the brand's places. No 2 ads share a camera or a light until the list runs out. The product is only dealt a way of being shown that a real photo backs: "open, with what is inside in view" is dealt only to a product that has a photo of it open or in use.

Since 2026-10-08, 4 more things are dealt. In 50 ads made 1 to a set, the same woman with her hair in a bun was in 6, the same striped dish towel in 6, and the pack with the headline on top in 6: the planner had been left to choose these, and chose the same ones every time.

Dealt to each adFrom
The person, where the picture has oneAn age (20s to around 70), hair and skin, and a build, each from its own list, so an age does not always come with the same hair. Always an adult. Where the ad's buyer fixes an age or who they are, the buyer stands. A real person on file is never swapped for a dealt one
1 thing in the sceneAn ordinary object (a mug, a set of keys, a houseplant), or one of the brand's own props where the brand brain records them
The ground or backdropEach of the brand's own colours in turn, and a neutral
Where the headline sitsAcross the top, across the bottom, down the left, down the right, or over the quiet part of the scene

Sets made one after another carry on through the same lists, so 10 sets of 1 ad differ as 1 set of 10 does. A carousel is 1 story and is dealt nothing, and so is a set that is 1 ad with new openings.

What is dealt gives way to your direction, to the choice of whether the product is in the picture, and to a format that fixes it. An ad that follows a library layout keeps that layout's camera, its light and where its headline sits, and is dealt the rest. An ad that rebuilds its reference exactly is dealt nothing. An ad cut from a script keeps the place, the person and the things the script's own moment names.

The product never changes: its shape, colours and label are checked on every picture against the photo of it from the front. A picture that fails on the product is mended against that photo, and one drawn again goes back to the photo exactly as it was taken.

Step 5: review the images

A finished set: each image with its check, and a box for feedback

Under each image:

  • Passed the check, Check it or Failed the check, with what was seen.
  • A box for what is wrong. Type it or press Speak. Then:
    • Make again renders the image again with your note as a correction, and keeps the note as a lesson.
    • Saving the note alone keeps it as a lesson without rendering.
  • The download icon saves the image. Click the image to see it at full size.
  • N earlier versions opens the versions made before this one, each with its check and a download.

At the top of the set, Download all N images saves 1 zip of every finished image, named and numbered, with the ad copy as a spreadsheet inside. Ad copy saves the spreadsheet alone: 1 row per ad with its on-image text, primary text, headline, description and button.

What is checked without being asked

Claim checks are a choice. These are not, and they call no model:

  • A figure without the words that limit it ("67% of users" where the claim is "67% of surveyed users"; a first-order price stated as the price).
  • A testimonial that is not, word for word, a customer's words in the research.
  • A price that is not one of the brand's, or not the price of the offer you chose for the set.
  • A founder ad whose words are not in the founder's questionnaire answers.
  • Across the set: 2 ads with nearly the same headline, feed copy that opens on the same sentence, most ads written for 1 buyer, an ad for a buyer you did not choose.

Each is a warning. You decide.

Edit the words on a finished ad

Under every finished picture there is Edit the words. It opens the picture with each line as real type that you can change, instead of words painted into the picture.

  • Change a line. Type the new headline or subheadline. The picture updates as you type.
  • Change its font. The list has the brand's own fonts first, then 3 standard ones: Inter (a clean sans), Playfair Display (a serif) and Anton (tall and heavy). Pick a weight, a size and a colour. The colours offered are the brand's own, plus white and black.
  • Move it. Drag a line to where you want it. It snaps to the middle of the picture when it is close. Pull the handle on its right edge to change where it wraps. The arrow keys move it in small steps.
  • Put a button or a panel behind it. The small rectangle beside the capitals switch puts a filled shape behind a line, sized to its words: a button for "Shop now", a panel for a line that must read over a busy photo.
  • Add or remove a line.
  • Save keeps it as the newest version of this ad. Keep as a variant keeps it under the ad as another version of it with these words, and leaves the editor open: change the headline again and keep the next one. The download button gives you the picture without saving it.

Saving makes no new image, so it costs nothing and takes no time. The words are set on the picture you already have.

The first time, for an ad whose words were painted in. The editor needs the picture without its words. If the set was made with "Also give me each image without its words", it is already there. If not, the editor says so and offers Make this picture editable: the words are taken off once (this makes 1 image and takes about a minute), and from then on every change is free. A photoshoot frame, or an ad made without words, needs nothing: you can add words to it straight away.

The lines open where they were. The first time an ad is opened, the app looks at the picture once and finds each line on it: where it sat, how big it was, its colour, and the button or panel it sat on. The editor opens with the lines in those places, set in the brand's own font. This takes a few seconds ("Putting the lines where they were") and is done once for a picture. Hold Hold to see the original to compare.

Variants: the same picture with other words

Under the picture in the editor is Variants of this ad. An ad is made once. Its variants are the same picture with another headline and subheadline, so you can test many from 1 picture.

  1. Pick how many with the minus and plus buttons, or type a number (1 to 30). It starts at 5.
  2. Press Write 5 variants. The words are written in 1 go, for the buyer this ad is for, in the brand's voice. Every other line on the picture stays as it is.
  3. Each variant appears under the ad, with its words set in the same places, fonts and colours as the ad's. A headline that runs longer than the ad's is set a little smaller, so it does not run into the line below.

No image is made for a variant. Writing 5 costs about what 1 small piece of writing costs, however many you ask for.

For each variant you can:

  • Fix a word. The pencil opens that variant in the editor. Change anything, then Save this variant.
  • Download it, or tick several and Download the ticked as 1 zip. With none ticked, the link downloads them all.
  • Remove it.

The variants stay with their ad. On the set's page they sit in a strip under the ad's picture, and Download all N images at the top of the set includes them, named after their ad.

A variant never states a number the ad and the product do not already state: one that does is not kept, so you may get 1 or 2 fewer than you asked for.

The brand's own fonts. The editor has a box, "The brand's own fonts". Get the fonts from the site reads the font files the brand's website uses and keeps them with the brand. If the site uses a font it does not serve as a file, the box says which one, and you can upload it (WOFF2, WOFF, TTF or OTF). The same box is on the Brand brain screen, under the brand look. A brand's fonts are licensed to the brand: they are kept in its own folder and used only for its own images.

What it does not do yet. The picture without words loses the graphics that carried them: arrows, icons inside a badge and a cut-out outline are not drawn again. A badge comes back as a plain button with its words. The variants are written for the size you have open; the other sizes of the same ad are not set. Ads made from a script on the production board do not have the editor yet. The number of variants is chosen in the editor, after the ad is made, not yet on the screen where an ad is made.

Concept ads for cold audiences

An ad from a layout borrows a structure that has worked. A concept ad has no layout: it is 1 argument for 1 buyer, shown as 1 picture in 1 visual world. It is for people who have never heard of the brand, where the picture has to stop them and the headline has to argue.

Start it from Create, Ads, "A concept ad for cold audiences".

  • Pick the product and how many (4, 6, 8 or 12).
  • Each ad gets a visual world, and no world is used twice in a set. There are 23: photographic (a problem lit like a thriller, a studio comparison, the product as a monument, something absurd shot straight), stylised 3D (clay, a 3D character scene, the body part as a character, a product mascot, a miniature diorama), illustration (a vintage comic panel, a hand sketch, an engraving, a retro game screen, a thermal image, an infographic) and native (a message between 2 people, a photo of a billboard). Other worlds deals a different hand.
  • Each ad gets a mode: creative (stops the scroll), communicative (the picture explains), native (looks like a post) or brand world (the product as a monument).
  • The app finds the argument and the picture for each one from your research, and writes a headline and a line under it of 90 characters or fewer together.

From there it is a set like any other: the plan to read, the images, the checks, feedback. This path is new, so it starts with 4 ads and reads the plan before it renders.

Proof ads and offer ads: for buyers who are not cold

A concept ad argues with a stranger. A buyer who is already comparing options wants proof, and a buyer who knows the product wants the offer. The same screen makes both: at the top, pick who the ads are for.

WhoWhat each ad isThe kinds
Cold1 argument, found from your researchThe app finds it (above)
Comparing1 kind of proofA customer's own words. 1 number that proves it. This against the usual way. The product, labelled
Ready to buyThe offer, said plainlyThe price. The bundle and what it saves. A real deadline. The review that closes it

Start them from Create, "Proof ad for buyers who are comparing" or "Offer ad for buyers who are ready".

Under each row the screen says what that kind needs. The rule is the same for all of them: the ad is made only from what your brand's facts and research hold.

  • A quote is a real customer's words, word for word. No person in the picture is shown as that customer.
  • A number is one your brand's facts state.
  • A price, a saving and a guarantee are the offer's own, and the sum adds up.
  • A deadline is a real one. With no end date on the offer, the deadline ad is not made as a deadline: the plan says so and makes the nearest honest ad.
  • A comparison is against the usual way of doing it, unbranded. No other brand is named.

Read the plan before you render: the line under each ad says what it proves and where the proof came from.

An ad in a creative format: a claymation image, a comic static, a UGC photo

Claymation, comic, UGC, talking object, podcast clip, CGI explainer, satisfying and founder used to be video formats only. Each can now be a still image too.

There are 3 ways to ask for one:

  • On the new set screen, pick it under Creative format. It starts at None, which is an ordinary static ad. Pick Claymation and every ad of the set is a claymation image.
  • In words. Type it in the set's notes or in the chat: "5 claymation ads", "a comic book static", "an AI UGC image". The app reads the format from the words.
  • From a script. When you make a static from a script (Production, Make assets), pick the format in Creative format on the image line. From the terminal: pnpm produce <script id> static_image --as=claymation. Use --as=script to take the format the script was written for.

What each one is as a still:

FormatThe pictureThe words on it
ClaymationA small clay set: 1 character and 1 thing that is plainly wrong or put right1 headline of 8 words or fewer
ComicA page of 1 to 4 panels: the problem, then the turn1 caption of 5 words or fewer in each panel
UGC photoA phone photo someone posted, mid-action with the product1 typed-on caption, or none
Talking objectAn object from the buyer's life with a face1 line in a speech bubble
Podcast clip2 people at microphones, 1 reactingThe line just said, as a subtitle
CGI explainerThe mechanism in 1 rendered picture: a cutaway or a scale shot1 headline and 2 or 3 labels
SatisfyingThe product's 1 motion, frozen just before it resolves1 short line, or none
FounderA real photo of the founder, or a note in their words. Nobody is generated1 decision, signed

The format decides what the picture is made of. The layout you picked still decides what the ad argues, and your brand still decides the colours and the type. A format works best on a concept ad or on an ad from a script, where there is no other brand's photograph to follow.

A named look now delivers that look (2026-10-08). Name a look for the set (cinematic 3D, anime, claymation) and 2 things hold it. First, no ad of the set follows a library layout: a layout is a photograph of another brand's ad, and a picture planned on one stayed a photograph (anime on an ingredient layout came back as a pack shot). The layout's type still says what the ad argues, and an ad you picked yourself as the reference is kept. Second, the check on the finished picture asks whether the whole picture is plainly made in that look, edge to edge. One that is not fails the check, as a wrong product does, and is drawn again once in the look. What is still wrong stays on the picture for you to read. The product keeps its real shape, colours and label inside any look.

Some formats stay images only: a static, a long-copy static, an advertorial static, a carousel and a native ad. The long narrated video (VSL) stays a video only.

How it works underneath: each format's skill is a short shared part and 2 further parts, 1 for images and 1 for video. An image ad is sent the shared part and the image part. A video is sent the shared part and the video part. Nothing is sent both.

These image formats are new and have not been rendered yet. Look at the first few before you make many.

What phase 47 changed in how a picture is made

The choices on the set screen are now the taste bar (chapter 19): how close it stays, the product in the picture, brand styling. Each changes what is sent. 5 things follow from it.

What of a reference is sent.

StopThe reference pictureThe reference in wordsYour own images
Close to my brandNot sentOnly what kind of ad it is, in 1 lineUp to 2 designed images and 2 past ads
In betweenNot sentIts layout, zone by zone, with the colour words taken out1 designed image
Exactly like this referenceAttachedNone: the picture is the layoutNone. Colours, fonts and logo only
Try something newNot sentNoneNone. Colours, fonts and logo only

The forms menu. The planner names the form of each ad from a list of 10: pack hero, life scene, note, comparison, close-up, testimonial, editorial, metaphor, demonstration, type poster. It also lists the forms the brand's own ads already use. At Close to my brand the form must be one the brand uses. In between, it is whichever form makes the argument best. At Try something new it must be a form the brand has not used, and not the pack hero. Code checks the choice, and a plan that broke the rule goes back to the planner once. An ad that follows a reference takes the reference's form.

How much of the frame the product takes. In the scene: 5 to 10% of the frame, in the outer third. Clearly shown: 15 to 30%. The hero: 45 to 65%, on the centre line. These shares are a first estimate, not measured, and in the test the last 2 came out alike.

The logo. It is attached only when all of these are true: the picture has words, brand styling is not None, the finish is not Phone photo, and the product is shown. An ad with no product in it carries no logo. The image model is told to place the logo file, never to redraw or retype it.

A failed picture is mended by the kind of fault.

The fault is aboutWhat happensWhat is sent
Text, colour, the logo, the layout, an objectThe picture is changedThe failed picture, the product photo, the type specimen, the logo and the list of faults
How the product is handled or opened, or its shapeThe picture is drawn again from the planThe plan, with the product photo named as the truth
The look: the picture is not in the named look it was asked forThe picture is drawn again from the planThe plan, told what kind of picture the first one was instead

Measured on 2 mends: a wrong word was fixed by a change. A sachet torn open at the corner was drawn again and still failed. Handling faults are still the hardest to fix; a photo of the product open helps more than words (chapter 9).

From a script. A static made from a script cost 15, 25 and 53 cents in words on 3 runs (clay, comic, native), before the picture itself. That is too much, and it is being trimmed.

Getting better ads

  • Give each ad a different buyer or stage. The planner varies them, but your direction is stronger.
  • If the research is thin, write the hook you want in the direction.
  • Cut words. 20 on the image is the limit, and fewer render better.
  • A native layout (a note, a message, a post) carries no logo and no brand colours. That is the point of it.
  • If an image is wrong in the same way twice, say so in feedback. It becomes a rule for every later image.

Start from a format

Create shows every kind of creative as a card or a tile with an example picture (Quick start on a card, Make this on a tile). Read what it is, and press Generate now. It asks 3 things:

  1. Which product.
  2. Who it is for: your buyers in general, or 1 persona.
  3. How many, and what you get: the 3 choices about words, and for an instant photoshoot a short reel.

Each step has Add a note, typed or spoken: "show it open", "use the summer offer", "never show it in water". The notes go to the writer with the step they were left at. The app picks the layouts, looks or visual worlds for the product by itself. To choose those by hand, use Create.

The example on each tile is the format on a plain pack with no brand on it. A video format shows a still.
