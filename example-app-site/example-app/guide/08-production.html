Production: from a script to an asset

Guide, chapter 8 of 20

Production turns an approved script into something you can run: a brief for a human creator, a static image, a carousel, or a video. Every paid step waits for your click.

The production board: each job, the step it is waiting on, and the caps

Starting a job

On a script, press Make assets (or use "Make assets" on a concept). Choose:

ChoiceOptions
OutputA brief (creator, founder, editor), a static image, a long-copy static, an advertorial static, a carousel, AI UGC, AI claymation, an AI explainer
Recipe and presetThe visual treatment, for example flash photography, editorial, comic
Aspect4:5, 1:1, 9:16, 16:9
Cards or clipsCards for a carousel; the number of clips for a video, each up to 10 seconds. A video starts at the clips its script needs to be said whole: 1 clip for each scene of the script, about 6 seconds of words (19 words when the speaker's age is not known). It is no longer held to 6 clips or 60 seconds: a 10-minute sales video is more than 100 clips (an estimate from 6 seconds a clip, not measured). The box takes up to 720 clips, which is there only to catch a mistyped number; what a job may spend is held by the video budget. The screen says the clips and the length ("15 clips, about 2 min 30 s"), and whether that is what the script needs. Before a plan exists the screen counts every clip at the full 10 seconds, so the length and the cost it shows are the most the video can be. The plan then gives each clip its own, shorter length
Who is on screenFor a video: A new character for this video (written for this script, with 3 to choose from) or a saved avatar, which gives the video the same face and voice as the others made with it. The choice is shown only when the brand has saved avatars that fit the format. A saved person fits every format with 1 person or character on screen. See Saved avatars below
ReferenceA reference ad to borrow from, and how closely
NotesAnything specific to this job

The job appears on the board. The top right shows how much of the image cap and the video budget is used.

Starting from a format

You do not need a script first. On Create, under More formats, pick a video format (AI UGC, podcast clip, talking object, claymation story, comic, CGI explainer, satisfying hook and demo), a carousel or a brief. Say the product and who it is for, and add a note if you want one. The app writes 1 script for that format, with the rules of that format (2 speakers for a podcast clip, a first-person monologue for a talking object, captions of 5 words for a comic), and the job appears here with its plan to approve.

A job, step by step

A job: the next step and its cost at the top, then every step and what it was given

Click a job to open it. The green box at the top is the next step and what it costs. Under Steps, each step can be opened to see exactly which brand fields it was given.

A brief

1 step and free: the script, the concept, the persona, the guardrails and a shot list, as a document for a creator, a founder or an editor.

A static image or a carousel

flowchart LR
  A[Plan: words for each card and 3 lengths of feed copy] --> B[You approve the plan]
  B --> C[Render each card]
  C --> D[2 looks at each card, 1 retry if it fails]
  D --> E[Review]
  1. Plan. Gemini cuts the ad from the script: what each card shows, the exact words on it, and the feed copy in 3 lengths with 3 headlines. The words go through the voice check and the ad review. With Check claims on, they are also read against the brand's claims, and a line that fails is written again once. GPT-6 Sol writes each card's image prompt, and each prompt is read before rendering.
  2. Approve the plan. You can edit every word here. What a check flagged is listed above the Approve button. It never stops you: approve the plan as it is, or edit it first.
  3. Render. About 12 cents a card, then the automatic checks. The result of the check shows under each card.

Check claims

Every screen that makes a creative has a Check claims switch. It is off unless you switch it on. In Make assets it starts as the script was written, and you can change it for the jobs you are making.

The switchWhat happens
OffNothing is read against the brand's claims. Nothing is rewritten. The plan is written and waits for you.
OnEvery word of the plan is read against the brand's approved claims, offers and guardrails. A line that fails is written again once and read again. What is still flagged is listed on the plan.

Either way the plan is yours to approve. A claim check never refuses an approval and never stops an ad being made.

A plan written with the switch off has a Check claims button beside Approve. It turns the checks on for that job, reads the plan and rewrites a failure once. It costs a few cents.

The 1 thing that does hold a plan is a video plan that no longer fits its clips after an edit by hand (too many spoken words for a clip, for example). The paid video steps would fail on it, so it is fixed first.

A video

flowchart LR
  A[Shot plan] --> B[Character sheet]
  B --> C[Look frame]
  C --> D[Storyboard]
  D --> E[Video clips]
  E --> F[Join the clips]
  F --> G[Motion finishing]

  1. Shot plan. The script is split into clips, each with its shots, dialogue and on-screen text. A clip is 1 scene of the script, about 6 seconds. It is no longer filled to 10 (from 2026-10-08). A script line is a scene. A clip takes whole scenes while their words fit about 6 seconds at the speaker's pace (19 words when the speaker's age is not known), and it ends where a scene or a sentence ends. A sentence the script wrote over 2 lines stays in 1 clip: a line with no full stop runs on when the next line starts with a small letter. A new line that starts with a capital is a new sentence, so a script written with no full stops is still dealt line by line, and a sentence longer than a clip is cut at a comma, a dash or "and". A clip is then as long as its own words need at that pace, in whole seconds (at 3.3 words a second, 13 words are 4 seconds, 17 are 6, 28 are 9). It is never shorter than the video model makes (4 seconds on Seedance 2.5) and never longer than the job's clip length. That length (10 seconds unless changed, 4 to 15) is now only the longest a clip may be, so no clip waits in silence after its line. A clip that says too little to fill the shortest clip is said in the clip beside it where the 2 fit, never in a clip of its own. A clip with nothing said keeps the full length. On a video model that makes only a few lengths (Seedance 2.0 makes 5, 10 or 15 seconds) clips are still filled to the job's clip length, each clip is the shortest of those lengths that holds its words, and only a line of under 4 words ("Shop now.") is moved into the clip beside it. Every clip of "same ad, new format" is still filled to the job's clip length and keeps it. The 6 seconds is a starting choice, not measured. Each clip's length is kept in the plan with the clip: the storyboard, the clip prompt, the prompt pack and the timing of the captions all follow it, and the plan's notes list the lengths when they differ. The spoken lines are never reworded: the app lays the script's sentences across the clips, whole and in order, before the planner is asked, and puts them back word for word after it answers. The planner decides only what is seen. If you choose fewer clips than the script needs, each clip first takes more of the script, up to the most a full clip may carry (38 words in 10 seconds when the speaker's age is not known), and then whole lines are left out (the opening line and the call to action last); the plan's notes say how many and which. If you choose more clips than it needs, the clips left over have no spoken line. A plan of more than 6 clips is written 6 clips at a time, and a run that stops part-way carries on from where it stopped. The plan also offers 3 people to cast (or 3 characters, for the character formats), each with a line on why the buyer would believe them. They are under Who is in this video on the job. The first is used unless you press Use this one on another. Choosing costs nothing: no picture is made until the character sheet.

    Why a clip is a scene (2026-10-08). The owner watched a test clip made at 10 seconds. The voice was fine and the picture was slow: 1 small action stretched over the whole clip. With clips filled to 10 seconds, 1 request of 3,200 characters had to direct 2 or 3 scenes of the script in up to 6 shots. Measured on 1 script, with no model called: 97 words in 11 lines are dealt to 6 clips of 5, 6, 7, 4, 4 and 5 seconds, 31 seconds in all. Before, the same script was 3 clips of about 10 seconds.

    What it costs. A video has more clips, so more joins between clips. A voice the video model makes afresh for each clip can change at each join. Nothing at this clip length or this pace had been rendered when this was written.

    How fast the words are said (from 2026-10-08). The app holds 1 table of how fast people talk, by the speaker's age: 3.6 spoken words a second under 30, 3.4 for 30 to 44, 3.2 for 45 to 59, 3.0 for 60 to 74, 2.8 from 75, and 3.3 when the age is not known (the table is in Write scripts). A Calm video pace is 0.85 of that, and speech that runs past a minute 0.9. A clip may be filled to 1.15 times the pace before anything is left out. The speaker is the saved avatar when the video has one, else the presenter the brand's creative kit casts for the persona, else the persona's own age. A format with only a made character and no person uses the pace for an age that is not known. The same pace sets how many clips the video needs, how long each clip is, how many shots it is cut into and what the planner writes about the voice. The script was timed from the same table when it was written. The table rests on published studies of how fast people speak. The 0.85, the 0.9 and the 1.15 are starting choices, not measured.

    A clip is a run of shots. A clip is not 1 held picture. It is planned as a short run of shots with hard cuts between them. How many shots a clip gets is a range, and the planner chooses inside it by what the clip's idea needs. The range follows the Video pace choice of the taste bar and the kind of video:

    The videoNormalFast cutsThe most shots in a clip
    A filmed personA shot every 1.2 to 2 seconds: 3 to 5 shots in 6 seconds, 5 or 6 in 10Every 1 to 1.6 seconds: 4 to 6 in 6 seconds, 6 in 106
    A made world (claymation, a cartoon, an explainer, 3D and the other formats drawn with a made character)A shot every 0.75 to 1.25 seconds: 5 to 8 shots in 6 seconds, 8 to 10 in 10Every 0.6 to 1 second: 6 to 10 in 6 seconds, 10 in 1010

    With Calm there is about 1 shot every 5 seconds in either kind, as before. A slower speaker's clip is cut a little slower: in a 6-second clip of a filmed person, a speaker of 75 or over gets 3 or 4 shots and a speaker under 30 gets 4 or 5. Shots are timed to a tenth of a second (0.8, 1.5, 2), and Calm keeps whole seconds. The planner is told no shot of a filmed person is under 1 second and no shot of a made world under 0.5. When the planner's shot lengths miss the clip's length by 1 second or less, the app puts the difference on the longest shot, so a plan is not sent back and paid for again over a tenth of a second. Every number in this paragraph and the table is a starting choice, not measured.

    What the planner is told about cutting (2026-10-08). The owner sent 2 prompts written by other people, each an 8-second film in 10 cuts: "look at how much is happening", "that is what we have to aspire to". Then: "don't take the exact style... take the principles", "it's going to be case dependent", "give the model that amount of flexibility and creativity". So the planner is told principles, not a pattern, and the wording of the 2 prompts is not kept or sent to it. A clip has a shape: an opening picture, a build, the picture the words are about, and a landing. Shots are quick where the clip builds and longer where it lands. The number of shots is the planner's choice inside the range, "never the fewest out of habit". In a made world the framing words of a shot may also say how fast the camera goes and what the light does, and 1 shot of a clip may run slow. It is told "None of this is a formula". And it is told to draw the line happening, never a diagram of it: what the words say is done is seen being done. At a Calm pace none of this is sent.

    A test plan under these rules (a CGI explainer, 97 words): 6 clips of 5, 6, 7, 4, 4 and 5 seconds with 4, 5, 6, 4, 4 and 4 shots. The plan before the rules had 3, 3, 4, 2, 2 and 3 shots, all in whole seconds. In the new plan every clip has the fewest shots its range allows.

    Every video is planned by the same director's rules, whatever its format: the film is planned before its shots, the picture changes every 2 seconds or sooner, every cut changes both how close the camera is and where it stands, the camera travels in about half the shots, each shot is 1 action that changes the picture, the product comes in when the words reach it, and the voice runs on across the cuts. The planner is also told to make it tight: to plan a clip as if it had half its seconds, so that each shot is something new being done, and to count 2 moves that run on in the same hands as 1 action. And it is told to give each clip a picture of its own, never the last clip's again. The planner is told how long each clip is and how many shots a clip that long is cut into. The plan keeps the pace it was planned at. For such a plan the storyboard draws 1 still for each shot, up to the frames you chose, and a clip that never shows the product is sent no photo of it. "Same ad, new format" keeps the shots of its reference. A plan made before shots is still made as 1 continuous take.

    How each shot is filmed (from 2026-10-08). Every shot of a new plan says 4 things in fixed words, and its exact framing in the planner's own words ("from the chest up, the camera at counter height looking up"). The planner is told to write "the camera" or "the lens", never "the phone": a phone named in a shot line was drawn into a test clip.

    WhatThe words it is chosen from
    Sizewide, full, medium, close-up, extreme close-up
    Angleeye level, low angle, high angle, overhead, over the shoulder, point of view, profile, framed
    Camera movelocked off, hand-held, push in, pull back, pan, tilt, track, truck, crane up, orbit, fly over. The planner picks each by what the viewer should feel, and the clip prompt writes the move out ("dolly in toward the subject, the foreground sliding past")
    Subjectface, person, hands, product, object, place

    A planner's own word for one of these is read as the word of the list ("top-down" is "overhead"). Each clip is then checked:

    • No 2 shots in a row have the same size or the same angle, so every cut changes both.
    • A clip of 3 or more shots uses at least 3 sizes, 3 angles and 3 kinds of subject. A clip of 2 shots uses 2 of each.
    • The camera travels in at least half the shots, rounded down. Locked off and hand-held do not count as travelling. This is not checked at a Calm pace.
    • No shot runs over 3 seconds, or 2.5 in a made world. This is not checked at a Calm pace.

    A clip with too few shots, or one that fails a check, goes back to the planner to be fixed, up to 2 times. A plan that still fails then stops the step with the list of what is wrong. The plan also names up to 4 other places the video cuts to (the car, the desk, the doorstep), each with what it looks like, and a shot that leaves the main place names its place. On the plan each shot shows its size, angle and camera move under its framing. You can edit the framing words there, and set a shot's seconds to a tenth of a second. A clip of a made world can have up to 10 shots there, and a clip of any other plan of shots up to 6. The size, the angle and the move can't be changed on that screen. On a plan you edit by hand these 4 checks are advice: what they find is listed on the plan as warnings and never stops it. So a shot you add by hand, which has no size, angle or move, gives a warning and the plan can still be approved. What still holds an edited plan is what a clip can't be made without, such as shots whose seconds do not add up to the clip's length, too few shots, or too many spoken words.

    A plan of shots made before this keeps the rules it was written to: 3 to 5 shots in 10 seconds (4 to 6 with Fast cuts), each shot saying how it is filmed in its own words, and no 2 shots in a row framed in the same words. Not yet proven on a rendered clip: whether the video model follows a named camera move in a shot of under 2 seconds, follows cuts timed to a tenth of a second, or draws 5 to 8 different pictures cleanly in 6 seconds with a voice over them. Nothing cut this fast had been rendered when this was written.

    A made character or a filmed person. A format drawn with a made character (claymation, a talking object, a comic, cinematic 3D, anime and the like) is offered 3 characters, never 3 filmed people. The planner is told which kind the format is. A plan that comes back with the wrong kind (a filmed person described for a 3D film, and no character) is sent back to be fixed, up to 2 times.

    Choosing a man where the plan says "she". The plan is written once, for the first person. When you choose a person of the other sex, the directions follow the person chosen: "she tips the pack into her palm" becomes "he tips the pack into his palm" in what each scene shows, in the storyboard frames and in the note on how the voice sounds. The spoken lines are never touched. With 2 people on screen (a podcast clip) nothing is changed, because "she" could be either of them. Where the voice is a narrator off screen, the note on the voice is left as written.

  2. Character sheet. For formats with a character or presenter: 6 views of the person you chose in 1 picture, in 2 rows of 3 (from 2026-10-08; it was 3). For a person: the face from the front, waist up at three-quarters, the whole body from the front, then the face in profile, the whole body from behind, and the hands close up. For a made character: front, three-quarter and side, then from behind, the face close up, and the hands. A podcast clip, with 2 people together, keeps 3. A person's sheet is made on the picture model that makes people (see Which picture model below). In the 1 test so far (2026-10-08) a person's sheet and a clay figure's sheet both came back as 6 clean views in 2 rows, first try. Approve it, or go back and choose another person: the sheet is then drawn again. Once the look frame is made the cast is settled. To see the people before you choose, the job can draw a portrait of each of the 3 (1 picture each, no product in it), and Show 3 more adds 3 at a time, up to 9. "Show 3 more" has not been run live yet. A video made with a saved avatar has nobody to choose between: no portraits are drawn, and when the avatar already has a character sheet it is used as it is, with no picture drawn.

  3. Look frame. 1 still of the character with the real product, in the setting. This fixes the look of the whole video. Give notes and regenerate until it is right: it is cheap, and every clip follows it.

  4. Storyboard. The whole video in stills, before any clip is made, because a still costs cents and a clip costs dollars. You choose the storyboard frames for each clip (1 to 8, 4 unless you change it). The number is the most pictures a clip gets. A clip cut into more shots than that has its pictures spread from its first shot to its last (they used to be its first shots only), and the shots between are directed by the words of the prompt. You set it in Make assets, with --frames= on pnpm produce, or in the chat ("6 frames a clip"), and you can change it on the job until the frames are drawn. More frames cost more images and hold the clip closer to what you approved. Each clip gets its key moments in the middle of the action and the frame it ends on; the frame it starts on is the look frame (clip 1) or the last frame of the clip before, so it is not drawn twice. The job says how many images that is (clips × frames) before anything is drawn. A long video is many frames, so they are kept clip by clip as they are drawn: a run that stops part-way carries on from the clip it stopped in. Every frame is drawn from the look frame, the product photo, the character sheet and the frame before it, so the person, the product and the place stay the same. The frames are laid out clip by clip: change a frame's words, then Change this picture (the same picture goes back with your change and the rest is kept) or Draw a fresh one, then approve the storyboard. The character sheet and the look frame have the same 2 buttons. Each costs 1 image. No clip prompt is written and no clip is made until you do.

  5. Video clips. The expensive step. Each clip is asked for with every picture that helps, up to the 30 Seedance 2.5 takes: the person in 3 views (and, from a sheet of 6, the profile, the back or the hands only in a clip with a shot from that side), the photos of the product the clip shows (see Which product photos a clip is sent below), the frame the clip starts on, the place, and the clip's whole approved storyboard in time order. The prompt names each one by its tag and its job ("@Image7 is key moment 2 (5s): she tips the pack into her palm"). For a new plan of shots the first line of the prompt says each cut is to a new camera set-up, a different size and angle. Each shot's line carries its size, angle, camera move and framing. The other places the clip cuts to are listed with what each looks like, and a line says every shot is moving from its first frame, at a quick real-life pace, with its action done before the cut (at a Calm pace the line says only that the shot is moving from its first frame). The prompt of a made world differs in 3 ways. With more than 4 shots its first line says "hard cuts at the times given" and does not list them again, because each shot's line carries its own times. The line about the camera of the whole video is left out, so the room goes to the shots. And its line on motion reads "Every shot is moving from its first frame and its action is done before the cut. Nothing waits or drifts. Slow motion only in a shot that asks for it." The spoken lines are given as 1 track across the cuts. For a speaker who talks at 3.2 words a second or more, and not at a Calm pace, the prompt adds that there is no pause between sentences. A later clip is also given the clip before it. In a plan of shots that clip is the reference for the cast, the voice and the look only, and the new clip opens on a hard cut to a new shot. In a plan made before shots, the new clip is asked for as an extension of the one before. Every prompt ends on the sound: the room, or the sounds the action itself makes when nothing is said, then "No music: the soundtrack is the voice and those sounds only, with no score and no BGM." Today the app stops here at "Prompts ready": no provider of Seedance 2.5 is set up, so no clip is made and nothing is paid. The job shows the prompt pack: for each clip, the pictures numbered in the order to attach them, then the prompt with a copy button. The same pack is on disk in data/assets/<job>/handoff, 1 folder per clip (01_face.png, 02_waist-up.png ... prompt.txt), with 1 page, index.html, that shows every clip. A prompt in the pack is held to 3,200 characters: the longest Seedance 2.5 has taken from this app on Higgsfield is 3,180 (measured on 5 clips; its real limit is not published). A prompt the app sends to seedanceapi.org is held to that provider's 2,000. When a prompt must shrink, what the pictures already show goes first (a frame's words, the place, what the objects look like); the action and the spoken words go last, and no tag is ever dropped. The line on what the product is and how it opens used to be the first whole line dropped. In a new plan of shots it is now cut to 160 characters and kept until the last step before the shots' own lines are shortened, because no picture of a closed pack says it. Clips you made yourself (from 2026-10-08). Under Clips, each clip has Add the clip you made. Make each clip from the prompt pack in the video tool, then add its file (MP4, MOV or WebM, up to 300 MB) to its row. A clip is cut to the length the plan gives it, or held on its last frame when it is shorter, and the job says so. The video is made at the quality of the clips you add: the lowest of them, so none is blown up. When every clip is in, the job moves on to Join the clips. It is free. This has been tested in code only: no real clip has been added through it yet.

  6. Join the clips. Free. The clips are joined into 1 video with no captions drawn in, so they can be added where the ad is edited or run. The video prompts also tell the model to add no subtitles and no on-screen text. To have the plan's on-screen text drawn in, tick Draw the on-screen text into the video in Make assets, or on the job itself at any time before the clips are joined. A human brief follows the same switch: its delivery notes say "Captions off" unless it is on.

  7. Motion finishing. Optional overlays timed to the video. From 2026-10-08 they are drawn in the brand's own colours and its headline typeface, read from the brand's look on file: the brand's main colour as the tile, with the brand colour that reads best on it as the words. The plan only says where each overlay sits, when, and what it says. A brand with no look on file gets white on near black in Arial, and the job's log says so. This has been tested on drawn tiles only: none has been laid on a real video yet.

Which product. A job is made about the product its script is about: the product its offer names, else the one the script's own words name, else the brand's first. When a brand has more than 1 product, Make assets has a Product choice to set another, and a video job has the same choice until its storyboard is drawn. Changing it on a job writes the plan again for the new product (a few cents) and the look frame after it; the character sheet is kept.

A part of the product with no photo. If the plan shows a part of the product that no photo on file shows (the small pack inside the big one, the product open), the job says so before the storyboard is drawn: "The plan shows the X, but there is no photo of it. Upload one, or it will be drawn from a description and small print may come out wrong." Upload the photo there. It is a warning, not a block. In the test with no such photo, the small pack was made up and its small print came out garbled.

Which product photos a clip is sent (from 2026-10-08). A photo that is attached is drawn in. So a clip of a plan of shots is sent the photo of each product object it shows, and no photo of another one: the single serving's own pack, or the product out of its pack. The plan says which photo on file shows each object. The photos on file are the ones named by job in the brand's products file (see "What a camera needs" in Set up a brand) and any uploaded for 1 part. The photo of the pack as it is sold is sent only when that pack is on screen. When no photo on file shows any product object of the clip, the sold pack's photo is still sent, with a purpose that says it is there for its colours, artwork and printed name, and that the pack in it is not in the clip. Up to 3 photos of the clip's own objects are sent, and the views the brain names for a hand or for use fill what is left of the 3. This is how the prompt pack is built. A "same ad, new format" plan, and a plan made before shots, is sent what it always was: the sold pack's photo, then up to 2 more views, from the ones the brain names and the photos of the parts the plan uses. The app's own clip step for the older video models is not changed: where the model takes reference pictures, it still attaches the sold pack's photo to every clip that shows the product.

The hand-off pack. For each clip the pack holds prompt.txt, attach-in-this-order.txt and the pictures, numbered in the order to attach them. The prompt names each picture by its tag: the person's face, waist-up and full figure, the product photos (as said above: the sold pack where it belongs, and up to 3 views), the first frame, the place (later clips), each key moment, the last frame. @Video1 is the clip before. Where a line speaks of several pictures, every tag is written out by itself ("@Image5, @Image6 and @Image7"), never as a range ("@Image5 to @Image7"): the video model's guide says a range of tags is not followed. The 1 range left is in the shortened prompt of a plan made before shots. In a plan of shots a line says the pictures of the person or character give the look only, that nobody holds the pose of a reference picture, and that no shot copies its framing: left unsaid, the video model keeps people standing as they stand on the character sheet. Each clip's own length is written beside it, to set when you make it. You attach the pictures in Seedance 2.5, paste the prompt, and run it yourself. No clip has been made from inside the app in these tests, and none from this pack was paid for in the phase 47 test, so the pack is checked as text only. The 3,200-character limit the prompts are held to is not a published figure.

A second kind of pack, on no screen yet. The app can also build a pack with no storyboard still for the shots and without the clip before as a video reference, in a folder of its own beside the usual one. The shots are then directed by the words of the prompt alone, and the pictures are the cast, the product and the opening frame. It is for a plan of shots. In a new plan of shots the opening frame is attached to clip 1 as a reference for the place, the light and the look only, not as the first frame: a picture beats the words on how a shot is framed, and an opening frame drawn as a plain front view turned a point-of-view shot into one. In an older plan of shots it is still attached to clip 1 as the first frame. Today it can be built from code only: no button and no command makes it. The usual pack is unchanged. Which of the 2 gives the better clip is not known: neither has been made into clips and compared.

Which picture model (from 2026-10-08). The app has 2: GPT Image 2.5 Sunburst, which makes every picture unless said here, and Nano Banana Pro, which makes people: an avatar's portrait, the photo-real picture from the avatar library, a person's character sheet, the people to choose from, and the frame a video filmed with a person opens on. A character that is drawn or built, and every storyboard frame, stay on the first. When Nano Banana Pro is not connected, or turns a request away (its account's spending limit was reached, for example), the picture is made on the other model and the job says so in its log. Nothing is charged for a request that was turned away. Both count against the 1 picture limit. In the test of 2026-10-08 Nano Banana Pro made 6 pictures at 14 cents each, and was never refused.

A script you pasted in. Its words are kept exactly, as before. It used to be planned as 1 continuous take a clip; it is now cut into shots like any other script (from 2026-10-08). "Same ad, new format", which follows a reference ad shot for shot, is unchanged.

Which product a script lands on (fixed 2026-10-08). A job reads which product its script is about from the words of the script when the offer names none. A word for what a product comes in (pack, box, bottle, pouch, jar, sachet and the like) no longer counts: a script that said "one pack of gummies a day" had been made about the 1 product with "Pack" in its name, which had no photos, so the product in every picture was made up.

Which format. A job is refused at the start, with the list of formats, when its format is not one the app makes or its style does not belong to that format. A podcast clip describes both people: the plan writes the host and the guest, and both are in the character sheet and in every clip prompt.

Which video model. Prompts are written for Seedance 2.5 by default (MODEL_VIDEO in .env). The video provider the app can call today sells Seedance 2.0 only, so with 2.5 set the Video clips step writes every clip's prompt, stops at Prompts ready before anything is paid, and shows the Prompt pack on the job, which is written for 2.5 with every reference tagged. Set MODEL_VIDEO=seedance-2.0 to make clips in the app on 2.0.

Approve each still before moving on. A mistake caught at the look frame costs 3 cents; the same mistake caught after the clips costs dollars.

Saved avatars

An avatar is a person or character saved for the brand, with its pictures and a voice, so every video can show the same face. Avatars are added and edited on the persona sheet (see Personas).

  • Use one. In Make assets, under Who is on screen, pick the avatar in place of A new character for this video. A saved person can be used in any format with 1 person or character on screen (from 2026-10-08). In a filmed format they are filmed. In a format that is drawn or built (claymation, paper, anime and the rest) they are that format's character: the picker says "Drawn in this format's look, as the same person." A made character is offered only to a character format, and one saved in 1 look only to videos of that look. The avatars of the script's buyer come first. A format with 2 people on screen, or with nobody on screen, can't use one. Changing the format keeps the avatar when the new format can use it, and a new line starts with the avatar of the line before it.
  • The same person in another look. The first video of a look draws a sheet of the person in that look, from their picture, and keeps it on the avatar under that format. The next video of that look draws nothing. The person's own sheet is not touched. A new picture of the avatar drops every sheet, since they are sheets of the old face. In the 1 test so far (2026-10-08, a claymation video) the clay figure had the person's hair, freckles and clothes, and was the same figure in every storyboard picture.
  • What changes in the job. The planner is told who is on screen and writes every shot for them. How fast the words are said follows the avatar's age when it is a person with an age on file. Under Who is in this video the job shows the 1 avatar: there are no 3 options, no portraits are drawn and no more can be asked for. The character sheet is the avatar's own when it has one, with no picture drawn. When it has none, the sheet is drawn from the avatar's picture and kept on the avatar, so its next video draws none. Notes on the sheet still draw a new one, for that job. If you edit the person in the plan by hand, the video has a character of its own again.
  • Save one from a video. Once a video's character sheet is approved, the job has Save this character as an avatar: give it a name and press Save as an avatar. It is free: nothing is drawn. The description, the pictures and the voice as the plan describes it are kept on the sheet of the persona the script was written for. A video that started from an avatar is not offered this.
  • The voice. How the voice sounds, in words, goes into the plan and so into every clip prompt. A voice picked from the list, or a recording you uploaded, is not made by the app: the app has no connection to the voice service yet. It is a choice kept with the avatar. The prompt pack says it in 1 line at its top, and a recording is copied into the pack folder for you to give to the video tool.

These screens were built on 2026-10-08 and are covered by tests in code. They have not been looked at in a browser yet.

Reviewing and handing off

A finished job moves to In review. Approve or reject it with notes, in the job or by dragging its card: pick the card up and drop it on Approved, Rejected, In review or Sent. The columns that take the card light up while you carry it. Queued and Running are where a job is while the app makes a step. Drop a card there to run its next step: a question shows the step and what it costs, and only Run it starts it. A job whose plan you approved sits in Queued, so drop it on Running. A job that waits for you to approve its plan, character sheet, look frame or storyboard is in its own column, Waiting for approval. Click the card to look at what it made. Press the button under the card to approve it: the job goes to Queued, ready for its next step, and nothing has been run. Or drop the card on Queued to approve, or on Running to approve and run the next step after 1 question. In review holds only finished work. In every column the card that changed last is the first: a job you just made, or a card that just moved there, is at the top. The last column, Discarded, is for a job you do not want at all. Drop its card there from any column, or open the job and press Discard: nothing is asked, nothing is run and nothing is deleted. To get it back, drop the card on the column it came from (the only one that lights up) or press Put back in the job. To remove a job and its files for good, use Delete job. The feedback box on a job works like the one on a studio image: the note is kept and becomes a lesson.

The board asks why. A moment after a card lands on Approved, a question opens: what went well? Tick what applies and, if you like, type or say a few words. After a drop on Rejected it asks what went wrong and how it could be better, and what kind of problem it is: this kind of ad, the brand, the script or copy it was made from, or only this ad. Your answer is kept as a lesson for next time, in the place that fits (see Feedback and lessons). Skip closes the question; the card has moved either way.

Each job has a Meta ad name built from the brand, concept, script, format and hook. The handoff pack gathers the approved assets with their names and copy, ready to upload.
