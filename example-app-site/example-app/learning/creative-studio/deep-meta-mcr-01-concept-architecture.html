Meta creative strategy · 5 min read

Build concepts from a buying situation, an argument, and an offer

Define a creative concept as persona × angle × offer, then choose the format that can communicate it. This makes the briefing and the learning specific enough to explain why a test worked.

When this strategy makes sense

Use when your creative backlog consists of formats, demographics, or cosmetic variations. Start with people who have bought before; broad population messages require stronger copy and more education.

The concept is a commercial argument

A useful concept connects three decisions: whom you are talking to, why they should buy, and what they can buy. The format is the delivery vehicle. Starting with “we need UGC” leaves all three commercial decisions unresolved. A creator can deliver a weak argument naturally; a professional shoot can make an irrelevant argument look attractive. Neither resolves the underlying persuasion problem.

Define the persona as an archetype created by a circumstance, a problem, a desire, and a buying trigger. Age and gender can help cast the ad, but they do not explain the purchase. A woman experiencing acne in her early twenties may interpret the problem differently from a woman experiencing it in her forties. The language, failed alternatives, embarrassment, timing, and proof needed can differ even when the product is identical.

Build the persona before generating scripts

Start with actual buyers. Write down what happened before they bought, the job the product performed, what they had tried, what they doubted, and what finally convinced them. Spend time making these situations concrete. Replace “health-conscious parent” with a parent who has tried several ways to make a toddler eat vegetables and needs a practical routine the child will accept. This specificity makes the hook, casting, demonstration, and objections easier to identify.

Build an initial set manually. A planning approach is to develop 20–40 genuinely different situations before using an assistant to propose adjacent ones. Give the assistant the business context, existing situations, product boundaries, and customer evidence. Ask for 20 candidates; retain only the few that describe a plausible new buying situation. Feed the accepted examples back and repeat. Treat generated personas as hypotheses until customer evidence supports them.

Write the angle as the complete argument

An angle is more than a product feature. It is the reason this buyer should choose this solution now. Assemble the argument from the elements the situation needs: recognition of the problem; a useful explanation; a contrarian perspective if supportable; comparison with failed alternatives; relevant authority; and proof of the proposed solution. Different buyers need different combinations. A first-time buyer may need category education. An experienced buyer may need evidence that a familiar solution has been improved.

For video, write the whole script rather than stopping at a one-sentence angle label. For a static, write the headline, supporting copy, image evidence, and the next step. Choose proof that matches the persona. A customer describing a similar routine can be more persuasive than an unrelated celebrity. A technical claim needs evidence applicable to the product and the claimed use, not a vaguely related study.

Choose the offer, then choose the format

The offer contains both the product and the packaging of value. A bundle, quantity saving, gift, subscription, or single unit can change who buys and why. Select a structure that fits the buyer’s use and your contribution margin. Once that is fixed, ask whether the argument needs education, a visible demonstration, a personal story, or a single-frame comparison. Those answers determine the format.

Worked brief

Illustration: the buyer is a consultant who flies twice weekly and needs clothing that works in a client meeting and a restaurant. The argument is that one coordinated set reduces packing and outfit decisions while preserving an appropriate appearance. The offer is a three-piece travel set. The proof is a real carry-on packing demonstration, two styling sequences, and customer feedback about the fabric after travel. A carousel can show the two situations; a video can demonstrate packing and styling. Neither format changes the concept by itself.

Keep the learning attached to the concept

Name each test with persona, argument, and offer. Record which variable changes between batches. The next brief should state what the result supports: perhaps the packing argument worked for frequent flyers, while a comfort argument did not. “UGC worked” is too vague to guide production. Broader messages can have more spend capacity, but specificity is usually easier to execute well. Expand the audience after learning how to persuade a concrete buyer, rather than using breadth to hide an unclear argument.

Your implementation checklist

Put the method to work

  1. 1

    Describe one buyer through their circumstance, problem, desire, and buying trigger.

  2. 2

    Write the argument that makes your solution appropriate for that circumstance, including objections and proof.

  3. 3

    Specify the product and its value packaging; choose the format after the argument is complete.

  4. 4

    Name and test the concept consistently so the results feed the next brief.

Worked application

Illustration: a consultant flying twice weekly needs one carry-on wardrobe for boardrooms and dinner. The angle is fewer outfit changes without looking underdressed; the offer is a coordinated three-piece set, supported by packing and styling demonstrations.

Measure and decide

Judge the concept against the predefined acquisition contribution and spend goal; inspect audience breadth and cumulative spend before treating cosmetic variants as new demand.

Where this can fail

“Busy professionals, age 25–45” leaves the writer without a specific problem or proof requirement. A new format can improve access to viewers without constituting a new buying argument.
