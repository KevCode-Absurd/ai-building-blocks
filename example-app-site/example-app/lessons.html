How Grüns products are opened, held and used (5)

Facts. Every picture and video of the product follows them, and an image that breaks one is made again.

  • The big Grüns pouch opens only at its top, along the resealable closure. Nobody tears it: never ripped at a corner, down the side or along the bottom. A daily pack is lifted out through the open top.

    Why: Images showed the pouch torn at a corner, at the side and at the bottom.

    Broken 7 times since

  • A daily pack (the small single-serve sachet) is torn open straight across its top edge, the edge above the Grüns name when the name reads upright. Never torn from the side, the bottom or a corner, and never cut open.

    Why: Daily packs were drawn ripped from the side.

    Said 2 timesBroken 50 times since

  • The gummies come out of the torn top of the daily pack: the pack is tipped and they pour out like cereal, into a palm or a bowl. Gummies never come out of the side or the bottom, and never fall from a sealed pack.

    Why: Gummies were shown falling out of the side of a pack.

    Said 2 timesBroken 60 times since

  • The daily pack is a small, flat, roughly square sachet that sits in a palm, with the same artwork as the big pouch. Not a stick, not a bar, not a box, and never as large as the pouch.

    Why: The daily pack was drawn as a stick in one image, a square in another and a bar in a third. Written from the brand's own product photos: correct it if the pack is different.

    Said 2 timesBroken 16 times since

  • Keep Grüns packaging formats distinct: show either the large 28-pack standing pouch with resealable zip and complete label details, or the small flat daily sachet. Never swap their sizes, labels, or openings, and never show the large pouch torn.

    Why: The checks repeatedly found the large 28-pack pouch and the small daily sachet being confused, swapped, or rendered with the other's size, opening, or label details. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): L-001 and L-004 already say which pack is which and how each opens.

    Broken 4 times since

Rules for Grüns (5)

Mistakes pointed out on earlier work, and this brand's own taste.

  • End lines and sentences on strong, weighted nouns or active verbs that deliver the point. Never let copy trail off on weak pronouns, prepositions, flat qualifiers, or subordinate trailing clauses.

    Why: Scripts and copy lines repeatedly ended on weak words, prepositions, or trailing clauses instead of finishing with weight. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): A device (how a sentence ends), not a mistake: the kind of rule the owner had taken off the writers on 2026-10-07.

    Broken 5 times since

  • Describe problems, enemies, and symptoms using customers' visceral, sensory words (such as chalky, bloated, or better poops). Never substitute generic, polite, clinical, or formal marketing phrasing to describe how a pain feels.

    Why: Scripts and copy repeatedly relied on generic, clinical, and formal phrasing instead of the buyers' own sensory words for the problem. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): The voice block already sends the customers' own words and the brand's Don't list.

    Broken 12 times since

  • Frame routines and habits as easy, fun, and casual. Never lecture, shame, blame, or frighten the audience with guilt over failed habits, health chores, abandoned goals, or wasted money.

    Why: Copy repeatedly used preachy, guilt-driven, or fear-based framing about failed health routines instead of keeping the tone light, fun, and easy. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): The brand's Don't list (no lecturing, no guilt) and the copy floor already say it.

    Broken 31 times since

  • Write copy in a casual, cheeky friend voice with punchy three-beat cadence and plain speech. Never use generic wellness clichés, stiff salesy phrasing, earnest heavy drama, or formal spec-sheet language.

    Why: Copy repeatedly lapsed into generic, earnest, formal, or salesy wording lacking Grüns' cheeky friend tone and punchy cadence. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): A device (three-beat cadence) the owner named as the cause of clipped lines; the brand's tone is in the voice block.

    Broken 6 times since

  • Always use conversational contractions in copy and scripts (such as you're, don't, that's, there's). Never leave verbs or negatives uncontracted unless deliberate, heavy rhetorical emphasis is explicitly required.

    Why: Scripts and copy repeatedly used stiff, uncontracted verbs instead of standard conversational contractions. Retired 2026-10-13 (E-20): The voice rules already require contractions; the code check for stiff verbs stays.

Rules for every brand (10)

They apply to all brands, so change them with care.

  • A product is opened, fastened, worn, poured and used the way it really works. It opens only at its real opening, and anything inside it comes out of that opening and nowhere else. If the brand's facts do not say how it opens or works, show it closed or at rest.

    Why: Packs were drawn torn at the side, the bottom and a corner, with the contents falling out of the wrong place.

    Broken 19 times since

  • Whenever a product is shown open, worn or in use, say the whole action: which part or pack, where it opens or fastens, how it was opened, what comes out or moves, which way it goes and where it lands or rests. "Being opened" or "in use" on its own is an unfinished instruction.

    Why: The image model was left to guess how the pack opens, and guessed wrong.

    Broken 1 time since

  • A hand that holds the product is described in full: which hand, where the thumb and fingers sit, which way the front of the product faces, and what the other hand is doing. The grip must be one a real hand can make. Never a hand floating in front of the product, a hand that covers its label or name, or more hands than the people in the frame have.

    Why: A model's hand sat in the wrong place on the pack and the way she held it did not look real.

    Broken 5 times since

  • Read every picture as a real scene before it is made: could a camera photograph this? Every object rests on something or is held by something, things fall downward, and the sizes of the product, its contents and the hands agree with each other. A deliberately stylised frame (pieces frozen in mid-air in a studio shot, a designed layout) is a choice the plan states, never an accident.

    Why: Images were checked only against the product photo, so a picture could match the pack and still make no sense.

    Broken 10 times since

  • The product looks the same in every image of a set: one shape, one size against the hand, one design. Describe the product, and each pack or part it has (outer and inner), once, in the same words, in every prompt that shows it.

    Why: The single-serve pack was drawn as a stick in one image, a square in another and a bar in a third.

    Broken 4 times since

  • No children are generated. Nobody in a picture is, or looks, under 18. A product for children is shown with the product itself, what is inside it, an adult's hands, and the places it lives: a lunchbox, a coat peg, a school bag on a chair, a shelf in a child's room.

    Why: A house rule since 2026-10-02: generated children are not needed for now and will be looked at later.

  • A generated person is never the founder, a customer, an expert or a reviewer. A founder ad shows the founder only from a real photo or video the brand uploaded; without one it is the founder's words as text, with no face. A review is the customer's words with no face beside it.

    Why: A made-up face presented as a real person is a false endorsement. A house rule since 2026-10-02.

  • Everything a clip uses is in view when the clip starts, or comes in on a cut and is already in view when its shot opens, standing where the plan says. Nothing is taken out of a cupboard, a bag or a drawer, or brought in from off screen inside a shot: the video model has to invent what it cannot see. The 1 exception is the product on an ad whose buyer's stage keeps it out: it is in no frame until the words reach it, and comes in on that cut.

    Why: A cupboard was opened in a clip and the stack of plates inside turned into a basket of supplements. Reworded 2026-10-13: read as "every prop in view from second 1", it put the product in the first frame of top-of-funnel ads, against the stage rule.

  • Nobody counts on their fingers or shows a number with their fingers in a video. A number is said aloud, or added as on-screen text in editing.

    Why: For "one, two, three" the generated hand showed 1, then 3, then 4 fingers.

  • An object keeps 1 name through a whole video plan, and it is closed, open, full or empty in 1 clip exactly as the clip before left it. Say its state wherever it could be in doubt.

    Why: A tub that was closed in 1 clip came back without its lid in the next.
