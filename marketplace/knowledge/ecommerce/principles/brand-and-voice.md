# Brand, positioning and voice: craft principles

This book is for the brand trade on a store's team: the strategists, creative directors and PR people who decide what the store stands for, how it looks and how it sounds. Read it before each job and write to it. Then check every draft against the review checklist before you hand it over.

## What great looks like

- The position fits in one sentence, names one buyer and one situation, and stops being true when a rival's name is pasted in.
- Every differentiator is a specific promise, provable from the store's own records, that rivals cannot honestly say.
- Buyer language is quoted exactly, with its source, and none of it was made up by a model.
- With the logo covered, a stranger can still tell the brand's ad, page or box from a rival's.
- Voice rules are markers a reviewer can count (sentence length, reading grade, person, contractions), not adjectives.
- The same promise, phrase and facts appear in ads, pages, emails, pack and press, word for word where it counts.
- Every name, tagline and values claim has been screened in each market the store sells in before money is spent.
- Changes to anything the brand already owns come as a proposal with evidence, a reason and a launch story, never as a side effect.

## The principles

### 1. Claim an open position for one sizeable buyer group that rivals ignore, instead of selling a better copy of the leader

**Why it works:** A me-too product loses on cost per purchase: the incumbent has more budget and richer offers and can afford a higher CPA. A group nobody speaks to has no incumbent to beat.

**How to apply:** In the Meta Ad Library, play rivals' scaling ads and note who they speak to; those groups are taken unless the product truly works differently. Find candidates in long image and video reviews of the category's top Amazon listings, and in openings of situation, life stage or format (a gummy where everyone sells tablets). Confirm size: count separate Reddit threads on the problem phrase; a sub-niche of a few hundred to a thousand people cannot fund paid social. Bloom sold a near-identical greens powder to women. Before rebuilding the brand, run the group as its own ad concept and page, and compare cost per purchase.

**Judge it by:** One group, one situation, evidence it is unserved and large, and a cheap market test. Weak work says "for people who care about quality".

**Breaks when:** The product is visibly better, so a new-mechanism claim to the main group can win; or the group is too small or poorly suited (returns, weak repeat).

**From:** BRD-6487, AVT-0014, AVT-0013, BRD-6491, AVT-0016; logic from Ries and Trout, Positioning.

### 2. Keep a differentiator only if it is a real proposition, rivals cannot say it honestly, and it would move a stranger

**Why it works:** Copy that sounds like rivals gives no reason to choose. Table stakes such as free shipping, great service and fast delivery are expected everywhere and add nothing. Heritage lines like "family owned since 1994" are unique, but they rarely change what a stranger buys.

**How to apply:** Score every claim on the page, pack and ads on three questions. Does it promise a specific thing? Can no rival say it truthfully? Would it move a cold buyer? Keep only claims that pass all three; published third-party lab results usually do. To find more, have an AI model scan every review for benefits the brand never marketed, or stack two benefits the category sells apart (a pillowcase both acne friendly and very comfortable). Then paste a rival's name into the positioning statement. If it still reads true, rewrite it.

**Judge it by:** Each kept differentiator has a proof file and passes the rival-name test. Weak work leads with "premium quality", "made with love" or "fast shipping".

**Breaks when:** Warm or local audiences for whom origin or heritage is the desire itself (gifting, made-in-country buyers); test it there rather than assume it is weak. With only a few hundred reviews, mine support tickets and rival reviews instead.

**From:** OFF-6657, BRD-6488, OFF-6631.

### 3. Build positioning and copy from buyers' exact words, retrieved with links, never from words a model invents

**Why it works:** Buyers trust lines that sound like their own thoughts. Their phrases carry the emotion (fear, embarrassment, money already wasted) that drives the choice. An AI asked what buyers feel returns the same average list for every brand.

**How to apply:** Work down the sources by yield: call recordings, then support tickets and return reasons, then reviews (cheapest), then multiple-choice surveys and forums. In 1-star and 5-star reviews of the top 5 to 10 rivals, hunt for money already spent on failed fixes; that failure is the first thing the page must answer. Paste exact quotes with source and star rating, because summaries strip the vocabulary. Prompt AI with "retrieve, do not generate" and reject any quote without a link. A self-describing phrase ("as a nurse") seen once is an anecdote; seen many times, it is a segment. Keep the quote bank in the org context.

**Judge it by:** Every pillar in the positioning carries at least two buyer phrases with sources. Weak work paraphrases buyers into marketing language ("customers love the quality").

**Breaks when:** A new brand has no buyers yet; use rival and marketplace reviews and mark the work as a draft to test. Private survey quotes may not appear in public copy without permission.

**From:** AVT-0007, AVT-0026, AVT-0016, AVT-0028, AVT-0009.

### 4. Make every touchpoint speak to the chosen buyer, not just the hook

**Why it works:** A group's name in the hook, with a page and proof built for everyone, reads as cosmetic, and conversion falls at the first mismatch. When every element makes one claim, the buyer feels the brand was built for them, and broad targeting works because the creative does the targeting.

**How to apply:** For each persona, line up the first frame, first spoken line, overlay, primary text, headline and top of the landing page. They should make one claim to one awareness stage. Swap the page's photos, testimonials and examples to that persona, or give it its own page. Someone outside the group should feel slightly in the wrong place. A US running shoe brand courting nurses shows a nurse after a 12-hour shift and quotes nurses' reviews, not race times.

**Judge it by:** With the brand name covered, a reviewer can still say who each piece is for, and it is the same person on every piece. Weak work shows luxury in the image, a discount in the text and science in the headline.

**Breaks when:** Early markets where a broad promise still converts; segments too small to fund their own creative; deliberate tests that change one element on purpose.

**From:** AVT-0015, ADC-6218, LPG-6627, AVT-0030.

### 5. Match the proof to the boldest claim, and make every proof something a sceptic can check

**Why it works:** Proof has to answer the doubt the message creates. Proof that cannot be checked now lowers trust: bare press logos and anonymous reviews read as padding to a cold visitor.

**How to apply:** Write down the boldest thing the brand says. Checkable by eye: demos and before and after. Needs expertise: a real, credentialed expert. No claim: admired people the buyer already follows. Rank the proof the brand holds (demonstration, third-party verification, volume of specific social proof, named testimony, mechanism, founder assertion) and lead with the highest. The wording sets the bar: under FTC substantiation policy, "tests prove" needs the tests on file and "doctors recommend" needs the doctors. Under each press logo, add what the outlet said; one practitioner reports a 38% conversion lift from that alone, with no sample given. Show reviewer name, photo and source in the page flow, not in carousels.

**Judge it by:** Each claim on a page sits next to proof of the matching type, and a reviewer could trace it. Weak work: "as seen in" logo strips, "doctors recommend" with no doctor, "thousands of happy customers".

**Breaks when:** Regulated claims are banned whatever the proof; intimate categories where buyers will not show photos, so verified-buyer badges are the ceiling.

**From:** MCR-6754, MCR-6712, LPG-0037, MCR-6022.

### 6. Make price part of the position: signal the tier before checkout, and use offers that frame the product rather than discount it

**Why it works:** A premium price met only at checkout shocks the buyer into leaving. Constant discounts teach buyers to wait; Blue Sense sees offer-led fashion brands stuck near 5% to 10% EBITDA. A framed offer keeps margin and often lifts order value.

**How to apply:** Name the tier and its product reason (durability, materials, testing), and show it in ads through setting, materials and a price shown or implied, so the page confirms rather than surprises. Answer price with cost per use: Lovevery's best ad sets a broken cheap toy beside one that served four children. Write the offer against five parts (positioning, price architecture, value mechanic, risk reversal, urgency) and fill the empty parts before deepening any discount; a bundle can take 20% off while lifting units to three or four. Measure the share of the last 12 months' revenue that needed a discount. If it is most, cut promo windows and cap promo budget near 20% to 30% of spend while full-price creative rebuilds.

**Judge it by:** Price cues in the ads match the page, the premium has a stated reason, and the offer names the parts it uses. Weak work runs luxury imagery with "40% off today".

**Breaks when:** Pure price-led categories or clearance.

**From:** OFF-6624, OFF-6631, OFF-6670, MCR-6637.

### 7. Choose ideas that are non-obvious but provably true, and drop any idea the first ten people or an AI would suggest

**Why it works:** Obvious ideas are what every rival and every model has already made, so they blend in. A surprising fact the brand can prove stops the scroll, and it is believed because a doubter can check it.

**How to apply:** List many ideas and strike every one a generic prompt would return, such as David versus Goliath for a brand called David. Keep ideas built on a fact the store holds: a comparison chart, a test result, an odd review pattern. David's own chart put its bar second only to cod on protein per calorie, so the team shot boiled cod against the bar. Make sure the fact is visible on the site or pack. If the idea cannot yield ten different executions, it is a line, not an idea. Borrow looks from far-off industries, never from rivals.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer can name the fact behind the idea and find it on the store, and the idea would surprise a regular buyer in the category. Weak work: puns on the brand name, "unleash your potential", seasonal clichés.

**Breaks when:** The comparison needs proof the store does not hold, or the idea is surprising but not clearly true, which reads as a gimmick.

**From:** BRD-0021, BRD-0003, ORG-0025.

### 8. Choose one to three distinctive assets the brand can own outright, and put them inside every ad, page and box

**Why it works:** Buyers give ads a glance, so recognition must happen in that glance. Ehrenberg-Bass research says an asset works when it is famous (category buyers link it to the brand) and unique (it calls up only this brand), and uniqueness matters more. Martin Lindstrom's Buyology adds that cues beyond the logo can carry a brand once repeated.

**How to apply:** List candidates: a colour, a pack shape, a character, a visual device, a sound, a phrase. Strike any a rival uses (green in supplements, beige in skincare). Pick one to three and put them inside the image, not in a corner: the same prop in every video, or a numbered series that shows the product each episode. Then cover the logo on five random pieces: could a buyer still tell whose they are?

**Judge it by:** The cover-the-logo test passes. Weak work relies on a corner logo and changes colour every campaign.

**Breaks when:** The brand is too new for any asset to have earned a link, so the name must still do the work. Assets must also fit platform rules: Google bans added logos on the main product image.

**From:** ORG-0012, ORG-0018, GAD-6043; Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, Brands of Distinction (https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction).

### 9. Localise the voice and proof for each market, and do not lead with being foreign unless origin is the selling point

**Why it works:** Fame, reviews and recognition from the home market do not travel. Buyers in a new country fear surprise duties, slow shipping and customs holds, so a brand that looks foreign converts worse. Polished brand shoots that work at home were being carried by awareness the brand does not have abroad.

**How to apply:** For each new market, use local currency, spelling and sizing, a local domain or storefront, and local delivery and returns. Remove home-country cues from ads and pages. Re-voice or reshoot winners with local creators and accents; UK and Australian accents swap fairly well with each other, but both underperform in the US. Lead with ads that explain or prove (a demo, a creator testimonial, a clear offer) and use brand imagery as support. Rewrite the voice markers for local idiom without changing the traits. A UK tea brand entering the US keeps its dry humour but writes "cookie" instead of "biscuit".

**Judge it by:** A local buyer finds no cue that the store ships from abroad, unless origin is the point. Weak work: the home site with a currency switcher bolted on.

**Breaks when:** Origin is itself the desire (skincare from native ingredients), where it can lead as long as shipping and duties are clearly local.

**From:** LPG-6612, MCR-6628, MCR-6629, MCR-6677.

### 10. Tell the founder story through the buyer's own problem, and give the founder only the lines a founder can credibly say

**Why it works:** Strangers do not care about a founder; they keep watching a story they have lived. A founder is most credible on why the business started, why the mechanism works and what failed in rivals' products, and least credible on how it feels.

**How to apply:** Open from the viewer's experience, such as a lifetime of breakouts from makeup. Keep the shared problem alive, explain the mechanism, then answer every question the story opened. Draft three founder lines: origin, mechanism, and a factual comparison with products they tested; move how it feels to customer testimony. Add stakes ("I stopped going to the hairdresser", not "I was looking for something"). Voice only, a proxy or animation can carry the script. Run it as a partnership ad from the founder's handle only if that profile posts relevant content; the reported lift is about 5% to 30%.

**Judge it by:** The first line describes the buyer's problem, not the founder's biography, and every comparison can be proven. Weak work opens with the year the journey began.

**Breaks when:** The founder's problem is rare or hard to relate to, so lead with the customer's problem and bring the founder in as the solver; or a comparison cannot be substantiated.

**From:** MCR-6771, MCR-6763, MCR-6720, MCR-6766, ADC-0003.

### 11. Get creative variety by changing register and format inside the voice, not by changing the brand

**Why it works:** Ad platforms reward variety, and look-alike ads tire fast. Brands often answer this by drifting off brand. Changing the register (casual UGC, a flat documentary voice, a private diary entry) or the format gives new-feeling ads from the same research, with no new claims and no new persona.

**How to apply:** Once a concept works, brief two more registers before inventing new angles. Count live ads by format; if one family (any kind of UGC) is over about two thirds, add a mechanism explainer, a comedy static or a voice-only founder ad. Run the scrappier pain-point hooks through creators' handles as partnership ads, so the brand page stays clean. The best fashion accounts Blue Sense sees run 30% to 40% pain-point hooks, against a typical 10%. Keep fixed: assets, claims, facts and the do-not-say list. Vary: register, person, pace and setting.

**Judge it by:** The batch looks different in the feed, yet a reviewer can see the same brand in every piece. Weak work: ten UGC clones, or an ad in a voice the brand would never use.

**Breaks when:** A register clashes with the brand or the placement (a slow documentary voice in a fast Reels slot), or a luxury house where any problem framing jars.

**From:** MCR-6697, MCR-6639, MCR-6836, ADC-0003.

### 12. Fund brand building as a planned budget line once performance channels flatten, and read it on brand search and blended efficiency

**Why it works:** Performance ads can carry a brand to roughly $20M a year, but past about $30,000 to $40,000 a day, more spend stops buying growth. Blue Sense found a $300M fashion account beat an $8M one on CPMs, click-through, CPCs and ROAS at about 20 times the spend, thanks to associations built outside the ad account. Binet and Field's IPA work found the best average balance near 60% brand and 40% activation, mostly in large brands.

**How to apply:** Below about $20M, build the brand inside performance creative: distinctive assets, one voice, founder content. Near the ceiling, add a brand line for creators, events and communities where buyers already spend attention. Judge it on branded search, direct traffic, blended new-customer cost and survey answers, not code ROAS. Put weekly Meta spend next to weekly branded searches; brand search rising after Meta increases means Meta creates demand. Keep brand search ads lean.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the store's stage, the budget line and the reads. Weak work applies a 60/40 split to a small store.

**Breaks when:** Small brands far from performance limits, or weeks of heavy PR or TV that blur the brand search read.

**From:** BRD-6489, MEA-6356, GAD-6223; IPA, The Long and the Short of It (https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it/).

### 13. Lead the first purchase with a selfish benefit, and keep the values story as the reason to stay, stated in specific, provable terms

**Why it works:** At the point of choice, most buyers will not pay extra for someone else's benefit. They buy for scent, look, taste or results; the ethical layer is what makes switching away feel like a loss. Vague green words also carry legal risk. Since 27 September 2026 the EU bans generic claims like "eco-friendly" unless proven, the UK Green Claims Code wants claims specific and substantiated, and the FTC Green Guides warn against unqualified general claims.

**How to apply:** Write the headline promise in terms of the buyer's senses, status or results, and check it sells without the values claim. Move values to the pack, inserts and post-purchase content, stated as scoped facts ("bottle made from 100% recycled plastic, excluding the cap"), not "sustainable". Keep a proof file for each, per market.

**Judge it by:** The hero line sells without the cause, and each values claim says what, how much and how it is proven. Weak work puts "clean, conscious, planet-friendly" in the headline.

**Breaks when:** Mission-led buyers choose on ethics first, so the cause is the headline; or the sensory benefit is weak, so the values claim is all there is.

**From:** BRD-0001, MCR-6022; EU Directive 2024/825 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2024/825/oj); CMA Green Claims Code (https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/green-claims-code-making-environmental-claims); FTC Green Guides, 16 CFR Part 260 (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-260).

### 14. Tie the brand to the buying moments it should own, then name those moments in product names, packs and ads

**Why it works:** People think of brands when a need or situation comes up. Byron Sharp and Jenni Romaniuk at Ehrenberg-Bass call these triggers category entry points; the more of them a brand is linked to, the more often it comes to mind. A daily ritual also keeps buyers talking long after the purchase.

**How to apply:** From reviews, surveys and search terms, list the situations that start a purchase: a time of day, an occasion, a problem, a gift, a season. Pick two to four the brand can credibly own and rivals do not claim. Attach the product to a habit that already exists (morning coffee, the school run). Put the moment in names, instructions, pack and creative. Package existing products as the kit for a seasonal job, such as a full-price New Year reset bundle, before reaching for a discount. A US coffee brand might own the 3pm slump rather than "great taste".

**Judge it by:** The work names specific moments, each with evidence that it triggers buying. Weak work speaks only of attributes (premium, clean, tasty).

**Breaks when:** Products used only now and then; rituals so effortful that buyers drop them; daily-use health claims that regulators read as therapeutic.

**From:** ADC-0002, OFF-6624; Ehrenberg-Bass Institute on category entry points and mental availability (https://marketingscience.info).

### 15. Keep one fact sheet for every number and claim, and update live work the day a fact changes

**Why it works:** Mismatched numbers look careless at best and deceptive at worst. Blue Sense found IM8 ads launched on the same day claiming over 700,000 and about 395,000 customers, while its filings showed 414,000. AI assistants also repeat what the web says most often, so stale facts spread.

**How to apply:** Keep one fact sheet in the org context: customer count and how it was counted, review count and rating with the date, ingredient or material lists, delivery and returns terms, awards with the year. Check every new ad, page, email, press kit and pitch against it before launch. When a fact changes, list every live asset that carries the old one, then update or pause it. State key facts plainly on the store's own pages, so assistants do not fill the gap from forums.

**Judge it by:** Every number in the work matches the sheet, and the sheet is dated. Weak work uses rounded or inflated counts that differ by channel.

**Breaks when:** Very small accounts with a handful of live assets, where a hand check is enough.

**From:** MCR-6609, AEO-0017, MCR-6022.

### 16. Treat owned assets as fixed: audit them for drift, and change one only with evidence and a plan

**Why it works:** Each inconsistency in colour, font, logo or phrase weakens the memory link the store has paid for, and a redesign can erase years of recognition. Ehrenberg-Bass cites Tropicana's pack redesign, which the brand soon reversed, at an estimated $26.3 million in lost sales.

**How to apply:** Collect the live home page, top product page, two recent emails, the pack and two live ads. Mark every drift in colour, type, logo lock-up, tagline or voice. Rank fixes by how many buyers see each touchpoint, so ads and the top product page come first. To change an asset, propose it with the reason, the evidence (not recognised, clashes in a new market, legal conflict) and a staged roll-out that keeps one familiar asset constant. Never let a freelancer or image tool restyle an asset as a side effect.

**Judge it by:** The audit names each drift with a screenshot and a fix ranked by reach. Weak work: a new look every quarter, or campaign fonts that differ from the site.

**Breaks when:** An asset still registers as neither famous nor unique after real effort, so revise or replace it; or a trademark conflict forces the change.

**From:** ADC-6218, BRD-0020; Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, Brands of Distinction (https://marketingscience.info/brands-of-distinction).

### 17. Write the voice as three or four traits, each with a this-not-that rule and a marker a reviewer can count

**Why it works:** Adjectives like "friendly" mean something different to every writer and AI tool, so copy drifts. Counted markers (sentence length, reading grade, person, contractions) hold when five writers share the voice.

**How to apply:** Have the owner sort real lines from the store's and rivals' copy into loved and disliked. Measure both sets: sentence length and spread, reading grade, person, contractions, emoji, jargon. The gaps become the traits. Place the voice on Nielsen Norman Group's four tone dimensions (funny to serious, formal to casual, respectful to irreverent, enthusiastic to matter-of-fact). Online, aim at grade 5 to 8, cut sentences over about 20 words, and vary length. Give each trait a rule: "it lasts two winters", not "built to last". Paste the rules into every AI prompt; humans write first-person originals, AI only remixes. Blind test: can a reader pick the brand's lines from rivals'?

**Judge it by:** Every trait has a number or check and a before-and-after line taken from the store's own copy. Weak work: "Our voice is warm, witty and authentic."

**Breaks when:** Technical or clinical buyers expect exact terms; very low grades read as childish for premium brands unless the rhythm varies.

**From:** ORG-0024, ORG-0025; Nielsen Norman Group, The Four Dimensions of Tone of Voice (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/).

### 18. Choose a casual, conversational voice that still reads as trustworthy, and use humour only where the brand already speaks that way

**Why it works:** In a Nielsen Norman Group survey of 100 US adults, casual, conversational tones performed best, and trust explained 52% of how desirable people found a brand, against 8% more for friendliness. Irreverent versions were always friendlier but mixed on trust: less trusted for an insurer, more for a home security firm. Humour that fits the brand can build recall and reach colder buyers cheaply.

**How to apply:** Default to plain, warm, conversational language: contractions, "you", short sentences. Go easy on irreverence where the buyer takes a risk (health, money, children, safety). If the brand is already funny, brief a few comedy statics built on a real category insight, with the product present but not the point. Judge them on spend share, cost per purchase and new-customer reach over a few weeks, not on click-through alone. Never make the buyer's problem the joke.

**Judge it by:** The copy sounds like a helpful person, and no joke undercuts a claim, a price or a policy. Weak work: forced jokes in a clinical brand, or stiff corporate copy in a casual one.

**Breaks when:** Luxury or clinical positions where restraint is the signal, or categories where buyers fear being mocked.

**From:** MCR-6622, ORG-0024; Nielsen Norman Group, The Impact of Tone of Voice on Users' Brand Perception (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-voice-users/).

### 19. Hold the voice constant and shift the tone by moment: welcome, launch, sale, delay, complaint, refund

**Why it works:** Buyers judge a brand most when things go wrong. A cheerful late-order email reads as not listening and creates tickets; a plain, specific delay notice keeps trust, and a complaint handled generously becomes a story the buyer retells.

**How to apply:** Build a tone-by-moment table. Welcome: warm, brief. Launch: more energy. Sale: clear terms, no hype. Delay: facts first, the new date, what the store is doing. Complaint: own it, no jokes, one fix. Refund: fast, no guilt. For each moment, write one line in the brand's voice and name the tone dimension that moves. State real delivery times on pages and order emails (US shoppers expect about two days), and track where-is-my-order tickets as a share of orders. True Classic gives support a fixed weekly budget to go beyond the fix on serious complaints, logs each gesture and watches the friend-or-family survey share.

**Judge it by:** The guide has lines for at least the delay and complaint moments, and support copy passes the same markers as marketing copy. Weak work: exclamation marks in a delay email, or a different persona in support than in ads.

**Breaks when:** Legally required wording (recalls, safety notices) sets the text, so the voice applies only around it.

**From:** CXP-6609, CXP-0022; Nielsen Norman Group, The Four Dimensions of Tone of Voice (https://www.nngroup.com/articles/tone-of-voice-dimensions/).

### 20. When demand rests on one person, price that risk and build selling that works without them

**Why it works:** Where the lever is, the risk is. Coty paid $200M for 20% of Kim Kardashian's beauty brand and sold the stake four years later for $74M, after her attention moved to Skims. rhode reported $169M of net sales on $19.5M of marketing in 2024, because its founder created the demand; its simple catalog ads only worked on top of her.

**How to apply:** If a founder, celebrity or creator drives sales, measure how much revenue moves with their posts. Then build what sells without them: product innovation, customer content, explainers, and a voice that belongs to the brand rather than only to the person. Sign partners who already use the product daily, on multi-year terms. Tie pay to sales or equity, with a share that steps down as revenue grows. Never copy a talent-led brand's ad account as a template.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the dependency with a number and a step to reduce it. Weak work says "partner with a celebrity" with no plan for when they move on.

**Breaks when:** Demand is spread out, so no single person drives sales.

**From:** BRD-6492, BRD-6493, BRD-6486, ORG-0039.

### 21. Plan word of mouth only where others see the product, a social script prompts the question, and the owner gains by answering

**Why it works:** Recommendation spreads when three things hold. A non-buyer meets the product in normal use, it is normal to ask about it, and the person answering gains status or belonging. TOMS shoes met all three. TOMS coffee, made alone with the label out of sight, did not.

**How to apply:** For each hero product, write three lines: where a non-buyer sees it, what makes them ask, and what the owner gains by answering. If one is missing, design it in: a visible mark on the product, a serving or unboxing moment, a result worth showing. If all three are missing, plan paid acquisition, referrals or a review engine instead of hoping for organic spread. A daily ritual adds daily mentions. Measure word of mouth with a "how did you hear about us" question after purchase, and check it against spend.

**Judge it by:** The plan states the three conditions for each product and the fix for each gap. Weak work says "this will go viral" with no visible moment of use.

**Breaks when:** Private products (supplements, intimate care) still spread through forums and close friends when the problem is shared; there, an outcome worth reporting matters more than visibility.

**From:** BRD-0007, ADC-0002, CXP-0022, MEA-0045.

### 22. Design the pack for where it is bought: a shelf pack sells in a second, and a delivery box sets up first use and the next order

**Why it works:** On a shelf, a shopper has a moment to see what the product is, so the pack works like direct-response creative. A delivery box is seen after purchase, so its job is first value and repeat; inserts are reportedly opened about 95% of the time, against about 20% for email.

**How to apply:** For retail, the variant and top two claims must read at a glance from a step away; lay out the legally required panel first, since it fixes much of the surface. Ross McKay describes Cadence's minimal DTC can selling poorly in stores until a coloured rim and plain claims went on. For a delivery box, a basic branded moment costs under $0.50 and a mid tier $1.50 to $3, about 2% to 5% of product cost. Give the insert two jobs: a QR code to a first-use guide and a reason to reorder.

**Judge it by:** Each pack part has a job and the cost fits the margin. Weak work: a beautiful box with no first-use help, or a coupon posing as a gift.

**Breaks when:** Very low-price items, where any extra eats the margin; sustainability-led brands, where extra layers draw criticism.

**From:** CXP-0008, BRD-0003; logic from Ross McKay on building Cadence.

### 23. Generate names wide from one jumping-off word and three briefs, break the category's naming pattern, and expect the best name to split the team

**Why it works:** Comfortable, descriptive names test well because people instantly know what they are, then vanish among rivals. Lexicon's founder puts Swiffer, picked over P&G's preferred ProMop, at about a $5B brand, while the descriptive Ready Mop stayed far smaller. Founders usually stop at 50 to 100 names; Lexicon makes about 2,000.

**How to apply:** Write the one word for the feeling the brand must carry. Map rival names and rule out the main pattern. Run three briefs: the real product, the product with a twist, and a nearby world with the product unnamed. Judge nothing until the list is long. Climb from attributes to the buyer's ultimate benefit, pick a familiar word that surprises here, and let a descriptor line carry the category. Mock finalists in context (a headline, a pack) before any vote. Sound is only a tiebreaker: P, K and B read fast and reliable, which suits tools, not sleep. Avoid initials.

**Judge it by:** The shortlist came from a wide pool, and each name has a one-line reason tied to the position. Weak work: names that describe the category.

**Breaks when:** Marketplace-first or search-led brands need a descriptive keyword in the name, or a new category needs the name to explain the product.

**From:** BRD-0002, BRD-0017, BRD-0018, BRD-0016, BRD-0019.

### 24. Screen every name for sound-alikes and same-meaning marks in each market and marketplace before buying stock, domain or packaging

**Why it works:** Trademark offices refuse marks that are confusingly similar on related goods, not only identical ones. Similar sound, look, meaning or overall impression counts, including different spellings said the same way. Earlier unregistered use can also block a store. A late conflict means relabelling stock and losing the search history the name has earned.

**How to apply:** For each finalist, search the national register in every market the store sells in (such as the USPTO, UK IPO, EUIPO and IP Australia), plus US state registers, marketplaces, social handles and the open web, for the same or similar name on related goods. Check meanings and translations in every language the buyers speak. Check the domain and handles. Hand the survivors to a trademark attorney before money is spent. Plan the filing early: Amazon Brand Registry needs a pending or registered trademark and the brand on the product or pack, and Google limits a rival's use of a brand in ad text only where the owner holds rights.

**Judge it by:** A check table for each name in each market, with the attorney hand-off noted. Weak work: an exact-match search in one database.

**Breaks when:** This is screening, not legal clearance; whether a mark can be registered is an examiner's or attorney's call.

**From:** BRD-6484, BRD-6485, AMZ-6441, GAD-6120.

### 25. Rename only when the name causes measurable friction, and launch the change with a why-and-what-you-gain story

**Why it works:** Fear of losing momentum keeps weak names alive, yet a bad name taxes every sale through misspellings, lost searches and legal risk. Lexicon's founder reports no case of a rename losing equity when it launched with a clear story. New ranges face a related choice: a new name keeps both positions clean, but it means building a second brand.

**How to apply:** List the name's friction with numbers: misspelled branded searches, confusion with other brands, legal letters. If renaming, plan a launch story across site, email and pack: where we were, where we are going, what you gain, and that the product is not changing. Plan redirects, marketplace brand records and old pack run-out. Expect six to nine months of extra effort, and track branded search for 90 to 120 days. For a new range, keep the house name for variants and companions; consider a new name for a big, different product for a new buyer, per Ries and Trout's line-extension rules.

**Judge it by:** The case for change has numbers, and the launch plan has a story and a tracking window. Weak work: a quiet name swap.

**Breaks when:** Established brands with large earned search, reviews and listings tied to the name, where redirects lose rank; heritage brands whose name is the main asset.

**From:** BRD-0020, AMZ-6441, MEA-6356.

### 26. Pitch the press a true story that serves the writer's readers, on the outlet's lead time, with a kit that checks out

**Why it works:** Journalists delete pitches that miss their beat. In Muck Rack's 2026 survey, 47% said pitches are seldom or never relevant, and 51% said only 1% to 10% of their stories began with one. Coverage that lands can be quoted on the store and cited by AI assistants.

**How to apply:** Find a true angle first: a milestone, a number from the store's data, a launch, or a product with a clear recipient and price band for a gift guide. Pick writers who covered the category recently, mention their piece, and keep it short. Print gift guides are planned months before the season and online ones much later, so ask each editor's deadline and pitch before it. The kit holds a fact sheet matching the brand's fact file, the founder story, prices, links and cleared images. Label paid placements as ads. Quote the outlet's line under its logo on the store.

**Judge it by:** Each pitch names the writer's recent piece and a reader benefit, and every claim matches the fact sheet. Weak work: a mass release calling the product revolutionary.

**Breaks when:** No true angle exists, so wait for one; or stock will not last through the coverage window.

**From:** LPG-0037, AEO-0013; Muck Rack, State of Journalism 2026, via PR Daily (https://www.prdaily.com/journalists-want-your-help-but-say-most-pitches-miss-the-mark/).

### 27. Repeat one exact positioning phrase and the key facts across the site, founder content and earned media, so AI assistants learn them

**Why it works:** AI assistants answer many questions from what the web says most often, sometimes without searching, so old or wrong claims persist for years. In one case, one consistent statement repeated across the site, founder videos and third-party articles moved a model's answer from 9 months or more to about 6, over two years. Widely syndicated releases can be cited for their headline phrase.

**How to apply:** Choose the phrase the brand wants to be known for, plus the facts buyers ask about (delivery, returns, materials, price range, who it is for). State them plainly on the store's own pages. Repeat the exact phrase in founder videos, social posts, press kits and releases for real news, in the headline and first line. Claim only what is proven; invented ratings are a consumer-protection risk. Test models with search off, add "AI assistant" to the how-did-you-hear survey, and expect change over training cycles, not weeks.

**Judge it by:** The same phrase and facts appear word for word across owned and earned pages. Weak work: a different tagline on every channel.

**Breaks when:** The brand lacks the reach to outweigh existing sources; when the answer comes from live search, fix the cited pages instead.

**From:** AEO-0017, AEO-0013, AEO-0010.

## Working from the org context

- Read the positioning, pillars and proof bank first. If they are missing, draft them with the owner and mark them as draft, because voice, names and campaigns all sit on top of the position.
- Read each persona's own phrases and the brand's reading level. Write at that grade, and send any new buyer phrases you find back to the org context, not into the deliverable.
- Read the design system and the distinctive assets as fixed inputs. Put them in every brief exactly as stored, and never let a freelancer or image tool restyle them.
- Read the fact sheet (counts, ratings, terms, approved claims) and quote product facts and approved claims word for word. A number that is not in the brief or the sheet does not go in the work.
- Read what ran and won: top ads by spend and new-customer cost, the best emails, the top pages. Keep what the winners share (hook type, register, asset, phrase) before proposing anything new.
- Read the owner's loved and disliked lines and the do-not-say list. They outrank your taste.
- Read the markets and languages the store sells in, and check every name, phrase, joke and values claim in each one.
- Where the brand can do better (asset drift, generic claims, table stakes in the lead), put the proposal in a separate note: what to change, why with one number, the evidence, what stays the same and how to test it. Nothing live changes without the owner's yes.
- After the job, log what you learned (a register that won, a phrase that fell flat) to the org context so the next job starts ahead.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does the position name one buyer group and one situation, and does it stop being true when a rival's name is pasted in?
- [ ] Was a new position run as its own ad concept and page, with cost per purchase compared, before the brand was rebuilt around it?
- [ ] Does every differentiator promise a specific thing, belong only to this brand, and have a proof file?
- [ ] Is every buyer phrase an exact quote with a source, with none invented by a model?
- [ ] Do the ad's first frame, first line, headline and landing page make one claim to one buyer?
- [ ] With the logo covered, can you still tell whose work it is?
- [ ] Do colours, type, logo and tagline match the org context with no drift?
- [ ] Does the hero promise sell on a selfish benefit, with any values claim specific and provable in every market?
- [ ] Do the price cues in the creative match the price on the page, and does the offer add value through its five parts before any deeper discount?
- [ ] Is every proof word ("tests prove", "doctors recommend", "clinically shown") backed by that exact level of evidence on file?
- [ ] Does every number match the fact sheet and its date?
- [ ] Does each claim sit beside proof of the right type that a sceptic could check?
- [ ] Does the copy meet the voice markers (reading grade, sentence length, person, contractions) for its channel?
- [ ] Do delay, complaint and refund messages put the facts first in the tone set for that moment?
- [ ] Does any founder story open on the buyer's problem and answer every question it opens?
- [ ] For each new market, are currency, spelling, sizing, delivery terms and creators local?
- [ ] Has every name been screened for sound-alikes and meanings in every market and language, with an attorney hand-off noted?
- [ ] Does each press pitch name the writer's recent piece and a true angle for their readers?
- [ ] Are proposed changes to owned assets kept in a separate note, with evidence, waiting for the owner's yes?

## Common failures

- Positioning built from adjectives ("premium, clean, natural"). Fix: one buyer, one situation and one proven differentiator, then the rival-name test.
- Leading with table stakes such as free shipping or great service. Fix: run the three-question test and move table stakes to the FAQ.
- A voice guide that lists adjectives. Fix: three or four traits, each with a counted marker and a before-and-after line from the store's own copy.
- Customer language invented by AI. Fix: retrieve real quotes with links, or mark the line as a draft to test.
- A new look every campaign, or freelancers restyling the logo and colours. Fix: lock the assets in the brief and audit drift by reach.
- Generic green or "clean" claims in the headline. Fix: sell the selfish benefit first and state values as specific, scoped, proven facts.
- Founder stories that open on the founder's biography. Fix: open on the viewer's problem and give the founder only origin, mechanism and tested-comparison lines.
- Names that describe the category or were picked because everyone agreed. Fix: generate wide from three briefs, break the naming pattern, then screen in every market.
- Different customer counts or ratings in different ads. Fix: one dated fact sheet, checked before every launch.
- Copying the polished look of a famous or talent-led brand. Fix: their ads ride demand made elsewhere; lead with proof-led creative and build the brand's own assets.
- Proof words stronger than the evidence ("clinically proven" with no study, "doctors recommend" with no doctor). Fix: cut the wording down to the level of proof on file, or get the proof.
- Mass press releases full of hype. Fix: a short, true pitch to writers who cover the category, timed to their lead times.
