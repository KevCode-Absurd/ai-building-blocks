# Customer experience and support: craft principles

This book is for the customer experience trade: the people who run the support desk, the order and delivery messages, returns and refunds, reviews, chat and the first weeks after a buyer pays. Read it before each job, write to it, and check the draft against the review checklist before hand-over. Every principle is judged the same way: did it keep more buyers coming back, or cut the cost of serving them, without breaking the law or the brand's voice?

## What great looks like

- Contacts per order is the headline number, split by reason, and every reason above a set share has a named owner and a fix date.
- Every delivery promise is a specific date the store beats on at least 9 of 10 orders.
- Buyers hear about their order before they think to ask: confirmed, shipped, out for delivery, delivered, and any delay first.
- Returns offer an exchange or credit first, refund fast when chosen, and log a reason for every line.
- Every return and refund is costed in full (outbound shipping, fees, labour, unsellable stock), not only the refund amount.
- Review asks go to every buyer after they have used the product, inside each platform's rules, and every one to three star review gets a reply.
- Bots answer only from approved facts and hand over to a person with the context attached.
- Front-line staff can fix a problem in one contact, within a written, logged budget.
- Replies sound like the brand, name the buyer's actual problem and give one clear next step with a date.

## The principles

### 1. Promise a specific delivery date, padded so the store beats it at least 90% of the time

**Why it works:** A clear date answers the main worry before purchase and the main question after it. Vendor studies report a 13 to 25% conversion lift from specific dates over vague ranges; trust the direction more than the size.

**How to apply:** Build the date from dispatch cut-off, pick time and carrier transit by zone from the last 90 days of scans. Show "Arrives by Thursday 14" on the product page, cart and confirmation, padded so 90% or more of orders land on or before it. Track accuracy weekly by carrier and zone: orders delivered by the promised date divided by orders shipped. On Amazon seller-fulfilled orders, keep late shipments under 4% and valid tracking over 95%, or the account is at risk. US buyers expect about two-day delivery, so a brand shipping from abroad must state real times on pages and in emails or face more tickets and refunds. The FTC mail order rule needs a reasonable basis for any ship time stated, and 30 days applies if none is.

**Judge it by:** A promise with its on-time rate by zone and carrier. Weak work shows "3 to 7 business days" everywhere, or a fast date the store misses.

**Breaks when:** Made-to-order, pre-order or erratic cross-border lead times; give a window and update it.

**From:** CXP-0010, CXP-6609, CXP-6160, AMZ-6443

### 2. Get each product to its first 80 or so reviews fast, then chase photo reviews that prove the claim, not a perfect score

**Why it works:** Going from no reviews to about 80 roughly doubled conversion in one presenter's stores; the Spiegel Research Center found five reviews made a purchase 270% more likely than none. Past that, count matters less, and purchase likelihood peaked around 4.2 to 4.5 stars, not 5.0.

**How to apply:** For a new product, give it to about 20 real users for specific, honest reviews, disclosed as such; on Amazon, use Vine under 30 reviews. Past the early count, ask for photos and video, and prompt for the outcome the ads promise, such as how it fits or the result after four weeks. Sort photo and claim-matching reviews first. Five reviews about the advertised benefit beat 400 generic ones. Never remove fair low reviews to lift the average.

**Judge it by:** Review count and photo share per product, and a sort order that leads with proof. Weak work celebrates a 4.9 average on 12 reviews.

**Breaks when:** Very high-priced items, where the lift levels off after fewer reviews; regulated categories, where a review can make a claim the brand cannot.

**From:** LPG-0062, LPG-6616, LPG-6620, MCR-6684, AMZ-6440, Spiegel Research Center (https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/How-Online-Reviews-Influence-Sales)

### 3. Read every contact as a defect report: track contacts per 100 orders by reason, and send each reason to whoever can remove it

**Why it works:** Most tickets start upstream, in a page, a carrier, a product or a policy. Faster answers cut cost a little; removing the cause cuts it for good and lifts repeat buying.

**How to apply:** Tag 90 days of tickets by one main reason (order status, delivery, damaged or wrong item, sizing, use, returns, payment, cancel). Report per 100 orders, not raw counts, so growth does not hide progress. Gorgias's merchant data runs from about 20 tickets per 100 orders in food and drink to about 46 in electronics; compare to your category, then to your last quarter. Name an owner (ops, product page, factory, carrier) and a change for each top reason. A UK apparel brand whose top reason is fit sends it to the size chart and factory spec, not to a better macro.

**Judge it by:** A reason table in contacts per 100 orders, with an owner and a fix per top reason. Weak work counts tickets and writes better replies.

**Breaks when:** Most tickets are untagged; fix tagging for two weeks first.

**From:** AVT-0028, MCR-6727, CXP-6612, COG-6323, COG-6361, Gorgias Ecom Lab benchmarks (https://www.gorgias.com/research/stop-benchmarking-against-the-average)

### 4. Send the order messages before the buyer asks, and keep where-is-my-order contacts under 3% of orders

**Why it works:** Order-status contacts are often 30 to 50% of tickets in normal weeks and 50 to 80% at peak. Shoppers check tracking about 4.6 times per order; each unanswered check is a chance to write in.

**How to apply:** Measure order-status contacts as a share of orders: under 3% is healthy, 3 to 8% normal but costly, above 8% points to a fulfilment or carrier problem messages cannot hide. Send four messages: confirmation with the delivery date, shipped with tracking, out for delivery, and delivered with one line of use help. Host tracking on the store's own domain in its own design, with the date, the current step and a help link. Add a delay message the moment a scan stalls past its normal gap. Fix the slowest carrier lane before writing more copy.

**Judge it by:** Contact rate before and after, by reason, and each message written in full for each channel. Weak work adds a tracking link to the confirmation and calls it done.

**Breaks when:** Same-day or next-day delivery, where tracking is rarely checked; or a carrier so unreliable that clearer messages only expose delays, so change carrier first.

**From:** CXP-0012, CXP-0014

### 5. Cut returns at the source: give fit and use facts on the page, and drop claims that bring returns

**Why it works:** A return costs margin, effort and often the customer. In Baymard's 2022 testing, 83% of desktop and 87% of mobile apparel sites gave too little sizing information, and size doubt was a common reason testers abandoned. An angle that over-promises lowers acquisition cost and then refunds the gain.

**How to apply:** Rank return reasons by product. For fit reasons, add garment measurements, model height and size worn, and a fit note drawn from reviews. For not-as-described reasons, compare images and claims with the product; one agency keeps AI imagery off fashion garments because it drifts from the real item and pushes returns up. Set an accurate Shopify product category on every product, since it decides which return reasons buyers can pick. Ask creators to show size details on screen. Check return rate by ad angle and drop any angle with a high return rate even if its return on ad spend looks great; one example ran 50%. Send defect reasons to the factory.

**Judge it by:** A reason-by-product table and a page or ad change for each top reason. Weak work tightens the policy to cut returns.

**Breaks when:** One-size or simple products, where a use guide does the job; or a return rate already at the category norm.

**From:** CXP-6161, MCR-6830, MCR-6833, MCR-6727, CRO-6609, CXP-6612

### 6. Cost every return in full and, in high-return categories, run returns as their own profit line

**Why it works:** The refund line understates the loss. On a $150 order, the returns line shows about $22, but adding the original outbound shipping and the payment fee Shopify does not give back makes it about $34. In fashion, returns can rival sales: one retailer booked about $1.4B of return provisions against $1.1B of net revenue in a year.

**How to apply:** For each SKU, add return label, outbound shipping already paid, unrefunded card fee, handling and repack labour, and the share of returns that cannot be resold. Report it monthly beside return rate by product and reason. Adjust acquisition cost for returns: a 50% return rate doubles real acquisition cost. Use it to rank fixes. US online returns ran about 19.3% of sales in 2025, and retailers judged about 9% of returns fraudulent, so check returned goods before charging a factory for defects.

**Judge it by:** A full cost per return by product and a ranked list of fixes valued in gross profit. Weak work reports refund value only.

**Breaks when:** Low-return categories such as consumables, where the effort is better spent elsewhere.

**From:** MEA-6276, CXP-6610, FIN-6243, MEA-6383, CXP-6613

### 7. Offer an exchange or store credit first in the returns flow, and refund fast when the buyer chooses money

**Why it works:** A return is a fork: handled well, the buyer shops again; handled badly, 71% say they are less likely to buy again (NRF and Happy Returns 2025, 2,006 consumers). Refunds that wait 10 to 14 days for inspection lose the buyer's intent to rebuy.

**How to apply:** Build a self-serve portal that offers, in order, an exchange (with a fit hint for size swaps), store credit, then a refund, with the refund always visible. In low-fraud categories, issue credit or exchange on first carrier scan, not warehouse arrival; one vendor reports 11 times higher repurchase after instant refunds, uncontrolled, so test it. Count days to refund from the buyer's request. Set Shopify return rules so the window counts from delivery and shortest-override windows match the policy page.

**Judge it by:** The portal flow screen by screen, exchange rate and days to refund, and the success test: buyers who returned something rebuy at the same rate as those who did not. Weak work hides the refund option or makes the buyer email to start a return.

**Breaks when:** High-fraud or wardrobing categories, or thin margins that cannot carry paying out before inspection. Where law gives a money refund, credit can be offered but never forced.

**From:** CXP-0007, CXP-0015, CXP-6284

### 8. Remove customer effort before you add delight

**Why it works:** In a study of over 75,000 customers, 96% of those with high-effort service contacts became more disloyal, against 9% with low-effort ones. Exceeding expectations adds little loyalty; repeating details, transfers and chasing take a lot away.

**How to apply:** In problem moments (where is my order, returns, breakages, complaints) cut effort to one touch and never try to delight mid-problem. In chosen moments (unboxing, first use) add one small planned peak. Score each problem journey: contacts per resolved case, and whether the buyer repeats details or chases. Build in this order: accurate dates and proactive tracking, then fast, kind returns, then an unboxing peak and first-use insert, then second-purchase nudges, and only then loyalty tiers and surprise programmes. An Australian homewares store that needs three emails to swap a broken mug builds a photo-upload replacement form before it buys handwritten notes.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the top effort points with counts (contacts per resolved case, reopen rate) and removes them before any delight spend. Weak work leads with gifts, notes and "wow" moments while buyers still chase replies.

**Breaks when:** The operation is already low effort and reliable; then gains move to peaks and community. Severe harm cases still need a human gesture on top of a fast fix.

**From:** CXP-0009, CXP-0011, Dixon, Toman and DeLisi's idea of the effortless experience

### 9. Answer pre-sale chats while the shopper is still deciding, resolve the doubt, and ask for the order

**Why it works:** A pre-sale question is a buyer one answer from purchase. Bigger tickets bring more of them: about 12 to 18% of orders generate a phone call at a 250 USD order value, against about 3% at 40 USD. A reply hours later lands after they bought elsewhere.

**How to apply:** Put pre-sale chats at the front of the queue, ahead of post-sale email. Answer size, delivery date, compatibility and returns with a fact and a link, then make a clear next step: "Size M fits your measurements; it arrives Friday. Want the link?" For follow-up texts, wait about 5 minutes, keep each under 80 characters, end with an open question and hand to a person at the first reply. Adding "anything I can help with?" to an existing automated text drew about 5,000 replies in a week at large brands, and subscribers who chatted showed about 1.5 times the lifetime value (Postscript's figure). Only add this where someone answers fast.

**Judge it by:** Reply time on pre-sale chats in minutes, and chat-to-order rate. Weak work treats pre-sale chats as tickets in the same queue as returns.

**Breaks when:** Low-ticket products at scale, where one-to-one chat costs more than the margin.

**From:** OPS-0006, EML-0031, EML-0035, CXP-6302

### 10. Hold the warehouse to a written pick and ship standard with penalties, and audit its bills every month

**Why it works:** Support cannot apologise its way out of a warehouse that does not ship. One fast-growing brand lost over $20M of revenue when a logistics provider could not ship in a forecast $45M to $50M October and November, and buyers publicly called it a scam.

**How to apply:** Put a picked-on-time standard (for example within 24 to 48 hours of order) in the service agreement with financial penalties, plus a named escalation contact. Before signing, ask for error rate, peak throughput and past fourth-quarter surges, and visit two or three sites. Investigate when delivered-on-time falls under 95%, splitting each delay into pick and courier time; at peak a 24 to 48 hour pick promise can slip to 7 days. Every month, match warehouse and courier invoices to order data; one operator estimates 30 to 40% of brands he mentors are overcharged. Give customs paperwork one owner per region: one missing document once got a brand's stock seized before its biggest selling window.

**Judge it by:** A written standard with penalties, an on-time chart and a monthly invoice check with any overcharges found. Weak work blames "carrier delays" in tickets with no data.

**Breaks when:** Volume is small and flat enough that in-house packing is safer and cheaper.

**From:** OPS-0015, OPS-0008, OPS-0012, OPS-0001

### 11. Refund before a buyer disputes, and keep chargebacks well under the card networks' warning lines

**Why it works:** A chargeback costs the refund plus fees and internal time, about $128 each by one estimate, and processors flag merchants whose rate passes about 0.9%; above 1% a store can lose card acceptance. High dispute or refund rates can also make Shopify hold a reserve on payouts.

**How to apply:** Track disputes as a share of orders weekly, by reason (not received, not as described, fraud). Most "not received" and "not as described" disputes start as an unanswered or refused support request, so refund or replace when the store is at fault before the buyer goes to the bank. On Shopify Payments, submit evidence within the window (usually 7 to 21 days): tracking with delivery scan, order and contact history, policy shown at checkout. Remember card fees are not returned on refunds, so cost them in. For fraud disputes, review Shopify's risk report and 3D Secure on high-risk orders.

**Judge it by:** A dispute rate with reasons and a rule for refunding first. Weak work fights every dispute or ignores the rate until a reserve appears.

**Breaks when:** Organised fraud rings, where refunding on request rewards abuse; route those to fraud review.

**From:** SEC-0004, SEC-6201, FIN-6242, FIN-6243, SEC-0005

### 12. Make cancelling a subscription easy and honest, and save with one offer matched to the reason

**Why it works:** Hard cancelling breaks US negative option law and similar rules, and drives disputes. An honest flow still saves many: about 15% of attempts is good, 20% strong, under 10% weak.

**How to apply:** Keep the cancel button visible in the account and answer cancel requests in support the same day. In the flow, show a short founder video (one-minute videos took weak flows from about 5 to 6% saved to about 15%), then ask the reason from 6 or 7 options and match one save: too much product gets a delay or lower frequency, price gets a next-order discount, which beat gifts in tests. Offer a delay of up to about 30 days rather than an open pause, since only about 10% of open-ended pauses ever restart. Every path must still end in a cancel in a few clicks.

**Judge it by:** Save rate by reason, with cancel completion in a few clicks. Weak work blocks cancel behind a phone call or loops offers.

**Breaks when:** Margins too thin for a deep next-order discount; churn caused by the product, which the product owner must fix.

**From:** CXP-0001, CXP-0002, CXP-0004, CXP-0005, OFF-6304

### 13. Time first-order and subscription messages to when buyers actually cancel: heavy before delivery, quiet at first use, a reason to stay before the first rebill

**Why it works:** Cancels cluster. One operator sees almost none between purchase and delivery, then a spike at delivery and first use (about days 7 to 14). Another sees subscription peaks on day 1, around day 28 when the billing reminder lands, and on days 31 to 45. Every message also reminds the buyer they can cancel.

**How to apply:** Chart 90 days of cancels by day since first order to find the store's own peaks. Before the parcel lands, send education daily or every other day: how and when to use it, what other buyers noticed. Send tips on delivery day, one check-in at week one or two, then go quiet. Give day-1 and day-28 cancellers a reason to stay announced early, such as a month-two gift named on the product page and in the billing reminder; unannounced gifts rarely pay.

**Judge it by:** A cancel-by-day chart with the send calendar laid over it, and a reason to stay named before the first rebill. Weak work sends one weekly cadence to everyone, or a surprise gift nobody was told about.

**Breaks when:** One-off purchases with no rebill; products with an instant, obvious effect; churn caused by the product itself.

**From:** EML-0004, CXP-0021, CXP-0006, CXP-0005

### 14. When an order will be late or is out of stock, tell the buyer first and give them the choice to wait or get a full refund

**Why it works:** A buyer told about a delay keeps trust; one who finds out alone writes in, disputes or posts a review. US law requires notice and a free way to cancel with a prompt refund.

**How to apply:** Send a delay notice as soon as dispatch will miss the promise, with a new date and two buttons: keep my order, or cancel for a full refund. Under the FTC rule a backorder allows only consent to the delay or a full prompt refund; a materially different item (colour, style, fabric) needs the buyer's agreement first, and the refund cannot be store credit. Keep offers out: on WhatsApp a delay update with a discount becomes a marketing template. On pre-orders, show the ship date on the page and check Shopify's out-of-stock settings match it.

**Judge it by:** Delay messages that go out before the promised date, with both choices and a new date. Weak work waits for tickets, or quietly ships a substitute.

**Breaks when:** The buyer agreed to a substitute or credit in advance; outside the US, check local law.

**From:** CXP-6160, OPS-6281, OPS-6286, OPS-6282, OPS-6283

### 15. Refund without a return when sending it back costs more than the item is worth, and watch who claims it twice

**Why it works:** Processing a return can cost 20 to 65% of the item's cost, and low-value returns often cannot be resold. A keep-it refund saves that cost and the buyer's effort. In a 2023 survey, 59% of retailers offered them, mostly on items up to about $20.

**How to apply:** For each SKU, compare the full return cost (label, handling, repack, resale chance) with the recovered value. Where return cost is higher, refund or replace without asking for the item back. Do not advertise the policy; apply it case by case from support. Create a no-ship return with the reason and a photo before refunding: a refund issued without a return records no reason, so the defect vanishes from the data. Track claims per customer and per address, and route a second claim within a set period to a normal return. A US pet treats store would refund a $12 damaged bag on a photo and keep the label cost.

**Judge it by:** A threshold per product backed by the cost math, and a repeat-claim check. Weak work offers keep-it refunds to everyone, or to no one, without numbers.

**Breaks when:** High-value or easily resold items; advertised policies where abuse rises past the roughly 9% of returns retailers already judge fraudulent.

**From:** CXP-0018, CXP-6613, MEA-6276, CXP-6612

### 16. Ask every buyer for a review after they have used the product, and follow the strictest rule of the platform it lands on

**Why it works:** Reviews sell only while buyers believe them. Asking only happy buyers or hiding bad reviews skews the score and breaks rules in several markets.

**How to apply:** Time the ask from the delivery scan plus the time to see results: days for apparel, weeks for skincare. Send it to every buyer with the same wording, and never put a satisfaction survey in front of it that filters out unhappy buyers. The FTC allows incentives only with no stated or implied sentiment, bans threats to remove reviews, wants good and bad reviews moderated alike, and wants rewards disclosed. Google bans any review incentive and selective asking. Amazon allows only Request a Review and Vine, with no asks in the box. The UK bans fake and concealed incentivised reviews and fines up to 10% of global turnover. EU stores must say whether and how they check reviewers are real buyers.

**Judge it by:** An ask schedule per product from real delivery and use times, and a rules table per platform. Weak work asks for five stars the day the order ships.

**Breaks when:** Nothing here relaxes it; local rules only add to it.

**From:** CXP-6300, CXP-6301, CXP-6303, AMZ-6448, SEO-6465, CMA fake reviews guidance (https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/67eeb64fe9c76fa33048c790/CMA208_-_Fake_reviews_guidance.pdf), EU Directive 2019/2161 (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/2161/oj)

### 17. Reply in public to every one to three star review: name the problem, fix it privately, and never trade anything for a change

**Why it works:** A reply is written for the next buyer, not the reviewer. In a TripAdvisor study, hotels that began replying saw ratings rise about 0.12 stars and review volume about 12%, and got fewer short, unfair negative reviews.

**How to apply:** Sort each review by type: product fault, delivery, expectation gap, service, or fake and abusive. Reply within days, in under 80 words and the brand's voice: name the issue, say what was or will be done, and give a direct contact. Fix it privately, then let the buyer decide whether to update; the FTC bans intimidation to remove reviews, and Google bans any incentive to change one. Log each cause; a cluster after one batch or carrier change is a fix, not a reply.

**Judge it by:** Reply rate and time on low reviews, replies that name the problem, and a cause log. Weak work is defensive, generic, or offers a discount for an edit.

**Breaks when:** A review is abusive or holds personal data; report it instead of replying.

**From:** CXP-6301, CXP-6300, Proserpio and Zervas, Marketing Science 2017 (https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Want-to-improve-your-business-s-online-ratings-Make-sure-to-respond-to-reviews)

### 18. Hand buyers' exact words from tickets, reviews and surveys to the people who write pages and ads, every month

**Why it works:** The trade that reads every complaint holds the best copy research in the business. Words lifted from a real buyer answer the next buyer's doubt more credibly than brand claims, and automated summaries strip out the emotional words copy needs.

**How to apply:** Each month, pull verbatim quotes by objection: 2 to 4 star reviews for doubts, tickets for confusion, return reasons for broken promises, and a thank-you page question ("what almost stopped you buying?"), which one presenter's clients see completed 85 to 90% of the time with no incentive. Paste the exact text with source and date, grouped by the objection it answers (taste, fit, results timing, ease). Hand the top near-miss reason to the page owner with three quotes that answer it, and flag any claim buyers say the product fails. Keep private survey quotes out of public copy unless the brand has permission.

**Judge it by:** A quote bank grouped by objection, each quote verbatim with its source. Weak work hands over "customers love the quality" summaries.

**Breaks when:** Too few reviews or tickets to quote fairly; then interview about 20 buyers instead.

**From:** AVT-0027, AVT-0007, AVT-0028, LPG-0005, LPG-0042, CRO-0018, EML-0033, AVT-0017

### 19. Use the first 14 days after purchase to reassure, teach correct use and set the time to results

**Why it works:** Buyer's remorse and wrong use both cause returns, cancels and bad reviews. A buyer who does not use the product properly will not reorder, however good the emails are. Most second purchases happen within about 30 days of the first.

**How to apply:** Send a thank-you within about 10 minutes of the order, a reassurance email about 2 days later, and a how-to email timed to arrival, not order date; one documented sequence saw a 47.4% open rate on its reassurance email. For products without an instant effect, frame a trial: a before photo on day 0 and day 30, or a 7-day prove-it challenge. Find the routine linked to the best retention (one brand found buyers who took its creatine in morning coffee stayed longest) and teach that habit. Add a support link and the delivery date to every message.

**Judge it by:** A sequence keyed to delivery and use, with time-to-results stated in the brand's voice. Weak work sends cross-sell offers before the parcel has arrived.

**Breaks when:** Simple, self-explanatory products where how-to emails add noise; same-day delivery, which removes the pre-arrival window.

**From:** EML-0051, EML-0062, CXP-0003, EML-0005, EML-0004

### 20. When goods arrive faulty, damaged or wrong, the store pays and fixes it in one reply; know each market's remedy rights

**Why it works:** A fault is the store's failure, and most markets' law says so. Making the buyer pay postage or argue turns a fixable problem into a review, a dispute and a lost customer.

**How to apply:** Accept a photo as proof for low-value items and send the replacement or refund in the same reply. Never make the buyer pay to return a faulty item; in the EU the trader carries that cost. In Australia, for a major failure the buyer chooses refund or replacement, for a minor one the store picks repair, replacement or refund, and "no refunds" or "exchange only" signs are unlawful. Log each case against batch and supplier, check returned stock before charging the factory, and book the cost as external failure for the quality owner.

**Judge it by:** One-reply resolution rate for damage and wrong-item cases, each linked to a batch. Weak work asks the buyer to return it at their cost for inspection.

**Breaks when:** High-value items or repeat claimers, where inspection first is fair; pay the postage.

**From:** CXP-6613, COG-6361, OPS-6281, ACCC consumer guarantees (https://www.accc.gov.au/business/selling-products-and-services/small-business-toolkit/consumer-rights-and-guarantees/what-if-a-consumer-guarantee-is-not-met), NSW Fair Trading on refund signs (https://www.nsw.gov.au/legal-and-justice/consumer-rights-and-protection/repairs-replacements-and-refunds/rights-to-a-repair)

### 21. Set the return window and postage terms from the legal floor and your category's return habits, not from what rivals do

**Why it works:** About 15% of abandoners cite the returns policy, so a fair policy sells. But free returns in fashion invite heavy returning, and a long window need not mean more claims: on low-ticket consumables a 180-day guarantee drew fewer claims than a 30-day one.

**How to apply:** In the EU and UK, online buyers may cancel within 14 days of receiving goods without a reason; the refund is due within 14 days of notice, though the seller may wait for the goods or proof of sending. The buyer pays return postage only if told before buying. Then by category: Google's Top Quality Store score weighs the returns window and return cost, and one agency advises 30 days or more for it. In fashion, test free against paid returns on full profit, and budget US fashion returns at up to double many home markets.

**Judge it by:** Terms per market with the legal floor named, and a cost test for any free-returns change. Weak work copies a rival's policy.

**Breaks when:** Made-to-order, personalised or perishable goods, which are exempt from the EU withdrawal right.

**From:** CXP-6611, OFF-0060, GAD-0018, MEA-6266, OFF-6669, EU right of withdrawal (https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/shopping/returns/index_en.htm), EU Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/83/oj)

### 22. Let a bot answer only from approved, sourced facts, give a clear path to a person, and treat what it says as the store's promise

**Why it works:** A bot that invents a policy commits the store to it: in Moffatt v Air Canada (2024), a tribunal held the airline liable for its chatbot's wrong refund advice. Models also drift to generic answers unless fed only the brand's own material.

**How to apply:** Build the bot's knowledge from real tickets: each question with an approved answer, a source and an owner. Allow order lookups; never refunds, legal or medical advice, promises outside policy, or posing as a person. WhatsApp requires a prompt, clear path to a human for automated replies; apply the same rule on site chat. Hand over with the order and conversation attached. Test on 50 past conversations before launch, review wrong answers weekly, and update the bot the day a policy changes.

**Judge it by:** A sourced answer set, a never-do list, a handover rule and a test sheet with pass rates. Weak work points a bot at the help centre and hopes.

**Breaks when:** No one is available to take handovers; then say so and offer email with a reply time.

**From:** CXP-6302, CRO-6361, OPS-6610, OPS-6611, Moffatt v Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149 (https://www.canlii.org/en/bc/bccrt/doc/2024/2024bccrt149/2024bccrt149.html)

### 23. Staff support to the lowest month and flex for peaks, sized from contacts per order and cost per resolved ticket

**Why it works:** Tickets follow orders, and order status alone can be 50 to 80% of tickets at peak. Teams sized for the fourth quarter lose money all year; teams sized for March drown in November.

**How to apply:** Forecast tickets as forecast orders times contacts per order by reason. Convert to agent hours from handle time per reason. Size the full-time team to the lowest month's load and cover the rest with flexible staff (seasonal hires, an outsourcer); one operator caps all full-time payroll at about 12% of the lowest month's revenue. Route simple tickets (order status, address changes) to lower-cost or automated handling and nuanced ones to staff close to the team, who one operator found can be about twice as efficient; compare on cost per resolved ticket, not hourly rate. One brand doing $100M across November and December hired about 40 support reps within a month; plan hiring and training weeks before the peak, not during it.

**Judge it by:** A staffing plan built from the forecast with a peak line and a cost per ticket. Weak work adds headcount after the backlog appears.

**Breaks when:** Very small stores where one person covers everything; regulated or technical products that need specialist agents.

**From:** CXP-0012, OPS-0006, OPS-0013, OPS-0010, OPS-0019

### 24. Set first-reply and resolution targets per channel, publish them, and report the median, not the average

**Why it works:** Buyers judge speed by the channel: chat feels live, email does not. Averages hide the tickets that wait days, which become disputes.

**How to apply:** Set targets per channel and queue: live chat in minutes while the store is staffed, social messages and email within a stated number of business hours, damage and wrong-item cases first. Gorgias's data puts the median first reply at about 6.3 hours for $10M brands, from 1.6 hours in hardware to about 9 hours in the slowest category, so a brand that answers in under two hours is ahead of most. State the reply time on the contact page and in the auto-reply, and meet it. Report median and 90th percentile first reply and resolution time, and reopen rate, weekly. On WhatsApp, answer inside the 24-hour window so a free-form reply is still allowed.

**Judge it by:** Published targets per channel and a weekly median and 90th percentile. Weak work quotes an average reply time and no resolution time.

**Breaks when:** A one-person store; then publish an honest window (one business day) and keep it.

**From:** CXP-6302, EML-6322, Gorgias Ecom Lab benchmarks (https://www.gorgias.com/research/stop-benchmarking-against-the-average)

### 25. Give every agent a small, written fix budget they can spend without approval, and log every use

**Why it works:** Every approval step adds a wait and another contact, which is the effort that turns buyers disloyal; the recovery research says to resolve in one contact. Ritz-Carlton has let any employee spend up to $2,000 per incident since 1983 against an estimated guest lifetime value near $250,000; the store version is far smaller and works the same way.

**How to apply:** Set a per-incident cap from margin and repeat value, about $50 to $200 for many stores, covering a replacement, refund of shipping, a credit or a small gift. List what it may be used for and what needs approval. Log every use with reason, amount and order, and review the log monthly for patterns and abuse. One apparel brand gave its team a fixed weekly budget for upset and top-value customers and tracked word of mouth in surveys.

**Judge it by:** A written cap, a use list and a monthly log review. Weak work tells agents to "go above and beyond" with no budget, or makes them escalate every $10 refund.

**Breaks when:** Low-margin, low-repeat products whose lifetime value cannot carry the spend; or no logging, so the budget leaks or buyers learn to complain for gifts.

**From:** OPS-0009, CXP-0022, CXP-0009, CXP-0011

### 26. Recover a failure in proportion to the harm: fix it first, then make good, and never count on recovery to build loyalty

**Why it works:** The idea that a well-fixed problem makes a buyer more loyal than no problem is real but rare: in over 11,000 banking interviews, only 5.4% of customers with a problem rated the recovery much better than expected. It shows up mainly on a first, non-severe failure where the buyer sees the firm act to stop it recurring.

**How to apply:** Sort failures by harm. Minor (a late parcel, a small error): apologise once, fix in the same reply, and give a small courtesy only if the buyer lost money or time. Major (a ruined gift, a missed event, a faulty item that caused damage): call or write personally, fix at the store's cost, and add a gesture sized to the loss, from the agent budget or escalation. Never answer a failure with a discount code alone. Name what happened without blaming the carrier or buyer, give a date, log the cause, and tell the buyer what changed so it will not happen again.

**Judge it by:** Replies that fix first and size any gesture to the harm. Weak work sends "sorry for the inconvenience, here's 10% off" to every complaint.

**Breaks when:** Repeated failures for one buyer; escalate to fix the cause.

**From:** CXP-0011, CXP-0009, OPS-0009

### 27. Keep order and service messages free of promotion, and never treat a delivery phone number as marketing consent

**Why it works:** Service messages get read because they are trusted. Load them with offers and they count as marketing: a US order email with a sales subject line becomes commercial email, and a WhatsApp delay notice with a discount becomes a marketing template.

**How to apply:** Write confirmation, shipping, delay and return messages to inform: what happened, what is next, the date, one help link. A small, clearly separate product recommendation below the service content is the limit, and only where the market allows it. Text marketing in the US needs express written consent with a disclosure above the box, and consent cannot be a condition of purchase; a number given at checkout for delivery updates is not consent to promotional texts. Keep service and marketing sends on separate templates and lists.

**Judge it by:** Service templates with no offers in the subject or body, and a consent field kept apart from the delivery number. Weak work adds "use code SAVE10" to the shipping notice.

**Breaks when:** A market's rules differ; check each channel's local rules, and send pay-on-delivery confirmation and failed-delivery rules for India to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).

**From:** EML-6341, OPS-6286, EML-6345, EML-6327

### 28. Spend on unboxing and inserts only where the repeat lift needed to pay for them is small and a holdout can show it

**Why it works:** Inserts feel generous but cost money on every order. Unannounced second-order gifts often do not pay: 900 gifts at $2 to $5 cost about $2,000 for perhaps 45 extra orders, most from people who would have reordered anyway.

**How to apply:** Price the moment: a basic branded touch (tissue, sticker, card) is under $0.50 an order, a mid-tier box $1.50 to $3, premium $2.80 to $4.50; budget about 2 to 5% of product cost. Work out break-even: extra cost per order divided by the profit of a repeat order; a $3 insert against $25 repeat profit needs 0.12 extra orders per buyer. Send on triggers (first order over a value, VIP tier), not every order, and hold out a random share of eligible orders before rolling out. Put inserts to work: a use card that cuts misuse returns, a review ask timed to results. Letters to one-time buyers doubled second-purchase conversion against a control for one German pet retailer.

**Judge it by:** Cost per order, required lift and a holdout plan for every insert. Weak work adds handwritten notes because a vendor quotes a high open rate.

**Breaks when:** Very low-price items where even $0.50 eats margin; sustainability-led brands where extra layers draw complaints.

**From:** CXP-0008, CXP-0006, CXP-0020

### 29. Judge the experience by contact rate, effort, resolution and repeat buying, and read satisfaction scores by reason, not as one number

**Why it works:** A single satisfaction score moves with sample size and survey timing, and hides which reason hurts. Net Promoter Score is percent of 9 or 10 scores minus percent of 0 to 6 scores; small samples swing it, and cross-brand comparisons mislead.

**How to apply:** Build one weekly page: contacts per 100 orders by reason, median and 90th percentile reply and resolution time, reopen rate, one-reply resolution rate, satisfaction score by reason and channel, return rate and days to refund, review reply rate, dispute rate. Each quarter, compare the 90-day repeat rate of buyers who contacted support with those who did not, by reason; a gap shows which failures cost customers. Ask satisfaction right after a ticket closes, and ask NPS on a fixed schedule so scores compare over time. Put every number beside last quarter, not a vendor average.

**Judge it by:** A page that links service measures to repeat buying, with reasons. Weak work reports one satisfaction average and ticket counts.

**Breaks when:** Too few responses per reason to read; group months into quarters.

**From:** CXP-6305, CXP-0007, CXP-0009, CXP-0012

## Working from the org context

- Read the voice and five real brand lines before writing any reply, macro or message; support is where buyers hear the brand most, and a cold policy tone undoes the ads.
- Read buyers' own words for each persona, and use their terms for problems ("runs small", "bitter aftertaste") in macros, FAQs and bot answers.
- Pull the live policies (shipping, returns, refunds, warranty, cancel) and the delivery promise as shown at checkout; every reply must match them word for word on terms, and conflicts go to the owner before anything ships.
- Check what ran and what worked: past ticket reasons, return reasons by product, review themes, the current contact rate and any past fixes, so you build on them rather than restart.
- Keep the brand's promises and tone even where you would write differently; a playful brand keeps its warmth in an apology, and a formal one stays formal.
- Improve where the data shows a cost: a slow refund, a vague delivery window, an unanswered review backlog. Propose the change with its number (contacts, return cost, repeat rate), what it changes in the buyer's words, and how to measure it.
- Check the design system for tracking pages, return portals and emails so service screens look like the store, not the carrier or the app.
- Note the markets the brand sells in, and apply each market's consumer and review rules to the policy and messages for that market.
- Write any new rule learned on the job (a carrier that stalls, a product with a fit issue) back to the org context so every agent uses it.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does the work report contacts per 100 orders by reason, with an owner and a fix for each top reason?
- [ ] Is every delivery promise a specific date with its on-time rate, padded to land on time at least 9 times in 10?
- [ ] Are confirmation, shipped, out for delivery, delivered and delay messages written in full for each channel the store uses?
- [ ] Does every delay or stockout message offer the choice to wait or take a full refund, with a new date?
- [ ] Does the returns flow offer exchange or credit first while keeping the refund option visible?
- [ ] Is the cost of a return shown in full, including outbound shipping, unrefunded fees and unsellable stock?
- [ ] Is every refund logged as a return with a reason, including no-ship returns, so defect data stays whole?
- [ ] Is the post-purchase and subscription send calendar laid over the store's own cancel-by-day chart, with a reason to stay named before the first rebill?
- [ ] Are return windows and postage terms checked against the legal floor in every market the store sells to?
- [ ] Do faulty, damaged and wrong-item cases get fixed at the store's cost in one reply?
- [ ] Are review asks sent to every buyer, timed to use, with no sentiment gating or undisclosed reward?
- [ ] Does every one to three star review have a specific, non-defensive reply with no offer tied to changing it?
- [ ] Does the bot answer only from sourced, approved facts and hand over to a person with context?
- [ ] Are reply targets set per channel, published, and reported as median and 90th percentile?
- [ ] Is there a written, logged no-approval budget for agents?
- [ ] Are service messages free of promotions, and is marketing consent kept apart from delivery phone numbers?
- [ ] Is any insert, gift or delight spend backed by a break-even lift and a holdout?
- [ ] Do all replies and messages match the brand's voice and quote live policy terms exactly?
- [ ] Is every number traced to the store's data or a named source, with its period?

## Common failures

- Writing better macros for a problem that should not exist. Fix: send the top ticket reasons to their owners first, then write replies for what remains.
- Vague delivery ranges and silent delays. Fix: a specific padded date, proactive messages and a delay notice before the promised date.
- Hiding the refund behind exchange screens or emails. Fix: exchange first, refund always visible, refund paid fast.
- Counting only the refund amount as the cost of a return. Fix: add outbound shipping, fees, labour and unsellable stock, per product.
- "Love it? Leave 5 stars!" emails sent at dispatch, or asks only to happy buyers. Fix: ask every buyer after use, inside each platform's rules.
- Defensive or copy-paste replies to bad reviews, or credit offered for an edit. Fix: short, specific replies that fix the issue and log the cause.
- A bot that invents policy or traps buyers in loops. Fix: approved answers only, a never-do list, and a handover with context.
- Apologising with a discount code for every failure. Fix: fix first, size any gesture to the harm, log the cause.
- Promotions stuffed into order and shipping messages. Fix: keep service messages informative and send offers through marketing with consent.
- Copying a rival's generous returns policy. Fix: start from the legal floor and test the full profit effect in your category.
- Reporting one satisfaction average. Fix: report by reason and channel, and link contacts to repeat buying.
