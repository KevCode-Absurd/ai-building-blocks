# Direct-response copywriting: craft principles

This book is for everyone on the team who writes words meant to make a sale: ad copy and scripts, product pages, landing pages and advertorials, emails, texts and chat replies, offers and guarantees. Read it before the job, write the draft to it, and run the review checklist on the draft before it goes to the owner. The skills say what to make; this book says what great looks like and why it sells.

## What great looks like

- Every piece is written for one buyer at one awareness stage, and a reviewer can name both from the first line.
- The piece makes one promise, stated in one plain sentence, and that promise is smaller than the proof behind it.
- A named mechanism explains why the promise comes true, and every feature mentioned links to it.
- The problem, the hook and the objections use phrases a real customer wrote, not phrases the brand invented.
- Proof sits next to the claim it backs, and the strongest available proof type is used, not the easiest.
- The offer is written as a full deal (terms, risk reversal, reason to act now, effort), not as a discount line.
- The ad, the headline, the text on screen and the top of the landing page all make the same claim.
- Nothing in the copy could be said by the product next to it on the shelf.
- Read aloud at real pace, the copy has no stumbles, no stock phrases and no machine rhythm.
- Every objective claim, count, price cut and urgency line is true today and could be shown to a regulator tomorrow.

## The principles

### 1. Write the offer as five parts before you write a word of the pitch, and improve it with what the business already does

**Why it works:** Practitioners rank what decides a sale as offer first, then the market, the mechanism, proof, and only then the rest of the copy. A discount touches one part of the offer and is usually the weakest to lead with.

**How to apply:** Write five parts: the thing in the buyer's words, the terms framed against them, what happens if it fails, the reason to buy now, and the effort asked. Use price architecture before price cuts: a 20% bundle saving that moves orders to three or four units may cost no margin. Then list what the business already does (free returns, a long warranty, lab tests) that the copy never says; in one example, four of five offer upgrades already existed unmentioned. Reframe too: one bundle sold as a New Year reset at full price beat its old sale price.

**Judge it by:** The draft names all five parts, and at least one comes from an unused existing policy. Weak work treats '20% off' as the offer.

**Breaks when:** The brand is aspirational and keeps price out of creative on purpose; then the offer lives on the page, not in the ad.

**From:** OFF-6651, OFF-6652, OFF-6650, OFF-6631, OFF-6677, OFF-6624, OFF-6667

### 2. Build the copy from the buyer's own words, and if no buyer has written the desire down, you do not have a concept yet

**Why it works:** People trust words that sound like their own thoughts, and they skip words that sound like a label. A desire that only appears in the brand's own marketing is a product description, not a reason anyone buys.

**How to apply:** Mine reviews (2 to 4 stars for doubts), support tickets, return reasons, call transcripts and forum threads, and paste exact quotes; automated summaries strip the emotion you need. Hunt for five things, each feeding one part of the copy: the accommodation (the problem), the failed solution (the objection), the near miss ('I nearly didn't', the risk reversal), the word (the hook) and the moment (the urgency). Rewrite pack language into buyer language: hair thinning becomes 'I can see my scalp'. Never ask an AI what buyers feel; use it only to fetch real quotes with links.

**Judge it by:** The reviewer can match each key line to a quote in the research file. Weak work uses category words like 'supports wellness' that no customer said.

**Breaks when:** A brand new product has no customers yet; then use competitor reviews and forum threads, and label the language unproven.

**From:** MCR-6700, MCR-6699, MCR-6731, AVT-0007, AVT-0026, AVT-0027, AVT-0028, MCR-6623

### 3. Write every piece for one awareness stage, and let that stage decide the opening and the length

**Why it works:** From Eugene Schwartz's idea of market awareness: the opening must start where the reader already is. A piece that mixes stages fails both groups, and on Meta it also confuses who the ad gets shown to.

**How to apply:** Before writing, write the stage on the brief: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware or most aware. Unaware and problem aware pieces open on the felt situation and teach; product aware pieces open on the product and the offer. Weight an account about 80% to the first three stages and 20% to the last two. A UK sleep brand writes one ad for people who wake at 3am (problem aware) and another for people comparing sprays (solution aware).

**Judge it by:** The stage is named on the brief, and the image, script and primary text all speak to it. Weak work opens with the product name in a cold ad.

**Breaks when:** A famous brand harvests demand made elsewhere: 96% of Alo Yoga's hooks needed you to know the brand, against 3% to 18% for Meta-built brands.

**From:** ADC-0001, MCR-0056, MCR-6692, MCR-6719, MCR-6772, MCR-0057, MCR-0064, MCR-6751

### 4. State one promise in one sentence, put it before the mechanism, and keep it smaller than your proof

**Why it works:** Readers decide in seconds whether a piece is about something they want. A mechanism heard before the promise is jargon with no reason to care, and a promise bigger than the proof makes the whole piece feel like a scam.

**How to apply:** Write the promise as one sentence a stranger would understand: outcome, for whom, by when. Then check the four failures: no promise, five promises, a feature dressed as a promise, a promise bigger than the proof. About 30 to 40% of feed ads lead with the mechanism ('lowers ghrelin') before saying what it does for the reader ('no 11am hunger'). Keep the order hook, problem, promise, mechanism, objection, proof, offer, risk reversal, call to action. If a product has three strong benefits, that is three ads, not one.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer can underline the single promise, and the proof shown is at least as strong as it. Weak work lists every benefit in one breath.

**Breaks when:** The audience is most aware and only needs the offer; then the promise can be implied by the product name.

**From:** MCR-6706, MCR-6711, MCR-6614, MCR-6721, MCR-6748, MCR-6814

### 5. Give the promise a named mechanism, and chain every feature to it as feature, which means, because

**Why it works:** A mechanism answers 'why would this work when other things did not?', which is the question that stops a sceptical buyer. Claude Hopkins took Schlitz from fifth to first in 1907 by naming a cleaning step every brewer used and none described.

**How to apply:** Find the root cause the buyer was never told, then make each part of the product map to it one to one. Write each feature as a chain: 320 mg saw palmetto, which means you stop losing hair at the part, because it blocks the DHT enzyme. Every physical product has one, even a t-shirt: merino's crimped fibre traps odour in the fibre, not on your skin. Explain the mechanism before you name the product, or you join a category where the cheapest wins. A contradiction such as 'as much energy as coffee with no crash' is not a mechanism; explain it at once or cut it.

**Judge it by:** The mechanism has a name or a clear picture, and each feature in the copy points to it. Weak work lists ingredients with no reason they work.

**Breaks when:** Your product is the same as a scaled rival's; a mechanism claim needs a real product difference, so win with an unserved buyer instead.

**From:** MCR-6704, LPG-0028, MCR-6695, MCR-6725, MCR-6683, MCR-6691, MCR-0032, AVT-0014

### 6. Make the hook find the buyer, not just stop the scroll

**Why it works:** On Meta the creative is now the targeting. The system reads each ad's words, transcript and visuals to choose who sees it, then learns from who keeps watching. A meme or bait opener that grabs everyone teaches it to find people who will never buy.

**How to apply:** Open on a problem, belief or detail that only your buyer feels. Lead with the problem rather than a callout like 'busy mums, listen up', which reads as an ad and gets skipped; and avoid running many narrow persona callouts, since the same viewer sees several and none feels personal. A broad belief-challenging hook (drinking 3 litres a day does not mean you are hydrated) can carry large spend, while narrow callouts cap out fast. Never put the product in the first seconds of a cold video.

**Judge it by:** A stranger reading the first line can say who it is for and what problem it is about. Weak work has a high hook rate and a low purchase rate.

**Breaks when:** A cheap gadget with a visible mechanism; then showing it working in the first 2 seconds is the hook.

**From:** MCR-0026, MCR-6736, MCR-6812, MCR-6675, ADC-6169, MCR-6855, MCR-6786, MCR-6811, MCR-6805

### 7. Use the strongest proof you have, and make it prove the exact claim you made

**Why it works:** Proof lowers the risk the buyer feels, but only proof about the advertised benefit does that. Five reviews about the exact benefit beat 400 generic ones, and trust badges close nobody.

**How to apply:** Rank what you have from strongest to weakest: demonstration, third-party verification, a count of specific social proof, named individual testimony, mechanism explanation, founder assertion. Use the top one the brand can honestly supply. Match proof to claim risk: modest claims, creator demos; big claims, an expert; a borrowed-trust category, a founder or known person. A new brand with no reviews should lean on demonstration and seed about 20 detailed, honest testimonials. Put proof no rival can copy (an advisory board, a completed trial) above table-stakes badges.

**Judge it by:** Each claim has its proof beside it, and the proof type is the strongest available. Weak work uses 'customers love it' under a specific benefit claim.

**Breaks when:** The store has fewer than about 80 reviews. There, count itself is the proof: going from 0 to about 80 roughly doubled conversion in one practitioner's stores, and past that count barely mattered, so build volume first.

**From:** MCR-6712, LPG-6620, MCR-6754, MCR-6684, LPG-0062, LPG-6606, MCR-6624

### 8. Make the ad, the headline, the on-screen text and the top of the page one claim, and give each new angle its own page

**Why it works:** A buyer clicks because of one promise. If the landing page leads with a different one, they feel lost and leave; a page listing every benefit lowers belief in each.

**How to apply:** There are six places a hook appears together: first frame, voiceover, on-screen text, primary text, headline and the top of the landing page. Make them say the same thing. When an ad changes the persona, angle or offer, send it to a page built for that one message, opening on the symptom the ad ended on, as IM8 does for its persona pages. Find the winning angle cheaply: test 4 or 5 headlines on one ad to one page, then rewrite the page top around the winner. Check the offer matches too: a pop-up offering 10% off one-time orders undercuts a 20% subscription offer.

**Judge it by:** Reading the ad and the page top side by side, the claim, persona and offer match. Weak work sends five angles to one generic product page.

**Breaks when:** Spend is small and you cannot build pages yet; then change the page headline only, and keep the rest.

**From:** ADC-6218, LPG-6623, LPG-0064, LPG-6605, LPG-0067, MCR-0080, OFF-0037, OFF-6612

### 9. Find the beat where a losing ad loses people before you rewrite it, and fix only that beat

**Why it works:** Each metric points to a different beat, so a rewrite aimed at the wrong one burns a test. Hook and hold rates do not predict sales, but they show where a video breaks.

**How to apply:** Read spend first (Meta moves budget to ads that cause sales), then CPA on 7-day click, then hook rate (past 3 seconds) and hold rate (past about 15 seconds). Weak hook rate: new hooks on the same body. Strong hook rate, steep drop by 5 to 10 seconds: the bridge broke, so rewrite the first body lines. A cliff where proof turns into the offer: rewrite that turn. High hook rate, few buyers: the hook finds the wrong people. Before asking for more ads, check what share of new ads ever get real spend; one agency saw accounts launch over 2,000 ads a month on about $250,000 of spend and stay unprofitable.

**Judge it by:** Every rewrite names the metric, the beat and the one change. Weak work rewrites the whole script after one bad week.

**Breaks when:** Spend is too low for the numbers to settle, or the ad is a static; then judge on spend and CPA.

**From:** ADC-6298, ADC-6268, MCR-6715, MCR-6812, MCR-6658, MCR-6738

### 10. Answer the top objections in order, each with its own proof from a different kind of customer

**Why it works:** Most visitors leave without buying because of a specific doubt, not a lack of desire. An answered doubt is a sale saved; an unanswered one is the exit.

**How to apply:** Source objections from people who nearly bought: a post-purchase 'what almost stopped you' question, checkout abandoner calls, chat logs and low-star reviews. Handle six in this order: not for me, it won't work, it won't work for me, not worth it, what if I'm wrong, I'll do it later. Give each one a testimonial from a different customer type. For buyers who tried rivals, explain why those failed (extract strength varies, so 400 mg can act like 10 mg) and how long yours takes. Pre-empt through an experience they already had: 'you will react in week one, everyone does, so the first tube is 0.05%'. On product pages, put 3 or 4 hand-picked reviews that answer these doubts beside the add-to-cart button.

**Judge it by:** The reviewer can list the five or six objections and point to the line and proof for each. Weak work has one generic FAQ.

**Breaks when:** The product is a low-price impulse buy; then one or two objections are enough and more copy slows the sale.

**From:** MCR-6698, MCR-6709, MCR-6619, MCR-0043, LPG-0005, LPG-0053, LPG-0027, LPG-0042, CRO-0018

### 11. Make every objective claim, count, price cut and urgency line true and provable before it runs

**Why it works:** A claim that breaks the rules costs more than it earns: disapproved ads, banned accounts, refunds and a business no buyer will acquire. Dark-hat supplement brands beat honest rivals on acquisition cost but face constant bans and are likely unsellable.

**How to apply:** Hold the evidence for every objective claim before it runs; in the UK the ASA judges the claim as an average reader takes it, and testimonials alone are not proof. Never imply the reader's health, age or money, which Meta bans. Use only real reviews, with paid links disclosed. A 'was' price must be one the item really sold at; in the EU it is the lowest of the last 30 days. Timers and 'high demand' lines must be true; the UK CMA has acted on resetting timers. Keep counts consistent: IM8 ads claimed over 700,000 and about 395,000 customers. For India-specific rules, hand the job to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).

**Judge it by:** Every number and claim traces to a dated source in the claims file. Weak work invents a customer count or a deadline.

**Breaks when:** The line is clear opinion or obvious exaggeration; those need no proof, but they must still not mislead.

**From:** MCR-6022, MCR-6023, MCR-6045, MCR-6025, OFF-6145, MCR-6609, MCR-6608, MCR-6750; https://www.asa.org.uk/advice-online/claims-in-testimonials-and-endorsements.html; https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/using-urgency-and-price-reduction-claims-online; https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/compliance/legal/pricing-indication-directive

### 12. Read the market's sophistication from competitor ads, then write one stage higher

**Why it works:** When every rival makes the same claim, the claim stops working; buyers have heard it. Schwartz described markets moving through stages: a claim, a bigger claim, a mechanism, a bigger mechanism, then identity. Copy pitched at a stage the market has passed sounds like everyone else.

**How to apply:** Pull 10 or more competitor ads that have run at least 60 days, from brands about one or two size steps above yours, and mark which stage each one works at. Write one stage above the most common one. If rivals all say 'hydrates for 24 hours', lead with how it works differently; if rivals all name mechanisms, lead with who the buyer becomes. Proven hook formulas can build an eight-figure brand, but an ad that holds very large spend usually needs an opening rivals have not used, and you refresh it once they copy it.

**Judge it by:** The brief names the stage found and the stage written to. Weak work copies the category leader's headline structure.

**Breaks when:** The category is brand new and buyers have heard no claims yet; a plain, direct claim then wins.

**From:** MCR-6696, ADC-0001, MCR-6758, MCR-0034, MCR-6682, MCR-6797, MCR-6865

### 13. Frame the price four ways before you ever write 'was, now'

**Why it works:** Buyers judge price against a reference point. A strikethrough gives them your old price as the reference; a better frame gives them their alternative, their daily cost or the cost of doing nothing.

**How to apply:** Script price as one of four frames: anchor to the alternative (a $200 specialist visit), shrink it to a daily cost (73 cents a day for 90 days), price the problem (years of failed treatments), or make low effort the price. Show the saving as whichever number is bigger: dollars on high-ticket items, percent on low-ticket ones; above about $100 order value, a dollar welcome offer usually wins. Write 'buy 2, get 1 free' rather than '33% off three'. Put the dollar saving in each bundle's title ('3 pack, save $10'), which lifted conversion 25% in one agency test. For a premium product where price is the main doubt, say it outright: it costs $120, it is our best seller, here is why.

**Judge it by:** The price line uses a reference the buyer cares about. Weak work leads with a percent off in small print.

**Breaks when:** The brand never discounts and keeps price out of ads; then the frame belongs on the page only.

**From:** OFF-6654, OFF-0017, OFF-0026, OFF-0030, LPG-0008, LPG-0035, MCR-6651, LPG-0024

### 14. Write a guarantee that is specific, a little uncomfortable for the brand, matched to how long the product takes, and said in the ad

**Why it works:** The guarantee removes the last doubt, 'what if I'm wrong?'. A generic 30-day line is invisible, and a guarantee that ends before the product works tells the buyer you expect it to fail.

**How to apply:** Match the length to the time to result: a 90-day hair product gets a 90-day guarantee that refunds all three tubs. On low-ticket consumables, one agency reports 180-day windows bring fewer claims than 30-day ones, with no figures, so test it and track refund rate. Put the guarantee right before the ask, and say free shipping and easy returns in the ad; one brand's 30-day guarantee appeared in none of its ads. Put the answer to the top objection on the bigger pack only (a lifetime guarantee on the two-pack) so it lifts order size too. In the US, 'money back guarantee' must mean a full refund on request, and 'lifetime' must say whose lifetime.

**Judge it by:** The guarantee names a length, what is refunded and how, and appears in the ad. Weak work says 'satisfaction guaranteed' with no terms.

**Breaks when:** The product truly wears out; generous unconditional promises forced L.L.Bean and Eddie Bauer to pull back.

**From:** OFF-6653, OFF-0060, MCR-6616, OFF-6669, OFF-6690, OFF-6501, OFF-6502, OFF-0064

### 15. Make variants by changing the persona, angle or offer, not the wording

**Why it works:** Meta groups near-identical ads and shows them to the same people, so 200 similar ads may count as about 20. A new synonym is not a new ad; a new buyer, argument or deal is.

**How to apply:** Define each concept as persona plus angle plus offer, with format as a weaker fourth lever, and change at least one of the first three for each new piece. Two personas times three angles gives six concepts. Once a script wins, swap only the persona (joints, menopause, GLP-1 users) and rewrite the body to match. Cheap extra variants come from register: casual creator voice, a flat documentary voice, a diary entry written to nobody, or the same story in another tense or person. Do not launch eight near-identical text hooks on an unproven ad; test about three very different ones on proven winners.

**Judge it by:** Each variant in a set names what changed among persona, angle and offer. Weak work is five rewordings of one line.

**Breaks when:** You are refreshing a fatigued winner; then new hooks on the same proven body are the right move.

**From:** MCR-0022, MCR-6631, MCR-6847, MCR-6782, MCR-6612, MCR-6697, MCR-6701, MCR-6617

### 16. On a product page, answer what it is, why this one, why now and why trust you on the first screen

**Why it works:** Agencies cite (without a source) that about 60% of visitors never scroll past the first screen, so a page that saves its case for lower down loses most readers first.

**How to apply:** Replace the product-name headline with the core value in buyer terms ('creatine gummies you will actually take every day'). On mobile, order the first screen: value line, proof, price, button. Put risk reducers (free returns, guarantee) in the line under the button; one agency reports 40 to 60% lifts from that line alone, unsized, so make it the first test. Lead with the outcome, then the feature: firmer skin in four weeks, using a triple peptide complex. Below the fold, break the description into highlights, each a benefit with its feature; Baymard found this makes shoppers slow down and read. Keep the FAQ to the top three researched objections.

**Judge it by:** On a phone screenshot of the first screen, a stranger can answer all four questions. Weak work opens on the product name and a paragraph of brand story.

**Breaks when:** Traffic is high intent from search and already knows the product; then lead with proof and options.

**From:** LPG-6617, LPG-6611, LPG-6618, LPG-6621, LPG-0038, LPG-0016, LPG-6615, LPG-0050; https://baymard.com/research-articles/structure-descriptions-by-highlights

### 17. Put most writing effort into the body and the bridges, and raise the sense of being sold slowly

**Why it works:** The hook gathers about 80% of the audience but persuades almost nobody. Viewers leave at the jumps: from hook straight to pitch, or from education straight to the button.

**How to apply:** After a strong problem hook, add 4 to 6 seconds of mechanism teaching before naming the product; for a mechanism buyers have never heard, give it up to about 15 seconds (two to four sentences). Show the product before you name it, and name it at the offer or even the call to action. In cold video, introduce the product as late as you can; fewer people see it, but they buy at higher rates. Write each line with one job: to get the next line read. Stitching 20 separately written hooks onto one body breaks the bridge, so rewrite the first body lines for each new hook. Write the turn from body to call to action as carefully as the hook.

**Judge it by:** Read line to line, no line jumps in salesiness, and the product appears after the promise and mechanism. Weak work names the brand in line one.

**Breaks when:** Most-aware retargeting, where a fast product and offer ad is right.

**From:** MCR-6722, MCR-0027, MCR-6738, MCR-6715, MCR-6693, MCR-6852, MCR-0014, MCR-6801, MCR-6626

### 18. Agitate what the buyer has quietly changed in their life, not the symptom

**Why it works:** Everyone in a category names the same symptom, so naming it again sounds like every ad. The accommodation, the habit the buyer built around the problem, is what makes them think 'this is me'.

**How to apply:** Write the consequence they adjusted to: 'you plan outfits around bad days' beats 'bloating is uncomfortable'. Open the problem beat one of three ways: the accommodation, the moment they noticed, or a failed solution; note that the failed-solution opening locks the ad to solution-aware buyers. Speak to one person, argue at category level, and do not resolve the discomfort in the problem beat. Keep it about 10 to 15 seconds in a standard script and make it escalate: if you can swap two lines without changing the argument, you wrote a list. For brand teams who dislike negative ads, name the accommodation as a neutral observation, never the affliction; pity is what they object to.

**Judge it by:** The problem line describes a behaviour, not a feeling or a label. Weak work lists five symptoms of equal weight.

**Breaks when:** Women's fashion and other desire-led categories; one practitioner knows no $100M women's fashion brand built on problem ads.

**From:** MCR-6686, MCR-6707, MCR-6702, MCR-6703, MCR-6688, MCR-6826, LPG-0045

### 19. Score each hook on clarity, relevance and novelty first, and write all three layers

**Why it works:** About 80% of viewers leave during the hook, and a hook is read through three channels at once: what is seen, what is heard and the words on screen. A weak layer can sink a strong line.

**How to apply:** Score each hook out of 10 on clarity (a stranger gets it in 3 seconds), relevance (a real problem) and novelty, then specificity and credibility as bonuses; two or three done well is enough. Keep on-screen hook text to about 5 to 8 words at a low reading level. A small number signals a short, concrete payoff: 'the same two problems in almost every rival' beats 'problems'. Changing only the overlay to a bolder line doubled hook rate in one example, so write overlay, spoken line and visual direction separately. One spoken hook can become 20 to 30 hooks by varying visuals and overlays.

**Judge it by:** Each hook in the set shows its score and its three layers. Weak work delivers spoken lines only.

**Breaks when:** Wordless sensory openers (fabric handled, a pack opened) win in fashion, where clarity comes from the image.

**From:** MCR-6668, MCR-6741, MCR-0028, MCR-6743, MCR-6744, MCR-6644, ORG-0016, MCR-6868

### 20. Make each static fully clear or a deliberate curiosity gap, and send curiosity only to a page that teaches

**Why it works:** A static is read in about 4 seconds. Fully clear statics sell to people who already want the thing; curiosity statics earn the click of colder people. Half intriguing and half explaining does neither.

**How to apply:** Decide which end of the line each static sits on before writing. Clear statics may go to the product page; curiosity statics go to a listicle, advertorial or education page, which is where most brands wrongly decide statics do not work. Write primary text and headline with the same care as the image, paired like a video thumbnail and title, each raising a question the other answers. Make the element the eye hits first carry the strongest message ('new technology' rather than 'protein powder'). For colder buyers, add long story-led primary text: a product shot on white reaches only the most aware.

**Judge it by:** The reviewer can say which end of the line the static is on and where it sends people. Weak work pairs a vague headline with a product page.

**Breaks when:** The value fits one frame and the buyer is product aware; then a plain offer static is right.

**From:** MCR-6879, MCR-0019, MCR-0024, LPG-6632, MCR-6884, MCR-6882, MCR-6873, MCR-6874

### 21. Size the beats to the length, write each length as its own piece, and go long when the product needs explaining

**Why it works:** Each beat needs time to land. Squeeze a mechanism into 15 seconds and it confuses; cut a six-minute ad to two minutes in the edit and the bridges stop fitting.

**How to apply:** At 15 seconds only a hook, one problem line and a call to action fit. A mechanism needs 45 to 60 seconds; risk reversal needs over 60; ads over 4 minutes need several calls to action. To lengthen, loop problem, promise, mechanism, objection and proof about three times, or walk a results timeline (day 1, day 9, day 21) as IM8 does. When a product needs about three minutes of explanation, send cold traffic to an advertorial or long page, not the product page. For buyers 50 and over in Facebook feeds, long story-led copy with a static image can win cold traffic.

**Judge it by:** The beat list matches the length, and each cut-down has its own script. Weak work trims a long script by deleting the middle.

**Breaks when:** The product is understood at a glance and cheap; long copy then only slows the buy.

**From:** MCR-6718, MCR-6729, MCR-6724, MCR-6607, LPG-0004, MCR-0033, MCR-6848, MCR-6653

### 22. Give an advertorial one editorial identity, and never let the story break into a pitch

**Why it works:** An advertorial sells because it reads as something worth reading. The moment it turns into a sales letter, the reader's guard goes up and they leave.

**How to apply:** Choose the format by the buying obstacle: low trust, a story; needs permission, an expert; too many options, a buyer's guide; 'will it work for me', a case study. Commit to one identity (warning, discovery, comparison, local story) for the whole piece. A strong cold order: hook, lived pain, failed alternatives, root cause, mechanism, proof story, objections, price against the cost of the problem, guarantee, close. Edit by marking every sentence where the story stops and the pitch starts, and rewrite each inside the narrative. Open on curiosity, proof and promise rather than pain, since every rival shares the pain. Close with two options: keep the old ways, or try this risk-free, with a soft 'check availability' button.

**Judge it by:** No marked break points remain, and the identity holds to the end. Weak work runs a news headline over a sales letter.

**Breaks when:** The ad already sells hard with long copy; then the advertorial lead should be short.

**From:** LPG-0003, LPG-0044, LPG-0012, LPG-0025, LPG-0045, LPG-0054, LPG-0029, MCR-6720

### 23. Replace every adjective with something a buyer could check

**Why it works:** Adjectives are claims with no proof, and buyers discount them. A specific number, name or physical detail is proof and promise at once, and it is harder for a rival to copy.

**How to apply:** Swap each adjective for one of five specifics: a number, a named third party, a physical detail, a timeframe or a proper noun. 'Fast absorbing' becomes 'dry in 40 seconds, we timed it'. Swap 'customers love it' for counted specifics from reviews, such as the word 'finally' appearing over 6,000 times. In fashion, one named fabric mechanism beats five adjectives. A single specific detail can target, prove and cast at once, as John Carlton's one-legged golfer headline did. Plain, objective copy also reads better: in a Nielsen Norman Group study, a site rewritten to be concise, scannable and objective scored 124% higher on measured usability than the promotional version.

**Judge it by:** Count adjectives in the draft; each surviving one has a specific beside it. Weak work stacks 'premium, gentle, effective'.

**Breaks when:** The specific cannot be proven; then cut the line rather than invent the number.

**From:** MCR-6713, MCR-6714, MCR-0066, MCR-6828, MCR-6723; https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/

### 24. Cut any line the product next to yours on the shelf could also say

**Why it works:** Shared claims spend the reader's attention and leave them no reason to choose you. 'Vegan, cruelty free, locally made' can burn 7 to 8 words and about 4.5 seconds of an ad.

**How to apply:** Test every differentiator with three questions: is it a claim a buyer cares about, can competitors not say it, and would it move a stranger? Published third-party lab results pass; 'family owned since 1994' does not. Shrink table stakes (shipping speed, vegan, size) into a small line, and give the space to the one benefit no rival claims. Find that benefit by reading all reviews for praise rivals do not get. Stacking two common benefits rarely seen together (strong scent plus no scalp irritation) is a weak fallback that works only if the pair meets a real desire.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer swaps in a rival's name and the key lines stop being true. Weak work survives the swap.

**Breaks when:** A regulator or platform requires a standard statement; keep it, but do not lead with it.

**From:** MCR-6689, OFF-6657, OFF-6656, BRD-6488, LPG-6615

### 25. Write a call to action that names the action, what happens next, the effort, and the reason to act now

**Why it works:** 'Shop now' asks for a decision without lowering its cost. A call to action that says what happens next and how little it takes removes the last bit of friction.

**How to apply:** Write four parts: the action, what happens after the click, how small the effort is, and a true reason to act now. 'Take the 60-second quiz to see which kit fits your baby's age' does all four. Tie the reason to act now to how the product works: if results take 90 days, the call is to start the 90-day pack, and a 30-day pack is set up to fail. On pre-sell pages, soft steps such as 'check availability' with a line above telling readers to click convert better than a hard 'buy now'. Do not spend tests on call-to-action wording in video; almost nobody reaches it. Test hooks and bodies instead.

**Judge it by:** The call to action has all four parts, and its urgency is true. Weak work ends on 'shop now at the link below'.

**Breaks when:** Most-aware retargeting with a live sale, where a short button and the deal are enough.

**From:** MCR-6681, OFF-6655, LPG-0011, LPG-0054, MCR-6775

### 26. Write outcomes as scenes the buyer can picture, and close on who they become

**Why it works:** Believing is not the same as wanting. A correct, logical script can pass every review and still sell nothing, because people buy the life they picture, not the claim they accept. Scenes also carry less compliance risk than health results.

**How to apply:** Turn each result into something the buyer can now do: play on the floor with the grandkids, hike the whole trail, wear the white shirt. Tie the product to a desire they already have, and push features toward the end. Run a 'so what?' test on every line until it reaches a feeling. End on an identity line tied to the mechanism, such as 'the women who keep their hair began before the loss showed', which also filters out buyers who will not stick with it.

**Judge it by:** The promise and close name a scene or identity, not a percentage or a label. Weak work ends on 'clinically proven results'.

**Breaks when:** B2B buyers or spec-led categories (tools, parts) where the decision is mostly rational; lead with the spec and the cost saved.

**From:** MCR-6613, MCR-6716, MCR-6694, MCR-6818, MCR-6819

### 27. In founder copy, make only the claims a founder can make, and tell them through the viewer's problem

**Why it works:** Nobody cares about the founder for their own sake. A founder earns trust when they carry a real argument that only they can make, and the viewer keeps watching when the story is about their own problem.

**How to apply:** Build founder scripts on the three founder-only claims: why the business started, why the mechanism works, and what was wrong with the rival products they tested. Leave product experience to customers. A pattern that topped one agency's 15 best founder ads: a bold adversarial claim, proof of the claim, the mechanism through the founder's story, proof of the mechanism, then a call to action with offer and social proof. Vary the founder's role to get new scripts: builder, witness (reading a customer email), interviewer (asking the lab why), or reader (answering a one-star review). The founder need not be on camera; voice or a proxy works.

**Judge it by:** Every founder line is one only the founder could say, framed by the buyer's problem. Weak work is a founder telling their life story.

**Breaks when:** The founder does not match the buyer at all; retell a true story through someone close to them instead.

**From:** MCR-6763, MCR-6771, MCR-6761, MCR-6776, MCR-6764, MCR-6766, MCR-6767

### 28. In email and text copy, sell with the message, not the subject line, and let each review answer one doubt

**Why it works:** Retention gains come from the offer, the structure and the message far more than from subject-line tweaks. Plain notes from a person read like mail from a friend, and a reply invites a conversation that sells.

**How to apply:** Put writing effort into what the email says and offers. Mix designed emails with plain-text founder notes; plain notes with no product link can still drive large revenue. In abandon flows, hold any discount until about the fifth email with a real 48-hour expiry, then close with a plain founder note. Pick each review in an email to answer one known doubt (taste, ease, results timing), not 'I love it'. Keep each text under 160 characters with no emoji where possible, since one emoji cuts a segment to 70. End some texts with a simple question; buyers who reply are worth about 1.5 times more. Never use a misleading subject or a fake 'Re:'.

**Judge it by:** Each email has one job and one ask, and every quoted review answers a doubt. Weak work is a grid of products under a clever subject line.

**Breaks when:** A sale or launch email to a warm list; then a clear subject naming the deal is the message.

**From:** EML-6390, EML-0057, EML-0044, EML-0033, EML-0024, EML-0035, EML-6340, EML-6341

### 29. Write for the ear and the scan, and strip every machine tell

**Why it works:** Shoppers now know the sound of machine copy, and once they hear it they trust the page less. AI drafts drift to the average because they learned from far more generic product pages than great copy.

**How to apply:** Write below grade 7 for general buyers (hook text at grade 5 or lower), in full flowing sentences: one idea per sentence, verb early, no hard words, contractions always, varied length (long, long, short), and a strong last word. Read the script aloud standing, at real pace, and fix each stumble or cringe; it takes about four minutes. Ban the 'it's not X, it's Y' pattern, three-part lists by reflex, and stock phrases. Use metaphors one step from the reader's picture (a clogged filter, not a factory). Feed AI only your research and proven structures, and keep choosing the idea a human job.

**Judge it by:** The draft measures at the brand's reading level and passes a read-aloud with no stumbles. Weak work has every sentence the same length.

**Breaks when:** The brand's own voice is formal or technical by design; then match the brand, but keep sentences short.

**From:** MCR-6717, MCR-6730, LPG-0047, ORG-0024, MCR-0016, MCR-6851, OPS-6610, MCR-6876

## Working from the org context

- Read the buyers' own words first: the review quotes, survey answers and support themes in the org context are the raw material for the hook, the problem line and every objection. Copy that cannot point to a quote there is a guess.
- Read the voice section and its real brand lines before writing. Match sentence length, reading level, use of 'you' and 'we', and the words the brand never uses. Customer words outrank invented brand words.
- Take every claim, number, ingredient, price and policy from the org context's facts and approved claims, word for word. If a claim you need is missing, write without it and list it as missing proof; never fill the gap.
- Read what ran and what won. Start from past winners: reuse a winning hook, angle or offer on a new persona before inventing a new concept, because teams drop winning ideas long before the market tires of them.
- Read the offers and policies live today, and list the ones the current copy never says. These are the cheapest improvements you can propose.
- Read the design system for limits that shape copy: headline lengths, button text, where the guarantee line sits, whether price may appear in ads.
- Keep the brand's voice, its approved claims, its legal lines and any rule it has set, such as 'no price in ads'. These are not yours to change.
- You may improve the awareness spread, the order of beats, the mechanism, the proof chosen and the specifics. Propose a change to voice or positioning as a side-by-side: the current line, your line, the customer quote or result behind it, and one number.
- When the brand's own practice breaks a principle here (for example, problem ads in a brand that bans negative copy), write to the brand's rule and note the trade-off in one line for the owner.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Can I name the one buyer and the one awareness stage from the first line, and do image, script and text all match that stage?
- [ ] Is there exactly one promise, stated in one sentence, and is the proof at least as strong as the promise?
- [ ] Does the promise come before the mechanism, and does the mechanism have a name or clear picture?
- [ ] Does every feature in the copy link to the promise through 'which means' and 'because'?
- [ ] Do the hook, problem line and objections use phrases traceable to real customer quotes?
- [ ] Would a stranger know who the hook is for and what problem it names within 3 seconds?
- [ ] Is the product kept out of the first seconds of every cold video and out of the first line of every cold ad?
- [ ] Is every proof beside the claim it backs, and is it the strongest type the brand has?
- [ ] Are the top objections answered in order, each with its own proof?
- [ ] Does the offer name all five parts, including at least one existing policy the old copy never said?
- [ ] Is the price framed against something the buyer cares about, not only a struck-through old price?
- [ ] Does the guarantee name a length matched to time to result, what is refunded, and appear in the ad?
- [ ] Do the ad, headline, on-screen text and page top make the same claim with the same offer?
- [ ] Would any key line stay true if a rival's name replaced the brand's?
- [ ] Does each variant change persona, angle or offer, not just words?
- [ ] Does every number, count, 'was' price, deadline and stock claim trace to a dated source and stay true today?
- [ ] Does the copy avoid implying the reader's health, age, money or other personal traits?
- [ ] Does the draft read aloud with no stumbles, at the brand's reading level, with no stock phrases or 'it's not X, it's Y' lines?
- [ ] Does the call to action name the action, what happens next, the effort and a true reason to act now?
- [ ] Does every rewrite of a losing ad name the metric, the beat it points to and the single change made?

## Common failures

- Leading with pain everyone in the category already names. Fix: agitate the accommodation the buyer built around the problem, or open on curiosity, proof and promise.
- Listing every benefit in one ad or one product page. Fix: one promise per piece; split the other benefits into their own ads and pages.
- Ingredients and specs with no reason they matter. Fix: chain each one as feature, which means, because, back to the single promise.
- Inventing customer language, counts or results, often by asking an AI what buyers feel. Fix: use only quoted reviews, tickets and survey answers, and leave out any number you cannot source.
- Treating '20% off' as the offer. Fix: write the five parts, and reframe the policies the business already has before touching the price.
- Cold openings that grab everyone (bait hooks, the product name in line one) and then pitch straight after the hook. Fix: open on a problem only the real buyer feels, teach the mechanism, show the product before naming it, and raise the sales feel slowly.
- An ad promising one thing and a landing page leading with another. Fix: match the six hook surfaces to the page top, and give each new angle its own page.
- Ten variants that are rewordings of one line. Fix: change the persona, angle or offer for each, or switch the register.
- Generic proof under specific claims ('customers love it', trust badges). Fix: put the strongest proof of that exact claim beside it, such as a demonstration or a counted review phrase.
- Fake urgency and inflated 'was' prices. Fix: use only deadlines and stock limits that are true, and reference prices the item really sold at recently.
- Rewriting a whole losing script, or asking for more ads, without reading where it lost people. Fix: read spend, then CPA, then hook and hold rate, and change only the beat the numbers point to.
- Machine-sounding copy: even rhythm, three-part lists, stock contrasts. Fix: read it aloud, vary sentence length, cut the tells, and rewrite toward the brand's real lines.
