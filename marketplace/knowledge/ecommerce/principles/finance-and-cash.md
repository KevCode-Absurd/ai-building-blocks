# Finance and cash: craft principles

This book is for the people who keep a store solvent while it grows: store finance, unit economics, reporting, cost forecasting, and the buyers who commit cash to stock, freight and duty. Read it before any job that touches cash timing, a stock buy, supplier terms, tax set-up, currency or funding; write the deliverable to it; and run the review checklist on the draft before hand-over. Margins, ad targets and attribution live in the measurement and profit book; this one owns timing, working capital, tax and the cost of money.

## What great looks like

- Cash is planned weekly for 13 weeks from real money movements, with a floor, and the lowest week is named with its fix.
- Every receipt is timed by the lag the store actually sees, net of fees, refunds, disputes and reserves.
- Every growth plan states the working capital it needs and who funds each dollar.
- Supplier terms are negotiated as hard as unit price, and every trade between price and timing is shown as an annual rate.
- Every stock buy traces to an open-to-buy line, and slow stock has a date and a route back to cash.
- Every SKU carries landed cost at the rate actually paid, and import VAT is kept apart from cost.
- Tax collected is held as someone else's money, with each market's registration and filing dates in the forecast.
- Every funding source is priced as an annual rate and matched to what it pays for.
- The same cost sits on the same P&L line every month, and interest sits below EBITDA.
- A weekly 15 to 30 minute finance meeting leaves dated actions on spend, stock or offers.

## The principles

### 1. Run the store on a weekly 13-week cash forecast with a cash floor, and treat profit as unproven until it reaches the bank

**Why it works:** Growth pulls profit into stock, receivables, tax and interest first. A brand growing 40% lifted EBITDA from $910,000 to $1.27M and still ended $200,000 shorter on cash; one showing $2M of profit with $3M of unsold stock is about $1M short. Unlike ads, a purchase order cannot be switched off, so stock timing is the main cash risk.

**How to apply:** Build 13 weekly columns from cash movements only: opening cash, receipts by source, payments by due date, closing cash. Set a floor; if the owner has none, use four weeks of fixed outflows (payroll, rent, apps, retainers, loan repayments) and label it a default. Every Monday, swap last week for actuals, note what missed and why, and add a week 13. A US apparel brand placing a $300,000 autumn order should see in week 2 that week 10 breaks the floor.

**Judge it by:** The lowest week and its fix lead the first screen. Weak work forecasts monthly from revenue and treats profit as cash.

**Breaks when:** A small store with no stock bets, daily payouts and no debt; a monthly check is enough.

**From:** FIN-6431, FIN-6426, MEA-6253, MEA-6312, FIN-0005, MEA-6280

### 2. Time every receipt by the store's measured payout lag, net of fees, refunds, disputes and reserves

**Why it works:** Sales are not receipts. US Shopify Payments settles in at least 3 business days, groups Friday to Sunday into one payout, pays nothing on weekends or holidays, and banks can add 1 to 3 days. Payouts arrive after fees, refunds, disputes and reserves, and card fees are not returned on refunds. Reserves are triggered by high refund or chargeback rates, long delivery times, pre-orders and sudden spikes: the weeks a store most needs cash.

**How to apply:** Measure the lag from the last 8 weeks of payout and settlement reports for each gateway, marketplace and wholesale buyer, and time each source by its own lag. Forecast the net payout, never gross sales. Show reserved money as its own line, not as opening cash. Before a big sale or pre-order launch, model a reserve on the spike. Pay-on-delivery remittance timing in India goes to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).

**Judge it by:** Each receipt line names its source and lag, and reserves are split out. Weak work drops this week's orders into this week's cash.

**Breaks when:** A store paid daily by one gateway with no reserve history; one lag for everything is then close enough.

**From:** FIN-6240, FIN-6241, FIN-6242, FIN-6243

### 3. Work out the cash conversion cycle, then shorten its biggest part before borrowing a dollar

**Why it works:** The cycle is inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days. At 182 plus 5 minus 45, the store pays for a jacket 142 days before the customer pays. When each order is funded from the last one's gross profit, with a 90-day lead time and full payment upfront, the store sits out of stock for about 90 days.

**How to apply:** Compute the three parts monthly and attack the largest first. Deposit: 20% upfront and the balance on shipment instead of 100%, which in one example cut the stockout gap to about the 20-day shipping time. Lead time: a maker quoting 30 days instead of 90. Sell-through: in one modelled brand doing about $92,000 a month, lifting 30-day sell-through from 30% to 40% raised ending cash from about $50,000 to $70,000 with no change in profit. Only then inventory finance, since it is costly and often needs a personal guarantee.

**Judge it by:** The cycle shown in its three parts, and the chosen lever sized in days and dollars. Weak work jumps straight to a loan.

**Breaks when:** Made-to-order, print-on-demand or consignment models, where inventory days are near zero.

**From:** MEA-6271, COG-6287, COG-6294, FIN-0011, FIN-0028

### 4. Price every growth plan in working capital, and name who funds each dollar of the gap

**Why it works:** Each new dollar of sales needs cash upfront in stock and receivables. At net working capital of 16% of revenue, adding $10M of sales needs about $1.6M. About five times, Blue Sense saw an agency take spend from $20,000 to $200,000 a month and revenue from $2M to $7M while inventory loans and interest drove net profit to near zero.

**How to apply:** Take net working capital (stock plus receivables minus payables) as a share of the last 12 months' revenue. Multiply it by the planned revenue gain and place that cash week by week in the forecast. Name the source of every dollar: retained profit, supplier terms, a facility or equity. If the source is expensive debt, model a slower plan beside it; growing a little slower at better ad efficiency with less interest often earns more. A UK homeware brand aiming to double gets one line stating the cash needed and its funding.

**Judge it by:** Every revenue target arrives with its cash need and funding line. Weak work presents growth with no balance sheet.

**Breaks when:** Pre-order, dropship or consignment models; brands with cheap equity already set aside.

**From:** FIN-6434, FIN-6426, FIN-0011, MEA-6324, MEA-6255

### 5. Negotiate when the supplier is paid, not only the unit price, and widen the float with every clean order

**Why it works:** Supplier float is payment terms minus the time to make and land the goods. Net 90 with 30 days to land gives 60 days; net 30 to 45 with 45 to 90 days to land is negative. Paying in full before shipment funds the factory and removes its reason to fix faults.

**How to apply:** Ask for terms in the first quote request. From the usual 30/70 split, ask in this order: 70% after a passed inspection; 70% against a bill of lading copy; or 20/50/30 with 30% after delivery where suppliers compete. For goods the factory could resell, ask for a 10 to 20% deposit. Review terms after the second on-time payment. Pitch your growth plan for net terms, as True Classic did, and honour every date. Factories can fund net 90 by factoring your invoices at about 6% a year; offer to share that cost. Consignment is the best float.

**Judge it by:** The quote request asks for terms and shows float days. Weak work negotiates price alone.

**Breaks when:** Goods with your moulds or logo, where factories insist on 30% or more; tiny first orders; escrow orders.

**From:** FIN-0005, COG-6341, COG-6338, FIN-0023, FIN-0004

### 6. Convert every trade between price and payment timing into an annual rate before saying yes

**Why it works:** Paying a supplier 2% more to move from net 30 to net 60 is a one-month loan costing about 24% a year, against a bank loan at about 15 to 16%. An early-payment discount is the same loan in reverse.

**How to apply:** Value a payment moved later as order value x share moved x days moved / 365 x your annual cost of cash. A $40,000 order with 70% moved 35 days later, with cash at 15%, is worth about $400, or 1%; against a 1% discount for paying before shipment it is a wash. For an early-payment discount, the annual rate is discount / (1 minus discount) x 365 / days paid early: 2% for paying 20 days early is about 37% a year, worth taking whenever cash costs less. When stockouts make cash the constraint, use a higher rate, such as what the cash earns in stock or ads.

**Judge it by:** Each terms option shows its annual rate beside the store's cheapest facility. Weak work calls 2% small.

**Breaks when:** The store cannot get bank credit, so supplier terms are its only finance; or longer terms come with no price change.

**From:** COG-6291, COG-6339, MEA-6268

### 7. Buy stock with open-to-buy by month and category, and re-cut future orders the week sales fall behind plan

**Why it works:** Stock is the biggest cash line that cannot be switched off. At Allbirds revenue rose about 30% while inventory roughly doubled; then came a $19.1M write-down, and gross margin fell from 52% to 43%.

**How to apply:** Open-to-buy is planned sales at cost plus planned markdowns plus planned ending stock, minus beginning stock, minus stock already on order. Build it monthly by category from the sales plan; at zero or below the store is overbought and stops ordering. Each week, shrink or push back unconfirmed orders when sales miss. Reconcile stock counts before each buy, and forecast from in-stock days only. Cap weeks of supply at lead time plus order interval plus safety stock (a 10-week sea lead time with monthly orders justifies about 15), using forecast sales, not past sales, so peak stock bought in September is not flagged as excess.

**Judge it by:** Every purchase order traces to an open-to-buy line and a weeks-of-supply cap, and the weekly review shows which buys were re-cut. Weak work orders on last year plus a growth rate.

**Breaks when:** Lead times so long that orders need a longer-range forecast too; made-to-order models.

**From:** COG-6292, OPS-6689, OPS-6688, COG-6289, OPS-0014

### 8. Clear slow stock on a deadline: grade every SKU by sell-through, push C and D lines back to cash, and retire one for each launch

**Why it works:** Stock that does not move is cash that cannot be spent. Many launches sell their first 30 to 40% to the list and fans, then stall with cash locked inside.

**How to apply:** Grade by sell-through time: A under 90 days, B 90 to 180, C 180 to 365, D over a year; track SKUs and dollars in each monthly. Push C and D stock through its own paid funnel, a creator page or a new market at break-even or a small loss. When unsold stock and rising MER hit together, a short rise in marketing share, from about 30% to 35% of revenue, aimed at that stock can restore cash faster than a 40% media cut. Size launch orders to what the organic pop plus a modest paid push can clear, and name the line each launch retires.

**Judge it by:** Dollars in each grade, with a date and route to cash for every C and D line. Weak work holds slow stock and hopes.

**Breaks when:** Made-to-order or print-on-demand stores; long-life staples where slow, steady sale is planned.

**From:** COG-6290, COG-6286, FIN-0026, FIN-0027, MEA-6329

### 9. Judge every minimum order and price break on total yearly cost, and counter at the break-even price

**Why it works:** A lower unit price on a bigger order means more stock held, and holding it (cash, space, the risk it goes stale) often costs more than the saving. In a textbook case a 4% discount for ordering 500 units saved $2,000 on goods but raised total yearly cost by about 2.5%.

**How to apply:** For each option, total yearly cost is units x price, plus orders x cost per order, plus average stock value x holding rate. The economic order quantity is the square root of (2 x yearly units x cost per order / (unit cost x holding rate)). Cost is flat near it, so ordering 20% away adds only about 2%. Find the discount at which both plans cost the same (6.4% there) and use it as your counter, or ask for the discount at a smaller quantity. Use a holding rate that includes the store's real cost of cash.

**Judge it by:** Total yearly cost for each option and the counter price. Weak work takes the lowest unit price.

**Breaks when:** Prices are about to rise, the goods cannot go stale, or storage is free and cash plentiful.

**From:** COG-6363, COG-6362, OPS-6665

### 10. Cost every SKU at full landed cost, at the exchange rate actually paid, and update it with every shipment

**Why it works:** Cost of goods is everything it takes to get the product onto your shelf ready to sell: price, inbound freight, container, duty and clearance. Brands that book freight elsewhere push the wrong products. A 2 to 3 point error in cost of goods on a $20M brand can wipe about $500,000 of EBITDA.

**How to apply:** Per shipment, landed cost per unit is goods plus freight, insurance, duty, fees, broker and inland delivery, over sellable units. US duty is charged on goods value only; EU and UK import VAT are charged on value plus duty plus transport, so VAT rides on the duty. For a VAT-registered store import VAT is cash, not cost: keep it in its own column. Spread freight and duty by weight or volume. Enter the result as cost per item, because Shopify reports profit only for variants that had a cost when sold.

**Judge it by:** Each SKU shows its landed cost build, rate paid and shipment. Weak work uses the factory price or last year's rate.

**Breaks when:** Dropship suppliers bill a bundled delivered price, which already is the landed cost.

**From:** FIN-0007, COG-6293, COG-6331, FIN-6439, FIN-6441, MEA-6402

### 11. Run refunds, chargebacks and reserves as one cash line, with a weekly alarm well below the processor's limit

**Why it works:** Every refund costs the original card fee, which Shopify Payments does not return. A chargeback costs about $128 on average in fees and handling. Processors flag a store above about 0.9% of transactions and can stop taking its cards above 1%, and high refund or chargeback rates trigger payout reserves.

**How to apply:** Track the chargeback rate weekly as a share of transactions, with an internal alarm near 0.5%. Watch for card testing, runs of small odd orders, which comes weeks before chargebacks. Cost each return in full: both shipping legs, both payment fees, handling and the unsellable share at landed cost; in one example about $34, against the $22 the returns line showed. Accrue expected returns daily at the known rate and true up monthly, so a big month's refunds do not fake a dip in the next. Fight a dispute only when expected recovery beats the evidence work.

**Judge it by:** One cost line for refunds and disputes per order, and the weekly rate beside its alarm. Weak work reports only Shopify's refunds.

**Breaks when:** Low-return categories with no disputes; check monthly.

**From:** FIN-6243, SEC-0004, FIN-6242, MEA-6276, MEA-0028

### 12. Pay suppliers only into the contracting company's own account, and confirm every bank change with a call you place

**Why it works:** A payment to the wrong account is a cash loss a store rarely recovers. Paying a Chinese supplier into a Hong Kong, personal, broker or related-company account creates fraud risk. On Alibaba.com, a wire keeps escrow protection only when sent to the details given at checkout, never to details sent in chat.

**How to apply:** Pay only the company that signed the contract, into its registered account, and keep a register of verified supplier bank details. Treat any change of details as fraud until confirmed by phone on a number from your records, not the email. Confirm key terms by email, not chat alone. On orders from roughly $30,000 to $50,000 up, ask for a letter of credit paying only against matching documents, an inspection certificate among them; a documentary collection is cheaper but banks check nothing. Pay the final balance only after a passed pre-shipment inspection.

**Judge it by:** A bank register with verification dates, and the payment method per order. Weak work pays the account on the latest invoice.

**Breaks when:** Orders paid through platform escrow, where the platform's release rules apply.

**From:** COG-6315, COG-6358, COG-6371, COG-6332, COG-6338, COG-6409, COG-0006

### 13. Put each cost on one fixed line of the chart of accounts, and keep interest below EBITDA

**Why it works:** Costs that drift between lines make months impossible to compare and hide the cause of a miss. Interest buried in operating costs makes a brand look less profitable to a buyer, who values earnings before financing.

**How to apply:** Agree these rules with the accountant and print them on the P&L. The branded retail box is cost of goods; the plain shipping carton is shipping expense. Inbound freight and duty are cost of goods. Outbound shipping, payment fees and per-order packing labour are cost of delivery ($400 a day over 100 parcels is $4 an order). Refurbishing a return for resale is cost of goods; return shipping is overhead. Coupons cut revenue, not cost of goods. Lay out net revenue, cost of delivery, CM2, marketing, CM3, operating costs, EBITDA, interest and tax, net profit. Show gross to net monthly, since Xero and QuickBooks often receive only Shopify's net.

**Judge it by:** A written line policy and the same cost on the same line every month. Weak work moves costs to flatter a month.

**Breaks when:** Local rules or an auditor require another layout; keep a management view beside it.

**From:** FIN-0008, FIN-0009, FIN-0017, MEA-6257, MEA-6256, MEA-6270, FIN-0010

### 14. Hold fixed costs nearly flat as revenue grows, and back-solve the contribution needed from the profit goal

**Why it works:** Fixed costs fall straight through to profit, unlike variable marketing. A $5M brand at 20% net ($1M) beats a $10M brand at 5% ($500,000), yet most seven-figure founders let people, software and office costs rise faster than revenue. In stores doing $50,000 to $300,000 a month, overspending on operating costs often forces the next stock order onto debt and ends in a cash crisis even while acquisition works.

**How to apply:** Before adding a fixed cost, check that monthly contribution after ad spend covers all current operating costs, and model the next two stock buys against cash on hand. Back-solve the target: a 20% net goal with 15% operating costs needs 35% contribution; $1M of profit plus $1M of operating costs needs $2M of contribution. A strong brand's shape: cost of delivery 30 to 40% of revenue, operating costs 10 to 12%, profit 15 to 20%.

**Judge it by:** Each new fixed cost arrives with its contribution test and cash check. Weak work approves a hire because revenue grew.

**Breaks when:** A brand deliberately investing ahead of growth, with funded runway and a dated plan to reach its margin.

**From:** MEA-6252, MEA-6307, OPS-6615, MEA-6254, FIN-0016, FIN-0028

### 15. Price every funding source as an annual rate, and match each one to what it pays for

**Why it works:** Lenders quote in different shapes. A revenue-based offer at 6% flat repaid over four months costs about 36% a year, more than double a 15 to 16% bank loan, because the average balance is about half the amount. Shopify Capital takes a share of daily sales until amount plus fixed fee is repaid, so strong sales raise the annual cost.

**How to apply:** For a flat fee, annual rate is fee / (average balance x years); rerun it at the store's forecast. Match money to use: a credit line for purchase order timing, opened while profitable and before it is needed; factoring for big retail orders, priced on the retailer's credit; inventory finance only for stock that sells at planned efficiency; equity for planned losses. Never fund fixed costs with short, sales-linked money. Repay the dearest debt first: clearing $100,000 at 36% saves about $36,000 a year.

**Judge it by:** One table of facilities with annual rate, schedule and covenants, wired into the forecast. Weak work calls a 6% fee cheap.

**Breaks when:** Only expensive money is on offer; the test is then whether the use returns more than the rate.

**From:** FIN-6435, MEA-6268, FIN-6244, FIN-0020, MEA-6272

### 16. Treat a longer customer payback as borrowing: measure it by cohort with churn, and fund it before you buy it

**Why it works:** Each month of payback ties up cash. In one model, moving from a 3-month to a 7-month payback made the capital needed five to six times bigger. The usual shortcut assumes nobody leaves: a 3.2-month payback on paper became about 8 months once 25% of a subscription cohort had gone by order two.

**How to apply:** Follow one monthly cohort: the full acquisition cost goes out upfront, and gross profit returns only from customers still active; payback is the month cumulative gross profit passes that cost. For subscriptions with skips, count in orders. Cash needed is new customers per month x acquisition cost, held until payback. A first-order loss is paid only by repeat profit, investors or debt, so name which. IM8's facility funds 70% of each month's marketing and allows 6 to 8 month paybacks, but it still owes the money if a cohort underperforms; model repayment under slower decay.

**Judge it by:** Payback per cohort, the cash it ties up, and the named source. Weak work quotes the shortcut.

**Breaks when:** Brands with cheap committed funding and long, proven retention choosing a land grab on purpose.

**From:** FIN-0014, MEA-6275, MEA-6334, FIN-0022, MEA-6232

### 17. Run bad, base and upside cases through the cash forecast, and open the facility for the worst one while times are good

**Why it works:** Beating plan often causes the crunch, because faster growth needs stock first. In Blue Sense's year-two example for a brand of about $20M, the bad case needed a $700,000 facility, the base case dipped under the $500,000 floor, and the upside left only about $200,000 in the bank.

**How to apply:** Build three revenue cases, each with its own stock buys, payouts, tax, payroll and debt, and note each lowest cash week. Size the facility to the worst case and open it while profitable. When a week breaks the floor, fix it in order: push back or split the supplier balance; move to net 60 or a second supplier; draw on the facility for the gap (about $5,000 to $10,000 of interest for a 3-week draw is a fair price to hold the floor); only then touch ads, which produce the payouts. Agree in advance what moves the plan to another case.

**Judge it by:** Three cases with lowest weeks, a facility sized to the worst, and a fix order. Weak work forecasts one line.

**Breaks when:** The worst case never nears the floor.

**From:** FIN-6432, MEA-6280, FIN-0020

### 18. Rank each spare $100,000 across the five uses of cash on return, time to cash and confidence

**Why it works:** Every dollar in a store goes to ads, stock, people, site and operations, or debt. Ads win by default only because they are easiest to spend.

**How to apply:** Size each option in contribution over the same window, with the date the cash comes back and a confidence grade. Blue Sense's example: paying down 36% debt saved about $36,000 a year with certainty; reordering an A-grade SKU earned about $59,000 per 4-month cycle, roughly $170,000 a year, with fairly high confidence; Meta at its marginal return earned about $43,000 within 30 to 60 days, and only if stock existed; a conversion project might net about $179,000 over 12 months but was untested and slow. Judge levers on 60 to 180-day cash, not 30 days: in one model, lifting average order value from $150 to $180 raised monthly net income from about $2,600 to $16,300.

**Judge it by:** A ranked list in one unit, with time to cash and confidence on each line. Weak work sends spare cash to ads by default.

**Breaks when:** A must-pay is due, such as tax, payroll or a loan covenant; that comes first whatever the return.

**From:** FIN-6430, MEA-6272, FIN-0029

### 19. Pay the owner from closed-month free cash flow, not profit, and only if the 13-week low still clears the floor

**Why it works:** A growing stock brand spends its profit on next season's stock first; in Blue Sense's $20M example the owners could take a dividend only by borrowing. A payout sized from reported profit takes the next purchase order's cash.

**How to apply:** Close the books by about the 15th, then take the month's free cash flow: operating cash after stock buys, tax, interest and capital spending. Rerun the forecast with the payment in; pay only if the lowest week clears the floor. One founder with wide supplier float pays out 65 to 75% of monthly free cash flow; with thin float or a growth plan, pay less or nothing. An owner who has reinvested everything can use a debt recap: lenders want about $2M of EBITDA for three years, the last audited, lend about 3 times EBITDA at 12 to 14% effective, and test interest cover near 2 times.

**Judge it by:** Each payout shows the month's free cash flow, the share paid and the lowest week after it. Weak work pays a share of P&L profit.

**Breaks when:** The founder takes only a salary; investors or loan covenants bar payouts.

**From:** FIN-0005, FIN-6426, FIN-0003

### 20. Hold tax collected as someone else's money, and register in each US state before its threshold is crossed

**Why it works:** Sales tax and VAT pass through the store's bank but belong to the tax office, so spending them turns the next filing into a cash crisis. Since the 2018 Wayfair ruling, US states can make a remote seller collect once its sales into the state pass an economic threshold, which differs by state.

**How to apply:** Move tax collected into a separate account at each payout, and put every filing date and amount in the forecast. In the US a business location creates nexus, and some states count stock in a 3PL too; economic nexus is measured on net sales and can include sales outside Shopify. Check Shopify's tax liability page monthly, which flags a state at 80% of its threshold, and register before collecting; Shopify files only with managed filing on. Concentrating ads in a few states crosses thresholds sooner. Trace every rate and registration to an accountant's answer.

**Judge it by:** A state list of sales against threshold, registration status and filing dates. Weak work turns tax on everywhere, or nowhere.

**Breaks when:** A store sells only where one national sales tax applies; the watch reduces to that rule.

**From:** FIN-6206, FIN-6425, SET-0004, https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/17pdf/17-494_j4el.pdf

### 21. Choose each export market's VAT and duty route before launch, and price per-parcel charges into shipping

**Why it works:** Cross-border tax decides whether buyers pay at the door, which causes refused parcels, and how much cash customs holds.

**How to apply:** Inside the EU, above €10,000 a year of cross-border consumer sales the buyer's country rate applies, usually via the One-Stop Shop. Non-EU sellers can use IOSS so VAT on parcels up to €150 is paid at checkout; ship bigger parcels duty paid. From 1 July 2026 parcels under €150 pay €3 duty per item, plus a €2 handling fee per item at any value expected from 1 November 2026: about €5 on a €40 single item, so bundles cut the hit. UK parcels of £135 or less have VAT charged at sale and no duty, but the seller must register for UK VAT and the relief ends by March 2029. UK bulk imports can use postponed VAT accounting, keeping about £8,400 on a £40,000 shipment.

**Judge it by:** Each market names its route, per-parcel charges and recheck dates. Weak work leaves buyers paying at the door.

**Breaks when:** B2B, excise goods or EU warehouse stock; recheck IOSS, due to lose its €150 cap from July 2028.

**From:** FIN-6438, OPS-6658, OPS-6656, OPS-6631, FIN-6440, https://taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu/taxation/vat/vat-e-commerce_en

### 22. Be the importer of record on regular stock, with a classification file per SKU

**Why it works:** The importer of record owes reasonable care on code, value and origin; negligence can cost up to twice the lost duty. US duty-free entry for low-value parcels ended on 29 August 2025, so samples pay duty too.

**How to apply:** Buy regular orders on FCA or DAP with your own broker, so duty, import VAT and the entry are in your name; under DDP the seller can pick the importer, declare a value you never see and mark up the duty. US duty is on the price paid, excluding international freight but including packing, commissions, moulds you supply and royalties. Keep a file per SKU (code, reasoning, photos, materials) and review it when the product changes. Size the US continuous bond at the greater of $50,000 or 10% of last year's duties. Once US sales justify it, hold US stock so duty is paid on import value. IEEPA refunds close 80 days after liquidation.

**Judge it by:** Each import shows its importer, Incoterm and classification file. Weak work accepts an all-in DDP price.

**Breaks when:** The supplier is a registered importer in your country, or a tiny trial. Recheck tariffs with a broker.

**From:** OPS-6686, COG-6261, COG-6295, COG-6262, OPS-6668, COG-6288, FIN-6437

### 23. Set a currency policy for supplier payments: cover the next 13 weeks of foreign bills on a rule, and record the rate paid

**Why it works:** Most factories price in or peg to the US dollar, so a store selling in pounds, euros or Australian dollars sees product cost move all year. Outside the US, a 1% fall in your currency against the dollar raises import prices about 0.6 to 0.8% within a year.

**How to apply:** List the dollar payments due in the 13-week forecast, decide what share to buy ahead, and set a trigger rate for converting. Record the rate paid against each purchase order so landed cost stays true. Budget your bank's spread on top of reference rates such as the ECB's. Payment fees run about 2.5 to 3.5% for domestic cards and 4 to 5% when Shopify converts a currency. Hedging is for the bank or accountant to advise; Blue Sense adds a currency policy at about $20M to $50M of revenue and formal hedging above $50M.

**Judge it by:** Each foreign-currency order shows the rate assumed, rate paid and share covered. Weak work uses last year's rate.

**Breaks when:** The store buys and sells in one currency, or swings are minor next to other costs.

**From:** FIN-6427, COG-6389, COG-6379, MEA-6375

### 24. Forecast product cost in three cases from named public price series, phased by each input's lag and pass-through

**Why it works:** Input prices reach finished goods slowly and partly. ECB research finds intermediate price moves take about six months to reach producer prices of consumer goods, with only about a quarter passed through. Over the year to autumn 2026 raw cotton rose about 23% but the US price index for woven fabric mills only 6.5%, so a supplier citing cotton overstates its case.

**How to apply:** Split the factory price into materials, labour, overhead, freight and margin, and tie each line to a named series: the BLS import price index for China (excluding duty, so add freight and duty), US producer price indexes for boxes and resin, FRED cotton. Apply each move only to its input's share, after its lag. Set high and low cases at plus or minus 1.28 x sigma x the square root of months ahead gives an 80% band. With steady volume, trade a committed schedule for an index clause: base price x current index / base index.

**Judge it by:** Each case traces to series codes, shares and lags. Weak work applies one flat inflation guess.

**Breaks when:** Labour or brand premium dominates the price.

**From:** COG-6378, COG-6307, COG-6308, COG-6364, COG-6329, COG-6368, COG-6344

### 25. Book pre-order money, gift cards and prepaid subscriptions as money owed until the goods ship

**Why it works:** Pre-orders are the cheapest funding a store has (Ridge started on about $200,000 of Kickstarter pre-orders), but the cash is a promise. If shipping slips the store must refund, and pre-orders and long delivery times raise the chance of a payout reserve.

**How to apply:** Hold pre-order, deposit and gift card money as deferred revenue, a liability, and recognise revenue when goods ship or the card is redeemed. Shopify pre-orders can take full, partial or no payment upfront; partial payment cuts refund exposure. Keep cash to refund the orders a delay would hit. In the US, the FTC mail order rule requires shipping within the stated time, or 30 days if none is stated; when that slips, tell the buyer and offer to cancel with a prompt refund. US gift cards generally cannot expire within five years of issue, so balances sit on the books for years. Check each market's consumer law.

**Judge it by:** A deferred revenue line and a refund reserve in the forecast for every pre-order campaign. Weak work spends pre-order cash as this month's revenue.

**Breaks when:** Pre-orders ship within days, or gift card volume is tiny.

**From:** OPS-6282, FIN-6242, FIN-0020, https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/business-guide-ftcs-mail-internet-or-telephone-order-merchandise-rule, https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1005/20/

### 26. Reconcile money at four levels every month: order, payment, payout and bank

**Why it works:** Two totals that roughly match can hide fee errors, missed refunds and unpaid settlements. A payout is net of fees, refunds, disputes, reserves, adjustments and currency conversion, and accounting software usually receives only Shopify's net revenue.

**How to apply:** Match each order to its payment, each payment to a payout, and each payout to a bank line; list every break by type, value and age, with an owner. Check fee rates by payment method and country against the contract. Measure cash collected as net sales plus shipping charges: total sales includes tax, and gross sales ignores discounts and returns. Report gross to net each month (refunds, discounts, shipping collected), because a $10,000 rise in net revenue can come entirely from fewer refunds. Shipping labels may be billed to the card on file at a threshold rather than out of payouts. Marketplaces settle on their own cycles, and some deduct tax at source.

**Judge it by:** A break list with values and owners, and a gross-to-net bridge on the monthly report. Weak work calls the month reconciled because totals are close.

**Breaks when:** A very small store with one gateway and few refunds; a quarterly check can do.

**From:** FIN-6241, MEA-0010, FIN-0010, MEA-6263, SET-0006

### 27. Move to accrual books and a monthly balance-sheet check once stock or credit terms matter

**Why it works:** Cash books record money when it moves, so a big stock buy reads as a loss and a pre-sale as a profit. Days of inventory, sales and payables, turns and stock age come out cleanly only from accrual books; Blue Sense says a brand above $10M still on cash accounting should switch at once.

**How to apply:** Close the books by about the 15th of the next month. Monthly, fill a one-page sheet: inventory, receivable and payable days and the cash cycle; stock turns (cost of goods sold / average inventory); GMROI; net working capital as a share of revenue; dollars in each stock grade. Act on any metric worsening two periods running. Build the finance stack by stage: at $10M to $20M, accrual books, the weekly forecast, open-to-buy, channel P&Ls and landed cost per SKU; add the rest as the store grows. Below about $5M, basic books, landed cost and a simple cash forecast are enough.

**Judge it by:** The sheet exists and shows trends, not single readings. Weak work runs a stock-heavy brand on cash books.

**Breaks when:** Local rules require cash accounting or make it clearly better for tax; confirm with the accountant.

**From:** FIN-6436, FIN-6428, FIN-0005

### 28. Hold a 15 to 30 minute weekly finance meeting on four fixed items, and a monthly plan that ties demand, supply and cash

**Why it works:** Forecasts change nothing unless someone reads them every week and moves spend, stock or offers in response. A fixed agenda turns numbers into decisions in time.

**How to apply:** The weekly agenda has four items: actual cash against the 13-week plan, and any week now under the floor; open-to-buy, and whether future buys need cutting or pulling forward; marginal acquisition MER per channel against plan; and the volume any planned price change or discount needs to hold margin. Leave every meeting with named, dated actions. Monthly, run a short planning cycle: in week 1 update sales, stock, open orders and the slow-stock list; in week 2 agree one demand plan with promotions and launches; in week 3 check supplier capacity, lead times and landing dates, then compute open-to-buy and the cash for deposits and balances; in week 4 decide the gaps. For a small brand the cycle fits in a few hours a month.

**Judge it by:** A dated action log from each meeting. Weak work builds a dashboard nobody acts on.

**Breaks when:** A solo founder already reviews these daily; meetings held without data prepared turn into talk.

**From:** FIN-6433, OPS-6637

### 29. Fund a new market or sales channel as its own business, on first-order profit and its own cash plan

**Why it works:** A launch into the US from abroad needs a second stock buy, earns less at first, and running out of working capital is a top way such launches fail. Wholesale pays later: in one example it earned 10% contribution on 60-day terms against DTC's 22.5% paid within days.

**How to apply:** Before launch, compare first-order gross profit with expected acquisition cost and count no lifetime value; US CPMs can run about double. Build a 12-month cash plan for the market alone: stock buys, tariffs, a lower first-year margin, creative and staff. Ring-fence the money, keep a floor for the home business, and agree in advance when you would pause. For wholesale, put each buyer's terms and usual delay in the forecast, and price factoring for big retail orders. Review six ways a launch fails (price, complexity, fulfilment, tax, focus, cash) and launch only when each has a number.

**Judge it by:** A separate cash plan with a pause point. Weak work funds it from the home business's cash.

**Breaks when:** Lean tests shipped from home with no local stock; launches paid for by capital raised for them.

**From:** FIN-6424, MEA-6267, MEA-6274, OPS-6606, FIN-0020

### 30. Value the business on normalised earnings, and keep the records a buyer will ask for from year one

**Why it works:** Buyers pay a multiple of earnings before financing, after removing the founder's hidden subsidy. A founder paid $80,000 may be doing a $200,000 job; at $20M to $50M the founder often covers roles worth $600,000 to $700,000, and a buyer deducts that cost.

**How to apply:** Walk from reported EBIT: subtract the founder's market-rate replacement cost, deal legal costs and any unbooked stock write-down; add back personal costs run through the business. Practitioner bands: dropshipping stores about 1 to 2 times EBITDA, ordinary brands about 3 to 6 times, with a revenue multiple earned only by retail distribution, repeat purchase and brand pull. Private equity looks for around 20% EBITDA growing about 20% a year with a moat, and conservative buyout debt is 2 to 3 times EBITDA. Keep three to five years of accrual books, a stock provision policy, reconciled taxes, new versus returning revenue, top SKU shares and documented add-backs.

**Judge it by:** A normalised earnings walk with each adjustment sourced. Weak work multiplies this year's revenue.

**Breaks when:** Strategic buyers pay for IP or channel access above earnings; venture deals are priced on growth.

**From:** FIN-6429, FIN-0001, FIN-0021

## Working from the org context

- Read the sale and launch calendar first: every planned sale, drop and launch moves stock buys, ad bills, payouts and reserve risk, so it sets the shape of the 13 weeks.
- Read what ran and won to grade stock: a past winner due for relaunch justifies an A-grade reorder; a product the brain marks as fading belongs in the C and D plan.
- Take the store's markets, currencies, sales channels and fulfilment set-up from the brain, and build one cash and tax column per market and channel it names, never one blended store.
- Keep the owner's stated cash floor, risk appetite, debt rules and any accountant's answers exactly as recorded; never swap in a default without saying so in one line.
- Keep the brand's offer habits in view: a brand that never discounts should not clear slow stock through sitewide sales; use isolated funnels, bundles or new markets that protect the brand.
- Write owner-facing summaries in the brand's plain voice and reading level, with money in the store's own currency and the names the owner already uses for its numbers.
- Where the brand does something that costs cash (pays 100% upfront, books freight in operating costs, spends tax collected), propose the change as one option with its cash effect in dollars and the date it lands, and leave the decision with the owner.
- Never change payment, tax or banking settings, move money or accept a financing offer on the brain's facts alone; the deliverable is the analysis and the recommendation.
- Send back to the brain any fact the job confirmed, such as the measured payout lag, supplier terms, the cash floor or a registration date, so the next job starts from it.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does the forecast run weekly for 13 weeks from cash movements, not revenue, with a stated floor?
- [ ] Is the lowest cash week named on the first screen, with its fix and the date to act?
- [ ] Is every receipt timed by a measured lag for its source, net of fees, refunds, disputes and reserves?
- [ ] Are reserved and held funds kept out of opening cash?
- [ ] Does every growth target state the working capital it needs and the source of each dollar?
- [ ] Is the cash conversion cycle shown in its three parts, with the chosen lever sized in days and dollars?
- [ ] Is every trade between price and payment timing, and every funding offer, shown as an annual rate beside the cheapest facility?
- [ ] Does every proposed purchase order trace to an open-to-buy line, with stock counts reconciled first?
- [ ] Is slow stock graded A to D in dollars, with a date and a route back to cash for each C and D line?
- [ ] Is cost of goods built from landed cost per SKU at the exchange rate actually paid, with import VAT kept apart?
- [ ] Does each cost sit on its fixed P&L line, with interest below EBITDA and a gross-to-net bridge shown?
- [ ] Are tax registrations, thresholds and filing dates listed by market, each traced to an accountant's answer?
- [ ] Does each export market name its VAT and duty route and include per-parcel charges in landed cost?
- [ ] Are pre-order, deposit and gift card balances shown as owed, with refund cash held against a delay?
- [ ] Is the chargeback rate tracked weekly against an alarm below the processor's limit?
- [ ] Are bad, base and upside cases run through the forecast, with the facility sized to the worst?
- [ ] Is any owner payout sized from closed-month free cash flow, with the lowest week after payment still above the floor?
- [ ] Is every number in the store's own currency and period, with time-bound rules given a recheck date?
- [ ] Does the deliverable stop at analysis and recommendation, leaving payments, borrowing and filing to the owner and accountant?

## Common failures

- Reporting P&L profit as if it were spendable cash. Fix: put closing cash and the lowest week beside profit on the same page.
- Dropping orders into the week they were placed. Fix: time each receipt by its measured payout or settlement lag and forecast the net.
- Cutting ad spend first when cash is short. Fix: move supplier timing, then use a short facility draw, and touch ads last, because ads produce the payouts.
- Calling a flat fee or a 2% price bump cheap. Fix: convert it to an annual rate and compare it with the store's cheapest money.
- Using the factory price, or last year's exchange rate, as cost of goods. Fix: build landed cost per SKU from each shipment's invoices at the rate actually paid.
- Treating import VAT as a cost, or tax collected as revenue. Fix: keep import VAT in its own reclaimable column and sweep tax collected into a separate account.
- Approving a growth plan with no balance sheet. Fix: multiply net working capital as a share of revenue by the revenue gain and name who funds it.
- Ordering stock on last year plus a growth rate. Fix: run open-to-buy by month and category and re-cut unconfirmed orders when sales miss.
- Spending pre-order cash as this month's revenue. Fix: book it as owed until shipped and hold refund cash against a delay.
- Accepting a supplier's all-in DDP price for regular stock. Fix: buy on FCA or DAP, act as importer of record and keep a classification file.
- Paying a new bank account from an emailed invoice. Fix: confirm every bank change by a call to a number on file, and pay only the contracting company.
- Giving tax or financing answers as settled facts. Fix: frame each as analysis, trace each rate to an accountant, and leave filing and borrowing to the owner.
