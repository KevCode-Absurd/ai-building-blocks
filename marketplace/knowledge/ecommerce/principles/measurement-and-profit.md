# Measurement and profit: craft principles

This book is for the people who tell a store what is really working: unit economics, tracking, reporting and store finance. Read it before any job that sets a margin, target, budget, report or tracking fix; write to it; and run the review checklist on the draft before hand-over.

## What great looks like

- Every money figure starts from gross profit per order on a delivered basis, with each cost line named, never from product margin or revenue.
- Paid media is judged on new customers: acquisition MER and cost per new customer, beside first-order gross profit.
- Lifetime value is always gross profit over a named, closed window, from observed cohorts.
- Every platform number carries its attribution window and is checked against store orders before anyone acts on it.
- Big budget moves rest on the top of the evidence ladder (finance-grade numbers or a lift test), not on platform ROAS alone.
- Scale calls use the return on the next dollar, not the account average.
- Returns, refunds and discounts sit in the month and the order that caused them.
- Profit and cash are reported side by side, with the lowest cash week in view.
- A dashboard has few numbers, each with a written formula, a source and an action rule.
- Each sale is counted once, with the same ID on every copy of the event.

## The principles

### 1. Build gross profit per order from the cost of getting it to the door, and refuse to rank anything until it is trusted

**Why it works:** Product margin hides most of the cost of a sale. In one worked example a $100 tee bought at $30 looks like a 70% margin, but after shipping, pick and pack, payment fees, inbound freight, tax and a returns and discount allowance, true gross margin was 24%. Every break-even, CAC ceiling and offer test built on 70% is wrong by a factor of three.

**How to apply:** List the cost lines per order: landed product cost (purchase price, inbound freight, duty, branded retail box), outbound shipping, pick and pack (daily packer pay divided by parcels, so $400 over 100 orders is $4), payment fees, platform fees, and a returns and discount allowance. Book the plain shipping carton as shipping, not product cost. State which choice the P&L uses for outbound shipping. A US candle brand should show the before and after: price, every line, the gross profit in dollars and the share.

**Judge it by:** A cost line per row with its source and date, and one gross profit number the whole team reuses. Weak work quotes "60% margin" from the supplier price.

**Breaks when:** Costs move often (fresh food, freight spikes); then refresh the figure each purchase order rather than yearly.

**From:** MEA-0023, MEA-6251, MEA-0037, FIN-0007, FIN-0008, FIN-0017, MEA-6257

### 2. Recompute break-even ROAS as 1 divided by delivered gross margin for every offer, and keep product cost live in the store

**Why it works:** Discounts come off gross profit, not revenue, so the return an ad needs rises much faster than the discount. At a $100 price with $60 delivered cost, break-even ROAS is 2.5; at 30% off the same costs need about 7. A store that breaks even at 2 needs about 4.5 at 40% off. Holding a profit target rises steeper still: 15% off might need 8x where 25% off needs 20x.

**How to apply:** Tag cost per item on every product in Shopify so margin updates as prices change; Shopify only reports profit for variants that had a cost when they sold, and that cost is a snapshot, so update it with each new purchase order. Before any sale, table each discount depth: price, gross profit per order, break-even CAC, break-even ROAS and the ROAS that holds today's profit per order.

**Judge it by:** A separate target per offer and a written ROAS floor for the sale days. Weak work reuses the full-price target through a sale.

**Breaks when:** A gift with purchase or bundle lifts order value at near-equal margin; there break-even can hold steady or fall, so model it rather than assume a rise.

**From:** OFF-6633, OFF-0065, MEA-6361, OFF-6632, MEA-6248, MEA-6321, MEA-6402, OFF-6638

### 3. Judge paid media on acquisition MER and cost per new customer, never on blended MER or cost per order

**Why it works:** Blended numbers include repeat buyers who cost almost nothing to bring back. In one case blended MER went 4x, 5x, 4x over three months while acquisition MER slid 2.0, 1.8, 1.6, so the blend kept rewarding the purchase of worse new customers. Dividing spend by all orders gives a blended cost per order that always looks cheaper than true CAC.

**How to apply:** Acquisition MER is new-customer revenue divided by total ad spend. CAC is total ad spend divided by new customers in the period, counted from the store's own first orders. Keep blended MER, on net revenue after returns, as a finance control figure, not the media KPI. For an in-platform read, Meta on 7-day click without view-through usually tracks acquisition MER closely. Do not try to measure CAC per channel from store data alone; that needs the evidence in principles 13 to 16.

**Judge it by:** The headline paid number is new-customer based, with blended MER shown below it. Weak work celebrates a rising blended MER while new-customer orders fall.

**Breaks when:** Several channels drive new customers at scale (large influencer or retail budgets); then one platform cannot claim the whole acquisition MER and lift tests must split the credit.

**From:** MEA-0039, MEA-6235, MEA-6294, MEA-6305, MEA-0025, MEA-6236, MEA-6234

### 4. Set the CAC ceiling from new customers' first-order gross profit and read it on the ratio bands

**Why it works:** Store-wide AOV and margin include returning buyers' bigger baskets and full-price orders. In one example the store read $100 AOV at 70%, so $70 of gross profit, but new buyers spent $90 at 50%, about $45. A CAC target set from the store-wide figure loses money on every new customer.

**How to apply:** Work out first-order gross profit from new customers only: their AOV, less product cost, shipping, fulfilment, fees and the entry discount. Divide time-bound gross profit by CAC and read the bands: under 1 loses money, 1 to 2 is hold and scale only with proven repeat, 2 to 3 is the zone to scale, above 3 means under-spending unless cash is the limit. Set the target CAC as gross profit divided by about 2: $80 of 30-day gross profit gives a $40 CAC. Track both sides monthly, because first-order gross profit can swing from $40 to $70 with product mix while CAC stays flat.

**Judge it by:** A monthly table of CAC beside first-order gross profit dollars going back 2 years. Weak work sets CAC from blended AOV.

**Breaks when:** Subscription or high-repeat brands deliberately pay more than first-order profit; that needs the cohort and cash modelling in principles 5 and 26, which most brands lack.

**From:** MEA-6260, MEA-6318, MEA-0024, MEA-0020, MEA-6345, MEA-6371, MEA-6332, MEA-6343

### 5. Quote lifetime value only as gross profit over a closed, named window, from cohorts old enough to have lived it

**Why it works:** Open-ended revenue LTV keeps rising every month and justifies CACs the store cannot afford. Modelled LTV flatters too: one brand quoted a 3 to 1 ratio on 36 months when its oldest cohort was 20 months old. Category sets the ceiling: 365-day gross profit over the first order grows about 55% in fashion, about 110% in consumables and supplements, and near zero in furniture; most brands see only 20 to 40%.

**How to apply:** Build lifetime gross profit by first-order month at 30, 60, 90, 180 and 365 days, leaving cells blank until the window has closed. Use a 30-day window for low-repeat goods and 90 to 180 days for consumables; use 180 or 365 days only to compare across brands. Cut cohorts by the discount code used to acquire them: about 60% of one agency's brands saw much worse LTV on heavy-discount buyers. Ask of any ratio: observed or modelled, and over what window?

**Judge it by:** Every LTV figure names its window, basis (gross profit) and cohort age. Weak work shows "LTV $240" with no window.

**Breaks when:** The store is under a year old; then plan on first-order profit only and treat repeat as upside.

**From:** MEA-6261, MEA-6295, MEA-6370, MEA-0038, MEA-6228, MEA-0052, MEA-6341, MEA-6365, MEA-6327

### 6. Split the P&L into first-time and returning orders, so repeat profit cannot hide a loss on new customers

**Why it works:** A store can show healthy total profit while it loses money on every new buyer. In one audit the first-time P&L ran about $50,000 a month in the red against $300,000 of returning profit; in another it slid from plus $20,000 to minus $70,000 a month while total profit stayed at $80,000 to $100,000. First-order losses can only be funded by returning profit, investors or debt.

**How to apply:** Use Shopify's customer order type to split revenue, cost of delivery and marketing into two P&Ls. Put almost all ad spend on the first-time side and retention costs on the returning side. In a $200,000-a-month example, $140,000 of new-customer revenue on $50,000 of ads earned $20,000 of contribution, while $60,000 of returning revenue costing $2,500 earned $27,500. Report both lines every month, with acquisition MER from the first-time side.

**Judge it by:** Two contribution lines, one per customer type, on the first screen. Weak work reports one blended profit.

**Breaks when:** Guest checkout or several identities per buyer make new versus returning unreliable; fix the identity key first and say how much it moves the split.

**From:** MEA-0055, MEA-6334, MEA-6344, MEA-6259, MEA-6302

### 7. Lay out the P&L as a contribution waterfall, back-solve the contribution target from the profit goal, and look only above a missed line

**Why it works:** A P&L in the order net revenue, cost of delivery, gross margin (CM2), marketing, contribution margin (CM3), opex, EBITDA, then net profit shows where margin compresses. A miss always comes from the lines above it, so the waterfall turns "profit is down" into one or two named causes.

**How to apply:** Start from the owner's net goal. A 20% net target with opex at 15% needs 35% CM3; a $1M profit goal with $1M of opex needs $2M of contribution. If CM3 misses, look at marketing share or cost of delivery; if gross margin misses, look at delivery costs or the gross-to-net lines (refunds, discounts, shipping collected). Put loan interest below EBITDA, never in opex. A strong store runs cost of delivery at 30 to 40% of revenue and opex near 10 to 12%.

**Judge it by:** Each line shown as a share of net revenue with last month and the same month last year beside it. Weak work lists costs in the accountant's order and cannot say why profit fell.

**Breaks when:** The store keeps cash-basis books with lumpy stock purchases; switch to accrual above about $10M a year, or the shares mean little.

**From:** MEA-6270, MEA-6254, MEA-6291, MEA-6265, MEA-6328, MEA-6256, FIN-0016, MEA-6306, FIN-6436

### 8. Scale each channel on the return of the next dollar, not the average, and stop at the break-even frontier

**Why it works:** Averages hide the cost of growth. In one account 25% more spend ($80,000) moved the average cost per incremental customer from $337 to $392, still under a $400 limit, yet the extra money bought 76 customers at over $1,000 each. Valuing spare cash at the blended 4x instead of a marginal 2.7x makes ads look like the best use of every dollar.

**How to apply:** Add spend in steps (for example $10,000 a month) and read the change in new-customer revenue and contribution against the change in spend. Keep raising a channel until its marginal acquisition MER reaches break-even; add new channels only once the main ones sit there. During peak sales, write the rules beforehand: scale on a 3-day rolling read of marginal CM3, hold when flat, cut after two poor reads in a row. ROAS falling from 10x to 5x can be right if spend grew from $1,000 to $100,000 a month and net cash rose.

**Judge it by:** A marginal column next to the average in every scale recommendation. Weak work says "ROAS is 4x, so scale".

**Breaks when:** Spend steps are too small to read above noise (principle 17), or creative fatigue changes the curve mid-test.

**From:** MEA-6362, ADC-0049, MEA-6289, MEA-6279, ADC-6160, ADC-0050, ADC-0034

### 9. Read every platform on a named click window, Meta on 7-day click plus 1-day engaged view

**Why it works:** View-through credits anyone who scrolled past an ad and bought anyway: in one agency's audits 30 to 50% of reported Meta ROAS came from 1-day view. Defaults elsewhere run longer: Pinterest 1-day view and 30-day click, Snap 28-day swipe and 1-day view, Reddit 28-day click and 1-day view. Pinterest can claim a sale when any of 6 to 12 visible tiles was an ad.

**How to apply:** Set Meta columns to 7-day click and 7-day click plus 1-day engaged view, and TikTok and Pinterest to 7-day click. Around March or April 2026 Meta moved likes, comments, shares and profile clicks out of 7-day click into 1-day engaged view, so mark that date on trend charts and recheck windows each quarter. List every platform's window in one table and divide the sum of platform claims by store orders: that is the over-claim. If Meta says 17x on a 3x to 7x acquisition MER, compare its click-only and incremental columns.

**Judge it by:** Every figure names its window, on one basis across months, with an over-claim ratio. Weak work ranks channels on their own dashboards.

**Breaks when:** Sales run mostly through stores or Amazon, or a lift test gives the channel a factor that replaces the window debate.

**From:** MEA-0042, MEA-6320, MEA-6290, MEA-6346, MEA-6100, MEA-6101, MEA-6102, MEA-6310, MEA-6348

### 10. Treat email, SMS and win-back credit as claimed, not caused, and prove it with a random holdout

**Why it works:** Klaviyo's default credits an order to any email opened or clicked in the last 5 days, so the open window acts like view-through: a returning buyer who would have ordered anyway is credited to an email they glanced at. Sending more raises attributed revenue without adding sales.

**How to apply:** Set the window on purpose (about 3 days for impulse goods, near 5 for considered ones), exclude transactional emails, bot clicks and Apple privacy opens, and note the change date. Split attributed revenue into new and returning buyers. To prove value, split the list randomly in two, treat one half only (an extra SMS a week, a Meta win-back audience), keep all else equal, and read results from Shopify, not the channel. Cost per incremental win is spend divided by extra buyers; go always-on only if it is below a returning customer's gross profit. One agency finds about $1,000 of Meta spend enough for a win-back read.

**Judge it by:** Email shown as a share of store revenue, split new and returning, with any holdout named. Weak work sets "email at 30% of revenue" as the goal.

**Breaks when:** The list is too small for a split to show a difference (size it with principle 17).

**From:** EML-0056, EML-0013, EML-6386, EML-0036, WLD-0007

### 11. Strip existing customers out of prospecting and catalog results before trusting them

**Why it works:** A repeat buyer who clicks a reminder ad was usually going to reorder anyway, so prospecting that reaches customers looks great even on click-only reads. Catalog ads mostly reach bottom-funnel buyers: Meta's incremental view can halve them, 5x to 2.5x. One furniture brand had 57% of its Meta budget in a catalog ad set showing 7x that was 1.9x on 7-day click or incremental attribution, below every other ad set.

**How to apply:** Break the top-spending ads down by audience segment and attribution. Exclude existing customers (for example 180-day purchasers) from prospecting. Budget existing-customer ads in dollars: customers times monthly frequency times CPM divided by 1,000. For 300,000 customers at 3 a month and a $2.50 CPM that is about $2,250. Flag frequency on customers above about 8 a month. Treat catalog spend near 70% of budget as a red flag.

**Judge it by:** ROAS shown with and without existing customers for every campaign that touches them. Weak work moves budget into catalog ads because they "convert best".

**Breaks when:** Fashion with new drops every one to three weeks; one agency's inverse holdouts found spend on existing customers was incremental there.

**From:** MEA-6297, ADC-0010, ADC-6201, ADC-6196, ADC-6187, ADC-6190, MEA-6330, ADC-6280, ADC-0015, ADC-0026

### 12. Split brand from non-brand search, hold brand spend to a few percent, and expect non-brand ROAS to track acquisition MER

**Why it works:** Brand searches come from people who already know the store, often because Meta or creators made them aware; Google then takes the credit. Branded clicks inside smart-bid prospecting can cost about $4 against cents in their own capped campaign. One brand's holdouts said branded search should be under 1% of spend, and nearly every large fashion retailer one agency audited overspent on brand even with no rival bidding.

**How to apply:** Put exact-match brand negatives on every non-brand, Shopping and Performance Max campaign. Run brand in its own campaign with a CPC cap; keep it near 3 to 8% of Google spend. Read brand search volume against Meta spend before cutting Meta. Test a too-good Performance Max (20x with brand excluded) by adding about $1,000 and seeing whether anything like $20,000 of extra sales appears. If non-brand ROAS does not track acquisition MER, look for brand leakage, feed issues or Meta halo traffic.

**Judge it by:** Brand and non-brand reported apart, with brand share of spend stated. Weak work reports one Google ROAS.

**Breaks when:** A competitor bids hard on the brand name; then some brand defence pays, sized by a brand-off test in a few regions.

**From:** GAD-6184, GAD-0024, GAD-0027, ADC-0051, GAD-6223, MEA-6356, GAD-6142, MEA-6322

### 13. Rank every number on the evidence ladder and move big money only on the top two rungs

**Why it works:** Measurement runs from opinion, to platform ROAS (simple correlation), to marketing mix models, to quasi-experiments, to a geo holdout. Platform numbers are fast but least reliable; finance-grade numbers (acquisition MER, CAC, contribution) are slower but trustworthy; lift tests sit on top. Adding more attribution tools and events makes rival truths, not clarity.

**How to apply:** Tag each number in a report with its rung. Use platform ROAS for daily creative and ad set calls, finance-grade numbers for monthly budgets, lift results for big shifts. Run a post-purchase "how did you hear about us" question as a cheap second read: if Meta takes about 50% of spend, about 50% of first-time buyers' answers should name it. Agree a short ranked list of commercial metrics and decide from the top one. Do not use third-party multi-touch attribution for budgets; it often credits brand search as the first touch.

**Judge it by:** A visible rung label on each figure and agreement between two rungs before a verdict. Weak work adds a new attribution tool when numbers disagree.

**Breaks when:** Very small stores where survey counts are under about 100 a month; there read the survey quarterly.

**From:** MEA-6339, MEA-6315, MEA-6262, MEA-0045, MEA-6238, MEA-6298, MEA-6237

### 14. Match the measurement stack to the store's size, and spend about 1% of ad budget on it

**Why it works:** Lift tests and models need volume and money. Below a few million in revenue the noise swamps them, while basic hygiene fixes most of the error. Above eight figures, the cost of being wrong on a large budget dwarfs the cost of testing. About 80% of brands do not need formal incrementality testing.

**How to apply:** At $1M to $5M a year: 7-day click, cost per item tagged on every product, a daily new-customer and contribution dashboard, and existing customers excluded from prospecting. At $5M to $20M: add platform lift studies. Above $20M: run your own geo tests and apply incrementality factors to platform numbers. Budget about 1% of ad spend for tools; they pay for themselves if they make spend about 5% better. A paid attribution tool at $2,000 to $3,000 a month mostly reveals what basic hygiene would fix.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the store's stage and why each tool is in or out. Weak work proposes a geo-lift and a paid tool for a $1M store.

**Breaks when:** A smaller brand spends heavily offline or omnichannel; store openings and events distort online numbers, so testing pays earlier.

**From:** MEA-0041, MEA-6242, MEA-6308, MEA-6262, MEA-0036, MEA-6364, MEA-6311

### 15. Design geo-lift tests on matched region clusters, freeze everything else, and turn the result into a factor for platform numbers

**Why it works:** A geo test compares real store sales in regions that got the change against regions that did not, so it does not depend on any platform's tracking. Regions matched on how their ad spend, new-customer revenue and new-customer orders moved together predict each other well, so the gap is the effect.

**How to apply:** Cluster regions on those three histories and combine states or zones into groups. Change spend in one group (for example cut 50% for about 25 to 30 days) and compare it with the other group's prediction. Write the question, decision rule, freeze list (prices, emails, launches) and cost cap before launch. Incremental ROAS is revenue lift divided by extra spend: $40,000 extra driving $100,000 is 2.5. Divide incremental by reported ROAS to get a factor (4x reported, 2x incremental is 0.5) and apply it to daily platform reads. Use an open-source tool such as Meta's GeoLift for the maths. Test brand search inversely, switching it off in clustered states.

**Judge it by:** A pre-written rule, a size check and a factor carried into later reports. Weak work runs an unmatched before-and-after.

**Breaks when:** A country has few large regions; in Australia state tests need huge spend, so use commuting zones instead.

**From:** MEA-0050, MEA-6246, MEA-6317, MEA-0033, MEA-6325, MEA-6354, MEA-6301, ADC-6148

### 16. Treat in-platform lift results and Meta's incremental column as directional, and use them to pick creative, not to settle budgets

**Why it works:** Meta's incremental attribution setting is not a live holdout in the account; it applies factors from other advertisers' lift tests in the niche. In-platform lift tests do split users individually, but nobody outside can check the maths and they still rely on the platform's own tracking. TikTok and Google set high minimums, about $200,000 on TikTok.

**How to apply:** Use the incremental column to rank ads for iteration: sort by spend under that column and keep every concept above the brand's incremental ROAS floor, not only the top ad. One account read 1.42 ROAS standard against 1.23 incremental over 14 days on about $97,000. Use platform lift studies at the $5M to $20M stage, and confirm any big budget move with a geo test or a finance-grade read. Expect catalog and retargeting to shrink most on the incremental view.

**Judge it by:** Incremental figures labelled as modelled, with the decision they support kept small. Weak work treats the incremental column as proof.

**Breaks when:** The account is tiny or new; the niche factor then says little about this store.

**From:** MEA-6243, MEA-6309, MEA-6250, MEA-6304, ADC-0008, ADC-0006, ADC-0024

### 17. Know the noise band and size the test before calling any move a win or a loss

**Why it works:** Store numbers wobble on their own. A 5% dip in AOV plus a 4% dip in conversion compounds to about a 10% revenue drop that is usually normal noise, and below about $1,000 a day of Meta spend wild day-to-day ROAS swings are normal.

**How to apply:** Take recent normal weeks (no sale, launch or outage) and work out the usual week-to-week spread of orders, revenue and contribution; a change inside it is "no read". Size a split test first: visitors per variant is about 16 x p(1 - p) over the absolute lift squared (95% confidence, 80% power). At a 2% baseline a 10% relative lift needs about 80,700 visitors, or 1,600 conversions, per variant; halving the lift quadruples it. If that takes more than a few weeks of traffic, test a bigger change. Run tests at least one full week, ideally two; judge small slow ad accounts on 10 to 14 days.

**Judge it by:** The normal range sits beside each change, and each test shows its sample size and smallest lift worth shipping. Weak work calls a 6% one-week lift a result.

**Breaks when:** A clear break (site down, stock out, tracking broken) explains the move; then it is a fault, not noise, and needs fixing now.

**From:** MEA-0009, ADC-0064, MEA-6285, ADC-6209, MEA-0046, CRO-0005, CRO-0015

### 18. Credit pre-sale and upper-funnel spend with its delayed value, and set a separate target for sale-season buyers

**Why it works:** Shoppers who click in early November often wait for the sale. One agency sees the gap between 1-day and 30-day click revenue near 40% all year, above 80% in early November and near zero on Black Friday; one cookware brand's 1x real-time ROAS on 12 and 13 November became about 4x once value booked. Lift tests ending 3 or more weeks before Black Friday showed about 105% more lift after spend stopped, with 41% of value landing after the test window. Sale buyers are worth less: in a 10-brand sample November cohorts showed 38.5% six-month repeat lift against 63% for February.

**How to apply:** Pull last year's October to December results at 1, 7 and 30-day click and use the weekly gap as a multiplier on real-time pre-sale ROAS. Do not cut pre-sale spend that only looks weak. Set a higher acquisition MER target for November and December, net of their higher return rates.

**Judge it by:** A pre-sale read that shows both real-time and booked value. Weak work cuts spend in the second week of November.

**Breaks when:** Brands that do not discount or whose buyers do not wait; the multiplier must be measured per account.

**From:** MEA-6357, ADC-0046, MEA-6360, MEA-0034, MEA-6358, MEA-0035, MEA-6391

### 19. Accrue returns to the month that caused them and cost each return in full

**Why it works:** Shopify books returns on the refund date, so a big month's returns land in the next month and fake a dip; a January can show a false 20% return rate from December's orders. The returns line also understates: on a $150 order it showed about $22, but adding the original outbound label, the payment fee that is not refunded and unsellable stock made it about $34, turning $285,000 a year into $450,000.

**How to apply:** Accrue returns daily at the expected rate on that day's revenue (10% on a $1M December is about $3,333 a day) and reconcile monthly. Cost a return as return label plus original shipping plus unrefunded fee plus the share that cannot be resold. Adjust CAC for returns: a $200 order at 50% margin with a $50 CAC looks like 2.0, but a 50% return rate makes it 1.0. In fashion, keep raw and returns-adjusted acquisition MER side by side.

**Judge it by:** Returns shown by order month and a full cost per return. Weak work reads Shopify's refund-date returns as this month's performance.

**Breaks when:** Return rates are low and stable (under a few percent); a simple monthly figure is then good enough.

**From:** MEA-0011, MEA-0028, MEA-6276, MEA-6383, MEA-6393, MEA-6266, FIN-6243

### 20. Correct Shopify's and GA4's definition traps before any comparison, and judge the site on revenue or profit per visitor

**Why it works:** The default tiles mislead in known ways. Total sales includes tax and gross sales ignores discounts and returns. Dashboard AOV excludes shipping charged, which ran about 7% of revenue in one example. Returning customer rate is returning orders over all orders, so it rises when acquisition slows. Shopify sessions include bots that GA4 filters, so Shopify conversion runs low. Conversion rate falls as cold traffic scales (cold 1%, warm 4%, blended 2.8%).

**How to apply:** Measure cash collected as net sales plus shipping charges. Take sessions from GA4. Replace returning customer rate with cohort repeat rate. Judge the site on revenue per visitor (conversion rate times AOV including shipping) or gross profit per visitor, trended over about 2.5 years. Review revenue from gross to net every month, because accounting tools often show only net: a $10,000 rise may come only from fewer refunds.

**Judge it by:** A written definitions tab with each metric's formula and source. Weak work reports "conversion rate fell" while the store scaled cold traffic.

**Breaks when:** The store uses a non-Shopify platform; the traps differ, so check that platform's definitions before reuse.

**From:** MEA-0010, MEA-0013, MEA-0021, MEA-0012, MEA-6284, MEA-0015, MEA-6283, MEA-6263, MEA-6316

### 21. Diagnose a results drop top-down: prove it is real, split it into its drivers, then look only where the money is

**Why it works:** Most drops have a dull cause that a fixed order finds fast. Revenue equals sessions times conversion rate times AOV, so the split shows which lever moved. Google credits conversions to the click date, so the last 3 to 4 days always look weak. A campaign holding 10% of budget that doubles moves the account only about 5%.

**How to apply:** First check the drop against the seasonal norm and the noise band. Read change history over 90 to 180 days. Split the gap into order volume and AOV, then into new and returning: one $120,000 miss was $70,000 volume and $50,000 AOV, all from new customers. Check the store's own conversion, stock and site before blaming any ad account. Weight a cross-channel check by spend share; Google at 20 to 30% of spend is rarely the cause. Review only the top 5 campaigns by spend, using a conversion-time column on Google.

**Judge it by:** A one-screen answer naming the cause, the share of the drop it explains and the money at stake. Weak work lists ten possible causes with no shares.

**Breaks when:** Tracking itself broke; then reconcile to store orders first (principle 22) before reading any split.

**From:** MEA-0018, MEA-0051, MEA-6278, MEA-6381, GAD-6216, MEA-0009

### 22. Count each order once, with the same ID on every copy, and reconcile platforms to store orders by day

**Why it works:** Platforms optimise and report on what they are sent. Double counts teach bidding to chase ghosts; gaps make good ads look weak. GA4 removes duplicate purchases that share a transaction_id, so an empty ID collapses many orders into one. Meta deduplicates browser and server events only when one event_id travels on both. Two primary purchase actions in Google double count; GA4-imported purchases as the primary undercount by about 20% against the native tag.

**How to apply:** Give every purchase a unique transaction_id with value and currency. Send one event_id on the pixel and the server copy. Use one primary purchase action in Google Ads; keep the rest secondary. Remove any Meta test event code from published tags. Build a daily table of store orders against each platform's purchases, spot-check single orders by ID on both sides, and hold the rule that a platform can never exceed the store. Send server events within 7 days of the order; one stale event fails the whole batch.

**Judge it by:** A daily reconciliation with capture rate per platform and a named cause for any gap. Weak work says "the pixel fires".

**Breaks when:** Orders close off the store (marketplaces, phone or chat orders); count those as a separate source.

**From:** MEA-6223, MEA-0006, MEA-0017, MEA-6125, MEA-0005, MEA-6085, MEA-6224

### 23. Send platforms the real value of each order, and buy extra tracking only when its lift can beat the noise

**Why it works:** Bidding chases the value it is told about, and a sustainable bid is the store's unit economics: brands with higher gross profit and AOV win more auctions even with worse creative. Lost signal lowers the platform's estimate of conversion rate, but most tracking add-ons recover little. Meta's Conversions API Gateway only copies events after the browser pixel fires, so against blockers one practitioner expects about 1 to 2%, not Meta's quoted 13%.

**How to apply:** Send real order revenue per purchase, ideally margin-adjusted, not a fixed value. Turn on enhanced conversions, which hash email, name or phone before it leaves the site, and keep Google auto-tagging on. Use the platform's native CAPI integration first. Consider full server-side tagging only above about $50,000 a month of spend or when a few thousand dollars a month is easily affordable. Put effort into raising first-order profit (offers, upsells) before chasing match-quality scores.

**Judge it by:** The value sent per purchase matches store revenue for the same orders, and any tracking spend has a stated expected gain. Weak work sells server-side tracking as a growth lever to a small store.

**Breaks when:** Audiences with heavy ad-blocker use or custom checkouts lose large shares of events; there server-side work can pay.

**From:** MEA-0016, MEA-6126, MEA-6225, MEA-0001, MEA-6285, MEA-6286, ADC-0057, MEA-0046

### 24. Make consent and campaign tags part of the event contract, so lost and mislabelled traffic is designed out

**Why it works:** Under consent rules, tags behave differently before and after a visitor chooses. In Google's consent mode the basic setup sends nothing until consent, while the advanced setup sends cookieless pings that feed a store-specific conversion model. A pixel fired on page load before consent misses the landing page view after a visitor accepts. UTM values are case sensitive, and GA4 files a social visit as paid only when the medium matches its paid pattern.

**How to apply:** In the EEA, UK and Switzerland, use a consent banner connected to consent mode, and prefer the advanced setup where the store's lawyer agrees. Fire the Meta pixel on the consent update event. Write one UTM rule: source, medium and campaign always set, lower case, with each medium mapped to GA4's real channel rules. Rebuild checkout tracking with app or custom pixels after Shopify's Thank you page upgrade.

**Judge it by:** A consent test on a fresh browser and a UTM value list mapped to GA4 channels. Weak work shows paid social as organic because the medium said "social".

**Breaks when:** Markets without consent law; the banner design changes, but the UTM and event rules still hold. Recheck Google's consent rules yearly.

**From:** MEA-0007, MEA-6221, MEA-6220, MEA-6227, MEA-6226, Google Ads Help, About consent mode: https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/10000067

### 25. Judge a channel launched without a test with a counterfactual model, not a before-and-after

**Why it works:** Once a channel is live with no holdout, simple before-and-after comparisons blend in season, launches and other spend. A counterfactual model predicts what revenue would have done from spend, search seasonality and other drivers, then compares it with what happened. In one example YouTube's real lift was about $10,000 on $10,000 of spend, a 1x return.

**How to apply:** For one channel, use Google's open-source CausalImpact with ad spend, keyword seasonality, influencer spend and similar inputs. For many channels at once and a store with two or more years of weekly data, a marketing mix model such as Google's open-source Meridian can split credit, using geo-level data where it exists and priors from the brand's own knowledge. Calibrate any model with lift test results where you have them, and treat its output as the third rung of the ladder, below a holdout.

**Judge it by:** The model's inputs, period and uncertainty are written down, and its verdict is checked against a finance-grade read. Weak work presents model output as fact.

**Breaks when:** The store has under about a year of steady data or changes many things at once; the model then cannot separate causes.

**From:** MEA-6249, MEA-6323, MEA-6339, Google Meridian: https://developers.google.com/meridian/docs/basics/meridian-introduction

### 26. Report profit beside cash, keep a 13-week cash forecast with a floor, and fix a cash gap with supplier timing before cutting ads

**Why it works:** A store showing $2M of profit that put $3M into stock that has not sold is about $1M short of cash. Growth soaks profit into stock, receivables, tax and interest: one brand growing 40% lifted EBITDA from $910,000 to $1.27M and still ended $200,000 shorter on cash. Payouts are net of fees, refunds, disputes and reserves, and US Shopify Payments settles in at least 3 business days.

**How to apply:** Build a rolling 13-week forecast from cash movements, not revenue, rolled weekly, with a minimum cash line the store must never cross. Time receipts by measured payout lag. Run bad, base and upside cases. When a week breaches the floor, move or split the purchase order or ask for longer terms first, then a short facility draw, and cut ad spend last. Pay-on-delivery cash timing goes to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).

**Judge it by:** The lowest cash week is named on the first screen with its fix. Weak work shows only P&L profit.

**Breaks when:** A very small store with no stock bets and daily payouts; a monthly cash check is then enough.

**From:** MEA-6253, MEA-6312, FIN-6426, FIN-6431, MEA-6280, FIN-6432, FIN-6241, FIN-6240

### 27. Price every funding source as an annual rate and treat CAC payback as a financing decision

**Why it works:** Flat fees hide their cost. A revenue-based offer at 6% flat repaid over four months costs about 36% a year, more than double a 15 to 16% bank loan, because the average balance is about half the amount borrowed. Stretching CAC payback from 3 to 7 months can make the capital a store needs five to six times bigger.

**How to apply:** Convert each offer to an annual rate: flat fee divided by (average balance times years). Repay the most expensive debt first; paying down $100,000 of 36% debt saves about $36,000 a year. Work out the cash conversion cycle as inventory days plus receivable days minus payable days: at 182 plus 5 minus 45, the store pays for a jacket 142 days before the customer pays. Use net working capital as a share of revenue to price growth: at 16%, adding $10M of sales needs about $1.6M of cash. Rank each spare $100,000 on return, time to cash and confidence across ads, stock, people, site and debt.

**Judge it by:** Every funding option shown as an annual rate with its repayment schedule in the cash forecast. Weak work calls a 6% fee "cheap".

**Breaks when:** Supplier terms or consignment already fund stock; the payback limit then loosens.

**From:** MEA-6268, FIN-6435, MEA-6271, FIN-6434, MEA-6272, MEA-6275, FIN-6430

### 28. Measure stock like ad spend: rank products on gross margin per dollar of stock, and use paid media to turn slow stock back into cash

**Why it works:** Stock is money waiting to come back. GMROI, gross margin dollars divided by inventory cost, is the ROAS of the warehouse; one example brand earned 3.26 per dollar of stock. Profit-ranked campaigns starve slow stock, which then ties up cash for hundreds of days, while pouring spend into a product already selling fast just runs it out: in one example putting 90% of $100,000 on the viral item cut its contribution from $500,000 to $410,000.

**How to apply:** Grade stock A to D by sell-through and days of cover. Track GMROI and turns (cost of goods divided by inventory) per product. Pace ads on fast sellers to their stock. Push grade C and D stock through its own funnel or a new market at break-even or a small loss. When unsold stock and rising MER hit together, a short lift in marketing share (for example 30% to 35%) aimed at that stock can restore cash faster than a 40% media cut.

**Judge it by:** A push, fix or cut list ranked on profit per dollar of cash tied up. Weak work ranks products on revenue.

**Breaks when:** Made-to-order or print-on-demand stores hold no stock; GMROI then adds little.

**From:** MEA-6277, MEA-6385, MEA-6329, FIN-0026, ADC-6199, ADC-6288, MEA-6253

### 29. Run a separate P&L per sales channel and per country, down to contribution after acquisition

**Why it works:** Averages across channels and markets hide where money is made. In one example wholesale earned 10% contribution on 60-day terms against DTC's 22.5% paid in days. A higher-AOV country can be less profitable once variable costs land: US orders carried $43 of variable cost against $28 in Australia. About 20% of the value Meta creates for a brand also on Amazon lands on Amazon, not the site.

**How to apply:** Build one column per channel (DTC, wholesale, marketplace, retail), not per ad platform, with cash timing beside contribution. For countries, add one column each with rows down to profit contribution: revenue per session, product cost, carrier and fulfilment fees, payment fees (about 2.5 to 3.5% domestic, 4 to 5% with currency conversion, 5 to 6.5% where buy now pay later and conversion stack up), and ad spend by location. Show two contributions per country: after blended CAC and after new-customer CAC. Allow for reversed seasons and local return habits.

**Judge it by:** Each market and channel shows its own contribution and cash timing. Weak work cuts a lower-AOV market on revenue alone.

**Breaks when:** A channel or country is under a few percent of orders; group it as "other" until it has volume.

**From:** MEA-6274, MEA-6376, MEA-6374, MEA-6375, MEA-6380, MEA-6377, MEA-0029, MEA-6379

### 30. Forecast new and returning revenue from different drivers, and never annualise one month

**Why it works:** New-customer revenue depends on spend and its falling marginal return; returning revenue depends on the size and age of past cohorts. Forecasting both as one growth rate misses each. Cohort decay alone forecasts returning revenue within about 10%, and close to 1 to 2% with seasonality and planned events added. One record month times 12 overstates the year.

**How to apply:** For new customers, plot a year of daily acquisition MER against spend, remove sale days and very low-spend days, fit a curve, and read the revenue each spend level should return; floor CAC by lowest sensible CPC divided by best realistic conversion rate ($0.70 at 3% is about $23). Plan for CPMs rising about 20 to 30% a year. For returning customers, multiply each past cohort's size by its repeat rate at its age and by returning AOV. Show low, base and high, and the gap to the owner's target.

**Judge it by:** Two separate forecast lines with their inputs, and the target gap in dollars. Weak work forecasts "+15% month on month".

**Breaks when:** A new store has under a year of history; use first-order maths and a short-window range instead.

**From:** MEA-6264, MEA-6299, MEA-6258, MEA-6300, MEA-6368, MEA-6331, MEA-6230

## Working from the org context

- Read what the brand sells and how buyers repeat before choosing a lifetime window: consumables get 90 to 180 days, one-off goods 30 days, furniture first-order only.
- Take the brand's offers and discount habits from the brain and price each one through break-even ROAS before using any target it already runs.
- Note what ran and won, and check each claimed winner against its attribution window and new-customer share; a past winner on 1-day view is a claim to recheck, not a fact.
- Pull the brand's markets, sales channels and stores from the brain, because offline stores, Amazon and wholesale change what online numbers mean.
- Keep the brand's own metric names and reporting calendar where they exist, and add a one-line definition beside each so nothing changes meaning between months.
- Keep the owner's stated goal (profit, cash, growth) as the unit every option is sized in; if the brain shows no goal, state the one assumed in one line.
- Where the brand's current practice is weaker (blended MER as the KPI, revenue LTV, default windows), keep reporting the old number for one cycle beside the new one, show the gap in dollars, and propose the switch with the reason.
- Add any new definition, cost line or test factor back to the org context so the next job starts from it.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Gross profit per order is on a delivered basis, with every cost line, its source and date shown.
- [ ] Break-even ROAS and CAC are recomputed for each offer or discount depth in the work.
- [ ] Paid media is judged on acquisition MER and cost per new customer, with blended MER only as a control.
- [ ] The CAC ceiling comes from new customers' first-order gross profit, not store-wide AOV.
- [ ] Every LTV figure names its window, uses gross profit and uses only closed cohort windows.
- [ ] First-time and returning contribution are shown separately.
- [ ] Every platform number names its attribution window, and Meta uses 7-day click or click plus engaged view.
- [ ] No ROAS is compared across platforms without like-for-like windows and a store-order check.
- [ ] Email and SMS revenue is split new and returning and never read as incremental without a random holdout.
- [ ] Existing customers and catalog ads are shown with and without existing-customer credit.
- [ ] Brand and non-brand search are reported apart.
- [ ] Each budget call uses the marginal return of the next step, not the average.
- [ ] Each figure carries its evidence rung, and big moves rest on two agreeing reads.
- [ ] Changes are read against the store's normal noise band before being called wins or losses.
- [ ] Returns are accrued to the order month and costed in full.
- [ ] Shopify and GA4 definition traps are corrected in writing before comparisons.
- [ ] Platform purchases are reconciled to store orders by day, and no platform exceeds the store.
- [ ] Any test has its decision rule, size check and cost cap written before launch.
- [ ] Cash sits beside profit, with the lowest week in the 13-week forecast named.
- [ ] Every funding option is shown as an annual rate.

## Common failures

- Using product margin from the supplier price as gross margin. Fix: build delivered gross profit per order with every cost line before any target.
- Celebrating a rising blended MER while new-customer revenue per ad dollar falls. Fix: make acquisition MER and cost per new customer the headline.
- Quoting LTV as all-time revenue or from cohorts too young to have lived it. Fix: gross profit over a named closed window, observed only.
- Reporting Meta on the default window with 1-day view. Fix: 7-day click or click plus engaged view, with the window named on every figure.
- Moving budget into catalog, retargeting or brand search because they show the highest ROAS. Fix: strip existing customers, split brand, and read the incremental column or a holdout.
- Scaling on average ROAS. Fix: read the marginal return of each spend step and stop at break-even.
- Cutting pre-sale spend in early November because real-time ROAS looks weak. Fix: apply last year's 1 to 30-day click gap and read booked value.
- Reading Shopify's refund-date returns and returning customer rate at face value. Fix: accrue returns to the order month and use cohort repeat rates.
- Proposing a paid attribution tool, server-side tracking or a geo test for a small store. Fix: match the stack to revenue stage and fix basic hygiene first.
- Declaring a win from one week's small change. Fix: show the normal range and size each test from the smallest lift worth shipping.
- Setting email's goal as a share of Klaviyo-attributed revenue. Fix: split new and returning and prove value with a random holdout.
- Reporting profit with no cash view, then cutting ads when cash runs short. Fix: a 13-week cash forecast with a floor, fixed through supplier timing first.
