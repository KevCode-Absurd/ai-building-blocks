# Offers and pricing: craft principles

This book is for everyone who sets prices and builds offers for an online store: offer strategists, merchandisers, retention leads, wholesale sellers and growth leads. Read it before the job, write the offer to it, and run the review checklist on your draft before you hand it over. Every principle here is about one thing: getting more buyers to say yes, and to buy more, while the store keeps its margin.

## What great looks like

- Every offer shows its contribution per order and its new break-even ROAS before anyone sees the creative.
- The winner is picked on contribution per session, not on order value or conversion rate alone.
- The offer leads with value (pack, gift, guarantee, result timeline), and a plain discount is the last resort, not the first idea.
- Thresholds and bundle tiers are set from the store's own order value distribution and shipping costs, never from the average order value.
- Every number a buyer sees is true: the was price was really charged, the deadline really ends, the total charge is shown before payment.
- New buyers, subscribers and returning buyers each get an offer built for them, and no offer undercuts another.
- The offer answers the buyer's top objection in the buyer's own words, at the point where they decide.
- Each recommendation says what it does to gross profit per month, before and after.

## The principles

### 1. Price every offer on delivered contribution per order, and recompute break-even ROAS and CAC before it goes live

**Why it works:** A discount comes out of gross margin, so the profit left for ads shrinks faster than the price. Break-even ROAS is 1 divided by gross margin; break-even CAC is gross margin in dollars. A $100 item costing $60 to deliver breaks even at 2.5 ROAS; at 30% off it keeps $10 and needs about 7.

**How to apply:** Build the cost to the buyer's door: landed cost, packing, shipping, payment and marketplace fees, and a returns and discount allowance. For each offer, write contribution, break-even ROAS and break-even CAC. A $100 tee bought for $30 looks like 70% margin and was 24% once freight, shipping, fees and a 10% refund allowance were in. In fashion, set account targets on blended margin after markdowns. Hand the new targets to the media buyer the day the offer goes live.

**Judge it by:** Each offer has its own row with delivered cost, contribution and break-even ROAS. Weak work quotes product margin or reuses last month's target.

**Breaks when:** The goal is turning dead stock into cash, or the offer goes only to existing customers by email.

**From:** OFF-6633, OFF-6615, OFF-0065, OFF-0016, MEA-0037, MEA-6251, MEA-6361, MEA-6384

### 2. Pick the winning offer on contribution per session, never on AOV or conversion rate alone

**Why it works:** Conversion rate ignores how much each order is worth, and AOV ignores how many visitors buy. A bigger default pack can lift AOV and still lose profit; free shipping can double sales and still lose more money. Only profit per visitor joins both sides.

**How to apply:** For each offer variant, compute (orders x contribution per order) divided by sessions. Use the same traffic source and the same dates. If the offer changes repeat buying (subscriptions, refills), add expected 90-day repeat contribution, and say so. In one case a $14 sock on paid shipping got about 2% conversion; free shipping doubled sales but profit fell; a 4-pack at about $50 AOV moved the same ad spend into profit.

**Judge it by:** The result table has one profit-per-session column and the call follows it. One agency expects a good pack change to dip conversion slightly and lift revenue per session 10 to 20%. Weak work celebrates an AOV jump with no per-session line.

**Breaks when:** Traffic is so low that per-session numbers swing week to week; then use a longer window and a before and after read, and say so.

**From:** OFF-0015, CRO-6607, OFF-6613, OFF-0033

### 3. Build the offer in five parts and lead with value; a discount only touches one part and is usually the weakest

**Why it works:** Buyers judge an offer by how close it feels to the seller's cost. Adding things that cost the store little (a gift, a longer guarantee, a bigger pack funded by shipping savings) makes the deal feel near cost while the brand keeps margin. A discount gives away margin directly.

**How to apply:** Write the offer in five parts: product positioning, price architecture, value mechanic (bundle, gift, credit), risk reversal and urgency. Fill each part before you touch price. Look for what the business already does but never says: free returns, fast dispatch, a repair service. In one example, four of five offer upgrades already existed. Positioning alone can beat a discount: in one case the same bundle, renamed a New Year's reset at full price, converted better than it ever had on sale.

**Judge it by:** Each part is filled in and named. Weak work calls "15% off" an offer.

**Breaks when:** The product is a commodity bought on price comparison (shopping feeds, marketplaces), where buyers ignore added value.

**From:** OFF-6631, OFF-6677, OFF-6681, OFF-6650, OFF-6624, OFF-6651

### 4. Never run a 5 to 10% discount by habit; model units per order, because profit per order peaks where basket size jumps and falls past 30 to 40%

**Why it works:** Small discounts do not change how many units people buy, so they hand margin to buyers who would have paid full price. Deeper discounts can lift units per order, but the ROAS needed to hold the same profit per order rises steeply, not in a straight line.

**How to apply:** From past sales, chart discount depth against units per order. Find where units per order starts to rise; that is the only zone worth testing. Then work the target: at $100 AOV and $40 cost, a 25% sale that lifts units per order from 1 to 1.63 raises AOV to $122, but needs about 9 ROAS, not 6, to keep $43 profit per order. If the media buyer cannot reach that, the sale loses money even when revenue climbs.

**Judge it by:** The draft shows units per order and required ROAS at each depth tested. Weak work picks 10% because "it is standard".

**Breaks when:** The product is a single-unit purchase (a sofa, a mattress), where units per order cannot rise and any discount is pure margin.

**From:** OFF-6648, OFF-0014, OFF-6642, OFF-6689, OFF-6632, OFF-6678

### 5. Keep straight sitewide discounts to one or two a year, on high-margin SKUs or slow stock, because a year of rotating promos moves margin down for good

**Why it works:** Constant promotions teach buyers to wait. One example drifted from 60% to about 45% gross margin, and a 25% sale on $100 AOV cut gross profit from $60 to $35. A brand that trained buyers on 70 to 80% off saw orders fall hard when it cut to 25 to 30%.

**How to apply:** List every promotion in the last 12 months and count the weeks with a live code. Propose at most one or two straight sales a year, aimed at very high-margin SKUs or grade C stock. Fill the other weeks with value offers (gifts, bundles, launches). Before scaling any discount, check cohort LTV by discount code; at about 60% of one agency's brands, deep-discount cohorts were markedly worse.

**Judge it by:** A promo calendar with sale weeks counted and margin per week shown. Weak work adds another sale to rescue a slow month.

**Breaks when:** The brand is off-price or value-led, where low price is the promise and the cost base is built for it.

**From:** OFF-6645, OFF-6640, OFF-0008, OFF-6659, OFF-6670, CXP-0016, MEA-6365

### 6. Set a new product's price upward from a contribution target, never with keystone or a fixed markup

**Why it works:** A 2x markup ignores what it costs to win a buyer online. Paid acquisition, shipping, returns and discounts can eat several times the product cost, so a keystone price often cannot fund a single ad.

**How to apply:** Start with the contribution target per order (say 20% of price). Add expected CAC, shipping and fees, return and discount allowances, then COGS. Solve for the price. In one worked case a $6 product needed about $64 to $70, against $12 at keystone. Then check the market: if no rival or substitute supports that price, cut or rework the product rather than launch it at a loss. About 40% of new brands use keystone, by one agency's count.

**Judge it by:** The price sheet shows each cost line above COGS and a named contribution target. Weak work prices "a bit under the competitor" with no CAC line.

**Breaks when:** The product sells mostly through organic, wholesale or repeat channels with little CAC, or it is a deliberate entry product whose profit comes from what buyers order next.

**From:** OFF-6617, OFF-6636, FIN-0012

### 7. Before any price change, work out the break-even volume: a rise survives a drop in units of rise divided by (rise plus contribution margin)

**Why it works:** Price is the strongest profit lever because every extra dollar goes straight to contribution. At 47.5% contribution margin, a 3% rise still makes more profit unless units fall over 5.9%; a 10% rise survives about a 17% fall. The same maths shows a 20% cut needs about 73% more volume just to stand still.

**How to apply:** Get contribution margin from the profit sheet, not gross margin. Compute the break-even volume change for each proposed move. Compare it with what the store knows about demand: rival prices, past sales when prices moved, how unique the product is. A Canadian coffee brand facing a cost rise can show the owner "we can lose 9% of units and still be ahead" before deciding.

**Judge it by:** Every price move carries its break-even volume number in plain words. Weak work says "a price rise may hurt conversion" with no threshold.

**Breaks when:** Close substitutes sit one click away at a lower price (identical items on marketplaces), where volume can fall far beyond the line.

**From:** OFF-6619, MEA-6286, ADC-0057

### 8. Raise prices for new buyers first, lock active subscribers at their price, and warn your best buyers before the public

**Why it works:** A rise hurts most with people who know the old price. A price locked while a subscriber never cancels becomes a switching cost that grows with each rise, and reads as fair; BarkBox kept 2012 subscribers on 2012 prices. Real news of a coming change, told to loyal buyers first, pulls orders forward (Robert Cialdini's Influence).

**How to apply:** Run the principle 7 check first. Raise the price for new buyers; lock active subscribers and state that cancelling loses the rate. Tell the VIP segment first, with the real date and a short window, then announce. If a supplier cost drives the rise, pass on only the share of price that input drives: if $700 of a $1,000 price tracks an index, a 5.2% rise adds $36.40, not $52.

**Judge it by:** The plan shows the new price, locked cohorts and their margin, the notice date and the cost basis. Weak work raises everyone overnight, or blames "rising costs" with no numbers.

**Breaks when:** Costs climb fast on thin margins, so locked cohorts lose money; then end the lock on a set date.

**From:** OFF-0063, COG-6367, OFF-6619, and Robert Cialdini's Influence (named in words only)

### 9. Test prices by market, by time window or with a new variant, never two prices for one product to different visitors at once

**Why it works:** Buyers who find they paid more than someone else for the same item feel cheated; Amazon refunded 6,896 customers after a 2000 DVD price test. Price effects are also slow and noisy, so short split tests mislead.

**How to apply:** Choose one of four designs: split-test the offer structure (packs, tiers, bundles), a timed switch with a comparison group of untouched products, a market-by-market price, or a new variant at a new price. Run at least four weeks, in whole weeks. Judge on contribution per session, with refunds and complaints as guardrails. Revenue per visitor needs about six times the traffic of a conversion test, so stores under about 300 orders a month should test only big swings.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the design, the run time from real order volume and the profit line to beat. Weak work proposes "A/B test $39 vs $45".

**Breaks when:** The store has under about 30 orders a month; there, research buyers and competitors instead of testing.

**From:** CRO-0009, CRO-6608, CRO-0014, CRO-0011, CRO-0019

### 10. Fund bundle savings from the shipping and fulfilment you save by sending units together, and bundle more of the same SKU first

**Why it works:** Fixed per-order costs do not double with units. In one example shipping was $12 for one unit, $14 for two and $17 for three, so a three-pack saves about $19. Give part of that to the buyer and margin stays flat while order value rises. Same-SKU packs are simpler to read and to ship than mixed kits.

**How to apply:** Get cost per order at one, two, three and five units from the 3PL or carrier invoice. Set each pack's saving at or under the cost it removes. A $136 three-step routine sold at $122 kept margin flat because separate orders would cost about $14 more. Show deals as "buy 2, get 1 free", not "33% off 3". On Black Friday, buy one get one beat 50% off in one model ($69 versus $29 contribution) because the second unit cost little.

**Judge it by:** Each tier's saving sits beside the shipping it saves. Weak work picks "10%, 15%, 20%" with no cost basis.

**Breaks when:** Items are bulky or ship from different warehouses, so each unit adds near full shipping; or nobody needs more than one.

**From:** OFF-6634, OFF-6621, OFF-6616, OFF-0016, OFF-0030, OFF-0066

### 11. Offer three pack tiers, default the middle one, space the savings evenly and put the dollar saving in each tier's title

**Why it works:** Too many choices stall the decision; a pre-selected middle tier sets the norm; equal saving steps make it feel like the smart pick. Shoppers do not do maths on the page.

**How to apply:** Cut the picker to three options. Pre-select the middle tier and name only that tier in the ads. Make the step from one to three save as much as three to five. Write the saving in dollars in the option title (3-pack, save $20), not as a small percentage below. Tie each tier to a use, such as covering 700 sq ft versus 1,400 sq ft. Reported results: five options cut to three lifted conversion 53% and AOV 13.5%; equal $20 steps lifted conversion 11.7%; dollar savings in titles lifted revenue per visitor about 28%.

**Judge it by:** Three tiers, a default, equal steps, savings in titles. Weak work shows five tiers with percentages in grey text.

**Breaks when:** Margins cannot absorb a bigger middle saving, or one unit fully serves any buyer. Never inflate a compare-at price to make the steps look bigger.

**From:** LPG-0006, OFF-0049, OFF-0048, LPG-0021, LPG-0035, OFF-0018, OFF-0027, LPG-6618

### 12. Match the pack size to how long the product takes to work, and land any bonus on the second order

**Why it works:** A buyer who gets 30 days of a product that needs 90 is set up to fail, then refunds. Selling the pack that covers the result window protects outcomes; a second-order bonus protects first-order margin and pulls buyers to month six.

**How to apply:** From the product facts in the brief, find when results show. Lead with that supply (a 90-day pack) and say plainly that a 30-day pack is too short. For a benefit seen at about 120 days, sell the 90-day subscription and add one free unit in the second order, about 33% off that order. Carry the same logic into ads: the call to action names the timeline and the guarantee comes right before the ask.

**Judge it by:** The lead pack matches a stated result time, and the page or ad says why. Weak work defaults to "1 bottle" because it has the lowest price.

**Breaks when:** The product works on first use, or the longer pack is too big a jump for a first-time buyer; then test a smaller starter. Never claim a result timeline the evidence does not support.

**From:** OFF-6641, OFF-6698, OFF-6655, MCR-6616

### 13. Set free-shipping and gift thresholds from the peaks of the order value distribution, not the mean, and keep charged fees under about 10% of the order

**Why it works:** Orders often cluster in two humps. With peaks at $70 and $130 and a $100 mean, free shipping at $110 gifts margin to big spenders and is out of reach for small ones; it may move only about 10% of buyers. One agency estimates over 80% of brands set thresholds this way.

**How to apply:** Plot a histogram of order values for 90 days. Place the threshold just above the peak you want to lift (move an $80 peak toward $100). Check each threshold still clears margin after the perk. Stack a ladder if the range allows: True Classic used free shipping at $100, a shirt at $150 and joggers at $200 and its AOV rose from $36 to $112. Extra checkout costs under about 10% of AOV barely moved conversion in one operator's tests, so low-ticket orders can carry a fee.

**Judge it by:** The histogram and chosen peak are in the notes. Weak work sets "AOV plus 15%".

**Breaks when:** It is a single-product store with one price, or most orders are one high-priced item well above any threshold.

**From:** OFF-6646, OFF-6694, OFF-0013, OFF-6614, LPG-0072, OFF-6623, CRO-6180

### 14. Choose a gift with high perceived value, low landed cost and a real link to using the product, and list it for sale so its value is credible

**Why it works:** A gift adds to the deal without cutting the price, so margin holds. Two $50 towels plus a $4 bag earned $53.50 contribution against $26.50 for one towel, at almost the same break-even ROAS (1.87 versus 1.89). A gift that helps people use the product also cuts the reasons they cancel.

**How to apply:** Shortlist items costing $0 to $5 landed: samples, accessories that fix a use problem (a frother, a scoop), or a partner's product. Avoid more of the same product as a subscription gift; it drives skips. Sell the gift at a real price so "worth $30" is true. A gift pays when it cuts CAC by more than its cost.

**Judge it by:** Gift cost, listed price and link to use are stated. Weak work offers a tote bag "worth $25" that was never on sale.

**Breaks when:** The gift is bulky enough to raise the shipping tier, or buyers do not value it. In the US, a "free" gift must not be funded by raising the paid item's regular price.

**From:** OFF-6638, OFF-6625, OFF-0039, OFF-0032, OFF-6673, OFF-0073, OFF-6500

### 15. Build the guarantee from the buyer's top fear, size it on claim cost versus the lift it must buy, and match its length to how long the product takes

**Why it works:** A guarantee only helps if it answers the risk the buyer actually fears: will it work, last or fit. A long guarantee on low-ticket consumables can bring fewer claims than a short one, because buyers stop watching the deadline.

**How to apply:** Find the top fear in survey answers, chats and reviews. Put the answer on the tier you want to sell: if buyers fear towels wear out, a lifetime guarantee only on the two-pack lifts conversion and order size together. Cost it: claim rate x full cost per claim (shipping both ways, fees, unit) against the extra orders needed. Match length to results, such as 90 days for a 90-day hair cycle. In the US, "money back" means a full refund on request, and "lifetime" must say whose lifetime.

**Judge it by:** The fear, cost per order and break-even lift are written down. Weak work pastes a generic 30-day promise.

**Breaks when:** The top objection is price itself, returns are about fit, or claim rates would wipe out the margin; prefer repair-first terms on durables.

**From:** OFF-6649, OFF-6690, OFF-6653, OFF-0060, OFF-6501, OFF-6502, OFF-0064

### 16. Rank upsells by take rate x added value x margin, fill each slot with one offer, and run post-purchase offers from high to low

**Why it works:** A 10% take on a $50 add-on at 50% margin adds $2.50 per order, over 10% more profit on a $20 first order. Take rate alone favours cheap items. After payment the card is on file, so one-click offers take far better.

**How to apply:** Keep the best-scoring add-on per slot, readable in 2 to 3 seconds. Expect about 5 to 15% take in cart and 25 to 40% one-click post-purchase. At checkout, skip extra products; $4.99 shipping protection takes about 30% at near full margin. Post-purchase, sell more of the same or a fix for the problem the first product creates: open at $50 to $60, downsell to $20 to $30, then $30 to $40 after a yes. Aim for post-purchase revenue per visit near 10 to 15% of AOV.

**Judge it by:** A scored table per slot, main conversion tracked too. Weak work ranks by AOV lift.

**Breaks when:** The upsell lowers main conversion. Never pre-tick a paid add-on: EU law needs express consent for extra payments.

**From:** OFF-6686, OFF-0011, OFF-0010, OFF-6679, OFF-6676, OFF-6693, OFF-0052, OFF-6682, Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU, Article 22, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/83/oj

### 17. Front-load the subscription offer on the first order and make subscribe the default, but watch what it does to CAC

**Why it works:** Buyers who start on a subscription are worth far more: one supplement brand saw about 50% higher 6-month LTV ($195 versus $130), about 2x at 12 months. A bigger first-order saving pulls the decision forward; a smaller recurring one protects margin later.

**How to apply:** Offer about 25 to 30% off the first subscription order and 5 to 10% after, not a flat 15% forever. Or gate a better lifetime rate (25% versus 10%) behind the 90-day supply with subscribe ticked. Show three options, two of them subscriptions, with subscribe pre-selected and no discount on one-time. Set the max CAC from subscription LTV on margin, not revenue: one case allowed $62 instead of $40. Make the pop-up offer the subscription offer, or a 10% one-time code will undercut a 20% subscription.

**Judge it by:** First and recurring discounts differ, and CAC is tracked against the new LTV. Weak work offers "subscribe and save 10%" in small print.

**Breaks when:** Forcing subscription makes one-unit buyers bounce and CAC climbs; then bring back a visible one-time option. It does not fit products without a natural repeat cycle.

**From:** OFF-0029, OFF-6683, OFF-0041, OFF-0038, OFF-0075, OFF-0037

### 18. Match subscription cadence to real use, and never let a public sale make one-time cheaper than subscribing

**Why it works:** Too much product is the most common reason people cancel; a 30-day cadence on a product not used daily guarantees churn. And when a sitewide sale beats the subscription price, subscribers cancel to buy the public deal.

**How to apply:** Check servings per pack against how often the buyer really uses it (reviews, support chats, reorder gaps). Set the default cadence to that, with easy skip or delay up to 30 days. Before any sale, compare the sale price with the subscribe price; Gorilla Mind's sale made one-time cheaper, which misleads buyers. Give active subscribers a better members' offer, such as 50% off add-ons when the site has 40% off, and keep them out of sale campaigns. One brand's campaigns caused more cancels than starts until it excluded subscribers.

**Judge it by:** Cadence is backed by a usage figure, and the sale plan has a subscriber line. Weak work sends the Black Friday email to everyone.

**Breaks when:** The dose is fixed and daily, so cadence already fits; or the brand accepts subscriber churn as the price of a cash event.

**From:** OFF-0040, OFF-6612, OFF-0043, EML-0001, CXP-0005

### 19. Push prepaid or quarterly plans only when CAC rises less than order value, and always show the full charge before payment

**Why it works:** A bigger first charge lifts order value but makes the first yes harder. IM8's quarterly plans took about 40% of buyers and lifted first order value from $142 to $212, yet 6-month gross profit to CAC fell from 2.0 to about 1.03. A monthly price that becomes a quarterly total in the cart lifts take rate but drives exits and legal risk.

**How to apply:** Test the prepaid plan in its own funnel and judge it on 6-month gross profit per customer against CAC. Show the total and its timing on the product page ("pay $235 today for 3 months"). In the US, online subscriptions need clear terms before billing details, express consent and a simple way to stop. In one model, prepaid plans at 25 to 30% off lifted 12-month orders per customer from 1.2 to over 5.

**Judge it by:** The page states total, timing and how to cancel. Weak work shows "$78/month" on a plan that charges $235 at checkout.

**Breaks when:** Buyers want to try before committing, there is no proof yet, or the brand lacks cash to carry first-order losses.

**From:** OFF-0071, OFF-6609, OFF-0072, LPG-6622, OFF-6629, OFF-6304, OFF-0067

### 20. Show the saving as whichever number looks bigger: dollars on high-ticket items, percent on low-ticket ones

**Why it works:** Buyers react to the size of the number, not its meaning. "$178 saved" beats "10% off" on a high-ticket item; "61% off" beats "save $40" on a cheap one. Prices just under a round number also read as smaller because people encode the left digit first, and the effect is strongest when that digit changes ($2.99 against $3.00).

**How to apply:** Above about $100 AOV, lead with dollars ("$25 off"); below, lead with percent. Reframing a welcome pop-up as "$25 credit" instead of a free gift tripled opt-ins and lifted placed-order rate from 3.3% to 10.7% on a $250 to $300 AOV brand. Test a price just under a round number when the left digit changes; skip it when it does not (say $47 versus $46.99).

**Judge it by:** The frame matches the price point and is used the same way on page, cart and email. Weak work writes "10% off" on a $400 product.

**Breaks when:** The brand is premium and visible discounting erodes its price image, or AOV sits near $100 where neither frame reads bigger.

**From:** OFF-0017, OFF-0026, OFF-0028, LPG-0008, Thomas and Morwitz, "Penny Wise and Pound Foolish", Journal of Consumer Research 2005, https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/v32y2005i1p54-64.html

### 21. Anchor the price to the buyer's alternative, the daily cost and a premium tier, instead of to your own was price

**Why it works:** Buyers judge price against something. "Was $89, now $69" anchors on the store's own number, which buyers distrust. The specialist visit they would otherwise pay for, or the cost per day, makes the price feel small. A costly top tier makes the middle look reasonable; adding a clearly worse option can shift choice to the target, an effect first shown by Huber, Payne and Puto in 1982.

**How to apply:** Write four price lines and pick the strongest: against the alternative ("a $200 specialist visit"), per day ("73 cents a day"), the cost of the problem, or low effort. On tiered ranges, show per-serving prices and place a premium tier above the one you want sold, as IM8 does with its top stack. If price is the main objection on a premium product, state it outright.

**Judge it by:** The anchor is credible and checkable. Weak work invents a daily cost, or a top tier no one could want.

**Breaks when:** The alternative or daily cost is not credible, or per-serving maths cheapens a luxury product.

**From:** OFF-6654, OFF-6608, MCR-6849, MCR-6637, Huber, Payne and Puto, Journal of Consumer Research 1982, https://doi.org/10.1086/208899

### 22. Match exit and abandonment offers to how far the shopper got: generous for browsers, small for cart leavers, no discount at checkout

**Why it works:** The closer someone is to paying, the less they need a discount to finish. Cart abandoners often buy within one to two days and checkout abandoners within two to three. A discount at checkout pays people who were already buying; what stops them there is usually risk or a surprise cost.

**How to apply:** Give site browsers the welcome offer. Give cart abandoners a small nudge, held until late in the flow with a real expiry. Give checkout abandoners risk reversal or shipping help, not a code. Do not stretch the welcome discount: one founder cut it from 15% to 10% with no change in conversion, though 10% versus 20% did matter. If the cart's discount box tempts people to hunt for codes when no promo runs, replace it with a trust bar.

**Judge it by:** Each stage has its own offer and the checkout step has no code. Weak work sends 15% off at every step.

**Breaks when:** Checkout abandonment is caused by price shock or shipping cost at checkout; then fix the price display or shipping, not the flow.

**From:** OFF-6688, OFF-0009, EML-6389, OFF-0061, EML-0044, LPG-0058

### 23. Build separate offers for new buyers and returning buyers, and set CAC targets from new-buyer numbers only

**Why it works:** Returning buyers already trust the brand and buy bigger baskets; giving them the acquisition offer gives away margin for nothing. Blended AOV also hides that new buyers spend less: a $100 blended AOV may be $90 for new buyers, and lower margin after the entry discount.

**How to apply:** Split order data by new and returning. Compute first-order gross profit for new buyers only and set CAC targets from it. Give returning buyers offers that build value: early access, members' prices on add-ons, gifts that unlock with spend. Plan lifecycle offers by cohort with one goal each: first sale, upgrade to subscription, retain, win back. Win back cancelled subscribers 30 to 60 days later with a deeper first reorder, then return to the standard rate.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the offer per cohort and the CAC target is from new-buyer data. Weak work runs one sitewide code for everyone.

**Breaks when:** The store is so young that almost every order is a first order; then a single offer is fine until repeat buyers grow.

**From:** OFF-6647, MEA-6318, MEA-6366, EML-6393, EML-0006

### 24. Make every reference price, saving and deadline true, and show the full price up front

**Why it works:** Fake was prices and resetting timers win a short-term lift and then cost trust, refunds and fines. Regulators now check them. A real deadline also works better than an endless one: in a week-long sale much of the money comes at the end, which Alex Hormozi's $100M Offers describes.

**How to apply:** In the US, a was price must have been openly charged in good faith for a reasonable recent time. In the EU, an announced reduction must show the lowest price of the prior 30 days. In the UK, the headline price must include all mandatory fees, delivery included where it cannot be avoided. Deadlines end when stated and never restart. Meta catalogs need sale price below price. Rules change: recheck the CMA and EU guidance before each peak season.

**Judge it by:** Each was price has a sales record behind it, and each timer ends. Weak work invents a compare-at price to show "60% off".

**Breaks when:** Never in substance; only the exact rules differ by market.

**From:** OFF-6145, OFF-6500, ADC-6047, OFF-6661, European Commission guidance on Article 6a, Notice 2021/C 526/02, https://www.rpclegal.com/snapshots/consumer/spring-2022/european-commission-publishes-guidance-on-price-promotions-under-the-omnibus-directive/, CMA price transparency guidance under the DMCC Act, https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/price-transparency-guidance-lands-as-cma-launches-first-consumer-law-investigations-using-its-new-dmcc-act-powers

### 25. Plan peak sales ahead: one clear deal for a small brand, a pre-built ladder and a stricter holiday profit target

**Why it works:** Buyers who barely know a small brand are confused by offers that change by the hour. Holiday buyers return more and repeat less, so a peak order is worth less. A brand that rarely discounts gains far more from a sale (one agency models 70 to 80% efficiency gains) than a known discounter (about 5%).

**How to apply:** For a small brand, run one clear deal for the weekend. If the brand reacts to trade, pre-build a ladder (deeper discount, gift, free shipping, bundle) with pages loaded. Set a higher acquisition MER target for November and December than for January. A brand that never discounts can skip the sale; Home Reserve, a US sofa maker, beat the prior November with no deal. Check weeks of cover per hero SKU: selling out in November starves December ads, so Comfrt hid in-stock items.

**Judge it by:** One offer per weekend for small brands, a ladder written before day one, a peak profit target and a stock check. Weak work changes the offer mid-sale.

**Breaks when:** The brand has big, engaged lists that already run proven timed drops.

**From:** OFF-6660, OFF-0068, OFF-6658, OFF-6685, MEA-6391, MCR-0068, OPS-0019

### 26. For premium and aspirational brands, keep price and discounts out of the ads, and sell risk removal instead

**Why it works:** A visible discount tells buyers the full price is not real. Aspirational brands that hold full price are among the most profitable, by one agency's view of its fashion clients. Free shipping and easy returns remove first-order risk at no cost to price image.

**How to apply:** Check how often the brand's ads mention price or discount; Alo shows 0%, with only a 10% email sign-up offer. Keep the cheapest entry items out of paid traffic so buyers land on full-price products: Alo's $48 entry item is never promoted against a $118 median. State free shipping and easy returns in the ad itself. Set the anchor price at launch, because a brand that starts on "60% off, ends today" stays a discount brand.

**Judge it by:** Ads carry no price or discount, and paid traffic points at core-price products. Weak work runs "20% off sitewide" ads for a premium label.

**Breaks when:** The brand is value-led, needs a low-priced entry product to acquire, or must clear slow stock. Promoting returns where fit problems drive them raises return rates.

**From:** OFF-6667, OFF-6668, OFF-6669, OFF-6661, MCR-0064

### 27. Turn one hero product into several persona offers, each with its own angle, pack and page

**Why it works:** Different buyers want the same product for different reasons, and an offer built around one reason converts better than a general one. Grüns sells one greens gummy as gut health, fibre, hair, focus and more. Hostage Tape shapes the whole offer to each persona, such as a military discount for one group.

**How to apply:** From the org context's personas, pick the top two or three by size and margin. For each, set the angle, the pack that matches the result they want, the gift or bonus that fits their use, and a landing page. A partner's discount can join a persona offer at zero cost of goods. When a concept wins in ads, build the whole funnel around it: a named bundle, its page and its retention flow.

**Judge it by:** Each persona offer differs in more than the headline. Weak work swaps one word in the hook and keeps the same page.

**Breaks when:** The product has one narrow use, the budget is too small to split, or a persona's claim cannot be supported; health claims need checking before launch.

**From:** OFF-6637, OFF-6692, OFF-6662, OFF-6674, MCR-6734, MCR-6672

### 28. Price each country on its own costs and ad prices, not by converting currency

**Why it works:** Ad costs, shipping and return habits differ by market. US Meta CPMs ran about $22 against about $11 in Australia in one case, so the same ads cost twice as much per buyer. Converting a price down by exchange rate throws away the margin the stronger currency gives.

**How to apply:** Per country, compare price per unit, units per order, shipping collected against real carrier and fulfilment cost, payment fees and returns. In one example free shipping to the US lost about $23 per order. Check that volume discounts rise in step with units per order in every market. Consider one-to-one pricing (70 AUD becomes 70 USD) rather than converting to about 52. Budget payment fees per market: about 2.5 to 3.5% domestic, 4 to 5% with currency conversion.

**Judge it by:** A table per country with unit price, units per order and shipping gap. Weak work sets one price and converts it everywhere.

**Breaks when:** Local rivals are clearly cheaper at the new price, or tariffs and shipping already absorb the gain.

**From:** OFF-6618, OFF-6671, OFF-6665, OFF-6666, OFF-6664, MEA-6375

### 29. Set the shelf price so every channel still makes money, and hold one price across channels

**Why it works:** Channels pay very differently: in one agency's example, wholesale made about 10% contribution on 60-day terms, marketplace 19% in 14 days, DTC 22.5% in 0 to 3 days. A DTC price set without the retail waterfall leaves no room for a retailer.

**How to apply:** For a product that may go wholesale, work down from shelf price: retailer margin, distributor's cut, then trade spend. Ross McKay of Cadence describes US grocery retailers taking 45 to 50% of shelf price and trade spend of 20 to 30% of revenue. If margin at sell-in is thin, raise the shelf price or stay DTC. Track contribution and days to cash by channel monthly. Keep one price on your site, Amazon and key retailers. In the US, use a suggested retail price or a one-sided advertised price policy, never a signed resale price promise.

**Judge it by:** A channel table with contribution and days to cash, and a waterfall per product. Weak work offers wholesale at "half of retail" unchecked.

**Breaks when:** The brand sells only DTC. Some US states and countries ban agreed minimum resale prices; check each market.

**From:** MEA-6274, OFF-6384, AMZ-0007, and Ross McKay's Cadence interview (in words only)

### 30. Keep the main offer stable for 6 to 12 months, test challengers on their own pages, and prune perks after 90 to 120 days

**Why it works:** Constant offer changes reset what buyers expect and make results unreadable. Out of about 100 possible offers, about 10 are good, so the search takes time. Perks pile up; some help the first sale but hurt retention, and only buyers can say which.

**How to apply:** Pick the shortlist with four questions: gross margin floor, order value shape, one hero SKU or many, and repeat behaviour. Keep the live offer running while each challenger gets a dedicated landing page fed by cold traffic. Size the test to the store: under about 300 orders a month, test only big swings. After 90 to 120 days, survey buyers on which perk made them buy and drop what nobody values. Change one perk at a time.

**Judge it by:** One control offer, named challengers, a test length from real volume, and a review date. Weak work changes three perks at once and calls the winner after a week.

**Breaks when:** Creative is weak, so offer tests mislead; fix creative first. Stated reasons can differ from behaviour, so also test removals.

**From:** OFF-6687, OFF-6635, OFF-6675, OFF-6611, CRO-0011, CRO-6613

## Working from the org context

- **Read the costs first.** Pull delivered cost per unit, shipping by units per order and return rate before any offer idea. If the org context lacks them, state one assumption line and flag it.
- **Read the offer history.** List every code, sale, bundle and threshold that ran, with dates and results. Lead with the offer that already sells; never drop a proven winner for a new idea without a test.
- **Read the buyers' own words.** Use survey answers, reviews and chats to find the top fear and the top reason to buy. The guarantee and the value mechanic answer those, in those words.
- **Read the positioning.** A premium brand keeps price out of ads; a value brand can lead with price. Do not move a brand along that line without saying so and showing the margin case.
- **Keep the voice.** Offer names, tier labels and terms are written in the brand's voice and reading level. "The winter reset set" for a calm brand; plain counts for a no-nonsense one.
- **Keep the design system.** Badges, savings tags and tier pickers use the brand's colours and type, so the offer looks like part of the store, not a pop-up from a coupon app.
- **Respect what the brand will not do.** If the owner has said "no sitewide sales" or "no subscriptions", build within that. Propose a change only as a separate option, with its profit before and after.
- **Propose improvements as tests.** When the data says the brand is wrong (a mean-based threshold, a 10% code that loses money), write the fix, the number that shows why, and how to test it against the current offer.
- **Write learnings back.** After a job's results land, record which offer won on contribution per session and which perks buyers named, so the next job starts from them.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Every offer shows delivered cost, contribution per order and a new break-even ROAS and CAC.
- [ ] The winner is chosen on contribution per session, not AOV or conversion rate alone.
- [ ] The offer fills all five parts (positioning, price architecture, value mechanic, risk reversal, urgency), and a discount is not the lead unless the case is shown.
- [ ] Any discount depth is backed by units per order data and the required ROAS at that depth.
- [ ] The promo calendar counts sale weeks and keeps straight sitewide sales to one or two a year, or says why not.
- [ ] Any price change shows its break-even volume change, and a rise plan locks active subscribers and warns best buyers first.
- [ ] No plan shows two prices for one product to different visitors at the same time.
- [ ] Bundle savings are funded by shipping and fulfilment savings, with costs by units per order shown.
- [ ] The pack picker has three tiers or fewer, a default, even saving steps and dollar savings in titles.
- [ ] Thresholds come from the order value distribution, with the target peak named.
- [ ] Each gift has a landed cost, a real listed price and a link to using the product.
- [ ] The guarantee answers a named buyer fear, with claim cost per order and the lift it must buy.
- [ ] Upsells are ranked by take rate x added value x margin, one per slot, none pre-ticked.
- [ ] Subscription offers differ on first and later orders, cadence matches real use, and no sale undercuts subscribing.
- [ ] Prepaid or quarterly plans show the total charge, its timing and how to cancel before payment.
- [ ] Every was price, saving, stock claim and deadline is true and meets the rules of each market sold to.
- [ ] New and returning buyers get separate offers, and CAC targets use new-buyer numbers.
- [ ] A product sold wholesale or on marketplaces has a channel waterfall and the same price everywhere.
- [ ] The draft forecasts gross profit per month before and after the change.

## Common failures

- **Calling a discount an offer.** "15% off" is not a strategy. Fix: fill the five parts and lead with a value mechanic the store can afford.
- **Using product margin instead of delivered margin.** The offer looks profitable and loses money on every order. Fix: build cost to the door, including shipping, fees and returns, before any maths.
- **Keeping the old ROAS target.** The media buyer scales a sale on last month's target and buys losses. Fix: hand over a new break-even ROAS and CAC with every offer.
- **Declaring a winner on AOV.** A bigger pack shows "AOV up 40%" while profit per visitor barely moves. Fix: judge on contribution per session.
- **Setting thresholds from the mean.** Free shipping at AOV plus 10% gifts margin to big spenders. Fix: plot the distribution and push the peak you want.
- **Inventing values and deadlines.** A gift "worth $30" that was never sold, a timer that resets, a was price never charged. Fix: list the gift for sale, end deadlines when stated, keep sales records for every reference price.
- **Undercutting the subscription.** A 10% pop-up or sitewide sale makes one-time cheaper than subscribing. Fix: make the pop-up the subscription offer and give subscribers a better members' deal.
- **Five pack options with grey percentages.** Shoppers stall and do maths. Fix: three tiers, the middle pre-selected, "save $20" in each title.
- **Hiding the real charge.** A monthly price that becomes a quarterly total in the cart. Fix: show "pay $X today for Y months" on the product page.
- **One offer for everyone.** Returning buyers get the acquisition code. Fix: separate offers and CAC targets by cohort.
- **Generic guarantee text.** "30-day money back" with no link to the buyer's fear. Fix: answer the named fear, match length to results and cost it per order.
- **Market-specific rules everywhere.** India-specific matters such as cash on delivery, failed deliveries and local price display rules go to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce), not into the global offer.
