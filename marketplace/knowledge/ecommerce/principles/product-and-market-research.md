# Product and market research: craft principles

This book is for product researchers, product hunters, market analysts and range analysts: anyone who decides what a store sells next, to whom, and what it should stop selling. Read it before the job, write the shortlist, report or range call to it, and run the review checklist on the draft before it goes to the owner.

## What great looks like

- Every candidate shows gross profit per first order at the customer's door, next to the realistic cost to win that buyer, and the ratio between them.
- Every demand claim rests on two kinds of dated source that agree, and each number is labelled measured, estimated or proxy.
- No stock is bought without a paid money test whose go and no-go lines, and its budget, were written before it started.
- Entry products and angles are ranked on 90-day gross profit per new customer, not on front-end ROAS.
- The buyer is a situation in their own words (what they tried, what it cost, what they fear), not an age band.
- Every segment has a size check: enough separate people to take the planned spend.
- Rivals are read by what they prove (price paid at the door, ads live 60 days or more, counted review complaints), not by how busy they look.
- The pick changes one thing from the category norm, and the report names that one thing.
- Range calls rank SKUs by profit after returns and complexity costs, and launches are judged on sell-through and net demand after cannibalisation.
- The owner can read each verdict in two lines: what to do and why, with one number.

## The principles

### 1. Screen on gross profit per first order at the door before you look at demand

**Why it works:** A product scales on paid traffic only if each first order earns more than the buyer cost to win. The store that can pay more per customer wins the auction, so weak unit economics cap growth however good the ads are (ADC-0057).

**How to apply:** Build gross profit per first order: price minus landed cost, shipping, pick and pack, payment fees and a returns and discount allowance (MEA-0037). Cost to win a buyer is cost per click divided by conversion rate: about $23 at a best-case $0.70 and 3%, about $50 at a realistic $1 and 2% (MEA-0019). Want first-order gross profit at least twice the realistic figure; under about $70 is hard for paid growth (MEA-0024). For a new paid-ads store that means roughly $50 to $150 retail (PRD-0006); on Amazon, drop private-label items under about $25 or 30% net (AMZ-0004).

**Judge it by:** Each candidate shows price, delivered cost, first-order gross profit and its ratio to the realistic cost to win. Weak work quotes factory cost against price as a 70% margin, which was 24% once delivered (MEA-0023).

**Breaks when:** Demand is organic, marketplace or wholesale with little paid cost per order, or proven repeat lets 90-day gross profit carry the first order.

**From:** ADC-0057, MEA-0037, MEA-0023, MEA-0019, MEA-0024, PRD-0006, AMZ-0004

### 2. Call demand direction only when two kinds of dated source agree

**Why it works:** Each free source has a blind spot. Google Trends is a share of sampled searches scaled 0 to 100, so it shows direction and season, never how many people search, and a spike on a small term can be noise (PRD-6480).

**How to apply:** Pull the exact product term and its parent topic over 5 years and 12 months, per country, beside a steady reference term that has been flat for years. Pair it with a second kind of source: Keyword Planner volume, Merchant Center's free Popular products by country, refreshed weekly (PRD-6481), dated best-seller ranks, or review counts logged over time. Compare the last 12 months with the same months one and two years before. On marketplaces, drop listings younger than about 8 months, which hide fads and seasonal spikes (AMZ-0006). A UK pet brand that sees lick mats rising on Trends still needs ranks or volume moving the same way.

**Judge it by:** An evidence table with source, date, country and label on every row, and a grow, hold or avoid call that says what would change it. Weak work is one Trends screenshot, or a Trends score quoted as buyers.

**Breaks when:** Buyers search mostly on Amazon or TikTok, so Google is thin, or the category is under a year old.

**From:** PRD-6480, PRD-6481, AMZ-0006

### 3. Price rivals at the door and solve for price bottom-up before you set a target

**Why it works:** Buyers compare the total they pay, delivery and taxes included. If the price a product needs sits far above the rival ladder, only a clearly premium position can carry it (FIN-0012).

**How to apply:** Map rival products from cheapest to most premium: door price, pack size, unit price and what each step adds. For items with shared barcodes, Merchant Center's Pricing tab gives a free benchmark and the store's gap; own-brand items have none, so track named rivals by hand (PRD-6482). Then work the price bottom-up: profit target plus overheads sets a contribution target; add the cost to win a buyer, allowances, shipping and landed cost, and solve for price (FIN-0012). In the source that gave about $70 where doubling cost said $12; with rivals at $30 to $40, it was premium or no launch. Read unit price beside units per order by country, since a high unit price can cap baskets (OFF-6666). With no ladder, a Van Westendorp survey (too cheap, bargain, expensive, too expensive) gives a range to test.

**Judge it by:** Dated door prices, and the store's price with its working. Weak work doubles cost.

**Breaks when:** The item is a deliberate loss leader, or a strict category price anchor breaks any premium.

**From:** FIN-0012, PRD-6482, OFF-6666; Peter van Westendorp, price sensitivity meter (1976)

### 4. Prove buyers will pay before buying stock, with budget and go and no-go lines set first

**Why it works:** Likes and free sign-ups cost the buyer nothing. A paid pre-order at the planned price matches the stock decision, and lines set first stop the team reading noise as success.

**How to apply:** Write the decision (order the 500-unit minimum), the go line (paid pre-orders at or under target cost per buyer, clearing a named share of the batch) and the no-go line; the owner signs them. Budget it to be readable: target orders times cost per buyer, divided by test days; 20 at $100 over 14 days is about $143 a day (ADC-0066). Shopify pre-orders can charge in full, in part or later (OPS-6282). Promise only a ship date the lead time can keep: the US FTC rule needs a reasonable basis, or 30 days if none is stated, plus a delay notice and free cancel (CXP-6160); UK law defaults to 30 days. Size the first order to what the launch pop and modest paid push can clear (COG-6286).

**Judge it by:** Lines and budget dated before launch, results against them, and one call: order, retest or drop. Weak work moves the line afterwards.

**Breaks when:** The product is made to order, or no ship date can be promised; then a waitlist is the honest test.

**From:** ADC-0066, OPS-6282, CXP-6160, COG-6286; UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 s28, https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2015/15/section/28

### 5. Fail a product early on shipping, returns and product rules, before any sample

**Why it works:** The hidden costs sit after the sale. Bulky or fragile goods eat margin in freight and breakage (PRD-0013). Fashion returns run from 10% to 80% (MEA-0023), and Revolve booked more in return provisions than it kept in net revenue in 2022 (CXP-6610).

**How to apply:** Before samples, record packed size and weight, breakage risk, likely return rate and top return reasons from rival reviews, and every rule the product must meet in each market. Since 13 December 2024, a product sold into the EU or Northern Ireland needs a responsible operator established there, named with a postal address on the product, parcel or paperwork and on each listing (COG-6384), plus a risk assessment, technical file and a batch number on each unit (COG-6385). In the US, duty-free de minimis ended on 29 August 2025 (COG-6261), and importers must file CPSC certificate data with each entry from 8 July 2026 (COG-6313). Avoid gated Amazon categories such as baby and battery electronics (AMZ-0006). Fail what the store cannot certify.

**Judge it by:** A shipping, returns and rules line with pass or fail per candidate and market. Weak work finds the rule after the stock lands.

**Breaks when:** The store already holds the approvals, or bulky goods carry margin with freight priced in.

**From:** PRD-0013, MEA-0023, CXP-6610, COG-6384, COG-6385, COG-6261, COG-6313, AMZ-0006

### 6. Rank entry products and angles by 90-day gross profit per new customer, never by front-end ROAS

**Why it works:** An angle can win on day-one ROAS and still lose money if its buyers refund or never reorder. For one skin supplement, an energy angle showed a 5 ROAS, but its buyers refunded and never came back (MCR-6810).

**How to apply:** For each entry product and angle, take new customers only and divide their 30 and 90-day gross profit per customer by the cost to win them. Under 1 loses money; 1 to 2 needs proven retention; 2 to 3 is the zone to scale; above 3 means underspending (MEA-0024). Join post-purchase survey answers (which angle, why they bought) to that gross profit and rank on it (MCR-6810). Choose the entry product by which first SKU leads to the fastest second order (OFF-0073). Read November cohorts apart: in a 10-brand sample their six-month repeat lift was 38.5% against 63% for February (MEA-0035).

**Judge it by:** A table per entry product and angle: new customers, 90-day gross profit each, its ratio to the cost to win, and refund rate. Weak work ranks by platform ROAS.

**Breaks when:** History is under 90 days or new customers per product are too few to compare, so the money test decides; subscription brands with proven retention can scale at 1 to 2 (MEA-0024).

**From:** MCR-6810, MEA-0024, OFF-0073, MEA-0035

### 7. Prefer a big, familiar category where every rival looks the same over an empty one

**Why it works:** A known category needs no buyer education, so the store pays only to be chosen, not to explain what the product is. When every pack, name and claim looks alike, one clear difference stands out cheaply (PRD-0001).

**How to apply:** List the shared cues of the top 10 rivals: format, pack colour, how products are named, tone and main claims. Flag the two signs a category is ripe: it takes itself too seriously, or it is needlessly complicated, such as products sold by ingredient instead of benefit. Then look for a shift already visible in a nearby lifestyle category that has not reached this one. Bloom entered greens powder, one of the most crowded aisles of its time, by speaking only to women rather than trying to out-formulate the leader (BRD-6487). A crowded shelf is not a reason to avoid a category; a small category with no sameness is.

**Judge it by:** The report names the category's shared cues, the one gap and the outside shift that supports it. Weak work calls a market saturated from a count of sellers alone.

**Breaks when:** The sameness exists because of regulation or cost, buyers choose on price alone, or the shift is a niche trend that mainstream buyers have not taken up (PRD-0001).

**From:** PRD-0001, BRD-6487

### 8. Change exactly one thing from what buyers expect in the category

**Why it works:** A shopper can absorb one surprise while every other cue still says the product works as expected. Two or three changes at once force them to relearn the category, which needs an education budget most launches do not have (PRD-0002).

**How to apply:** Write down every attribute buyers expect: format, size, dose, colour, price band, channel and main claim. Pick the one to change and hold the rest at the category norm. Changing the format of a proven product is a strong, lower-risk choice, as when Grüns turned a multivitamin into a gummy (BRD-6491). If the concept needs two changes, launch one now and keep the other for a later SKU. Apply the same rule to the message: lead with one difference, not four. The warning case in the source is a 10x concentrated detergent in a shampoo-size bottle, which failed because buyers trusted the big jug.

**Judge it by:** The brief names the single changed variable and states that the rest match the category. Weak work stacks a new format, a new price tier and a new claim, then calls it differentiation.

**Breaks when:** The category is truly new and needs education anyway, the launch is well funded enough to carry more novelty, or the one change is cosmetic and gives no reason to switch.

**From:** PRD-0002, BRD-6491

### 9. With a parity product, win on an unserved buyer, not on a claimed mechanism

**Why it works:** A store selling the same product as a scaled rival to the same buyer loses on cost per buyer, because the rival has more budget, more proof and richer offers. A new mechanism claim is only believed when the product is visibly different (AVT-0014).

**How to apply:** In the Meta Ad Library, play the active ads of rivals who are clearly scaling and note the age, gender and desires they speak to; that profile is excluded (AVT-0013). Then mine long image and video reviews on top marketplace listings for other buyer groups. Pick the group with an acute, unmet want that nobody advertises to, start at the narrowest situation the research supports, and widen once ads work (AVT-0012). In the source, an ab stimulator sold to men aged about 40 to 65 found women after a C-section and people over 65 who cannot get to a gym. Test the group as its own ad concept and page first (BRD-6487, MCR-0002).

**Judge it by:** The report lists the buyers rivals own and the chosen buyer with its evidence. Weak work picks a slice of the buyer the leader already owns.

**Breaks when:** The product truly differs in function; the group is too small or does not feel the problem sharply; or ad policy blocks the claim.

**From:** AVT-0014, AVT-0013, AVT-0012, BRD-6487, MCR-0002

### 10. Count the honest angles a product can be sold on before you pick it

**Why it works:** Paid social now scales on a steady flow of truly different concepts. A product that can only be sold from three or four angles runs out of fresh ads and tires, so the creative cost of each new buyer climbs (MCR-6752).

**How to apply:** For each candidate, list every distinct problem, desire, buyer and alternative it can honestly be sold against. Aim for 10 to 15 that different people would care about. Blue Sense passed on mouth tape for this reason, while supplements can carry many angles and funnels. If you find only three or four, plan for a channel other than Meta or a bigger creative budget. Score each pain 1 to 5 on urgency, market size and how directly the product fixes it, and take the top two as the core angles (LPG-0046). Every angle must be one the product can prove; a health benefit needs evidence for this product, not for the ingredient in general.

**Judge it by:** The shortlist shows an angle count per product and the top two angles with their scores. Weak work treats every product as if it had endless angles.

**Breaks when:** The product has strong demand outside Meta (search, retail, community) where creative range matters less, or one angle carries huge demand on its own.

**From:** MCR-6752, LPG-0046

### 11. Size every segment against the planned spend: count separate people, not one loud story

**Why it works:** Specific segments make better ads, but each has a spend ceiling. A hook for CFOs of large Australian online stores reached perhaps 1,000 to 2,000 people, and $10,000 a day would show each of them hundreds of ads (MCR-6842).

**How to apply:** Search the problem plus Reddit and count separate threads; many threads mean a market, one viral story does not (AVT-0013). Search reviews for buyers naming who they are ('as a nurse') and count how often each identity repeats across own, rival and marketplace reviews (AVT-0016). Then test the ceiling: daily budget divided by CPM, times 1,000, is daily impressions; divide 30 days of them by the segment's people and the live concepts. Above about 2 views of one ad per person on cold traffic, purchase odds fall (MCR-0040), so cut budget or widen the segment. Prefer a segment inside a large parent market, and change every touchpoint for it (AVT-0015).

**Judge it by:** Each segment shows a count, where it was counted, a reachable size and the frequency the spend implies. Weak work names a segment from one forum post.

**Breaks when:** The market is so large that narrow segments are big, the audience churns and refills (MCR-6842), or the sale is high-ticket, where a few hundred buyers is enough.

**From:** MCR-6842, AVT-0013, AVT-0016, MCR-0040, AVT-0015

### 12. Reposition the hero for a new buyer before you launch a new SKU

**Why it works:** A new SKU means new stock, a second buying cycle and the risk of another slow C-grade line. A new angle on the same product needs only a page and ads, can be live in about a day, and reaches buyers the first angle never did (MCR-6838).

**How to apply:** When the cost of a new buyer on the main angle rises, list every benefit the product can honestly support and every buyer it serves. Build an ad and landing page per angle, test them as separate concepts, and watch cost per new customer by angle. Grüns sells one gummy as GLP-1 support, energy, immunity, beauty, cognition and gut support, and Blue Sense expects about 80% of revenue still comes from two angles. Build an offer per persona, not one for all (OFF-6637). Move to a new product only when honest angles run out.

**Judge it by:** The plan shows angles tried, cost per new customer by angle, and why a new SKU is still needed. Weak work treats a new product as the first answer to rising costs.

**Breaks when:** The product has one clear use, as most fashion and accessories do (COG-6286), or the new angle needs claims the store cannot back, especially health claims under FTC or TGA rules.

**From:** MCR-6838, OFF-6637, COG-6286

### 13. Back one or two proven heroes hard, and start the next product while the hero still rises

**Why it works:** Money and attention behind a proven winner compound, while spreading them early dilutes both. But a trend-driven hero decays, and a small range growing 20% a year cannot replace a hero that is falling fast (PRD-0003).

**How to apply:** For a young store or new market, put most spend behind the one or two SKUs with proven ads and margin, and keep reordering; a winner at 15% ad spend to revenue, scaled to $1M a month, is a business on its own (ADC-6275). Before one ad takes most of the budget, check stock depth and best-selling sizes: in fashion accounts, 80/20 applied twice means about 4% of ads and products drive about 64% of revenue (ADC-6276). Set a revenue milestone that starts range testing. If the hero rides a trend, start secondary products while it still grows: Oodie made about $200M in a year with only about $5M from anything else, then slid as rivals arrived.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the hero, its stock cover and the trigger for the next product. Weak work launches five SKUs at once, or waits until the hero peaks.

**Breaks when:** The category sells on breadth, as fashion does, or the hero is a staple with steady repeat demand.

**From:** PRD-0003, ADC-6275, ADC-6276

### 14. Rank the range by profit after complexity costs, and prune in waves

**Why it works:** Revenue rankings hide the cost of variety: storage, setups, slow-stock write-downs and listing upkeep all grow with SKU count. At one manufacturer, 21% of products made 380% of total profit while the other 79% destroyed 280% (MEA-6408).

**How to apply:** Per SKU, take contribution after landed cost, fees, fulfilment and returns, then charge a share of storage by volume and months held, photography and write-downs. Sort and plot cumulative profit; cut candidates sit on the falling tail. Before cutting, group SKUs by size, colour, scent or pack, remove duplicates within a group first, and keep at least one option in every size or format buyers rely on (MEA-6401). Cut in waves, not all at once: an online grocer that cut 24% to 91% of items per category lost sales through fewer visits (MEA-6400). Email recent buyers of cut items the closest alternative, and compare their 60 and 90-day repeat rates with similar buyers.

**Judge it by:** The sheet shows profit per SKU after complexity cost, a kill, keep, fix or grow call with its reason, and each SKU's basket role. Weak work cuts on a single margin cut-off.

**Breaks when:** Tail SKUs win first orders or lift baskets for the heroes, or the cut item is the only option in a size or price that loyal buyers need.

**From:** MEA-6408, MEA-6401, MEA-6400

### 15. Judge a launch on sell-through and stock grade, not on launch-week sales

**Why it works:** Many launches sell the first 30 to 40% of stock to the email list and existing fans, then stall. The launch week flatters the product while the cash stays locked in the warehouse (COG-6286).

**How to apply:** Grade every SKU by days of cover at its recent sales rate: A under 90 days, B 90 to 180, C 180 to 365, D over a year; track both the share of SKUs and the landed-cost dollars in each grade (COG-6290). Read a launch at weeks 4, 8 and 12 on sell-through after the organic pop has passed. Push C and D stock through its own funnel at break-even, or into a new market, rather than letting it sit (ADC-6199). Sell-through moves cash even when profit does not: in one modelled brand, lifting 30-day sell-through from 30% to 40% raised cash from about $50k to $70k with net income unchanged (FIN-0028). Each time products are added, decide which C and D lines retire.

**Judge it by:** The launch review shows sell-through by week and the grade mix in dollars. Weak work reports launch-day revenue as success.

**Breaks when:** The brand makes to order, or the stock is slow because the product is faulty, which needs a product fix, not a media push.

**From:** COG-6286, COG-6290, ADC-6199, FIN-0028

### 16. Judge a line extension on net demand after cannibalisation

**Why it works:** A new SKU's sales include buyers taken from the store's own range. Net demand can be far below total sales, and the mix shifts as the novelty fades (MEA-6405).

**How to apply:** Before launch, forecast the next 12 weeks for the SKUs most likely to lose sales: same use, same price band, same buyer. Use a seasonal naive baseline (each week equals the same week last year), since a method that cannot beat it is not worth using (OPS-6646). After launch, the shortfall against that forecast is the cannibalised units, and net incremental units are the new SKU's units minus that shortfall. Judge on contribution: the new SKU's margin, minus margin lost on cannibalised units, minus the extra stock, photography and inventory risk. Recheck at week 12. Count spillover too, but do not lean on it: in one study, promotions lifted complements by about 20% of the bump and cut substitutes by 11%, a net halo of about 9% (MEA-6403).

**Judge it by:** The review shows forecast, actual and net contribution for the new SKU and its siblings. Weak work reports the new SKU's sales alone.

**Breaks when:** Siblings had stockouts or promotions in the window, which breaks the baseline, or the launch sells mainly in a new channel.

**From:** MEA-6405, MEA-6403, OPS-6646

### 17. Sort each candidate into a product that wins new buyers or one that grows repeat value

**Why it works:** Not every good product can be advertised to strangers. An add-on that pairs with something the store already sells cannot win cold buyers, but it can lift lifetime value with the existing base (PRD-0012).

**How to apply:** Ask three questions in order. Can it win new customers on paid ads: priced above about $50, a large market, not a complement? What does it do for repeat and cross-sell across the range? Does it sell beyond the store's site, on Amazon or wholesale? Ridge's phone cases passed all three; its key case was repeat only, so it is sold to the base, not advertised. Score repeat drivers too: a product the buyer notices each use, that ships small, and that is bought both for oneself and as a gift (PRD-0013). Then rank the new-buyer products on 90-day gross profit (principle 6). Treat launches as bets and drop flops rather than forcing them with more marketing.

**Judge it by:** Each candidate is labelled new-buyer, repeat or both, with the reason and channel. Weak work scores everything on one demand number.

**Breaks when:** Repeat is already high, the brand cannot absorb a failed launch, or the product is too cheap to carry paid acquisition.

**From:** PRD-0012, PRD-0013

### 18. Name a rival's winning lever before you copy anything it does

**Why it works:** Brands past eight figures usually win on one of four levers: product, distribution, talent or risk. Each is paid for somewhere different, and none of the four case brands grew mainly through Meta ads (OPS-6626). Copy the ads without the lever and you get none of the results.

**How to apply:** For each rival the owner admires, name its main lever and where it shows: product in gross margin (often 70% or more) and an easy range to extend; distribution in stores and leases; talent in a founder or celebrity; risk in borrowed money funding growth. Check where its demand starts: Salt & Stone earned Amazon's top badge in deodorant before spending on Amazon ads, because buyers found it in stores and on social first (AMZ-0007). A long-running rival ad also runs on that brand's built-up trust (MCR-6682). Then state plainly which lever the store has, and borrow only plays that run on it.

**Judge it by:** Each rival profile names its lever and whether the store can match it. Weak work recommends doing what the leader does.

**Breaks when:** The rival truly grew on paid media, so studying its ads is the right move, or the rival is of similar size and awareness.

**From:** OPS-6626, AMZ-0007, MCR-6682

### 19. Read rival ads for what they prove, and log them by date

**Why it works:** The Meta Ad Library shows only ads running now for ordinary advertisers; stopped ads vanish, and only political, issue and EU-delivered ads keep a history (PRD-6483). It shows no spend, so an ad's presence is a hint, never proof of profit.

**How to apply:** Capture each key rival's live ads every week, with dates. Treat ads live 60 days or more, and several cuts of one angle, as the likely winners; if every start date looks recent, a bulk edit probably reset them, so lean on repeated angles instead (MCR-6758). Tag 20 to 30 rival ads by market stage: plain claim, bigger claim, mechanism, bigger mechanism or identity, then write one stage higher (MCR-6696). Write down what belief a viewer must already hold for the ad to work, because a famous brand can skip steps a new store cannot (MCR-6682). Search the product term, other buyer words and the wider niche, since rivals run ads from several pages and creators.

**Judge it by:** An ad log with date, run length, angle, stage and offer for each rival, plus a gap table. Weak work counts ads and calls the busiest rival the winner.

**Breaks when:** Rivals sell mainly through Google, marketplaces or TikTok, or most of their spend runs through creator and partner pages you have not found.

**From:** PRD-6483, MCR-6758, MCR-6696, MCR-6682

### 20. Mine rivals' 2 to 4 star reviews for the gap, and choose your buyer when they disagree

**Why it works:** Middle reviews come from people for whom the product almost worked, so they show structural gaps better than 1-star anger or 5-star praise (AVT-0008).

**How to apply:** Read the 2 to 4 star reviews of the top 5 to 10 rival products and write down every criticism, then group and count them. Sort them into two kinds. A repeated defect (fit, durability, leaks) is a fix your version must make. Complaints that contradict each other ('too simple' against 'too technical') are a segment choice: pick the buyer you serve, keep the core for them and satisfy the other cheaply with an add-on or a second tier. Also export the store's own reviews to find benefits no rival claims, or two category benefits nobody claims together (BRD-6488). Free shipping, fast delivery and good service are table stakes, not gaps. A UK baby carrier brand that sees "back pain after an hour" in 30 rival reviews has a design brief.

**Judge it by:** Complaint themes are counted, quoted and marked fix or segment choice, each with the response. Weak work sums it up as "mixed reviews".

**Breaks when:** One complaint dominates and points at a genuine defect, which is a fix rather than a choice; or the category has too few reviews to count.

**From:** AVT-0008, AVT-0027, BRD-6488

### 21. Look first for buyers who already paid for a fix that failed

**Why it works:** A buyer who paid for a rival product, course or treatment has admitted the problem and shown they will pay to solve it. Because that fix failed, they are still hunting, so the store skips the awareness work (AVT-0009).

**How to apply:** In 1-star and 5-star reviews of the top 5 to 10 rivals, look for sentences that list past spend or past attempts ('tried three serums', 'spent thousands on trainers'). Copy them verbatim with what the earlier fix failed to do. That failure is the first thing the page and ads must answer, and the past spend is the price anchor. Use the real names of what they tried, because named products and prices read as someone who has been there (AVT-0010). In a quiz, the 'what have you tried' answer reads awareness: mostly nothing tried means problem-aware buyers, while many named products means solution-aware buyers who need a mechanism and a comparison (AVT-0023).

**Judge it by:** The research names past fixes, what they cost and why they failed, with counts. Weak work describes "frustrated buyers" without one named attempt.

**Breaks when:** The category is new with no prior paid fixes, the item is a low-priced impulse buy, or ad policy limits naming a treatment or condition.

**From:** AVT-0009, AVT-0010, AVT-0023

### 22. Retrieve buyer words with a link for each; never let a model invent them

**Why it works:** A language model asked what buyers fear returns the category average, so every brand that asks gets the same list and the same ads (AVT-0026). The value of research is the exact phrase, the odd detail and the emotion, and summaries strip those out (AVT-0007).

**How to apply:** Tell the model to retrieve, not generate: find public posts, reviews and threads, quote them with a URL each, and reject any line without one. Paste quotes verbatim with source and star rating into one research doc, then tag repeats. Start with the high-yield, low-effort sources (reviews, support tickets and return reasons), then call recordings, post-purchase surveys, and forums for people who have not bought (AVT-0028). A store with no customers starts at forums and rival reviews. Write personas by hand from the last 20 real buyers before a model expands them, then cut to the best five (AVT-0031).

**Judge it by:** Every quote has a link or record id, and every persona line traces to quotes. Weak work has smooth, unsourced customer quotes.

**Breaks when:** The model has no browsing or file access, or the review set is so large that clustering must find themes first, with quotes still pulled by hand.

**From:** AVT-0026, AVT-0007, AVT-0028, AVT-0031

### 23. Find the job from recent buyers' timelines, then count it with a survey

**Why it works:** People hire a product to make progress in a situation. Feature questions give stated wishes; walking a recent buyer back to their first thought of buying shows the push of the struggle, the pull of the new option, the fear of choosing wrong and the habits that held them back (from Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta's jobs to be done work).

**How to apply:** Interview buyers from the last one to three months: start at the purchase, walk back to the first thought, then forward to first use. Themes repeat at about 20 calls of 30 minutes, so run 20 per segment, not 20 in total (AVT-0017). Use audience tools for how buyers act, interviews for why, and a survey to count each theme; group buyers by job, not age or title (AVT-0019). Recruit with a promise of no pitch (AVT-0018). In stigma categories, size the market by sufferers who buy nothing.

**Judge it by:** Job statements cite repeated circumstances, the interview count and a survey share. Weak work lists demographics and wished-for features.

**Breaks when:** The store has no buyers yet (use rival reviews and forums), segments are mixed in one interview set, or only happy customers were recruited.

**From:** AVT-0017, AVT-0019, AVT-0018; Clayton Christensen and Bob Moesta, Competing Against Luck (logic only)

### 24. Find the next product in the store's own data before you look outside it

**Why it works:** Customers prove demand for a new job when they use a product in ways it was never marketed for, or tell the store what they wish existed. A product built for that job starts with a known buyer and a known situation.

**How to apply:** Mine reviews, tickets and surveys for 'I also use it for' and other unmarketed uses, and count them; a use that keeps recurring is a candidate SKU or bundle named after the job (from the baking soda story in Christensen's jobs work). Add three to six single-tap research questions to the quiz that do not change its result; an unexpectedly large answer share is a new angle or segment (AVT-0022). Ask one identity question at email capture and compare conversion by tag: one fitness device brand found its 25% coach segment converted about 40% better (AVT-0020). Profile the top 20% of customers by persona, angle and first product, and the refunders, who often came in on an angle that oversold (MCR-6810).

**Judge it by:** Each idea cites a count from the store's own records. Weak work starts from a trend list.

**Breaks when:** Orders are few, the off-label use carries safety or claims risk, or quiz options only mirror the founder's guesses (AVT-0023).

**From:** AVT-0022, AVT-0020, MCR-6810, AVT-0023

### 25. On Amazon, choose products with many long-tail search terms and a market the cash can fund

**Why it works:** A new listing cannot win the main search term early. It wins page one on many smaller descriptive terms, and their combined sales build the velocity that later lifts rank on the main term; a product searched in only one or two ways has no side doors (AMZ-0003).

**How to apply:** Count descriptive search variants with real volume per candidate (slim, waterproof, for men) and favour products with dozens. Size the revenue band to cash: target products whose sellers make about $5,000 to $20,000 a month, since matching a $30,000 a month leader needs about three months of stock plus heavy launch ads (AMZ-0005). Multiply target units by landed cost and three months of cover, add launch ads, and lower the band until it fits. Look for beatable listings: at most about 300 reviews, at least 8 months old, at most 4 images, ungated categories (AMZ-0006). Vary categories so the shortlist differs from everyone using the same filters.

**Judge it by:** Variant count, revenue band, cash needed and weak-listing signals per candidate. Weak work picks the biggest market.

**Breaks when:** The seller has real capital, sales come mainly from brand search or outside traffic, or the filters are widely copied.

**From:** AMZ-0003, AMZ-0005, AMZ-0006

### 26. Enter a new country one at a time, with proven winners and products that fit its season

**Why it works:** Ads that win big in one large English-speaking market tend to win in the others, so proven winners lower the risk of a new country (ADC-6163). Seasons and return habits differ by country, so a range that sells at home can arrive in the wrong season or return far more.

**How to apply:** Size each country on its own: demand, rivals, door prices, duties and delivery. Lead with the one or two products with proven ads and margin (ADC-6275). Five or more home ads that each spent over $200,000 make a strong case for a US test. A southern hemisphere fashion brand should open the US with trans-seasonal products only (OPS-6607). Fashion returns can run 50 to 100% higher in the US than in Australia, so set a refund allowance per country (MEA-6379). Check whether a group aged 40 and over has the problem and the money, since many fast Meta-first brands sell there (AVT-0029).

**Judge it by:** Each country plan shows its own sizing, lead SKUs with proof, and season and returns checks. Weak work treats global as one market.

**Breaks when:** The brand has no proven high-spend winners, or the product is heavy or low-margin, so cross-border shipping and duties kill payback (PRD-0017).

**From:** ADC-6163, ADC-6275, OPS-6607, MEA-6379, AVT-0029, PRD-0017

## Working from the org context

- Read the buyers' own words first: each persona's phrases, the problems they bought for, what they tried before and what nearly stopped them. Research extends this bank; it never replaces it with generic personas.
- Read what the brand sells now and what won: hero SKUs, first products that led to the fastest second order, best and worst cohorts by 90-day gross profit, top return and complaint reasons. New candidates must fit or fix these, not ignore them.
- Keep the brand's position and its one difference. A candidate that needs a different voice, price tier or buyer is flagged as a new position, not slipped into the range.
- Use the brand's price ladder and margin floor as the screen. A product that drags average order value below the brand's paid-traffic floor needs a reason in writing.
- Check the design system and voice before proposing a format or name, so a new product looks and sounds like the range on the shelf and in the feed.
- Where the data disagrees with what the brand believes (the best buyer is older than the ads assume, a side product outsells the hero with one segment), say so with the number and propose a small test, not a rebrand.
- Write new findings back: segments with their counts, new verbatim phrases, rival moves with dates, and every kill condition the owner agreed.
- Stores selling in India hand payment, returns and marketplace questions to Ishita (`ishita-india-commerce`).

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does every candidate show gross profit per first order at the door, and its ratio to the realistic cost to win a buyer?
- [ ] Does every demand call rest on two kinds of source that agree, each with a date and country?
- [ ] Is every number labelled measured, estimated or proxy, and is any Google Trends score kept as direction only?
- [ ] Are rival prices compared at the door, and does the store's price show its bottom-up working?
- [ ] Before any stock order, are the test budget and go and no-go lines written and dated ahead of the test, with an honest ship date?
- [ ] Does each candidate carry a pass or fail on shipping size, return risk and product rules for each market it will sell in?
- [ ] Are entry products and angles ranked on 30 and 90-day gross profit per new customer, not platform ROAS?
- [ ] Is the buyer described as a situation in their own words, with what they tried and what it cost?
- [ ] Does every quote trace to a link or a record, with none written by a model?
- [ ] Does every segment show a count, a reachable size and the frequency the planned spend implies?
- [ ] Does the pick change one named thing from the category norm, and hold the rest?
- [ ] Does each candidate show its count of honest angles and its top two scored angles?
- [ ] Are rival ads logged with dates and run length, and is no profit claimed from ad presence alone?
- [ ] Is each candidate labelled as a new-buyer product, a repeat product or both?
- [ ] Are launches judged on sell-through and stock grade, and extensions on net demand after cannibalisation?
- [ ] Are range cuts ranked on profit after complexity cost, with every relied-on size or format kept?
- [ ] Does each verdict fit in two plain lines with one number, at the brand's reading level?

## Common failures

- Quoting product margin (factory cost against price) as gross margin. Fix: build delivered cost to the door, including shipping, fees and returns, before any verdict.
- Reading a Google Trends curve as market size. Fix: use Trends for direction and season only, and take volume from Keyword Planner, Merchant Center or marketplace data.
- Treating waitlist sign-ups or likes as demand. Fix: run a paid pre-order or deposit test against lines and a budget written beforehand, with an honest ship date.
- Crowning a product or angle on front-end ROAS. Fix: rank on 90-day gross profit per new customer and check refunds by angle.
- Writing personas from a model's average. Fix: retrieve real quotes with links, write personas by hand from real buyers, and let the model expand only after that.
- Calling a market saturated because it has many sellers. Fix: check the sea of sameness and look for the unserved buyer or the one changed variable.
- Copying the longest-running rival ad and its claims. Fix: name the belief and the lever behind it, and borrow the angle only if the store has the same lever.
- Picking a buyer from one viral story. Fix: count separate threads and repeated identities, and check the segment can take the spend.
- Reporting a launch on day-one revenue. Fix: read sell-through at weeks 4, 8 and 12, and the net gain after what the new SKU took from its siblings.
- Cutting the tail on revenue rank alone. Fix: rank on profit after complexity cost, check basket roles, and prune in waves while watching repeat rates.
- Launching a new SKU at the first sign of rising acquisition cost. Fix: test honest new angles and personas for the hero first.
