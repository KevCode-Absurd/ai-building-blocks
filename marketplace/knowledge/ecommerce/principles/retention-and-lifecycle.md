# Retention and lifecycle marketing: craft principles

This book is for the retention trade: the people who plan and write the emails, texts, subscriptions, loyalty perks, post-purchase steps and win-back that turn first buyers into repeat buyers. Read it before each job, write to it, and check the draft against the review checklist before hand-over. Every principle is judged the same way: did it add gross profit from repeat buyers, at a cost the store can carry?

## What great looks like

- The plan names one retention constraint (first order, second order, or top-customer depth) with its number, and every play points at it.
- Every lifetime value figure is gross profit per customer, by cohort, with a stated window (30, 90, 180 or 365 days).
- Every new flow, offer or program has a holdout, and results are read as gross profit per person, treated versus held out.
- Every message has a trigger, a day, a customer state (new, returning, loyal, subscriber) and one job.
- Every incentive shows the margin math that proves the returning order still earns after it.
- Campaigns go only to an engaged segment with written windows, and placement health is checked before sending more.
- The share of gross profit from the top 20% of customers is known, and lapsing best customers are reached before generic win-back.
- Subscription cadence matches how fast the product is really used, and cancelling stays easy.
- Consent, quiet hours and opt-out rules are checked for each market and channel before the first send.
- The words sound like the brand and use buyers' own phrases from reviews and surveys.

## The principles

### 1. Find the one retention constraint before you build anything, and fix it in order: product, range, buyer quality, experience, then email and SMS

**Why it works:** Email and SMS amplify what the product and buyer mix already do; they cannot make people want a second unit of a product that let them down. At most brands over 80% of customers never buy twice, so the biggest gain usually sits at the second order.

**How to apply:** Split retention into three stages (first order, first-to-second order, revenue from the top 20%) and pick the weakest from the store's numbers. Under about $10M to $20M a year, the constraint is usually the second order. Above that, top-customer depth matters more: VIPs should drive 50% or more of revenue and about 80% of profit. If reviews, returns or tickets show buyers leave over the product, send that to the product owner. A US supplement brand whose reviews say "didn't notice a difference" needs time-to-results teaching before it needs more campaigns.

**Judge it by:** One constraint is named with its number, and every play ladders to it. Weak work lists twenty tactics with no diagnosis, or prescribes more email for a product problem.

**Breaks when:** The brand is too new for repeat cycles. One-off categories (mattresses, furniture) need referrals and range, not reorder flows.

**From:** EML-6394, EML-6392, EML-6399, EML-0053

### 2. Measure retention only by cohort, in gross profit, over a fixed window

**Why it works:** Shopify's returning customer rate is returning orders over all orders, so it rises when acquisition slows and falls when you scale. Email-tool revenue measures how hard you send. Open-ended revenue LTV keeps rising and justifies acquisition costs (CAC) the store cannot afford.

**How to apply:** Group customers by first-order month, first product and first channel. For each cohort old enough to read, show repeat rate and gross profit per customer at day 30, 90, 180 and 365, with refunds taken out. Use 30 or 90 days to track the store against itself, and 180 or 365 to compare with others. Set expectations by category: fashion adds about 55% to first-order gross profit by day 365, consumables about 110%, most brands 20% to 40%, furniture close to zero. A UK apparel brand would show its March cohort's 365-day gross profit per customer beside March's CAC.

**Judge it by:** Every LTV number states its window, basis and cohort, and no cohort appears before it is old enough. Weak work quotes returning customer rate, or "email is 40% of revenue", as proof.

**Breaks when:** Cohorts hold only a few hundred buyers; use quarters. Wholesale or bulk buyers sit in the data; separate them first.

**From:** MEA-6335, MEA-6372, MEA-0014, MEA-6370, MEA-6295, MEA-6341, MEA-6327

### 3. Prove what lifecycle marketing adds with a holdout, not with attributed revenue

**Why it works:** Klaviyo's default credits any order within 5 days of an open or click. That works like view-through: much of that revenue would have come anyway, and Apple privacy opens inflate it further. Only a held-out group shows the real lift.

**How to apply:** For each new flow, win-back, loyalty program or gift, hold out a random 10% and compare orders and gross profit per person over the repeat window. Tighten reporting: exclude Apple privacy opens, bot clicks and order or shipping emails, and use a 3-day window for impulse products. Treat attributed share (a rough north star is over 30% of store revenue) only as a health sign beside sign-up rate, flow coverage and 90 and 365-day gross profit. One brand testing more texts ran 50% at normal cadence, 25% with none and 25% doubled; only the comparison with the no-text group showed that doubling paid.

**Judge it by:** Each play names its holdout size, read date and gross profit per recipient for each group. Weak work reports flow revenue from the email tool as the result.

**Breaks when:** Lists are too small to read a 10% holdout; test bigger changes, run longer, and call the read directional. Order, shipping and legal messages are never held out.

**From:** EML-0056, EML-0013, EML-6398, EML-0036, WLD-0007

### 4. Choose the entry offer and entry product by how well their buyers come back, not by first-order CAC alone

**Why it works:** The buyer you acquire sets your retention ceiling. At about 60% of Blue Sense's brands, customers won on deep discounts had much worse lifetime value. In a 10-brand sample, November buyers showed a 38.5% six-month repeat lift against 63% for February buyers.

**How to apply:** Cut the cohort table by discount code, first product, ad angle and month. Find the first product that leads to the fastest second purchase and ask acquisition to lead with it. Give retention a vote on welcome and sale offers: a cheaper first order that halves repeat buying is a loss. Ask for a lower CAC allowance for peak-season buyers. Check angles too, since a claim that lifts conversion can bring returns and weak repeat buying. An Australian skincare brand whose cleanser-first buyers reorder sooner than gift-set buyers should lead ads with the cleanser.

**Judge it by:** The work shows repeat rate or 90-day gross profit by entry offer and first product, and picks the entry point from it. Weak work treats acquisition and retention as separate problems.

**Breaks when:** Discount cohorts retain as well as full-price ones (check, do not assume), or discounts are the brand's permanent position. Single-product brands have no entry choice.

**From:** CXP-0016, MEA-6365, MEA-6358, OFF-0073, PRD-0012, CRO-6609

### 5. Win the second order inside its window: most second purchases come within about 30 days

**Why it works:** Intent and memory of the first order fade fast. A reminder timed to when the customer runs out lands on a real need, so vendor data shows replenishment reminders converting 8 to 15% against 1 to 3% for promotions.

**How to apply:** Read the store's median days to second order and the share within 30 days. Build the run: a thank-you with one relevant cross-sell about 10 minutes after the order, reassurance around day 2, how-to help about 48 hours after delivery, and a second-purchase nudge about 10 days after delivery. For consumables, send the reorder reminder at about 75% of the usage cycle counted from delivery: a 30-day supply with 7-day delivery means about day 35 after the order. Once a customer has two orders, use their own interval.

**Judge it by:** Every message has an order, delivery or usage trigger and a day, and the reorder day is worked out from pack size and delivery time. Weak work sends the same "you might also like" on day 7 for every product.

**Breaks when:** Durables repeat over years. Shipping is slow or erratic, so timing must follow delivery events. Usage varies so widely that a fixed cycle misfires.

**From:** EML-0062, EML-0049, EML-0051, EML-0029

### 6. Split every flow first by customer state (new, returning, loyal or subscriber), then by product

**Why it works:** Each group needs a different message and offer. A first-time buyer needs reassurance and the next step. A loyal buyer needs recognition, not a discount they did not need. A subscriber needs to stay out of sales. One offer for new and returning buyers is among the most common offer failures.

**How to apply:** At the top of welcome, browse, cart, post-purchase and win-back flows, branch on order count or subscription status before any product split. Give each group one goal: never purchased (first order), purchased but not subscribed (subscription or a second category), active subscriber (keep), cancelled (bring back). Stores without subscriptions use spend tiers or RFM groups (recency, frequency, spend). A Canadian coffee brand's cart flow could give prospects a small offer by email 3 and give returning buyers no code, only freshness and stock reminders.

**Judge it by:** The flow map shows the customer-state branch at the top and a different incentive in each branch. Weak work has one path for everyone, or gives loyal customers the same code as strangers.

**Breaks when:** The list is so small that branches hold too few people; start with new versus returning only.

**From:** EML-6396, EML-6393, EML-0053, OFF-6647

### 7. Cap every incentive at what the returning order can pay for in gross profit, and reach for credit, gifts and bundles before percent off

**Why it works:** A discount comes straight out of margin and teaches buyers to wait. A 20% sale on a $100 item with $50 of costs cuts margin to 37.5%, and a store that breaks even at 2 ROAS needs about 4.5 after a 40% discount. Credit, gifts and bundles feel worth more than they cost.

**How to apply:** Work out gross profit on a typical returning order (returning order value and margin, not store averages) and set the most you can give while it still earns. Prefer, in order: credit toward the next order (say on the page that it is credit), a low-cost gift with high perceived value, a bundle funded by shipping saved (a second unit adds only about 20% to shipping and fulfilment), free shipping, then a percentage. Instead of deepening a subscription discount, add a catalogue item whose landed cost is below the extra discount. Judge loyalty discounts on lifetime gross profit, not revenue.

**Judge it by:** Every offer shows gross profit per order before and after. Weak work says "send 20% off to lapsed customers" with no math.

**Breaks when:** The store is deal-led, or order value is so low that any gift erases margin.

**From:** MEA-6321, MEA-6361, OFF-6616, OFF-6626, OFF-0044, EML-6387, MEA-6318

### 8. Protect inbox placement first: send to engaged people, sunset the silent, and meet Gmail's sender rules

**Why it works:** Mailbox providers place mail by how recipients treat it, so mail to people who ignore you lowers placement for everyone, and every other play fails quietly.

**How to apply:** Define engaged in writing: a real open (not an Apple privacy open) or click within 90 to 180 days, or a sign-up or visit in 14 days. Sunset after about 120 to 180 silent days, after about 3 last-chance emails; drop hard bounces at once and soft bounces after 4. Warning signs: 90-day engaged opens under 30 to 35%, or clicks under 0.5%. Meet Gmail's bulk rules: SPF, DKIM, DMARC, one-click unsubscribe, spam rate below 0.30% and ideally under 0.10%. To repair, send 3 a week to 14-day engagers until opens hold 50% for 3 sends, then widen to 30, 60 and 90 days. Grow reach by adding random 10% slices of dormant profiles to strong sends; one activewear brand grew from 200,000 to 750,000 sendable and beat its prior Black Friday by 40%.

**Judge it by:** Windows are written down and complaints checked after each slice. Weak work blasts the full list for a sale.

**Breaks when:** Privacy opens hide poor Gmail placement, so check clicks by provider. Never re-warm a bought or unconfirmed list.

**From:** EML-0011, EML-0042, EML-0059, EML-6340, EML-0021, EML-6391

### 9. Send 3 to 4 campaigns a week to the engaged list, mostly not about discounts, and add sends only while clicks and unsubscribes hold

**Why it works:** Only about 3% of a list is ready to buy on a given day, so frequency decides how many of them see you. Too few sends under-earn; too many wear the list out.

**How to apply:** Scale with size: about 2 to 3 a week under $50k monthly revenue, 3 to 4 above it, 4 to 5 above $200k, rarely more than 5. Test a higher cadence with a split, judged on unique clicks, unsubscribes and complaints, not opens. Keep non-discount to discount emails at 5:1 or better (10:1 preferred). Mix education, social proof, community and product in about equal shares, with 3 to 4 designed emails per plain-text one. Send about 80% of campaigns to the whole engaged list and 20% to narrow goal segments; keep one-to-one personalisation for triggered flows.

**Judge it by:** The calendar shows sends per week, the segment for each send and the discount ratio. Weak work sends daily sale blasts, or fifty micro-segments of a thousand people.

**Breaks when:** Lists are small or new, the category is bought rarely, or filler would train people to ignore you. Active subscribers follow principle 19.

**From:** EML-0017, EML-0041, EML-0014, EML-0016, EML-6395, EML-0052

### 10. Build the list at 8 to 12% of visitors, and take the phone number as step two of the same offer

**Why it works:** Every flow is capped by capture, and typical forms catch only 1 to 3% of visitors. About 25% of double-opt-in SMS subscribers buy within 30 days, so the form is part of acquisition.

**How to apply:** Trigger at 4 to 12 seconds, 50 to 60% scroll or exit intent. Use four steps: a yes or no question, email, phone ("finish signing up by text to activate your discount"), then success. Offer at least 10%; near a 200 order value, a fixed amount usually beats the same percentage. Keep one offer for both steps: an extra SMS discount gave no meaningful lift in one test. Subscription brands make the form's offer the subscription offer. Send the code in welcome email 1, not on the success screen, so the first email gets opened. Consent wording sits above the button (principle 22).

**Judge it by:** Sign-up rate is a share of visitors, with triggers, steps and consent text stated. Weak work is a one-step 10% form at page load with a tiny close button.

**Breaks when:** Discount capture hurts a luxury position. Large mobile pop-ups hurt search traffic. Germany expects double opt-in. If welcome mail lands in spam, show the code on screen until placement is fixed.

**From:** EML-0065, EML-0040, EML-0038, EML-0027, EML-0047, EML-0034, OFF-0037, EML-6327

### 11. Front-load the welcome flow into the first 72 hours, then teach and prove over the next one to two weeks

**Why it works:** Most welcome revenue lands in the first three days, while the reason for signing up is fresh; later emails build belief in the rest.

**How to apply:** Send email 1 at sign-up with the code, a plain-text follow-up about 8 hours later, and the founder story at 24 hours. Then run bestsellers, proof and objections daily or every other day, with a real 48-hour deadline and a 24-hour reminder: about 7 to 8 emails over 7 to 14 days. Add 2 to 3 welcome texts, such as days 1, 3 and 5. Pick reviews that each answer one known objection (taste, fit, how fast it works), not generic praise. Test offer depth: one menswear brand found 10% to 20% off gave a large lift while 20% to 30% added nothing, so it kept 20%.

**Judge it by:** Each email has a day, a job and the objection it answers, and every deadline is real. Weak work keeps the tool's default of 3 emails across a week.

**Breaks when:** Products are costly or slow to research (items near $5,000 may need two months of welcome). There is no sign-up offer to expire.

**From:** EML-0060, EML-0045, EML-0019, EML-0058, EML-0033, EML-0029

### 12. Run cart and checkout as separate flows, time the first message by how close the shopper got, and keep discounts out of the early emails

**Why it works:** About 90% of audited Klaviyo stores' "abandoned cart" flows trigger on Checkout Started, so true cart abandoners get nothing. The closer a shopper got to paying, the sooner the reminder and the smaller the incentive.

**How to apply:** Set first delays by stage: checkout about 15 to 60 minutes, cart 30 minutes to 2 hours (not the 4-hour default), browse 1 to 3 hours, site abandon 1 to 4 hours. Send checkout email and SMS together; chaining them can delay the email about 20 hours. Match offers to stage: generous for browsers who left, small for cart, risk reversal or free shipping for checkout. If you discount, hold it until about email 5 (day 4 or 5), with a unique code so the 48-hour expiry is real. Set re-entry at about 30 days for browse and 14 for cart. Turn smart sending off on flow emails, or a recent campaign silently skips them.

**Judge it by:** Separate triggers, delays, stage offers and re-entry rules are visible. Weak work is one 4-hour "you forgot something" email with 10% off up front.

**Breaks when:** High-ticket buyers find a 15-minute email pushy. In the US, Klaviyo allows one abandonment text, within 48 hours.

**From:** EML-0018, EML-0046, EML-0064, EML-6389, EML-0044, EML-0012, EML-0043

### 13. Trigger win-back from each customer's own reorder gap, not a fixed day, and remove the unhappy before you send

**Why it works:** Lapsing is relative to a person's cycle: a fixed day-120 win-back with 20% off hits a customer who buys every 200 days at full price, so the discount is waste.

**How to apply:** With enough history, use RFM status or a predicted next-order date; Klaviyo's predictions need 500 or more buyers, 180 days of orders including the last 30, and some 3-order customers. Without that, start at about twice the average days between orders (45 days becomes 90). Run about 3 emails with a rising offer (vendor data shows escalating offers beating flat ones by about 34%) and one text with a 72-hour window. First remove anyone who returned, complained, charged back or left a 1-star review; if over 20% of the group did, fix that cause first. Sunset those who ignore it all.

**Judge it by:** Lapse is defined from data, each group has an offer ceiling, the complaint check is done and a holdout is set. Weak work sends "we miss you, 30% off" to everyone at day 90.

**Breaks when:** Most customers have bought once, so frequency carries no signal yet; days since order is fine until history builds. In long-cycle goods, 30 to 45 days is not lapsed.

**From:** EML-6397, EML-6325, EML-6326, EML-0019, EML-0050, EML-0029

### 14. Once the second order is healthy, grow the top 20%: profile them, catch lapsing champions first, and reward with access, not discounts

**Why it works:** Best customers return more often and rarely need a discount, so VIPs should drive 50% or more of revenue and about 80% of profit. A coupon to them gives away margin they would have paid.

**How to apply:** Work out the top 20% of customers' share of revenue and gross profit, with wholesale and bulk buyers taken out. Add a post-purchase question on why they bought, join answers to 365-day gross profit, and tell acquisition which persona, angle and offer brought the top 20% (and which brought the refunders). Build a segment of former RFM champions now at risk and reach them before general win-back. Reward with early access, VIP-only products, community and events, or by keeping subscribers at their sign-up price while they stay. Test hand-made gestures on a capped weekly budget, logged per customer.

**Judge it by:** The top-20% share is stated in gross profit, the persona behind it is named, and lapsing champions have their own message. Weak work sends VIPs a bigger coupon.

**Breaks when:** The store is under about $10M to $20M a year and the second order is weak; fix that first. Survey answers are too few or vague to tie to cohorts.

**From:** EML-6399, MCR-6810, EML-6397, OFF-0063, CXP-0022, WLD-0007

### 15. Put the subscription offer at the first order, front-loaded, and make it the default choice

**Why it works:** In one supplement brand, first-order subscribers showed about 50% higher 6-month value ($195 against $130), double at 12 months and triple at 24, partly because committed buyers choose it. A base stops growing where monthly churn equals new subscribers (10% churn with 2,000 new a month caps at 20,000), so take rate sets the ceiling.

**How to apply:** Front-load: about 25 to 30% off the first order and 5 to 10% after, rather than a flat 15%. Pre-select subscription, give one-time purchase no discount, and show three options of which two are subscriptions. First-order gifts should help people use the product (a frother, scoop or shaker), not add more of it, which drives skips. Show the schedule and real billed total before the cart, never a monthly price that turns into a quarterly charge.

**Judge it by:** The product page shows the default, schedule and true total, and first-order profit is checked against CAC. Weak work buries a 10% subscribe option below one-time.

**Breaks when:** The product is not used on a cycle, or margins cannot carry the discount. A default must never hide that the charge recurs; EU law bars defaults that add payments the buyer must untick (Consumer Rights Directive, Article 22), so check before pre-selecting there.

**From:** OFF-0038, OFF-0042, OFF-0029, OFF-0041, OFF-0039, OFF-0072, LPG-6624, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2011/83/oj

### 16. Match subscription cadence and quantity to real use and to how long the product takes to work

**Why it works:** Too much product is the most common cancel reason. A 30-day cadence on something used every other day builds a pile on the shelf, and every pile becomes a cancel. Products whose benefit shows after months lose people before it shows.

**How to apply:** Work out cadence from serving size and real use, from a survey or reorder data, and offer frequency changes at sign-up. When results take about 90 to 120 days, sell a 90-day plan and put the bonus on the second order: one free unit there is about 33% off that order and protects first-order margin. Frame the trial at onboarding with a prove-it challenge, a day-0 before photo or a reward for logging use. Survey when subscribers use the product, find the routine that retains best, and teach that habit; one creatine brand found buyers who mixed it into morning coffee stayed longest. Make frequency changes easy, since engaged subscribers adjust often.

**Judge it by:** Cadence is justified by usage math, onboarding teaches one named habit, and offer timing follows time-to-result. Weak work sets 30 days for everything.

**Breaks when:** Survey samples are tiny. The product works at once, so a challenge adds friction.

**From:** OFF-0040, OFF-6641, CXP-0003, EML-0005, CXP-0005

### 17. Chart cancels by day since first order, then rebuild the messages that sit on the spikes

**Why it works:** Churn clusters on days you can name: day 1 (discount hunters), the billing reminder around day 28, the first rebill at day 30, and days 31 to 45. In one 8-figure brand, the plain Shopify order confirmation on the first rebill caused about twice the cancels of the billing reminder.

**How to apply:** Plot cancels per day for 90 days and mark the message on each spike. Replace the plain renewal receipt with a branded confirmation that restates benefits. Rebuild the billing reminder with benefits first, the delay or skip button high and the manage link at the bottom, keeping the cancel path clear; one brand cut cancels from that email from 8.6% to 5.8%. Send daily onboarding in the 7 days before delivery, when almost no one cancels, then go quiet during days 7 to 14 after receipt. Read each fix in cancels per 100 subscribers.

**Judge it by:** The churn curve is shown, and each fix is tied to a spike with its measured change. Weak work says "reduce churn with engaging content".

**Breaks when:** Same-day delivery leaves no pre-delivery window. The platform receipt must stay on, or local law sets reminder content; keep every required disclosure.

**From:** CXP-0021, EML-0002, EML-0003, EML-0004

### 18. Build the cancel flow as one honest save per reason, offer a delay instead of an open-ended pause, and keep cancelling easy

**Why it works:** The cancel reason tells you which offer can work: too much product needs a delay, price needs a discount, dislike needs a clean exit. Open-ended pauses rarely return; only about 10% reactivate.

**How to apply:** Keep 6 to 7 reasons. Map each to one save: delay or skip up to 30 days, a frequency change, a swap, or a bigger discount on the next order, which saves more than gifts. Let product dislike leave. Benchmarks: a save rate near 15% is good, 20% strong, under 10% needs work. A one-minute founder video as the first step lifted weak flows from 5 to 6% to about 15%. Put delay, not skip, after it: skip raised saves but only deferred revenue. US law requires a simple way to stop recurring charges, and the UK's DMCC Act subscription rules were set to start in January 2027; recheck dates and duties before launch.

**Judge it by:** A reason-to-save table, the save rate, revenue per attempted cancel, and a cancel path finished in a few clicks. Weak work hides cancel, pauses forever, or offers everyone one generic 10%.

**Breaks when:** Margins cannot carry a big next-order discount, or customers learn to threaten cancelling.

**From:** CXP-0001, CXP-0004, CXP-0005, CXP-0002, OFF-6304, https://shlegal.com/insights/subscription-reforms-brought-forward-to-january-2027-is-your-business-ready/

### 19. Keep active subscribers out of regular campaigns and sitewide sales, and give them their own reasons to stay

**Why it works:** Every send reminds a subscriber they could reconsider, and a sitewide sale invites them to cancel and rebuy cheaper. One 8-figure brand traced about 9,400 cancels to campaign recipients, more than its campaigns started. After excluding subscribers, campaign cancels fell to near zero, worth about $24k a month, and with other changes churn fell from about 19% to 13 to 14%.

**How to apply:** Build an active-subscriber segment from the subscription app and exclude it from every campaign by default. Send subscribers 1 to 3 planned emails a month instead: founder news, community stories, a subscriber-only giveaway. During a sale, give them a better deal of their own, such as 50% off add-ons when the site is at 40%. Suppression stops emails, not ads or social posts, so design the subscriber offer first and have the public offer say what subscribers get. Check that no sale makes one-time purchase cheaper than subscribe-and-save. Watch cancels in the days after each send.

**Judge it by:** The exclusion sits on every campaign, a subscriber calendar exists, and each sale price is checked against the subscriber price. Weak work puts subscribers in the Black Friday blast.

**Breaks when:** The subscriber base is tiny. Exclusion turns into silence, which also loses subscribers.

**From:** EML-0001, OFF-0043, OFF-6612

### 20. Recover failed payments by decline type, and win back cancelled subscribers 30 to 60 days later with one strong offer

**Why it works:** Brands with a few million a year in subscription revenue typically lose 5 to 10% of it to failed payments and weak recovery, from customers who never chose to leave. Cancelled subscribers need time to run out before an offer feels useful.

**How to apply:** Split payment recovery by the failure type the platform passes: an expired card needs a fast update link, a soft decline needs retries, a hard decline needs a new method. Start win-back 30 to 60 days after a cancel with a deeper first reorder (30 to 50% off), then return to the standard rate. Test one strong offer for all of them, changing it every 1 to 2 weeks, rather than splitting by reason. Try paid social as a win-back channel: upload half the churned list as a custom audience, hold out the rest, and compare resubscribes, for about $1,000. Leave out people who disliked or outgrew the product.

**Judge it by:** Recovery is split by decline type and measured, and win-back shows timing and margin. Weak work sends one "update your card" email.

**Breaks when:** The platform does not pass the failure type. Margins are so thin that 40 to 50% off loses money if the customer churns again.

**From:** EML-0007, EML-0006, EML-0008, EML-6386

### 21. Treat SMS as the scarce, costly channel: real news only, short, at sensible hours

**Why it works:** SMS unsubscribes run about 3 to 5% per send against about 0.3% for email, and every text costs money. Its strength is reach and speed; filler burns the list.

**How to apply:** Send about one campaign a week, two for brands over 8 figures or with real news, kept to launches, restocks, sales and drops. Benchmarks: clicks 5 to 15%, unsubscribes under 1% per send (under 0.5% excellent), SMS at 5 to 15% of sales. Send around 11 a.m. to 3 p.m., never before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. recipient time. Stay under 160 standard characters; one emoji drops the limit to 70. Keep picture messages, about 3 times the cost, for launches, welcome and abandonment. Exclude anyone with 15 or more texts and no clicks. One vendor's data shows 8 to 10 texts a month with no unsubscribe rise; exceed the usual caps only after a holdout proves it.

**Judge it by:** Sends per week, character counts, send windows and the exclusion rule are stated. Weak work sends daily, emoji-heavy 300-character texts.

**Breaks when:** The list is a VIP drop channel that expects more. Other countries set other rules and prices. Margins cannot cover per-text cost.

**From:** EML-0025, EML-0039, EML-0030, EML-0024, EML-0028, EML-0036

### 22. Check consent and message rules for each market and channel before a single send

**Why it works:** Consent law differs by country and channel, and a breach costs fines, carrier blocks and sender reputation across every flow at once.

**How to apply:** US email: honest subjects, a postal address, opt-outs honoured within 10 business days, and no exception for business buyers. US texts: prior express written consent, never a condition of purchase, no sends before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. recipient time, STOP instructions monthly, double opt-in for cart texts, stricter rules in some states. UK: the soft opt-in lets a store email or text its own customers about similar products only if it offered an opt-out when collecting details and in every message; it never covers prospects or bought lists. The EU, UK and Canada need consent to the sender, so US tools that email unknown visitors are out. WhatsApp: opt-in must name the business, new accounts start at 250 users a day outside open chats, and templates can take 24 hours to approve.

**Judge it by:** Each segment shows its market, consent source and quiet hours. Weak work sends one global blast at 7 a.m. New York time.

**Breaks when:** Rules change; recheck the regulator's page before launch. This is a working standard, not legal advice.

**From:** EML-6341, EML-6385, EML-6345, EML-6323, EML-6327, EML-0066, EML-6320, EML-6321, EML-6343, https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/direct-marketing-and-privacy-and-electronic-communications/guide-to-pecr/electronic-and-telephone-marketing/electronic-mail-marketing/

### 23. Make the time between payment and delivery predictable: a specific date, proactive updates, low effort

**Why it works:** Effort drives disloyalty more than delight drives loyalty: in a study of over 75,000 customers, 96% of high-effort service contacts led to disloyalty against 9% of low-effort ones. Recovery rarely beats prevention: in a banking study only 5.4% of customers with a problem rated the fix much better than expected.

**How to apply:** Show a specific delivery date, padded so you usually beat it, and keep accuracy above 90%; specific dates lifted conversion 13 to 25% in research cited by Narvar. Send three proactive updates (shipped, out for delivery, delivered) with a branded tracking page, since shoppers check about 4.6 times per order. Track "where is my order" contacts: under 3% of orders is healthy, 3 to 8% normal but costly, over 8% a fulfilment problem messages will not fix. US shoppers expect about two-day delivery; if you cannot meet it, state real times in post-purchase emails. US stores need a reasonable basis for any ship time stated.

**Judge it by:** The plan shows the delivery promise, update triggers and contact rate. Weak work sends "your order is on its way!" with no date.

**Breaks when:** Goods are made to order, cross-border or pre-ordered; give a range. The carrier or 3PL is unreliable; fix fulfilment first.

**From:** CXP-0009, CXP-0011, CXP-0010, CXP-0014, CXP-0012, CXP-6609, CXP-6160

### 24. Run returns to keep the customer: exchange first, refund fast, log every reason

**Why it works:** 71% of US shoppers say a poor return makes them less likely to buy again (NRF and Happy Returns, 2,006 people). A refund that takes 10 to 14 days lets the wish to shop again fade, while vendor case data shows fast-refund customers reordering far more often.

**How to apply:** Offer exchange or credit first, with a refund always available, paid once the parcel is tracked or checked. Log every return with a reason: in Shopify, a refund issued without a return records no reason, so defect data is lost. Consider refunds without a return for items under about $20, since processing can cost 20 to 65% of item cost, but about 9% of returns are fraudulent, so flag repeat claimers. In high-return categories, run returns as their own P&L: Revolve booked $1.4B of return provisions against $1.1B of net revenue in 2022. In fashion, about 15% of abandoners cite the returns policy, yet losing them can earn more than free returns would cost.

**Judge it by:** Reason codes, refund speed and repurchase within 60 days of a return are measured. Weak work promises "hassle-free returns" with no data loop.

**Breaks when:** Return abuse is heavy. Local law sets a minimum right that overrides the store's window.

**From:** CXP-0007, CXP-0015, CXP-6612, CXP-0018, CXP-6613, CXP-6610, CXP-6611, CXP-6284

### 25. Build a loyalty program only where people already repeat, design it so progress pulls them forward, and make it pay against a holdout

**Why it works:** Points speed up and redirect purchases; they do not create need. People buy faster as they near a reward (Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng's coffee card study) and finish more often with a head start: in Nunes and Drèze's car wash test, 34% completed a 10-stamp card that came with 2 stamps, against 19% for a blank 8-stamp card. Every unredeemed reward is margin still owed.

**How to apply:** If few buyers order twice in a year, fix the product and second order instead. Put the first reward within reach of a typical second or third order, and give sign-up points so members start part-way. Price rewards from gross profit: cost per order, the share never redeemed, and the repeat lift needed to break even. Set expiry to the repeat cycle with reminders before points lapse; short expiry suits fast-repeat goods only. Launch with a 10% holdout and judge on 90 and 180-day gross profit per member.

**Judge it by:** A cost model, break-even lift, holdout and reachable first reward. Weak work copies an app default such as 1 point per $1.

**Breaks when:** The category repeats over years, margins are thin, or local rules limit points expiry.

**From:** CXP-0019, OFF-0063, https://doi.org/10.1086/500480, https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goal-gradient-hypothesis-resurrected-purchase-acceleration-illusionary-goal

### 26. Ask for referrals and reviews at the moment of proof, disclose rewards, and ask every buyer for a review, not just the happy ones

**Why it works:** Referred customers were worth at least 16% more over six years than similar non-referred ones in a large bank's data (Schmitt, Skiera and Van den Bulte), partly because friends pre-screen fit. Referrals and reviews both come easiest right after the customer sees the result.

**How to apply:** Time the ask for delivery plus time-to-result, or just after a reorder or a high 0 to 10 recommend score. Send promoters (9 to 10) to the referral offer and detractors (0 to 6) to service. Reward both sides with product or credit; Harry's pre-launch waitlist used product rewards and got 77% of 100,000 emails through referrals. Disclose rewards that could change how people weigh a recommendation (FTC Endorsement Guides). Ask every buyer for reviews: the FTC allows on-site incentives only with no sentiment required and treats asking only happy buyers as a risk. Google bans any incentive and selective asking; Amazon allows only Request a Review and Vine.

**Judge it by:** Ask timing follows the result, a disclosure line is present, and review requests go to all buyers. Weak work offers a discount for 5-star reviews.

**Breaks when:** People rarely talk about the product, or it is private or embarrassing to share.

**From:** CXP-6303, CXP-6300, CXP-6301, AMZ-6448, ORG-0030, CXP-6305, https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Schmitt-Skiera-vandenBulte-2011-Referral-Programs-Customer-Value.pdf

### 27. Spend on gifts, inserts and surprises only where the lift needed to break even is small and a test can show it

**Why it works:** An unannounced gift reaches many people who would have reordered anyway: 900 renewing subscribers times a $2 to $5 gift costs about $2,000 for perhaps 45 extra orders.

**How to apply:** Before adding a gift or note, divide its cost per order by the gross profit of a repeat order: that is the extra repeat orders each customer must place to break even. Announce gifts where they sway the choice: in the offer at acquisition, or at the months cohorts usually cancel, such as a welcome kit then gifts at months 3 and 6. Keep branded unboxing near 2 to 5% of product cost; a basic branded touch can cost under $0.50 per order. Test hand-made delight small: notes or founder videos to the top 50 customers, widening to 200 only if their 90-day reorders beat a control group.

**Judge it by:** Each gift line shows cost, break-even lift and control group. Weak work adds a free sample to every box "for delight".

**Breaks when:** The product rarely repeats, order value is tiny, or the 3PL charges per insert.

**From:** CXP-0006, OFF-6611, CXP-0008, WLD-0007

### 28. Test what moves gross profit (offer, flow structure, timing) at your constraint, with enough people to read

**Why it works:** Subject-line and open-rate tests are easy but rarely change profit; offer, structure and timing tests do. Small samples crown false winners, and acting on them wastes the next month.

**How to apply:** Aim tests at the constraint from principle 1. Use minimum sizes: sign-up forms 3,000 to 5,000 impressions per variant over 15 days (7 at least); flows 1,000 recipients per variant (500 if the gap is very large); campaigns a manual 50/50 split judged on revenue per recipient, not an automatic winner picked after about 4 hours. Of the cheap tests, flow delays earn one round because results often defy instinct: one store's 4-hour site-abandon email beat 30 minutes on placed orders, and a short reminder beat a long objection email. Read results on placed-order rate and gross profit per recipient, with unsubscribes and complaints as guardrails.

**Judge it by:** Each test names its hypothesis, metric, sample per variant and read date. Weak work runs ten subject-line tests and crowns the open-rate winner.

**Breaks when:** Placement is broken; fix sending health first. Lists cannot reach the floors; make bigger changes, run longer, or skip the test.

**From:** EML-6390, EML-0061, EML-0023

### 29. Plan four demand peaks a year, and treat peak buyers as their own cohort with their own target and follow-up

**Why it works:** A real reason to buy now lifts conversion at flat media costs. But peak buyers often arrive on deep discounts and repeat less: November buyers showed a 38.5% six-month repeat lift against 63% for February buyers in one 10-brand sample.

**How to apply:** Own one moment a quarter that fits the brand, beyond Black Friday and Mother's Day. Give best customers and subscribers early access framed as insider news. Re-warm the engaged segment in the weeks before (principle 8) so you can mail more of the list on the day. Pre-build the offer ladder (deeper discount, gift with purchase, free shipping, bundles) with copy and pages ready, so you never discount in a panic. Afterwards, run a peak-cohort second-order flow with a full-price reason to come back, and set a lower acquisition allowance for that cohort.

**Judge it by:** The calendar shows four peaks, an early-access send and a post-peak flow with its own repeat target. Weak work runs one sale a year and treats its buyers like any other cohort.

**Breaks when:** The moment does not fit the brand and reads as opportunistic. The peak leans only on discounts, which cuts margin and pulls demand forward.

**From:** EML-0055, MEA-6358, MEA-6391, OFF-0068, MCR-6778

## Working from the org context

- Read the voice first, then write every subject line, text and cancel-page line in it. A playful brand's win-back can be playful; a clinical brand's cannot. Keep the brand's sign-off, founder name and banned words.
- Pull buyers' own words from reviews, surveys and tickets into the brief: why they bought, what almost stopped them, why they left. Use those phrases in welcome objections, cancel reasons and win-back angles.
- Read "what ran and what worked" before proposing anything: current flows, cadence, offers, past win-backs and their results. Relaunch past winners before inventing new plays.
- Take the store's real numbers from the org context: margins per product, repeat curve, median days to second order, subscriber churn by month, return reasons. If one is missing, say what you assumed in one line and keep going.
- Keep the design system: logo, colours, type and email template. Propose plain-text founder emails as an addition, not a replacement for the brand's look.
- Keep offers the owner has ruled out (for example, "we never discount") and find the non-price lever instead: credit, gifts, early access, bundles.
- Where the brand does worse than these principles (a 4-hour cart delay, subscribers in sale blasts, no holdout), propose the change as a test: what to change, why, the number it should move, and the holdout.
- Note the markets the brand sells into and apply each one's consent rules. For India-specific matters such as pay-on-delivery orders and WhatsApp-first journeys, hand over to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).
- Write what you learn back: the store's own reorder gap, best-retaining first product and cancel spikes belong in the org context for the next job.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does the plan name one retention constraint with its number?
- [ ] Is every LTV or repeat figure a cohort figure, in gross profit, with a stated window?
- [ ] Does each new flow, offer or program have a holdout and a read date?
- [ ] Does every flow branch by customer state before product?
- [ ] Does every message have a trigger, a day and one job?
- [ ] Does every incentive show gross profit per order after the incentive?
- [ ] Is the reorder or replenishment day worked out from pack size, usage and delivery time?
- [ ] Are win-back triggers based on the customer's own reorder gap, with complainers and returners removed?
- [ ] Are campaigns sent only to a written engaged segment, with a sunset rule?
- [ ] Is campaign cadence stated per week, with the discount to non-discount ratio at 1:5 or better?
- [ ] Are active subscribers excluded from regular campaigns and sales, with their own calendar?
- [ ] Is subscription cadence matched to real use, and is the cancel path short and clear?
- [ ] Are cancel saves mapped one per reason, judged on revenue per attempted cancel, not save rate alone?
- [ ] Is the top-20% share of gross profit stated, with lapsing champions reached before general win-back?
- [ ] Are texts under 160 characters, inside quiet hours and capped per week?
- [ ] Are consent, opt-out and quiet-hour rules checked for each market and channel?
- [ ] Do review and referral asks go out at time-to-result, with rewards disclosed and no selective review asking?
- [ ] Is every message in the brand's voice, using buyers' own words where the brief gives them?
- [ ] Are all deadlines and scarcity claims real?

## Common failures

- Reporting email-tool attributed revenue or Shopify's returning customer rate as proof of retention. Fix: report cohort gross profit at fixed windows and a holdout result.
- Sending "we miss you, 30% off" to everyone 90 days after an order. Fix: trigger on each customer's own gap, cap the offer at returning-order profit, and remove the unhappy first.
- One flow for everyone, so loyal buyers get the same code as strangers. Fix: branch on new, returning, loyal or subscriber at the top of every flow.
- Discounts in the first abandonment email and in most campaigns. Fix: hold codes until late in the flow, keep campaigns at 5:1 non-discount or better, and use credit, gifts or bundles first.
- Blasting the whole list for a sale and wrecking placement. Fix: send to the engaged segment, re-warm dormant profiles in small slices, and check complaints after each.
- Subscribers in sitewide sales and every newsletter. Fix: exclude them by default and give them a better subscriber-only offer.
- A 30-day cadence for every subscription product. Fix: set cadence from serving size and real use, and make frequency changes easy.
- Hiding cancel or offering pause forever. Fix: a clear cancel path, one save per reason, delays capped at about 30 days.
- Generic, upbeat copy that could belong to any store. Fix: write in the brand's voice from buyers' own phrases, one objection per email.
- Daily texts, emojis and 300-character messages. Fix: about one real-news text a week, under 160 characters, inside quiet hours.
- Adding delight (gifts, notes, inserts) with no math. Fix: work out the break-even repeat lift and test it on a small group against a control.
- One global send time and one consent rule for every market. Fix: check each market's consent, quiet hours and opt-out rules before the first send.
