# Sourcing, costing and procurement: craft principles

This book is for everyone who gets a store's products made and bought: sourcing leads, buyers, should-cost analysts, tech pack developers and quality inspectors. Read it before the job, write the quote sheet, cost model, PO or inspection plan to it, and run the review checklist before hand-over. Every principle serves one goal: the right product, made to spec, landed at a cost that leaves the store real margin and cash.

## What great looks like

- Every SKU has one landed cost per unit, built line by line from FOB to the warehouse door, dated, and loaded into the store's cost per item.
- Every quote is compared on the same spec, quantity, packing, Incoterm and currency, and outliers are explained before anyone negotiates.
- Every target price comes from a should-cost per BOM line with a named, dated source for each input, not from a hunch or a percentage.
- Every negotiation has a target, a fallback and a walk-away on price, minimum order and payment terms, with terms priced in money.
- Every PO carries the spec, the golden sample reference, the AQL plan, the inspection dates, the penalties and the payment trigger.
- No balance is paid before a passed inspection, and no money goes to an account that is not the contracting company's.
- The store owns its tooling, its designs and a real alternative supplier, in writing.
- Every cost-down names its return risk and its test, and nothing buyers can see or feel changes without the owner's yes.
- Return reasons go back to the factory as defect data every quarter.

## The principles

### 1. Cost every SKU on full landed cost per unit, and update it in the store with every new PO

**Why it works:** Freight, insurance, duty, fees and the exchange rate all land on the unit before it sells. A brand that books FOB as cost gets margin wrong by SKU and pushes the wrong products; a 2 to 3 point COGS error on a $20M brand can wipe about $500K of EBITDA.

**How to apply:** Build one sheet per SKU: FOB, freight, insurance, duty, fees, broker and inland delivery, divided by units. The US charges duty on FOB value; the UK and EU add freight and insurance to the border. A VAT-registered UK store reclaims import VAT, so keep it out of COGS. The branded retail box is COGS; the plain shipping carton is not. Enter the result as cost per item before launch and update it per PO, since Shopify stores a static snapshot. US samples and small air shipments now pay duty; keep each under $2,500 to clear as an informal entry.

**Judge it by:** Each SKU shows every line, its source and date. Weak work shows one FOB figure and a freight guess.

**Breaks when:** Goods are made in the selling country, or a supplier sells delivered and duty paid at a fixed price.

**From:** COG-6293, COG-6331, COG-6262, COG-6353, FIN-6441, FIN-6439, FIN-0007, FIN-0008, MEA-6402, COG-6261, OPS-6675

### 2. Work out the most the store can pay per unit from its price and contribution target before you ask for a single quote

**Why it works:** Price talks anchor on the first number. Start from a factory's quote and you negotiate down from its anchor; start from your own ceiling and every quote is pass or fail. Product margin hides the gap: a $100 tee bought for $30 looked like 70% margin but delivered only 24% after freight, shipping, pick and pack, fees and allowances.

**How to apply:** Start at the selling price. Take off discounts and returns allowance, payment and platform fees, outbound shipping, pick and pack, and the CAC the media buyer expects. What is left, minus the contribution target, is the landed cost ceiling. Subtract freight and duty to get the FOB ceiling for the RFQ. If no factory can meet it, change the product or the price, or drop it.

**Judge it by:** The sheet shows the ceiling and its working before any quote. Weak work picks the cheapest quote and accepts whatever margin results.

**Breaks when:** The product is a deliberate loss leader or a test buy too small to matter.

**From:** MEA-0023, MEA-0037, FIN-0012, OFF-6636, OFF-6617

### 3. Rank BOM lines by cost, then build a should-cost for the top lines before you negotiate

**Why it works:** Cost-down effort should follow the money. In one textbook knife the bought-in blade was about 87% of factory cost and the moulded handle about 8%, so haggling on the handle wastes time. A should-cost turns "too expensive" into a claim the factory has to answer line by line.

**How to apply:** List every line with its share of unit cost. For the lines that make up about 80% of cost, rebuild the price from inputs: material quantity times price, conversion, overhead, outsourced steps, logistics and duties, then factory profit (10% in Fair Wear's garment example) and any agent fee (3%). For a moulded part, cost is resin (weight times waste factor times price) plus machine time (hourly rate divided by parts per hour) plus tooling (mould cost divided by lifetime units). Date and source each input price. Set the gap to the quote in money per year, so a $0.30 gap on 40,000 units reads as $12,000.

**Judge it by:** Every top line has inputs, sources and a gap in money. Weak work applies one "target 15% lower" to the total.

**Breaks when:** The item is a catalogue product with a public price list, or volume is too small for any line to matter.

**From:** COG-6304, COG-6305, COG-6299, COG-0001

### 4. Classify every SKU yourself and compare origins on the full duty stack, lead time and cash, rechecked each quarter

**Why it works:** Duty can now move landed cost more than any price talk: a Chinese item at $10 FOB with a 7% general rate, 25% Section 301 and the 12.5% July 2026 duty pays 44.5% and lands at $16.12. The importer carries a wrong code, and negligence can cost twice the lost duty.

**How to apply:** Classify under the General Rules of Interpretation; only the first 6 digits match worldwide, so never copy the supplier's code. For a big SKU, get a binding ruling before shipping: CBP in about 30 days, HMRC in 30 to 120. Per candidate country, model FOB, freight, that origin's full duty stack, extra weeks of stock in transit and where the raw material comes from; one agency's move to Egypt added about 30% to lead time and cut cost about 30%. A US store that paid the IEEPA tariffs struck down in February 2026 can claim refunds in ACE, except on entries over 80 days past liquidation.

**Judge it by:** The comparison shows duty by code and date per country, plus lead time and cash cost. Weak work copies the supplier's code.

**Breaks when:** Cash cannot carry longer transit, products are trend-led, or the new country lacks the raw materials.

**From:** COG-6331, COG-0004, OPS-6670, OPS-6685, OPS-6671, OPS-6684, OPS-6686, OPS-6677, OPS-6634, OPS-6662, COG-6387, FIN-6437

### 5. Cost conversion by time and efficiency, and gross up every line for scrap the right way

**Why it works:** Labour and machine time often matter more than the material buyers focus on. In the textbook handle, press time was about 86% of the part cost and resin about 10%. In sewing, cut and make cost is minute cost times standard minutes divided by line efficiency, so efficiency moves cost as much as wages do.

**How to apply:** For sewn goods, ask for SAM per style and the line efficiency. An 18 minute polo at 60% efficiency takes 30 real minutes; at 75% it takes 24, a 20% labour saving. Fewer colours and sizes per run raise efficiency, so offer that as a trade. For moulded parts, ask cycle time and cavities before resin price. Gross up each line for scrap: 100 pieces at 10% scrap means costing 110. Scrap compounds across levels, so 10% assembly then 10% component scrap on 200 units needs 242 parts, not 240. Do not load assembly scrap onto parts fitted after faulty units are pulled.

**Judge it by:** The model shows minutes, efficiency, cycle time and scrap per line. Weak work quotes one labour figure and ignores scrap.

**Breaks when:** The factory quotes a lump CM and will not share minutes, or the product is highly automated.

**From:** COG-6303, COG-6301, COG-6302, COG-6320, COG-6321, COG-6322

### 6. Send one identical RFQ to enough factories, hide your target, and drop outliers before you shortlist

**Why it works:** Quotes only reveal the market price when nothing else differs. With more than 5 matched quotes you see the average; with about 20 you see it well. Sharing a target first sets a floor the quotes cluster on. Overpaying 20% on FOB wastes about 10 to 15% of landed cost.

**How to apply:** Fix the drawing or reference sample, material spec, the quantity tiers you would really buy (for example 500, 1,000 and 3,000), packing down to master carton, the Incoterm with named place, and USD. For an off-the-shelf item, contact 10 to 50 factories. For a sensitive new design, qualify one or two first and share drawings only after checks and a signed NNN. Ask for FOB without a target, drop quotes about 20% above or below the average, then share your target with the shortlist. Compare forwarder and origin charges line by line on the same Incoterm, since EXW moves origin fees onto you.

**Judge it by:** One comparison table on one basis, with each outlier explained. Weak work compares an EXW quote with a DDP quote.

**Breaks when:** Only one or two makers can make the product, or the design is new to the world.

**From:** COG-6345, COG-6351, COG-6347, COG-6381, OPS-6681

### 7. Treat a quote far below the rest as a warning, and test every finalist with a hard price ask

**Why it works:** A very low quote usually means a factory filling idle lines, short of cash or owing its suppliers. It wins the order, then recovers margin by cutting materials, specs, checks or staff, or asks for more money later. How a factory answers a hard ask shows whether it knows its own costs.

**How to apply:** For any quote well below the cluster, ask why in writing and accept only a checkable reason, such as owning a key input. With each finalist, say your target is about 20% under its quote. A factory that comes back with options (a different material, a simpler finish, a dropped step, other packing, a higher tier), each with its saving, knows its costs. A factory that simply agrees is likely to cheapen the product later.

**Judge it by:** Low outliers carry a reason or are struck out, and finalists' answers to the ask are logged. Weak work picks the lowest number and moves on.

**Breaks when:** The item is a standard catalogue product with little cost structure to discuss.

**From:** COG-6352, COG-6343, COG-6326

### 8. Judge minimum orders and price breaks on total yearly cost and cash, not on unit price

**Why it works:** A bigger order buys a lower price but ties up cash and space, and new SKUs often stall after their first 30 to 40% of stock sells. In a textbook case a 4% discount at 500 units raised total yearly cost about 2.5%, and the break-even discount was 6.4%. Allbirds grew revenue about 30% while inventory doubled, then wrote down $19.1M.

**How to apply:** For each tier, compute price times yearly units, plus orders a year times cost per order, plus average stock (about half the order) times the holding rate. The lowest total wins, and the break-even discount is your counter. EOQ is a fair start; the total is flat near it, so ordering 20% off EOQ adds only about 2%. For a new SKU, buy for the launch plus a reorder, not a year. If the minimum is more than the store can sell or fund, ask for a split run, shared materials with another SKU, or a higher price at a lower minimum.

**Judge it by:** Each tier shows total yearly cost and cash tied up. Weak work takes the deepest break because the unit price is lowest.

**Breaks when:** Prices are about to rise, stock is seasonal or one-off, or freight rules force the larger order anyway.

**From:** COG-6362, COG-6363, OPS-6665, COG-6286, COG-6289

### 9. Price payment terms in money and trade them like price

**Why it works:** Small brands are usually limited by the cash cycle, not ads. Paying in full before goods ship ties up cash through production and transit, so a store can sit out of stock while its money sits at sea. Terms have a value you can calculate and trade.

**How to apply:** Value a later balance as order value times share moved times days moved divided by 365 times your yearly cost of cash. Turn any price-for-terms offer into a yearly rate: 2% more for net 60 instead of net 30 is about 24% a year, dearer than a typical bank loan. Supplier float is terms minus the days from production to your warehouse, so net 90 with a 30 day lead time gives 60 days. Realistic asks for a small buyer are a 10 to 20% deposit on non-custom goods, and the balance after loading but before the original bill of lading is released. Better terms usually come after one or two clean orders.

**Judge it by:** Every terms ask shows its value in money beside the price ask. Weak work accepts "2% for net 60" as free cash.

**Breaks when:** Cash is plentiful and borrowing is cheap, or first orders are so small the factory insists on full payment.

**From:** COG-6339, COG-6291, COG-6338, COG-6287, COG-6294, FIN-0005, FIN-0004

### 10. Tie the balance to a passed inspection and pay only the company that signed the contract

**Why it works:** Once goods are at sea, faults cannot be fixed and the balance is the only lever left. Payment fraud and weak enforcement both start when money goes to an account the contract does not name.

**How to apply:** The usual terms are 30% before production and 70% before shipment. Treat the 30% as a down payment you will not get back, sent only once the factory clearly understands the spec. Push the 70% to after a passed pre-shipment inspection or against a bill of lading copy; an inspection costs about $200 to $270 a person-day in Asia, about $0.05 a unit on 5,000 units. Pay only the signing company's approved account, never a personal, Hong Kong, broker or sister-company one, and confirm any "new bank details" by phone. On Alibaba.com, wire only to the details checkout gives, and claim within 30 days of delivery. On orders of about USD 30,000 or more, use a letter of credit listing your inspector's certificate, since the bank checks paper, not goods.

**Judge it by:** The PO names the payment trigger and the payee account. Weak work pays 100% on a chat request.

**Breaks when:** Very small first orders, where most factories demand full payment up front.

**From:** COG-6341, COG-0006, COG-6413, COG-6315, COG-6358, COG-6265, COG-6356, COG-6332, COG-6409

### 11. Plan order dates from total lead time with buffers for slow ships and factory holidays

**Why it works:** Quoted lead times assume everything goes right. Only about half of container ships arrived on schedule in September 2026, late ones by about 6.65 days. Chinese New Year closes many factories for two to four weeks, and the first runs after it are made by new staff.

**How to apply:** Add production (often 20 to 30 days), sea transit (30 to 40 days door to door, air 7 to 10), a supplier buffer (3 to 4 weeks new, about 2 regular) and about a week for ship delays. Launch with an air batch if needed, then restock by sea. For Chinese New Year 2027 (5 to 13 February, with factories closed longer), place orders by mid-December to cover sales to 1 April, with 15 to 30 days of extra safety stock, and inspect the first post-holiday runs more closely. Give the supply planner the full date chain so reorder points use real lead times.

**Judge it by:** Each PO shows the date chain from order to shelf, with each buffer named. Weak work uses the factory's "30 days" as the whole lead time.

**Breaks when:** A supplier has a long on-time record on the same product, where buffers just add stock.

**From:** COG-0008, COG-6337, OPS-6690, COG-6333, COG-6399

### 12. Lock the spec and verify it on every run, because quality fades quietly when price is squeezed

**Why it works:** Paul Midler named the pattern: each run carries a little less of the required input, a sub-supplier swaps a cheaper part, or a spec drops without notice. In one case an aluminium part weighed under 90% of its spec. The first order is not the risk; the fifth is.

**How to apply:** Every price cut must come with the spec it applies to; if the spec is not written down, the factory will choose. Put checkable numbers in the spec: fabric GSM, part weight, gauge, coating thickness, carton burst strength. Weigh and measure at every inspection, so a fabric cut shows on a scale. Retest materials on repeat orders, not only the first. Ban unauthorised subcontracting in the manufacturing agreement. When a factory offers "the same product cheaper", ask what changed in writing and run it through the cost-down test in principle 13 before saying yes.

**Judge it by:** The inspection checklist includes weights and measures from the spec, on reorders too. Weak work inspects looks only and approves silent changes.

**Breaks when:** The factory is under tight, frequent third-party testing with clear penalties, where fade gets caught early.

**From:** COG-6326, COG-0011, COG-6316

### 13. Approve a cost-down only when its saving beats the return risk it adds

**Why it works:** The saving per unit is certain and small; the cost of a worse product lands later, in refunds, reviews and lost repeat buyers, and product quality drives retention before email or SMS. Cuts buyers never see, such as a smaller carton, carry almost no return risk; cuts to parts buyers name in reviews carry the most.

**How to apply:** Write saving per unit times yearly units. Divide the saving per unit by the cost of one return (refund, both-way shipping, unsellable stock) to get the rise in return rate that wipes it out: if a return costs $20, a $0.40 saving is gone at a 2 point rise. Rank ideas: packing and cube first, hidden parts next, praised features and approved claims last, and those only with the owner's yes. Check the factory's material claims yourself (weigh the fabric, lab-test "organic cotton"). Run the change on one PO and compare its return reasons before rolling it out.

**Judge it by:** Each cost-down shows saving per year, the break-even return rise, the test and who signed off. Weak work reports the saving alone.

**Breaks when:** The product is being redesigned anyway, or volume is too small to read a change in return rate.

**From:** COG-6343, COG-6304, COG-6377, COG-6326, EML-6394, MEA-0023, COG-0001

### 14. Write the tech pack in numbers a stranger could check, with a tolerance on every measure

**Why it works:** Most wasted sample rounds come from ambiguity, not bad work. A word like "centred" lets the factory choose. A measure with no tolerance cannot be failed fairly at inspection, and one that is too wide shows up as size returns. A full tech pack removes about 90% of supplier errors.

**How to apply:** Include flat drawings front and back, a BOM with a Pantone code for each item, fabric spec with GSM and treatments, stitch type and stitches per inch, every graphic placed by distance (4 inches from the top, 2 from each side), label and hardware positions, packing, and a numbered revision log. Set a tolerance per measure; one shirt brand uses plus or minus 0.4 inch on chest width and 0.3 on collar and cuffs, doubling for circumferences. Send a physical reference sample with the pack.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer can pass or fail a unit from the pack alone. Weak work says "premium cotton, logo centred, good quality".

**Breaks when:** You buy a stock catalogue item with the factory's own spec, though you still record that spec.

**From:** COG-0005, COG-0007, COG-0011, COG-6407, COG-6330

### 15. Put the inspection standard in the PO: ISO 2859-1 level II with AQL 0, 2.5 and 4.0 by default

**Why it works:** AQL turns "good quality" into a sampling rule agreed before production. ISO 2859-1, ANSI Z1.4 and MIL-STD-105 share one set of tables, so inspectors anywhere draw the same sample. Rules set after a failure become arguments.

**How to apply:** Write general level II, AQL 0 for critical (any safety or compliance failure fails the lot), 2.5 for major and 4.0 for minor defects. For a 3,201 to 10,000 unit lot that is 200 samples, rejecting at 11 majors or 15 minors. The lot is the whole PO quantity of the style, not one colour. Use level III after recent problems or for high-value goods, level I only for suppliers with a clean record, S1 to S4 for destructive tests. Attach a defect list classing each likely fault, and move anything that drives your returns up a class. Even mature QC buyers see 8% of electronics and 11% of homeware orders fail, so budget for one failed lot in ten.

**Judge it by:** The PO names standard, level, AQLs and defect classes. Weak work writes "inspection before shipment" and nothing else.

**Breaks when:** The product is safety-critical, medical or for children, which needs tighter limits or full testing, or the lot is too small for sampling.

**From:** COG-6401, COG-6402, COG-6411, COG-6335, COG-6325, COG-6405

### 16. Time inspections to the work, not the calendar, and make failures cost the supplier

**Why it works:** Inspect too early and the factory has not run its own checks; too late and a defect has spread through the order. Penalties written down and applied every time change behaviour; ones raised after a failure do not.

**How to apply:** Book a during-production check at first finished output or about 20% made: day 12 on a 30 day, 20,000 piece order whose first units come on day 8. Book pre-shipment at 100% made and at least 80% packed, three days ahead. The inspector opens at least the square root of the export cartons, picked at random. Add loading checks with seal numbers for high-value orders. Track lots under the switching rules: 2 rejected of 5 in a row means tightened inspection, 5 straight passes return to normal, and 10 earn reduced inspection (200 samples drop to 80), a reward to offer in place of a price cut. After a failure, pay the inspector yourself and deduct re-inspection fees from the balance with a debit note.

**Judge it by:** The schedule ties each visit to a production milestone and the PO says who pays for re-inspection. Weak work books one visit when the factory says "ready".

**Breaks when:** Orders are tiny, or complex products need a technician on the line from day one.

**From:** COG-6334, COG-6404, COG-6403, COG-6349, COG-6412, COG-6408, COG-6346

### 17. Build the importer's compliance duties into the spec and the PO before production starts

**Why it works:** The importer of record, not the factory or broker, carries the legal duty for safety, labels and classification. A gap found after production means relabelling, retesting or seizure; one missing customs document got a brand's stock seized over Black Friday, the window producing about 80% of its yearly profit.

**How to apply:** List each market's rules per SKU and put the factory's part in the spec. US: a certificate per product from tests, its data filed at entry since 8 July 2026; third-party lab tests and tracking labels for children's products; textile fibre, origin and care labels; Prop 65 warnings for California. Make English origin marking a major defect, since an unmarked import owes an extra 10% duty. EU and Northern Ireland: a named EU responsible operator, batch or serial numbers and contact details on the product. Great Britain: the importer's name and address; CE marking is still accepted under most rules, so do not pay to switch to UKCA.

**Judge it by:** Each SKU has a compliance line in the spec and a test in the inspection plan. Weak work asks the factory to "handle compliance".

**Breaks when:** Category rules change often; recheck them before each new SKU.

**From:** COG-6374, COG-6375, COG-6373, COG-6312, COG-6313, COG-6384, COG-6385, COG-6388, COG-6370, COG-6397, COG-6263, COG-6383, COG-6395, OPS-0001

### 18. Once COGS passes about $1M a year, move to open-book costing and trade a steady schedule for a pricing formula

**Why it works:** Factories price risk. A buyer who sends random one-off orders gets a new quote with a safety margin each time. A buyer with a plan the factory can staff to is worth more than a bigger single order. Filling a line of dedicated workers closer to full use earns volume credits one acquirer of DTC brands puts at $1 to $3 a unit.

**How to apply:** Ask each factory to break every SKU into components, sub-suppliers, labour, overhead, financing cost and factory margin. Negotiate breaks per BOM line: at what volume does this zip, fabric or labour line get cheaper? When sales are steady and you have a year of history, offer a committed order schedule in an OEM agreement in return for a pricing formula, so price moves only with agreed inputs. Share your growth plan; factories that believe it fund better terms.

**Judge it by:** The supplier file holds an open BOM and a written formula. Weak work renegotiates the total price from scratch every order.

**Breaks when:** Volume is small or erratic, the factory serves many small clients with no dedicated line, or demand is new or seasonal.

**From:** COG-0001, COG-0002, COG-6344, FIN-0023

### 19. Test every supplier price rise against public indexes and real pass-through before you accept it

**Why it works:** "Materials went up" is the easiest claim to make and the hardest to check without data. Input moves reach finished goods slowly and partly: ECB economists found about six months for the first effect and only about a quarter of the move passed through. A raw cotton rise of about 23% moved US woven fabric prices only 6.5% in the same year.

**How to apply:** Name the input and its share of unit cost from the should-cost. Pull the matching series: the World Bank Pink Sheet or FRED for cotton and metals, US PPIs for resin, cartons and fabric, Shanghai or Dalian futures for inputs bought in yuan, and the BLS import price index for goods from China. Accept only that share times the index move, over a matching period. A 10% rise on a part where resin is a quarter of cost and resin rose 8% supports about 2%.

**Judge it by:** The reply to the supplier cites the series, code, months and the share indexed. Weak work accepts or rejects on feel.

**Breaks when:** One raw material is most of the cost, where pass-through is faster and fuller, or the input has no public index.

**From:** COG-6378, COG-6308, COG-6306, COG-6364, COG-6355, COG-6406, COG-6328, COG-6310, COG-6309, COG-6307

### 20. Design the pack-out for freight, and price carton cube in money

**Why it works:** Light, bulky goods fill a container by volume long before weight, so carton size sets freight per unit. Shrinking a carton is one of the few cost-downs that changes nothing the buyer sees in the product.

**How to apply:** Freight per unit is container rate divided by units that fit. At Drewry's 12 November 2026 Shanghai to Rotterdam rate of $3,504 per 40ft, 6,000 units carry $0.58 each, and a 10% smaller carton fitting 6,600 saves about $0.05. A 40ft high cube holds 76.2 cubic metres, 12.7% more than a 40ft standard. Air and express bill on the greater of actual and volume weight (L x W x H in cm divided by 6,000 for air, 5,000 for express), so cut dead air in the box. Group small air orders; one lane cost about $115 a kg at 10 kg but $9 at 300 kg. Drop-test any new pack to ISTA 1A heights for its weight, since a cheaper carton that crushes costs more in returns.

**Judge it by:** Pack changes show units per container, freight per unit and a drop test. Weak work picks the carton the factory had.

**Breaks when:** Goods are weight-bound, contract rates sit far from spot, or the smaller pack raises damage.

**From:** COG-6377, COG-6400, OPS-6687, OPS-6680, OPS-6682, COG-6324

### 21. Approve samples in stages, and keep the golden sample out of the factory's hands

**Why it works:** Each sample answers one question, and a skipped stage moves the problem into bulk, where it costs the whole order. An inspector can only judge against a reference, and a reference left at the factory can be swapped or worn.

**How to apply:** For hard goods, approve strike-off, pre-production and production samples. For a new garment, plan 5 to 7 rounds: proto, fit, size set, pre-production, sealed and top of production, with bulk only after size set and pre-production approval. Seal the approved sample, keep one, leave one at the factory, and courier one to the inspection company's local office yourself. Log each round with photos, measures and what changed. Price sample rounds into the launch plan, since each takes one to two weeks.

**Judge it by:** The file shows each stage approved in writing, and the inspector's reference came from you. Weak work goes to bulk on one photo of a sample.

**Breaks when:** The product is fully defined by measurable specs and lab tests, where the written spec matters more than a physical sample.

**From:** COG-0007, COG-6354, COG-6348

### 22. Count the full cost of quality and send return reasons back to the factory as defect data

**Why it works:** Inspection fees look like cost; failures after delivery cost far more in refunds, reviews and lost repeat buyers. Product quality drives retention ahead of email and SMS. ASQ's four cost-of-quality buckets let a store weigh QC spend against the failures it prevents.

**How to apply:** Each quarter, sum the four buckets per supplier: specs and planning, inspections and tests, rework and scrap, and returns, complaints and warranty claims. Pull returns by product and reason from Shopify; always create a return, since a refund issued without one records no reason. Check returned goods before charging a factory, because some returns are fraud or buyer's remorse. Turn the top defect reasons into spec changes and new defect list lines. For a new product, read the 1-star reviews of rivals rated 4.1 to 4.3 and write their complaints into the spec as must-fix lines.

**Judge it by:** Each supplier review shows external failure cost in money and the defect reasons sent back. Weak work tracks inspection fees only.

**Breaks when:** Return reasons are not logged, or volume is too low for patterns to show.

**From:** COG-6361, COG-6323, CXP-6612, CXP-6613, EML-6394, AMZ-0002

### 23. Audit a new factory before any custom deposit, and prove a cheaper one on about 500 units before moving volume

**Why it works:** An inspection asks whether this batch is good; an audit asks whether this factory can make your product to spec, at your volume, on time. A cheaper new supplier is an unknown until it has shipped once. One store's 10,000 unit order arrived with about 300 broken pieces.

**How to apply:** Book a third-party audit (about $400 a person-day, report in about 2 days) covering capacity, process, machinery and quality system before paying any deposit on custom work. For a supplier found online, check the business licence matches the company you will pay. Give a cheaper new supplier a trial of about 500 units before moving volume. Budget about 10% overage or an inspection on first bulk orders. To find makers for a personal-care or food product, learn who makes for leading brands, then vet capacity and certificates.

**Judge it by:** No custom deposit goes out without an audit report or a strong reason recorded. Weak work moves the whole order to a new factory on a sample and a price.

**Breaks when:** The factory is already audited under a scheme whose reports you can read, or the trial is a tiny catalogue buy.

**From:** COG-6414, COG-0009, COG-0012, COG-6315

### 24. Qualify a second source for every high-impact, high-risk item and split volume about 70/30, or protect sole supply in writing

**Why it works:** A supplier that believes it has no rival has room to raise prices or relax quality. Your leverage in any talk is your best option if it fails, an idea from Fisher and Ury's Getting to Yes. Peter Kraljic adds that high-impact, high-risk items need a different plan from cheap, plentiful ones.

**How to apply:** Sort items by profit impact and supply risk. For risky, high-impact ones, qualify a second source and let factories know it exists. With two suppliers, split volume about 70/30 to the top scorer. Score suppliers out of 100 on quality, on-time delivery, lead time and stock turns, leaving price out; under 90% on time means probation. Where only one supplier exists, get continued supply on change of control and most-favoured terms on price and spare capacity, as David Protein did with its key ingredient supplier.

**Judge it by:** Each key item shows its backup or the clause that replaces one. Weak work has one factory and no plan B.

**Breaks when:** The store is below a factory's priority level, where open shopping can lose the slot, or the input is truly proprietary.

**From:** COG-0003, COG-6391, COG-6350, COG-0013, Peter Kraljic, Purchasing must become supply management, Harvard Business Review (https://hbr.org/1983/09/purchasing-must-become-supply-management)

### 25. Sign an NNN before sharing a design, then a manufacturing agreement checked in the language that governs

**Why it works:** With an overseas factory the bigger risk is that it uses your design or sells round you to your customers, which an NDA does not stop. A PO and a chat thread leave out the terms that matter when things go wrong. In a Chinese bilingual contract the Chinese text usually controls in Chinese courts; in one case the English said 500 pounds of pressure and the Chinese said 300, and 300 applied.

**How to apply:** Sign a China-enforceable NNN (non-use, non-disclosure, non-circumvention) before drawings go out. Pay a China-business lawyer once for a master manufacturing agreement: measurable quality standards tied to the spec and your golden sample, inspection rights, tooling ownership, penalties, no unauthorised subcontracting, and the dispute forum. Then change only the exhibits (spec, price, dates) per order. Have every key term and number back-translated by someone not working for the factory. Confirm key terms by email, since chat screenshots can be disputed.

**Judge it by:** The supplier file holds a signed NNN, a master agreement and back-translation notes. Weak work relies on an English NDA.

**Breaks when:** You buy standard catalogue goods in small amounts where a full agreement costs more than it protects.

**From:** COG-6317, COG-6316, COG-6336, COG-6318, COG-6371

### 26. Own your tooling in writing, with a schedule, plates and a release deadline

**Why it works:** Free tooling is not free. A factory that pays for moulds, firmware or engineering recovers it in the unit price and keeps the tools, so the store cannot move production without paying again, which weakens every later talk. A "mould fee" invoice line does not prove ownership.

**How to apply:** Ask for tooling as a separate RFQ line. Pay at least part of tooling and engineering costs yourself, and get ownership of moulds, files and code in writing; if cash is tight, amortise over the first 10,000 units with ownership passing at that count. A mould you supply is an assist, so declare its value per unit in US and EU customs value. Keep a tooling schedule with photos, ID numbers and locations for every mould, die and jig, with ownership plates on each. Set a release deadline with a penalty if the factory does not hand the tools over when asked. Spread tooling over lifetime units in the should-cost, so a $6,000 mould over 30,000 units reads as $0.20 a unit.

**Judge it by:** The file holds the tooling schedule, photos and a release clause. Weak work trusts "buyer owns all tooling" on a PO.

**Breaks when:** The product is made on the factory's own standard tools.

**From:** COG-6340, COG-6319, COG-6305, COG-6262, COG-6353

### 27. Set late-delivery penalties and price limits in writing before the first order

**Why it works:** Factories rarely take a promise to cover "all losses" seriously, because lost sales are hard to prove. A fixed figure deters breach and gives a starting number. Penalties raised only after a miss read as a new demand.

**How to apply:** Write liquidated damages as a fixed rate, such as 5% of order value per week late, with the factory paying for air freight, or the store free to cancel after two weeks. Keep the figure near the real harm; Chinese courts can treat damages more than 30% above actual loss as excessive. Add a chargeback of about USD 500 per failed inspection, a limit on price rises per year, and the date the penalty runs from. Deduct penalties from the balance you still hold, and plan stock on the buffered dates in principle 11, not the penalty date.

**Judge it by:** The PO or master agreement states each penalty as a number with a trigger. Weak work says "supplier will compensate delays".

**Breaks when:** The store has no leverage, such as tiny orders with a sought-after maker, where heavy terms just lose the slot.

**From:** COG-6314, COG-6342, COG-6337

### 28. Buy on FCA for containers and DAP rather than DDP, and write what Incoterms leave out into the PO

**Why it works:** Incoterms split cost and risk, and the wrong one shifts both onto you silently. FOB was written before containers; a box handed over at a terminal can sit for days before loading. DDP makes the foreign seller clear your imports, which some countries bar. EXW makes you load the truck and handle export clearance.

**How to apply:** For container goods, buy FCA at a named factory or terminal; keep FOB for bulk cargo. Avoid EXW across borders. Prefer DAP to DDP, so the store controls clearance and duty. Under CIF the seller only insures at the minimum, Institute Cargo Clauses (C); ask for (A) cover or buy your own. Incoterms do not settle when title passes, how you pay, which documents you get or what happens if goods are faulty, late or disputed, so write each of those into the PO.

**Judge it by:** Each PO names the Incoterm 2020 rule, the named place, the insurance cover and the title and payment terms. Weak work accepts "DDP to your door" without asking who is importer of record.

**Breaks when:** A trusted forwarder runs an all-in service where the store is still clearly importer of record.

**From:** COG-6297, COG-6296, COG-6295, COG-6327, COG-6410

### 29. Write price-adjustment clauses on the share the input really drives, with a named index, base month and release

**Why it works:** A clause that moves the whole price with one index overstates every change, because labour, overhead and margin do not follow it. Vague clauses also invite disputes over which month and which revision counts.

**How to apply:** Split the base price into a fixed part and an indexed part. In the BLS example, $700 of a $1,000 price is indexed, so a 5.2% rise adds $36.40, not $52. Write: base price and base month, index title and code, adjustment dates, and adjusted price equals base price times current index divided by base index. For several inputs, use fixed weights that sum to 1. Say which release counts, since BLS revises PPI data for up to four months. Add a floor and a cap so both sides know the worst case, and make it two-way so falls lower the price too.

**Judge it by:** The clause holds every element and a worked example. Weak work says "price may change with material costs".

**Breaks when:** There is no cost breakdown, the order is a one-off, or the input has no public index.

**From:** COG-6367, COG-6368, COG-6366, COG-6365

### 30. Watch the store's currency against the US dollar, and know whether a yuan price includes VAT

**Why it works:** Most world trade is priced in dollars, so the dollar rate drives what importers pay far more than the rate with the supplier's currency. IMF and ECB economists found a 1% fall against the dollar lifts import prices about 0.6 to 0.8% within a year, against 0.2 to 0.3% for the bilateral rate.

**How to apply:** A non-US store buying in dollars should cost POs at today's rate plus its bank's spread, since reference rates from the ECB or the Fed's H.10 exclude it. Decide with the finance lead how many dollars to hold for orders 3 to 6 months out. If a factory or 1688 seller quotes in yuan, watch the CFETS central parity and ask whether the price is tax included (含税) or not (不含税); China's 13% VAT sits in a tax-included price that export sales do not normally carry. Show the FX rate and date on every landed cost line.

**Judge it by:** Each PO cost shows the rate, date and spread. Weak work uses last quarter's rate and compares a 含税 price with an export price.

**Breaks when:** The store is in the US, or buys in its own currency at a fixed price.

**From:** COG-6389, COG-6379, COG-6380, COG-6372, COG-6398, FIN-6427

## Working from the org context

- Read the product facts and approved claims first, word for word. If the page says "100% organic cotton" or "solid brass", that claim is a hard line in the spec and BOM; no cost-down touches it unless the owner changes the claim.
- Read what buyers praise in their own words in reviews and surveys. The feel, weight, finish or fit they name is the part of the product you protect; cut cost elsewhere first.
- Read return and complaint reasons by product. The top reason becomes a must-fix line in the next spec and a named defect class in the AQL plan.
- Read the design system for packaging: Pantone codes, logo placement, box finish and inserts. Put them into the tech pack as numbers, so the factory cannot drift from the brand's look.
- Read prices, discount calendar and contribution targets. They set the landed cost ceiling before any quote, and a planned sale changes how much margin a cost-down must save.
- Read what ran and won: hero SKUs, sell-through grades and seasons. Protect supply on A-grade sellers with a second source; buy new SKUs shallow.
- Read the markets the store sells into. Each one sets duty, labels and compliance lines per SKU. For stores importing into India, hand duty, labelling and certification questions to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).
- Keep the store's own supplier history: past prices, on-time record, defects and promises. Never restart a talk as if it were the first.
- Propose any improvement on what the brand does now (a cheaper material, a smaller box, a new factory) with its yearly saving, break-even return rise, first test and approver. Nothing buyers see, touch or read changes without the owner's yes.
- Write supplier messages in plain, polite, exact English (or the supplier's language with back-translation), not the brand's customer voice; numbers, dates and specs beat warmth.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does every SKU show landed cost per unit, line by line, with sources, dates and the FX rate used?
- [ ] Is the landed cost ceiling worked back from price and contribution, shown before the quotes?
- [ ] Do the top BOM lines have a should-cost with dated input prices and a gap in money per year?
- [ ] Are all quotes on the same spec, quantity tiers, packing, Incoterm and currency, with outliers explained?
- [ ] Is each minimum order or price break judged on total yearly cost and cash tied up?
- [ ] Are payment terms priced in money, with the balance tied to a passed inspection or a bill of lading copy?
- [ ] Does the PO name the payee account as the contracting company's own account?
- [ ] Is every supplier price rise tested against a named index, code and period, on the share of cost it drives?
- [ ] Does the tech pack give every measure in numbers with a tolerance, a Pantone per BOM item and a revision log?
- [ ] Is the golden sample sealed, kept by the store and sent to the inspector by the store?
- [ ] Does the PO state ISO 2859-1 level, AQLs per defect class, the defect list and the inspection dates by milestone?
- [ ] Are penalties, chargebacks and re-inspection costs written as numbers with triggers?
- [ ] Is tooling ownership recorded with a schedule, photos and a release clause?
- [ ] Is there a second source or a written supply protection for every high-impact, high-risk item?
- [ ] Does each SKU list its compliance duties per market, with the factory's part in the spec and a test in the inspection plan?
- [ ] Is the tariff code checked in each import tariff by the store, with time-bound duties dated for recheck?
- [ ] Does the order date chain include production, transit, supplier buffer, ship delays and holidays?
- [ ] Does every cost-down show its yearly saving, break-even return rise, first test and the owner's sign-off on anything buyers see?

## Common failures

- Booking the factory price as cost of goods. Fix: build landed cost per SKU with freight, duty, fees and FX, and load it into the store before launch.
- Negotiating down from the supplier's first number. Fix: set the ceiling from price and contribution first, and hide the target until the shortlist.
- Comparing quotes on different terms (EXW against DDP, 500 units against 3,000). Fix: send one RFQ that fixes spec, tiers, packing, Incoterm and currency.
- Picking the lowest quote without asking why it is low. Fix: demand a checkable reason, and test finalists with a 20% ask to see who knows their costs.
- Taking the deepest price break because the unit price is lowest. Fix: compare total yearly cost and cash tied up, and buy new SKUs shallow.
- Paying the balance before inspection, or to an account sent in chat. Fix: tie the 70% to a passed inspection and pay only the contracting company's approved account.
- Accepting "materials went up" as a reason. Fix: check the named index over matching months, and accept only the share of cost that input drives.
- Writing "good quality" and "logo centred" in the spec. Fix: exact measures with tolerances, Pantone codes, weights and a sealed golden sample.
- Inspecting looks only on reorders. Fix: weigh and measure against the spec on every run, and tighten the plan after two failed lots in five.
- Assuming the factory or broker handles compliance and tariff codes. Fix: the importer owns both, so list duties per market and classify the product yourself.
- Letting the factory pay for moulds and keep them. Fix: pay part of tooling, record ownership with plates and photos, and set a release deadline.
- Proposing a cost-down by its saving alone. Fix: show the saving per year beside the break-even return rise, the test and who signs off.
