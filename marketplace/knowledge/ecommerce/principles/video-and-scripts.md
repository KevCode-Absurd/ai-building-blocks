# Video ads and scripts: craft principles

This book is for everyone who writes, briefs, shoots, edits or buys short video for an online store: scriptwriters, UGC and creator producers, editors, TikTok and Snapchat buyers and organic content leads. Read it before the job, write the script and the edit to it, and run the review checklist on every draft before it goes to the owner.

## What great looks like

- Every video names its buyer, the awareness stage it speaks to and the one beat that does the selling before a word of script is written.
- The opening filters for buyers: a stranger knows within 3 seconds what the video is about and whether it is for them.
- The spoken line, the on-screen text, the first frame, the ad copy and the top of the landing page make one claim, not three.
- The promise comes before the mechanism, the proof is shown on screen, and every claim is smaller than its proof.
- The close fills all four slots of the ask and carries the store's real guarantee, sized to how long the product takes to work.
- The script reads aloud cleanly at a grade 5 to 7 level, in the buyer's own words, with no line a rival's product could also say.
- Each body ships with 3 to 5 openers that differ in kind, not in wording.
- The edit changes the picture every 1.5 to 3 seconds (except where one face carries the trust), works with sound off and keeps text inside the safe zone.
- Results are read on spend and store cost per purchase first; hook and hold rates only say where to re-edit.
- Rights, consent, disclosure and AI labels are settled before the file is exported, not after it is flagged.

## The principles

### 1. Write the hook to attract the buyer, not to stop everyone, because the creative is now the targeting

**Why it works:** On Meta, broad delivery reads each ad's transcript, text and visuals to decide who sees it, then learns from whoever keeps watching. A meme or bait opener that grabs everyone teaches the system to find watchers, not buyers, and cost per purchase climbs while hook rate looks great.

**How to apply:** Open on the buyer's problem, belief or moment, in words only that buyer would care about. Prefer a real problem over a callout like 'hey moms, listen up', which reads as an ad. A US hydration brand opens on 'drinking 3 litres a day does not mean you are hydrated', not on a man eating a mystery snack. Check the org context's persona phrases and use one in the first line.

**Judge it by:** You can say who the opener is for from the first 3 seconds alone. Weak work has a high hook rate, a low click rate and a cost per purchase far above the account's median.

**Breaks when:** The post is organic, where reach is free, or the category is gaming or entertainment, where a meme opener does match the buyer. Under about 50 conversions per ad set it matters more, since delivery leans harder on who keeps watching.

**From:** MCR-0026, MCR-6736, MCR-6812, MCR-6675, MCR-6855, MCR-6811

### 2. Build every direct-response script on nine beats, and put the promise before the mechanism

**Why it works:** A buyer must want the result before they care how it works. The nine beats (hook, problem, promise, mechanism, objection, proof, offer, risk reversal, call to action) walk a cold viewer from 'not for me' to 'why not now'. Leading with jargon such as hormone names loses viewers before they reach the point, a mistake in roughly a third of feed ads.

**How to apply:** Write the promise as one sentence the buyer would say back. Then give the mechanism as one named reason it works, not a list of ingredients. Answer one objection, show proof, state the offer, reverse the risk, then ask. Before handing over, check for the four promise failures: no promise, five promises, a feature dressed as a promise, or a promise bigger than the proof.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer can underline each beat in order and say the promise in one breath. Weak scripts list what the product contains and never say what changes for the buyer.

**Breaks when:** The ad is 15 seconds or shorter (see principle 17), the product is bought on look alone, such as most women's fashion, or the ad is a pure offer reminder to people who already know the brand.

**From:** MCR-6721, MCR-6614, MCR-6711, MCR-6706, MCR-6748, MCR-0032, MCR-6808

### 3. Read video results on spend and store cost per purchase first, and use hook and hold rates only to place the leak

**Why it works:** Attention metrics barely track profit: an ad spending twice as much at a slightly lower return usually earns more. Hook rate (plays past 3 seconds) and hold rate (plays past about 15 seconds) tell the editor where viewers leave, not whether the ad sells.

**How to apply:** Rank in this order: amount spent, then return or cost per purchase on 7-day click or measured lift, then click cost and click rate as early signals, then hook and hold rates as diagnosis. Fix the first half first: only about 15% of viewers reach the second half of a typical ad. Sort videos into keep, fix the body, fix the opener, or cut. As one practitioner's TikTok start point, wait for about $50 spend or 1,000 impressions, then look for click rate above 1% and watch time above 25% in 24 hours; under 20% watch time points to a weak hook.

**Judge it by:** Verdicts cite store cost per purchase and name the seconds to re-edit. Weak reviews kill a profitable ad for a low hook rate.

**Breaks when:** Very high order value with few purchases a day, where spend follows click and hold signals that barely correlate with return; judge on orders over longer windows.

**From:** ADC-6298, ADC-6207, ADC-6268, ADC-0037, MCR-0077, ADC-6183

### 4. Revive a tired winner with new openers and a new face or scene before making anything new

**Why it works:** A proven body still holds the logic that sold. A new opener gets its own creative ID and fresh reach; recut footage of the same person often does not, because the face is what tired.

**How to apply:** Spot fatigue at ad level: on cold campaigns over the last 30 days, frequency above about 2 means the same people keep seeing it, and purchase odds fall after the second view. Extend the hero in this order: new openers on the same body, a new format, its post ID in a scaling campaign, then a cost cap campaign for a short squeeze. Skip duplicating ad sets. Shoot the new openers with a different person where the old one is the sameness. One agency revived ads that had spent $50,000 to $100,000 with 50 new hooks shot in about an hour, for another $100,000 at similar efficiency.

**Judge it by:** The refresh plan names the old winner, what changes and what stays. Weak refreshes recut the same clips or throw the winner away.

**Breaks when:** The angle itself is worn out or rivals have copied the message; then refresh the angle, not just the opener. Small markets at high spend and retargeting run higher frequency by nature.

**From:** MCR-6850, MCR-6652, MCR-0047, ADC-6300, ADC-6217, MCR-6797, MCR-0040, ADC-6267

### 5. Ship 3 to 5 very different openers per body, not one, and not twenty near-copies

**Why it works:** About 80% of viewers leave during the hook, so the opener sets how far the body can reach. Ten 3 to 5 second openers cost about the effort of one 75-second body. But near-identical text variants get grouped or split budget too thin to read.

**How to apply:** For each proven body, record 3 to 5 openers, up to 8, that change the angle, offer, persona or format, not the wording. For the same hour of work, two solid bodies with 15 openers usually beat five bodies with two each. Test about three very different openers on a new concept, in its own ad set. Avoid 20 to 40 openers on one body: they spread spend too thin to exit learning. Cut every ad with 4 to 5 openers in the edit as standard.

**Judge it by:** The opener set looks different at a glance: different first frames, speakers or claims. Weak sets are one line reworded eight ways.

**Breaks when:** The body itself has not sold yet; new openers cannot rescue a body that does not convert, so prove the body first.

**From:** MCR-0013, MCR-6794, MCR-6863, MCR-6617, MCR-0076, MCR-0031, MCR-0048, MCR-0079

### 6. Spend most writing effort on the bridges after the hook, where good ads quietly die

**Why it works:** The hook gathers about 80% of the audience but persuades almost nobody. Viewers leave at weak joins: when the ad lurches from hook to pitch, or from education to the ask. Each line's only job is to get the next line watched, so the feeling of being sold must rise slowly.

**How to apply:** After the hook, move into a lesson, not the product. Script the turn from body to call to action as carefully as the opener. Run the swap test on the problem section: if you can swap any two lines and the argument does not change, you wrote a list, so rewrite it to escalate. Keep the problem beat to about 10 to 15 seconds in a standard 60-second ad. When 20 openers are stitched onto one body, write each one to land on the body's first line.

**Judge it by:** The retention curve has no cliff after second 3 to 5, and each hook flows into the body without a jolt. Weak work shows a steep drop right where the product appears.

**Breaks when:** The ad is a short offer reminder for warm buyers, where a quick jump to the deal is the point.

**From:** MCR-6722, MCR-6738, MCR-0027, MCR-6715, MCR-6703

### 7. State the awareness stage on the brief and match the opening to it, with about 80% of ads aimed at stages 1 to 3

**Why it works:** Buyers unaware of the problem need a different first line from buyers comparing brands. Mixing stages in one ad confuses the viewer and delivery. Colder stages cost more per result but hold most of the market.

**How to apply:** Put one of the five stages on every brief. Aim about 80% of new video at the first three and about 20% at the last two. Contrarian truths work at every stage; problem agitation only reaches people who already feel the pain and stalls as spend grows. Tag live ads by stage and fill gaps. If cold frequency climbs while spend stalls, count product-aware ads first: delivery keeps serving them to the same small warm pool.

**Judge it by:** The brief, the hook, the on-screen text and the primary text all sit at the same stage. Weak accounts are almost all feature, price or offer-led ads that only warm buyers act on.

**Breaks when:** The store is tiny and lives on retargeting, or the brand is so famous that brand-dependent hooks still reach new buyers. Before copying a rival, count how many of its hooks need the brand already known.

**From:** MCR-6719, MCR-0011, MCR-0057, MCR-6692, MCR-6656, MCR-6856, MCR-6635, MCR-6751

### 8. In cold video, reveal the product as late as the story allows, but make the benefit clear early

**Why it works:** An early product shot says 'this is an ad'. Teaching the problem and solution first means fewer people see the product, but those who do buy more often. Leaving viewers unsure what the video is about loses them.

**How to apply:** In a cold ad, keep the product and brand name out of the first 2 to 3 seconds. After a strong problem hook, add 4 to 15 seconds of mechanism education before naming the product. Give the reason to watch by second 3 and the benefit before second 7; a value line arriving 7 to 10 seconds in, or a curiosity gap left open for 24 seconds, loses most viewers. Cutting a slow 8 to 9 second product opening can turn the same footage into a prospecting ad.

**Judge it by:** The first frame shows the problem or the moment; the product arrives once the viewer knows why it matters. Weak work opens on the pack or a pour shot with the brand named in line one.

**Breaks when:** The visible mechanism is the hook (a gadget working within 2 seconds), the video is a reaction to a felt sensation, or the ad is for warm buyers.

**From:** MCR-6654, MCR-6852, MCR-6737, MCR-6638, MCR-6633, MCR-0014, MCR-6801, MCR-0077

### 9. Write the problem in the buyer's own words, found outside the brand's marketing

**Why it works:** People recognise their own phrasing instantly, and that 'this is me' moment holds attention better than any edit. The label on the pack ('hair thinning', 'bloating') is how the brand talks, not how the buyer feels it.

**How to apply:** Before scripting, hunt five finds in reviews, support tickets and forum threads: the accommodation the buyer makes (feeds the problem beat), the failed solution they tried by name (objection), the moment they nearly did not buy (risk reversal), the exact word they use (hook) and the moment it bites (urgency). Mine 2 to 4 star reviews for objections. 'I can see my scalp under the bathroom light' beats 'thinning hair'. If you cannot find the desire written by a buyer anywhere, you have a product description, not a concept.

**Judge it by:** Each key line traces to a real buyer phrase in the org context or the research log. Weak scripts use generic emotions and curiosity lines that any category could run.

**Breaks when:** The product is so new that no buyer language exists yet; then borrow phrases from the nearest category and test them as statics first.

**From:** MCR-6731, MCR-6699, MCR-6700, AVT-0027, AVT-0010

### 10. Show the proof on screen, using the strongest kind the claim allows

**Why it works:** Viewers trust what they see more than what they are told. Proof has a rough order of strength: demonstration, third-party verification, a large volume of specific social proof, named testimony, a mechanism explanation, then the founder saying so. Bigger claims need stronger proof.

**How to apply:** Put the claim in the frame: a full scoop next to an underdosed one, the toy that survived four children next to the cheap one. Match the proof type to the claim's risk: no risky claims, use creator demos; big claims, bring in an expert; a borrowed-trust category with no claims, use a founder or a known face. Use authority the brand already has; a famous endorsement sitting unused in only a few ads is wasted. Keep every claim smaller than its proof.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer can point to the second where each claim is proven visually. Weak work makes claims over B-roll that shows nothing related.

**Breaks when:** The benefit is invisible (sleep, gut health); then an animated mechanism carries the proof better than a talking head. In regulated categories, the claim itself may not be allowed: check with compliance first.

**From:** MCR-6712, MCR-6624, MCR-6754, MCR-6637, MCR-6625, MCR-6705

### 11. Replace every adjective with a checkable specific, and cut any line a rival could say

**Why it works:** Adjectives read as sales talk and cost seconds. A number, a timeframe, a named third party or a physical detail is believable and memorable. Shared category claims (vegan, cruelty free, locally made) can burn 7 to 8 words and about 4.5 seconds while setting the product apart from nothing.

**How to apply:** Swap 'fast absorbing' for 'dry in 40 seconds, we timed it'. Use one named mechanism over five stacked words; in fashion, one fabric claim beats a list of 'soft, light, breathable, premium'. Run a 'so what' test on every line: a correct script can still sell nothing, because believing is not wanting. Check for mixed messages, such as 'no crash' followed by 'you'll be buzzing'. Use small specific numbers in hooks ('the two problems in almost every rival') rather than vague plurals.

**Judge it by:** Circle every adjective: each one left has a specific beside it. Weak scripts read like the product page.

**Breaks when:** The specific is not approved or provable; then use the claim the brand can stand behind, word for word from its approved list.

**From:** MCR-6713, MCR-6689, MCR-6716, MCR-6813, MCR-6744, MCR-0066, MCR-6828

### 12. Close with a four-part call to action and a guarantee sized to how long the product takes to work

**Why it works:** 'Shop now' names the action only, leaving the viewer to guess what happens next and why today. A guarantee matched to the product's timeline shows belief in the mechanism and steers buyers to the larger supply the result needs.

**How to apply:** Fill four slots: the action, what happens next, the effort ('take the 60-second quiz and get your size') and a true reason for now, with a real date or stock count. Carry the site's guarantee and bundle into the script, guarantee just before the ask; many sites offer one no ad mentions. Size it to the result: a hair product needing 90 days gets a 90-day promise, not a generic 30. In the US, say 'money back guarantee' only if the full price comes back, and state any condition in the ad. Do not test call to action variants; few viewers reach them.

**Judge it by:** All four slots are filled and the guarantee matches the site. Weak closes end on 'link below' or an invented 'limited time only'.

**Breaks when:** No true reason for now exists; leave it out, since fake urgency can breach consumer law. Thin margins may not fund a bold refund.

**From:** MCR-6681, MCR-6616, OFF-6653, OFF-6501, MCR-6775

### 13. Fund volume above a quality bar, and price each video against a static by gross profit per ad

**Why it works:** Nobody reliably picks which good ad will win, so the number of ads above a set bar beats polish. But video costs several times a static to make, so at low returns its expected profit per ad falls fast.

**How to apply:** Define what a 7 out of 10 ad looks like and launch as many as you can above it, about one new ad per $1,000 of monthly spend; priority runs volume, concept, hook, storytelling, editing, then talent. Compare formats on expected gross profit per ad after production cost: at $1,000 revenue per ad and a 2x return, a $20 static leaves $480 and a $200 video $300. Test angles as cheap statics, then move winners into video. Size production to spend: under about $30,000 a month on Meta, founder-made content; $30,000 to $100,000, about 25% of media on production and 2 to 3 creators; above $100,000, about 10%.

**Judge it by:** The plan shows ads per month, cost per ad and expected profit per ad. Weak plans spend months on one polished film.

**Breaks when:** The product truly needs video to explain it, or the account is so large that a single ad can carry very large spend.

**From:** MCR-6661, MCR-6792, MCR-6878, MCR-0023, MCR-6846, MCR-6862, MCR-0039, MCR-6888

### 14. Treat the hook as three layers, the words, the text and the picture, all making one claim

**Why it works:** Viewers take in the spoken line, the on-screen text and the first frame together, then the ad copy and the page top. If they carry different claims (luxury in one, a sale in another), the message blurs. Because each layer can change on its own, one spoken line can become 20 to 30 openers.

**How to apply:** Write the spoken line first; one operator weights it at about 80% of the hook, the visual 15% and audio 5% for voiced ads. Make the first frame show the topic being done (eating the food, lifting in the gym), not unrelated footage. Keep on-screen hook text to about 5 to 8 words. Then vary the layers: the same line over a cut to a haul of rival packs, a whiteboard, or a 1 to 2 second clip that dramatises the problem. A bolder overlay alone has doubled hook rate in one account.

**Judge it by:** The text, the first frame, the voice, the headline and the landing page top tell the same story. Weak work opens on generic B-roll with a text line that says something else.

**Breaks when:** The format is music-led or wordless, where audio or the image carries far more than 5%.

**From:** MCR-0028, MCR-6743, ADC-6218, MCR-6739, MCR-0063, ORG-0016, ORG-0017

### 15. Score every hook on clarity, relevance and novelty before it is shot

**Why it works:** A hook that a stranger cannot place in 3 seconds fails no matter how clever it is. Clarity, relevance to a real problem and novelty do most of the work; specificity and visual credibility are bonuses. Two or three traits done well is enough.

**How to apply:** Score each hook out of 10 on the five traits, with the first three weighted most. Show it to someone outside the brand for 3 seconds and ask what it is and who it is for. Use the Persona, Problem, Pattern interrupt, Promise template to start, then write past it. For a premium product where price is the main objection, test opening on the price itself ('this costs $120 and it's still our best seller'). Expect that formula hooks can build a large brand, but an ad that holds very large spend usually needs a fresh opening nobody else has used.

**Judge it by:** The brief carries a score per hook and the weak ones were cut before the shoot. Weak work ships wordless or abstract hooks that strangers cannot decode.

**Breaks when:** Sensory or fashion openers where the texture itself is the message; judge those on whether the product is recognisable, not on words.

**From:** MCR-6668, MCR-6741, MCR-6742, MCR-6849, MCR-6865, MCR-6797

### 16. Pick the format after the script, choosing one that can carry the beat doing the selling

**Why it works:** Most teams pick UGC first and write to fit. But the beat that persuades comes from the product: an invisible benefit is carried by the mechanism, a visible result by demonstration, unusual terms by the offer. Each format is strong and weak in set ways: a talking face carries trust but cannot show the unseen; animation shows the unseen; a long native static reads like a person but loses sound and sequence.

**How to apply:** Find the load-bearing beat, then ask five questions: does it need education, can the value show in one frame, does it need a demo, what stage is it for, and does it need a face? A mechanism-led supplement leans on video and animation; one such brand runs about 78% video for that reason. A gadget with a visible result leans on a fast demo.

**Judge it by:** The brief names the load-bearing beat and the reason for the format. Weak work is a talking head trying to describe something the camera could have shown.

**Breaks when:** The store can only make one format; then write to its limits from the start.

**From:** MCR-6708, MCR-6705, MCR-6859, MCR-6611, MCR-6805

### 17. Size the beats to the length, and write each length as its own script

**Why it works:** Persuasion needs time. A 15-second ad fits only a hook, one problem line and the ask. A mechanism needs about 45 to 60 seconds, and risk reversal only fits past 60. Cutting a six-minute ad to two minutes in the edit breaks the bridges, so the short version feels jumpy.

**How to apply:** Choose the length from the beats the product needs, then write that length from scratch. For 6 to 15 second cuts, keep one claim and one ask. For 30 to 60 seconds, carry promise, mechanism and proof. For ads over 4 minutes, place calls to action at about 3, 5 and 7 minutes. To lengthen a winner, loop problem, promise, mechanism, objection and proof about three times, or walk a results timeline (day 7, 14, 30, 90). Weigh cost: a 5-minute ad takes about as long to script as ten 30-second ads.

**Judge it by:** Each cutdown has its own bridges and still holds every beat its length allows. Weak cutdowns are the long ad with chunks missing.

**Breaks when:** The platform caps length for the placement, or the product is an impulse buy where 15 seconds is all it needs.

**From:** MCR-6718, MCR-6729, MCR-6680, MCR-6724

### 18. Use long video sales letters and story ads when buyers do not understand the product yet

**Why it works:** When conversion is capped because buyers do not get the product (complex, low trust or a new ingredient), a long ad has room to teach. A story is not one beat added to the script; it carries all nine beats and moves the viewer from judging the ad to wanting to know what happened. Older Facebook buyers in particular read long copy and watch long video.

**How to apply:** Reserve long form (45 seconds up to 20 minutes) for products that need explaining. Send it to a long landing page in the same order as the script, not to the product page. Write story scripts in first person past tense first; rewrite winners in other persons and tenses for cheap variants. Never resolve the story in the hook. Once a long body works, test 3 to 6 hooks, then section order.

**Judge it by:** The page after the click continues the same argument in the same order. Weak long ads are padded short ads that hold no tension.

**Breaks when:** The product is simple, visual or impulse priced; a long ad then wastes spend that statics or short demos would use better.

**From:** MCR-0033, MCR-6685, MCR-6701, MCR-6848, LPG-6619, MCR-0031

### 19. Multiply a winning script by persona, register and speaker before inventing new angles

**Why it works:** A script that sells has proven its angle and offer. Swapping only the persona, the voice style or the person on camera reaches new pockets of buyers while keeping the proven logic, which is far cheaper than new research.

**How to apply:** Keep the angle and offer fixed and swap the persona (one brand runs the same timeline script for joints, menopause and gut, each to its own page). Rewrite the same line in three registers: casual UGC, flat third-person documentary, or a first-person diary. Reshoot the script with speakers who match each persona: mothers, first-car drivers, older buyers. When an opener clearly wins, brief every creator to make their own version of it. Two personas times three angles gives six concepts; ten ads each gives sixty from one research pass.

**Judge it by:** New ads trace to a named winner and change one element at a time. Weak programmes chase new angles every week and never learn which part won.

**Breaks when:** The register clashes with the brand or placement, or the persona needs a different offer too; then reshape the offer, not just the opener.

**From:** MCR-6612, MCR-0074, MCR-6697, MCR-6807, MCR-6820, MCR-6726

### 20. Spread each concept across formats so no single style carries the account

**Why it works:** Delivery learns which formats each person responds to. An account of only lo-fi talking heads never reaches people who ignore them. One brand had 148 of 188 videos as creators talking to camera, which an agency called the account's biggest risk.

**How to apply:** Once an angle works, run the same message, persona and offer as a talking head, an animated mechanism explainer, a demo, a native static and a story. Watch the share of live video in any one style; past about half, add others. A fashion starting mix one agency suggests: about 25% campaign shoots, 40% UGC and creator, 20% repurposed organic and 15% statics, then shift toward what sells. When testing a stalled account, change angles first, then offers, then personas, and formats last.

**Judge it by:** The live account shows several formats per winning concept. Weak accounts are one format in fifty colours.

**Breaks when:** Spend is very small and only one format can be made well; then make that one well and add a second as spend grows.

**From:** MCR-6621, MCR-6639, MCR-6657, MCR-6756, MCR-6829, MCR-6870

### 21. Make founder ads about the founder's argument, told through the viewer's problem

**Why it works:** Nobody cares about the founder for their own sake. A founder ad works because a real face lends trust to a real case. It converts when the story starts in the viewer's own problem so they think 'this is me', and when the founder says what only a founder can credibly say.

**How to apply:** Build the script on three founder-only claims: why the business started, why the mechanism works, and what was wrong with the rival products they tested. Leave product experience to customers. A proven shape: a bold adversarial claim, proof of the claim, the mechanism through the founder's story, proof of the mechanism, then the ask with offer and social proof. Open on real stakes, not a biography. The founder need not appear: use their voice, their evidence, a proxy such as a staff expert, or animation. Followers are not needed; strong founder ads have come from founders with zero.

**Judge it by:** The first 5 seconds are about the viewer's problem, not 'hi, I'm the founder'. Weak founder ads are origin stories with no argument.

**Breaks when:** The founder's story does not match the buyer at all, or the brand is large enough that one founder ad holding big spend complicates a sale of the business.

**From:** MCR-6761, MCR-6763, MCR-6771, MCR-6776, MCR-0006, MCR-6766, MCR-0005, MCR-6760

### 22. Batch founder shoots into hooks times bodies, and change the scene to break sameness

**Why it works:** Founder time is scarce, and the founder's face is the account's strongest sameness signal, so the same person in the same room tires fast. Separate hooks and bodies multiply into many ads from minutes of recording.

**How to apply:** Prepare 10 hooks and 3 bodies: about 5 minutes of camera time gives 30 ads, 60 with two calls to action. A one to two hour shoot can supply 3 to 4 months. Shoot in several settings; reshooting a fatigued founder script outside instead of inside a gym pushed about $40,000 more spend through it. Vary the founder's role: builder, witness reading a customer email, interviewer asking the lab why, or reader answering a one-star review. Keep founder ads as one weight in the mix; brands that rode a single founder concept slid when it tired.

**Judge it by:** The shoot plan lists hooks, bodies and settings in a grid. Weak plans film one long take in one place.

**Breaks when:** The founder cannot deliver naturally on camera; then use voice or proxy formats from the start.

**From:** MCR-0007, MCR-6773, MCR-6777, MCR-0012, MCR-6764, MCR-6774, MCR-0008

### 23. Brief creators on the concept and its beats, cast the buyer, and use local voices

**Why it works:** Unscripted creator videos drift into the same product dump. One brand ran 143 unique scripts that boiled down to about 35 real concepts. Buyers trust people who look and sound like them; accents that are foreign to a market underperform there.

**How to apply:** Give each creator a concept (persona, angle, offer) and the beats to hit, with room for their words. Cast creators who are the persona. Ask for two takes, one scripted and one natural, and for fashion, a no-talking cut with size details on screen. Use creators native to the target market; when taking a home winner abroad, re-voice or reshoot it with local voices, or use wordless video. When hiring, ask for past work plus the brief behind it to see how much direction they need. Audit returns against the beats: try-on hauls often hit only hook and proof.

**Judge it by:** Every creator video maps to a named concept and covers its beats. Weak programmes have dozens of creators saying the same thing in the same way.

**Breaks when:** The format's charm is the creator's own style, as in organic creator programmes; there, brief the outcome and let them write.

**From:** MCR-6615, MCR-6626, MCR-6807, MCR-6677, MCR-6629, MCR-6679, MCR-6830, MCR-6822

### 24. Run creator videos through the creator's own handle as partnership or Spark ads

**Why it works:** An ad from a person's handle feels like a post, not a pitch, and on Meta delivery uses the audience data of both handles to find buyers. Some agency accounts put up to 40% of spend through partnership ads, and founder-handle ads beat the brand page by about 5 to 30% when that profile has relevant posts.

**How to apply:** On Instagram, have the creator accept account-level partnership permission in their app; the brand can then run ads from their handle without a post and revoke it later. On TikTok, the creator sets a Spark Ads code duration (7, 30, 60 or 365 days in TikTok's help), so match it to the full campaign. Use the creator's handle for cold prospecting where trust matters; do not pay creators to front hard-sell ads for warm buyers. Pay flat fees, not a share of spend, and give partnership ads their own budget so they get delivery.

**Judge it by:** Creator ads run from the creator's handle with permissions dated to the campaign. Weak work rips creator footage onto the brand page and loses the trust it paid for.

**Breaks when:** The creator's profile is empty or off-topic, or rights and permissions are not signed.

**From:** ADC-6210, ADC-0003, ADC-6081, MCR-6815, ORG-0033, ADC-6271, https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/how-to-create-spark-ads-for-smart-campaigns-on-tiktok-ads-manager

### 25. Let organic posts and trial reels choose which hooks get paid spend

**Why it works:** Posting is a free pre-test. The retention graph on an unpaid post shows whether the hook, the bridge or the body fails, within about a day, before a cent of ad spend. Learning what holds attention is what makes paid ads good.

**How to apply:** Post draft cuts as Instagram trial reels with the call to action cut off; compare reach and retention and launch only the best 5 of about 20 openers (agency practice noted in April 2026, so recheck the feature). Track baseline views per organic post; an outlier at about 5 times the norm earns a place in ads, run as is with its ad code or with its hook kept and a product back half reshot. Small founders should post short video daily and read holds past 3 and 15 seconds.

**Judge it by:** Every new paid opener has an organic or trial read behind it. Weak programmes launch untested openers straight into paid tests.

**Breaks when:** The account is too new for a baseline, or the post went viral with an audience unrelated to buyers.

**From:** MCR-6861, MCR-0041, MCR-6664, ORG-0035, ORG-6427

### 26. Choose openers native to the category: wordless texture for fashion, a 2-second demo for gadgets, a reaction for felt sensations

**Why it works:** Some products sell through the eye, not the argument. In fashion, voice-free videos that show fabric in motion across several outfits work well, and problem-led scripts rarely build big women's fashion brands. A gadget with a visible mechanism sells by showing it working at once. A strong taste or cooling hit sells by watching someone feel it.

**How to apply:** For fashion, test 2 to 3 second wordless sensory openers (fabric handled, a pack opened) and rotating hauls of three or four outfits, which also survive single-item stockouts. Test problem-led scripts on the men's range first. For gadgets, show the mechanism within 2 seconds under a bold text hook, with the benefit clear by second 4. For reaction videos, judge them on revenue per 1,000 views, not view count; one went to 23 million views at under $2 per 1,000.

**Judge it by:** The opener style fits how buyers in the category actually choose. Weak work forces a talking-head problem script onto a product sold on look.

**Breaks when:** The benefit is delayed by weeks (nothing to react to), or the wordless clip leaves a stranger unsure what is being sold.

**From:** MCR-6868, MCR-6827, MCR-6826, MCR-6805, MCR-0045, MCR-0046, MCR-6830

### 27. Edit for pace: change the picture every 1.5 to 3 seconds, except where one face carries trust

**Why it works:** Editing is not decoration. The same winning script shot as one static talking head would fail, because frequent new visuals make a video feel organic and easy to keep watching. But in story and testimonial ads, constant product cutaways remind the viewer it is an ad.

**How to apply:** Put the pace in the brief, not only in the editor's taste. In problem-solution and demo ads, plan B-roll for every 1.5 to 2 seconds of voiceover: the problem happening, the product close up, the person in context. Flag any shot that runs about 10 to 12 seconds with no change, and cut or overlay it. In story or founder ads, hold on the speaker and test delaying B-roll to around second 20. Never let B-roll show the speaker out of sync.

**Judge it by:** A reviewer scrubbing the timeline finds a change at least every 3 seconds in demo ads, and steady holds in story ads. Weak edits use one pace for every format.

**Breaks when:** Premium brand films where slow pace is part of the positioning, or long story ads where trust comes from watching one person speak.

**From:** MCR-6800, MCR-0077, MCR-6624, MCR-6768

### 28. Make every video work with the sound off and keep every word inside the safe zone

**Why it works:** Many people scroll with sound off, and app buttons, captions and profile names cover the edges of a vertical frame. A key line under the caption bar or a price behind the like button is never seen.

**How to apply:** Burn in captions for all speech, and give every video review a text headline. TikTok's guidance asks for 9:16, at least 720p, sound or music, the message in the first 3 seconds, and on-screen text at about 5 to 10 words a second. On Meta Stories and Reels, keep about 14% of the top, 35% of the bottom and 6% of each side free of text and logos. TikTok publishes overlay files rather than fixed pixels, so check each export against the current template. Make 9:16, 4:5 and 1:1 versions where the placement needs them.

**Judge it by:** Watched muted, the video still makes its claim. Overlaid on the app's template, no text or logo is covered. Weak work relies on voice alone and puts the offer at the bottom edge.

**Breaks when:** Music-led trend formats where sound is the hook; still caption the claim.

**From:** MCR-6040, LPG-0049, ORG-0016, https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices, https://ads.tiktok.com/business/library/Auction_Ads_Creative_Tips.pdf

### 29. Use AI to draft, vary and dramatise, never to fake a demo, a result or a customer

**Why it works:** AI is strong at retrieving research, moving a winning script into a new persona or tense, cutting to length and making variants. It drifts to the average when asked for ideas. Viewers who spot a faked demo or testimonial turn on the brand, and believers who were misled become refunds and chargebacks; US rules ban fake and AI-made reviews and testimonials.

**How to apply:** Keep ideation, selection and final analysis human. Feed the model only the brand's research and proven scripts. Use AI video to show the problem, the story or an invisible mechanism; film the real product for any demo. Ban 'it's not X, it's Y' lines, which viewers read as AI copy. Label realistic synthetic content where the platform asks: YouTube requires it for a real person shown saying what they did not, and Meta adds an AI label when it detects AI signals.

**Judge it by:** Every product-in-use shot shows the real product, and every testimonial is a real customer. Weak work has an AI avatar 'trying' the product.

**Breaks when:** The format is plainly a cartoon or explainer that no viewer could mistake for a real event.

**From:** MCR-6809, MCR-6869, OPS-6610, OPS-6611, MCR-0016, MCR-6045, MCR-6064, MCR-6063

### 30. Clear disclosure, consent and music rights before export, not after a flag

**Why it works:** A video that is muted, pulled or ruled misleading sells nothing, and the brand carries the liability. US and UK regulators want paid relationships made clear at the start of a video, in plain words, not only in a platform toggle or the description.

**How to apply:** For any paid, gifted or employee endorsement, put 'ad' or 'sponsored' on screen and say it early in the video; the FTC wants both, and the UK ASA wants the label before people view the content. Endorsers must have really used the product, and an exceptional result needs the typical result shown. Use only audio cleared for the account type: TikTok business accounts are limited to its Commercial Music Library or music the brand licenses itself, and YouTube's Audio Library gives no guidance for use off YouTube. One TikTok analysis found licensed trending audio lifted ads about 30 to 40%. Get written consent from everyone on camera. A store selling in India should ask Ishita for local labelling rules.

**Judge it by:** The file log shows disclosure placement, track licence and consent per person. Weak work adds '#collab' at the end of the caption.

**Breaks when:** Rules differ by market and change often; recheck the regulator's current guidance for each country the ad runs in.

**From:** MCR-6060, ORG-6405, MCR-6025, MCR-6065, MCR-6837, https://www.asa.org.uk/static/9cc1fb3f-1288-405d-af3468ff18277299/INFLUENCERGuidanceupdatev6HR.pdf, https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/commercial-music-library

## Working from the org context

- Read the buyers' own words first. Pull the persona phrases, review lines and complaint wording into the hook and problem beats; if a line has no buyer source, mark it as a guess and test it.
- Read what ran and won. Every past winning video gets a revive plan (new openers, new face or scene) before any new concept is written.
- Read the awareness spread of live ads and aim new scripts at the stages that are thin.
- Keep the voice: match reading level, contractions, humour and words the brand never uses. Keep spoken scripts at or below the brand's reading level, and lower for general buyers.
- Keep the design system in every frame: fonts and colours for captions and text overlays, logo rules, and the settings and textures the brand lives in.
- Use only approved claims and product facts, word for word, and the current offer and guarantee; carry the site's guarantee into the ad right before the ask.
- Check the creator roster and rights on file: who is cleared for partnership ads, for how long, in which markets.
- Where the brand does something that the evidence says costs sales (product in the first frame of cold ads, one format only, no captions), keep it in the control and propose the change as a side-by-side test with one line of why and one number.
- Write back what you learn: winning hooks, buyer phrases that held, and lengths that worked go to the org context after the job.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Does the brief name the buyer, the awareness stage and the load-bearing beat?
- [ ] Can a stranger say what the video is about and who it is for within 3 seconds?
- [ ] Is the opener aimed at the buyer rather than a bait opener that grabs everyone?
- [ ] Do the spoken line, on-screen text, first frame, primary text and landing page top make one claim?
- [ ] In a cold ad, is the product kept out of the first 2 to 3 seconds unless the demo is the hook?
- [ ] Does the promise come before the mechanism, in one sentence?
- [ ] Does each claim have proof shown on screen, and is every claim smaller than its proof?
- [ ] Does the close name the action, the next step, the effort and a true reason for now, with the site's guarantee said just before the ask?
- [ ] Do the key lines trace to real buyer words in the org context or research?
- [ ] Has every adjective been replaced or backed by a specific, and every line a rival could say been cut?
- [ ] Does the script fit its length, with each cutdown written as its own script?
- [ ] Does each body ship with 3 to 5 openers that differ in angle, persona, offer or format?
- [ ] Does the script read aloud cleanly at a grade 5 to 7 level, with no 'it's not X, it's Y' lines?
- [ ] Does the edit pace fit the format, with no unchanged shot over about 10 seconds in demo ads?
- [ ] Muted, does the video still make its claim with burned-in captions?
- [ ] Is every word and logo inside the current safe zone for each placement and ratio?
- [ ] Are disclosure, consent, music licence and any AI label settled and logged?
- [ ] Is the video judged on spend and store cost per purchase, with hook and hold used only to place the leak?
- [ ] Is every product demo the real product, and every testimonial a real customer?

## Common failures

- Opening on the product, the logo or 'hi, I'm the founder' in a cold ad. Fix: open on the buyer's problem or belief and bring the product in once they know why it matters.
- Bait openers that win hook rate and lose money. Fix: rewrite the hook so only the buyer would stop, and judge it on cost per purchase.
- Ingredient lists posing as a mechanism. Fix: state the promise first, then one named reason it works, shown on screen.
- Twenty openers that are one line reworded. Fix: 3 to 5 openers that change angle, persona, offer or format, each landing cleanly on the body.
- A long ad chopped into a short one. Fix: write each length from its own beat plan.
- Every video a creator talking to camera. Fix: move each winning concept into animation, demo, static and story formats.
- Generic, adjective-heavy AI scripts that sound like the product page. Fix: feed the model buyer research and proven scripts, keep selection human and run the 'so what' and swap tests.
- Voice-only videos with text behind the app buttons. Fix: burned-in captions, safe-zone overlays checked per ratio, the claim readable with sound off.
- Fake AI demos or AI 'customers'. Fix: film the real product, use real customers, and use AI only to dramatise the problem or mechanism.
- Killing a profitable video for a low hook rate, or keeping a pretty one that does not sell. Fix: rank spend and store cost per purchase first, attention metrics last.
- Throwing away a tired winner. Fix: new openers and a new face or scene on the proven body before any new concept.
- Disclosure tucked at the end or only in the toggle, and music the account cannot use. Fix: label on screen and spoken at the start, cleared tracks only, consent on file.
