# Visual design for commerce: craft principles

This book is for everyone who designs what a shopper sees before they buy: static and carousel ads, product page galleries, marketplace images, store layouts, packs and labels. Read it before the job, design to it, and run the review checklist on every draft before hand-over. The skill tells you what to make; this book tells you what great looks like and how to tell.

## What great looks like

- A stranger can say what is sold, for whom and why it matters within 3 to 4 seconds, or the design is a deliberate curiosity gap that sends them to a page that teaches.
- The first thing the eye lands on carries the most broadly appealing message, not the product name or the logo.
- Every image in a gallery answers one named buyer doubt, in the order buyers raise them, and one of them shows true size.
- One accent colour is kept for the buy action, and the main call to action still stands out when the screenshot is blurred.
- Every word passes contrast and size checks on a phone at arm's length, and nothing important sits in a placement's cropped zone.
- The product shown is the product shipped: same colour, shape, contents and scale.
- Each new design in a batch changes the persona, the angle or the offer; colour swaps are not counted as new ads.
- Main images pass each marketplace's rules on the first upload, and page images load fast enough not to cost sales.
- The brand is recognisable from its colours, shapes and type even with the logo covered.
- Packs survive the trip, cost what the margin allows and carry every required mark where the rules put it.

## The principles

### 1. Design the eye path first: put the broadest message where the eye lands, and steer it with size, contrast and white space

**Why it works:** The eye jumps from spot to spot, tracing a path. If the first stop says 'protein powder', only people already shopping for it keep looking; a broad, intriguing first message pulls colder buyers through the awareness stages one stop at a time.

**How to apply:** Before any pixel work, sketch the path and label each stop: hook, problem, promise, mechanism, then proof, offer and call to action. Keep the first four in the ad; move proof and offer to the landing page when they will not fit. Make the first stop the largest, highest-contrast element with the most space around it. Example: a US hydration brand leads with 'the drink your coach never mentioned', not the product name, and the pack sits at the third stop.

**Judge it by:** Ask someone new to the draft what they saw first, second and third; if the product name or logo comes first, redesign. Weak work is a centred pack shot, a logo on top and a tagline.

**Breaks when:** The audience already knows the product (retargeting, existing customers); there, leading with the product and offer is the right first stop.

**From:** MCR-6882, MCR-6690, LPG-0010

### 2. Commit every static to one end of the line: total clarity in 3 to 4 seconds, or a deliberate curiosity gap that sends people to a page that teaches

**Why it works:** A clear static pre-qualifies: anyone who clicks already knows what is sold, to whom, and the offer. A curiosity static makes people read the copy or click to find out. The middle, half teasing and half explaining, neither intrigues nor informs, and it is where most failed statics sit.

**How to apply:** Write on the brief which end you are designing for. For clarity, a stranger names product, buyer, angle and offer after four seconds. For curiosity, give almost nothing away in the image (a native screenshot, a strange visual) and send it to a listicle, advertorial or educational page, never a bare product page. Example: a UK sleep brand's static of an odd bedside object goes to a 'five things I changed' article.

**Judge it by:** Ask a stranger what the ad is for. Clear ads get a correct answer; curiosity ads get 'I want to know'. A muddled ad gets a guess and a shrug.

**Breaks when:** A famous logo can partly rescue a muddled design; placements that hide the copy force the image to be clear alone.

**From:** MCR-6879, MCR-0019, MCR-0024, LPG-6632

### 3. Make each new design change the persona, the angle or the offer; never count a colour, pose or layout swap as a new ad

**Why it works:** Meta's Andromeda system groups near-identical ads and serves them to the same people, so a red-to-blue background swap buys no new reach. Its 1 to 2% effect cannot be measured without large split tests. New reach comes when a different buyer, reason to buy or deal is on the page.

**How to apply:** Before designing, write the persona, angle and offer at the top of each artboard. If three statics share all three, they are one concept in three layouts. When a format wins, keep the format and change one of the three. Example: an Australian supplement brand's winning comparison layout gets a longevity angle and a new parent persona, not a green background.

**Judge it by:** Cover the visuals and read the three labels. A batch where every label is different is a real batch; a batch of one concept in several colours is padding.

**Breaks when:** Very large accounts can afford split tests on visual elements and have a reason to act on small gains; catalogue and retargeting refreshes may only need to fight fatigue among the same audience.

**From:** MCR-0022, MCR-6885, MCR-6877, MCR-6886

### 4. Treat the product page gallery as the sales pitch: one buyer doubt per image, in the order buyers raise them, with thumbnails people can see

**Why it works:** On phones, more shoppers swipe the whole gallery than scroll the whole page. Baymard found 56% of users start a product page by exploring the images.

**How to apply:** Mine reviews, tickets, survey answers and return reasons for doubts, then rank them. Give each top doubt one image: what it is, the main benefit, how it works, what is in the box, true size, detail, proof. Show real thumbnails, not dots or a '1 of 5' counter: in one audit, tiny dots still drew 7.35% of clicks and the colour picker 16.68%, so the picker moved beside the main image. Give each colour its own true-colour image. Example: a Canadian cookware brand's slot 4 answers 'is it heavy?' with the pan hanging from one finger and its weight printed beside it.

**Judge it by:** Can you name the doubt each slot answers? Weak galleries are six studio angles of the same object.

**Breaks when:** Fashion and design-led products where shoppers expect clean imagery and text overlays look cheap; very technical goods where specs must come first.

**From:** LPG-0060, LPG-0034, LPG-0043, GAD-6043, Baymard, product image resolution and zoom (2020), https://baymard.com/blog/ensure-sufficient-image-resolution-and-zoom

### 5. Build the first mobile screen to answer four questions in this order: what it is, why this one, why now, why trust you

**Why it works:** About half of visitors leave right after the hero, so the first screen is the page for most people. Anything there that does not help the decision slows the yes.

**How to apply:** List every element on the first phone screen and tag it what, why this, why now or why trust. Order by persuasive weight: rating and review count above the title, a benefit headline, a sub-line naming the mechanism, price and saving, one add-to-cart button, then risk reducers directly under the button. Move anything untagged below the fold. Default buyers into the bigger size or bundle rather than showing a picker. End the screen with a fade or 'more below' cue so people scroll. Example: a US skincare page swaps 'Face serum 30ml' for an outcome headline, with the overnight mechanism underneath.

**Judge it by:** On a real phone, without scrolling, can a stranger answer all four questions? Weak work is the default theme layout: product name, price, variant picker and nothing else.

**Breaks when:** Apparel and footwear, where size choice is part of the decision; well-known brands whose visitors arrive already sold.

**From:** LPG-6617, LPG-6618, LPG-0034, LPG-0038, LPG-0051

### 6. Show true size and fit in the images themselves, never only in a size chart

**Why it works:** Baymard found 42% of users try to judge size from product images, yet its 2022 benchmark found 83% of desktop and 87% of mobile apparel sites gave too little sizing information. Buyers who guess wrong leave, or order and return, which costs the sale and the shipping twice.

**How to apply:** Give every product at least one in-scale image: in a hand, on a person with their height stated, beside a common object, or on a counter. For apparel, show the model's height and size worn, with measurements in inches and centimetres. If the top return reason is 'smaller than expected', the in-scale image is the first fix. Example: a UK bag brand shows each bag on a 170 cm model with 'fits a 13 inch laptop' on the image.

**Judge it by:** Could a buyer guess the size within 20% from the images alone? Weak work shows the product floating on white with nothing to compare it to.

**Breaks when:** One-size goods where scale is obvious (a phone case for a named model); these still need a fit or use image.

**From:** CXP-6161, MCR-6830, Baymard, in-scale product images, https://baymard.com/blog/in-scale-product-images

### 7. Build the main marketplace image to the platform's clean-image rule, and do the selling in the secondary images and A+ modules

**Why it works:** Search results and feeds show the main image, and a rule break gets it suppressed or disapproved, which costs more sales than any design gain. Platforms allow infographics, scale and comparison in the secondary slots, so persuasion belongs there.

**How to apply:** For Amazon's main image, use a pure white background (RGB 255, 255, 255), the product filling about 85% of the frame, no added text, logos, badges or props, and at least 1,000 px on the long side so zoom works. Google Merchant Center bans overlays, watermarks, borders and added logos on the main image and wants the product to fill 75 to 90% of the frame. A+ content may not show prices, shipping, warranty text, 'best-selling' claims or QR codes. Put infographics, in-use, scale and contents in the secondary slots. Recheck live rules first.

**Judge it by:** Does the main image pass every rule, and does each secondary slot answer a doubt? Weak work puts '50% off' on the main image and fills the rest with white-background angles.

**Breaks when:** Some categories have their own exceptions; other marketplaces set different rules.

**From:** GAD-6043, AMZ-6446, Amazon Seller Central, product image requirements, https://sellercentral.amazon.com/help/hub/reference/external/G1881

### 8. Show the claim happening on screen instead of stating it in words

**Why it works:** Demonstration sits at the top of the proof ladder, above third-party verification, review counts and testimony. When the viewer sees the difference, persona and angle land in seconds without a line of copy, and the image is believed in a way a headline is not.

**How to apply:** For each claim, ask what it would look like if a camera caught it: a side-by-side of a full dose against a rival's thin scoop, water beading on the fabric, the bag holding a 2 litre bottle. Make the opening visual the topic itself, with the action happening, never unrelated stock B-roll under a strong headline. In video, cut or overlay any shot that runs about 12 seconds with no change. Example: an EU cleaning brand's static splits one tile, half scrubbed with its paste, half with a leading spray.

**Judge it by:** Mute and hide the text: does the image still prove the point? Weak work is a pretty product shot with the claim typed over it.

**Breaks when:** The benefit cannot be shown honestly on camera (slow results, internal health effects), where animation or illustration must carry the mechanism, and policy limits what can be implied.

**From:** MCR-6624, MCR-6712, ORG-0017, MCR-6705

### 9. Put clarity of message above polish, and hold every ad to a defined 7 out of 10 bar rather than to taste

**Why it works:** What makes an ad look like an ad is marketing language, not production quality. Rough native statics have held about $250,000 in spend because viewers grasped the problem and solution fast. Once ads clear a quality bar, nobody reliably picks winners, so volume above the bar beats perfecting a few.

**How to apply:** Write the brand's 7 out of 10 bar as checks a reviewer can tick: message clear or curiosity deliberate, product accurate, type readable, brand cues present, inside safe zones. Ship everything that passes. Keep a mix: native-looking openers (screenshots, notes, lo-fi) do most prospecting, polished hero shots lift perceived value. Lowering resolution does not make a polished ad feel native; rewrite the words. Example: a US pet brand runs a phone note listing what its vet said.

**Judge it by:** Does the batch pass the written bar, and are reviews about message rather than kerning? Weak work is three over-polished ads a month.

**Breaks when:** Luxury and high-fashion positioning that requires polished creative only; keep native formats inside the brand voice or accept slower prospecting.

**From:** MCR-6887, MCR-6728, MCR-6710, MCR-6661, MCR-6841

### 10. Pick one scroll-stop route per static, native or pattern interrupt, and test it in a mock feed

**Why it works:** A static must stop the scroll first, one of two ways. Native: it looks like the posts around it (a notes screenshot, a text thread), so people read it as feed. Pattern interrupt: it looks unlike anything near it, so the eye stops. A rival's format gives little edge; a familiar format no rival uses yet wins until copied.

**How to apply:** Write the route on the brief. For native, copy a real post's look in that placement and write like a person; language, not polish, gives an ad away. For interrupt, screenshot the main rivals' live statics in the Meta Ad Library and pick a colour, layout or subject none of them use. Example: where rivals all show product on white, a UK coffee brand runs a handwritten receipt of a year's café spending.

**Judge it by:** Drop the draft into a feed screenshot between organic posts and rival ads: it should blend with the posts or jump out from all of them. Weak work looks like the rival ads.

**Breaks when:** Native looks that mislead about who is speaking (fake news pages, fake notifications) breach ad policy; novelty fades once copied.

**From:** MCR-6875, MCR-6728, MCR-6887

### 11. Design proof so it reads as real: names, faces and sources on reviews, a quote under every press logo, two kinds of proof in the first screen

**Why it works:** Shoppers discount bare proof: a row of press logos reads as padding, and an unnamed review could be invented. Specific proof matched to the claim works; five reviews about the exact benefit beat 400 saying 'great product'.

**How to apply:** Show reviewer name, a photo of them or the product in use, and where the review was left; never hide reviews in auto-rotating carousels. Put a one-line quote under each press logo; one client reported a 38% lift from that alone. Head the review block with an outcome and a count, not 'Reviews'. Place two proof types above the fold, such as a rating line at the top and a testimonial by the button. Example: a German posture brand tags reviews by back, neck and desk use, and each page shows the matching set.

**Judge it by:** Could a sceptic verify each proof item? Weak work is stars with no count, logos with no quotes and stock faces.

**Breaks when:** New brands with no genuine reviews (collect first, never invent); intimate categories where reviewers share no photos, so verified-buyer badges must do.

**From:** LPG-0037, LPG-0036, LPG-0049, LPG-6620, MCR-6714

### 12. Make the headline 3 to 4 times the body size and reserve one accent colour for the buy action, then prove it with a blur test

**Why it works:** Many visitors read only the headline, so a headline barely larger than the body text gives the value proposition no weight. When the accent colour appears on badges, icons and banners too, the add-to-cart button stops being the obvious thing to press.

**How to apply:** With 16 px body text, set the headline around 48 to 57 px on desktop; on a phone, keep the ratio but cut words so it wraps to three lines at most. Use one contrasting brand accent only for the primary button and the announcement bar. Blur a screenshot until text is unreadable: the call to action must still be the clear target. Fix hierarchy before copy, offer and proof, as it is the quickest win. Example: a US candle store moves its coral accent off its icons and onto the button.

**Judge it by:** In the blurred screenshot, where does the eye go? Weak work has three buttons in the accent colour and a headline that looks like body copy.

**Breaks when:** Long headlines on narrow phones push the button down; editorial brands may carry hierarchy through weight instead.

**From:** LPG-0010, CRO-6615

### 13. Treat on-image text as its own hook layer, and pair the image and headline like a thumbnail and its title

**Why it works:** A hook has three layers that change separately: visual, audio or script, and on-screen words. A bolder overlay alone has doubled hook rate and results on the same footage. On statics, headline and primary text deserve as much effort as the image.

**How to apply:** Before reshooting a tired ad, test new overlay lines on the same image; one practitioner weights a hook about 80% on its opening copy, 15% on the visual and 5% on audio. Keep the overlay to one idea at a 5th to 8th grade reading level. Pair image and headline so each raises a question the other answers. Use the buyer's words from reviews, not the pack label. Example: an Irish haircare brand overlays 'I could see my scalp in photos' instead of 'hair thinning formula'.

**Judge it by:** Does each version have its own overlay and headline pair? Weak work repeats the image's words in the headline.

**Breaks when:** Overlays claim what the product cannot support, breaching ad policy and consumer law; Stories and Reels hide most text, so the image carries the message.

**From:** MCR-6743, MCR-6884, MCR-0063, MCR-6731

### 14. Show only the real product, exactly as it ships; use AI image tools for parts of a scene, never for the product itself

**Why it works:** Buyers who receive something that does not match the image return it, charge it back and never buy again. Viewers who spot a fake AI demo turn on the brand. In fashion, AI images drift from what the garment really looks like and return rates rise.

**How to apply:** Build from real product photos matching the current batch in pack, colour, texture, contents and scale. Use AI for backgrounds, settings or a hand holding the product, then place the real product photo and set the type yourself, since generated text still has typos. Check every file for logo, label text, colour against the approved sample, item count and size. Example: a UK supplement brand's AI kitchen scene keeps the real bottle photo composited in.

**Judge it by:** Put the image beside the real product: would a buyer feel misled? Weak work has a generated pack with garbled label text or a garment in a colour that does not exist.

**Breaks when:** Clearly stylised animation that no viewer reads as a real demo; newer tools may render products reliably, so check rather than assume.

**From:** MCR-6732, MCR-6833, MCR-6809

### 15. Supply feed images at 4:5 and make them stand out from rivals within the rules, because the image fills about 70% of a Shopping listing

**Why it works:** Shopping has no keywords; price, title and main image drive most of the click. Shoppers see five rivals side by side, so an image that looks like every other white cut-out wins only on price. Tall 9:16 site images get auto-cropped by Google, often badly.

**How to apply:** Send 4:5 or 1:1 images through a supplementary feed instead of letting 9:16 photos crop. Test a lifestyle or soft-background image against plain white on your best sellers; one agency reports lifestyle won on click rate in about 90% of its tests. Treat the feed image like an ad hook: stop the right shopper, and judge on conversion, not only click rate. Keep the product large so it reads at thumbnail size. Example: a US activewear brand tests leggings on a model mid-stretch against the flat lay every rival uses.

**Judge it by:** In a screenshot of the real results page, does the eye land on your listing? Weak work is the site's 9:16 hero, cropped through the model's head.

**Breaks when:** Main-image rules require a plain background; badges or text risk disapproval.

**From:** GAD-6174, GAD-0019, GAD-6225, GAD-0054

### 16. Design to each placement's safe zones: a 9:16 master with words, logo and price clear of the top 14%, bottom 35% and 6% each side

**Why it works:** On Stories and Reels the profile icon, caption and button sit on top of the creative. Text there gets covered or cropped, so the ad pays for impressions that carry half a message.

**How to apply:** Build a 9:16 master at 1080 x 1920 and keep about 270 px at the top, 672 px at the bottom and 65 px on each side free of words, logo, price and the product's key detail; the background may run to the edges. Make a separate 4:5 (1080 x 1350) for feeds rather than cropping the master. Carousel cards are 1:1 with headlines around 20 characters, so each card image carries its beat. On Google responsive display, leave text off the image. Overlay a safe-zone template on every export before hand-over.

**Judge it by:** With the template on, is any word or the product's key detail inside a shaded zone? Weak work centres a headline at the very bottom of a story, under the button.

**Breaks when:** Platforms change their guides; older guides quote 20% at the bottom, so recheck the live spec before a large batch.

**From:** MCR-6040, MCR-6041, GAD-6042

### 17. Make every word pass contrast and size minimums on a phone, and never use colour alone to carry meaning

**Why it works:** Text people cannot read does not sell, and hard-to-read type also feels less true: in one study, statements in easy-to-read colours were judged true more often. About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have a colour vision deficiency, so a colour-only 'sale' cue disappears for them.

**How to apply:** Hold body text to at least 4.5:1 contrast, and large text (about 24 px, or 19 px bold), buttons and icons to at least 3:1, the WCAG 2.2 minimums. On images, set text on a calm area or a shaded band. Pair every colour cue with a word or icon. Make tap targets at least 24 px square (the WCAG 2.2 minimum), and 44 px where space allows. Check exports at phone size, then in greyscale.

**Judge it by:** Run a contrast checker on every text and button pair. Weak work sets thin grey type on a pale photo.

**Breaks when:** Decorative text with no meaning; some palettes need a darker tint for text, which you propose, not impose.

**From:** W3C, WCAG 2.2 (1.4.1, 1.4.3, 1.4.11), https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/; Reber and Schwarz (1999), https://doi.org/10.1006/ccog.1999.0386; Colour Blind Awareness, https://www.colourblindawareness.org/colour-blindness/

### 18. Choose the format after the concept, by what has to be shown: one frame, a sequence, a face, or something invisible

**Why it works:** A talking face carries proof but cannot show the unseen; animation shows an invisible mechanism in a set order; a long-form native static reads most like a person but has no sequence or sound. A format that cannot show the key beat wastes the concept.

**How to apply:** Ask four questions: does the idea need teaching, can the value fit one frame, does the product need a demo, which awareness stage is it for? One frame and product aware: a static. Several steps (a routine, a bundle): a carousel whose cards run problem, proof, product, offer. Invisible mechanism: animation or a diagram. For statics, put problem, native and founder formats at the top of the funnel; comparisons and feature call-outs in the middle; offer, review and hero shots at the bottom. Example: a Dutch gut-health brand animates its capsule dissolving instead of filming a founder explaining it.

**Judge it by:** Can you say why this format beats the others here? Weak work makes everything a square product shot.

**Breaks when:** A new format with a different mix of properties appears; score it fresh rather than assuming.

**From:** MCR-6662, MCR-6705, MCR-6883, MCR-6041

### 19. Build the brand so it is recognisable with the logo covered: a few distinctive colours, shapes and type used the same way everywhere

**Why it works:** Buyers glance at ads and packs and rarely read the logo. Consistent non-logo cues link the ad to the brand anyway, so paid attention builds memory. An asset works only if buyers link it to you (fame) and to no rival (uniqueness), an idea from Jenni Romaniuk.

**How to apply:** From the org context, pick two or three assets for every piece (a colour, a pack silhouette, a type style); drop any the category leader already owns. Put at least one at the first eye stop. Change angle, persona and offer freely, but never rotate the assets. For new looks, borrow from industries far from the category, not from rivals.

**Judge it by:** Line up ten recent ads and packs with logos hidden: do they look like one brand? Weak work changes palette and type every campaign or copies the category leader's look.

**Breaks when:** A new brand has no assets yet (pick them now and hold them); native formats carry fewer cues by design.

**From:** BRD-0003, MCR-6875, Jenni Romaniuk, Building Distinctive Brand Assets (idea source)

### 20. Use lo-fi footage for the problem and premium shots for the product; never put the product inside the rough footage

**Why it works:** Lo-fi openers feel native, and they only cheapen a premium brand if the product appears in them. Rough footage of the problem followed by a jump to high-end product shots reads as contrast: the old way looks grim, the new way looks better than it would alone.

**How to apply:** Shoot the problem or the before state on a phone, in real places, with your product kept out of frame. Reveal it in clean, premium shots with material and detail visible. In statics, use two panels: phone-shot problem left, studio product right. Example: a premium oral-care brand opens on a rival brush's mouldy base shot lo-fi, then cuts to its own brush in aluminium on stone.

**Judge it by:** Is there any lo-fi frame with your product in it? Is the jump in quality obvious at the reveal? Weak work films the hero product on a cluttered desk under bad light.

**Breaks when:** Brand guidelines or retail partners require premium visuals throughout; impulse or gadget products whose hook is the product working in the first seconds; footage that makes claims about named rivals you cannot support.

**From:** MCR-6769, MCR-0010, MCR-6710

### 21. Keep before-and-after and body imagery small and indirect, and use illustration for sensitive conditions

**Why it works:** Large, blunt before-and-after shots tend to raise CPMs or get ads disapproved on Meta, and graphic photos of health conditions draw reduced delivery. An illustration, or a small inset that flashes between states, still gives the viewer the proof while staying inside what platforms usually allow.

**How to apply:** Put the comparison in a small corner box or alternate the two states quickly rather than filling the frame. For hair loss, skin conditions or other personal topics, use an illustration or cartoon with a headline naming the persona and problem in few words, and never imply the viewer has the condition ('your hair loss'). Check the category's ad policy before designing: in weight loss and some health claims, even a small inset breaches the rules. Example: an EU haircare brand uses a drawn figure and the line 'Postpartum shedding hits hard' instead of a photo of thinning hair.

**Judge it by:** Would the image pass the platform's personal-attributes and health rules as written? Weak work shows a full-frame body transformation with a dramatic arrow.

**Breaks when:** Categories where implying a body result is banned outright; regulated claims still need substantiation whatever the image style.

**From:** MCR-6799, MCR-0020, LPG-6620

### 22. Design store navigation and collection grids for phones: picture menus, four products on the first screen, and labels on only a few items

**Why it works:** Every text link must be read and interpreted before the shopper finds a product; pictures are faster. Big banners that push products below the fold defeat a collection page's job, and labelling every product kills the herd signal labels exist to give.

**How to apply:** Replace text-only menus with image or icon categories (one agency test reported 60.7% higher conversion) and keep about seven visible items. Put a bestsellers row at the top of the menu, since new visitors open it first. On mobile collection pages, show a two by two grid with prices on the first screen and label a few items. Prune elements that get under about 1% of clicks, unless they do a reading job such as shipping notes.

**Judge it by:** On a phone, how many taps and how much reading does it take to reach a product? Weak work opens on a full-screen banner with one product peeking at the bottom.

**Breaks when:** Single-product stores, which should send people straight to the product page; very large catalogues, which need search and filters more than icons; image menus that slow mobile loading.

**From:** LPG-0039, LPG-0059, LPG-0061, LPG-0043, LPG-0033

### 23. Show savings as money in the option label, and make the price block readable at a glance

**Why it works:** Shoppers do not do maths on a product page. A saving in small grey percentage text must be found and worked out; the same saving in money in the option's title is seen at once. Fewer choices also cut hesitation.

**How to apply:** Put the saving in each bundle option's title ('3 pack, save $10'); one agency test reported a 25% conversion lift and about 28% more revenue per visitor. Show at most three options, with the preferred one marked and pre-selected. Set price, saving and button as one block, and show the real total before the cart, including how often a subscription bills. Example: a Canadian coffee brand cuts five pack sizes to three and labels them '1 bag', '3 bags, save $12' and '6 bags, save $30'.

**Judge it by:** Can a shopper tell in two seconds which option is the best deal and what they will pay? Weak work shows five options, percentage savings and a per-month price that jumps in the cart.

**Breaks when:** Luxury brands where showing discounts undercuts the position; marketplaces with their own price display rules.

**From:** LPG-0008, LPG-0035, LPG-0006, LPG-6622

### 24. Design images to load fast: eager-load the first image, keep heroes under 200 KB and product images under 100 KB, upload near 2048 px

**Why it works:** The hero or first gallery image is usually the largest element a phone paints, so it sets how fast the page feels. Many themes lazy-load or fade in that first image too, which delays it.

**How to apply:** Export WebP or AVIF about 100 to 300 px larger than the box it shows in, under 200 KB for heroes and 100 KB per product image; Shopify compresses little on upload. Upload product images near 2048 x 2048 px, not 5000 px. Ask the developer to load the first image eagerly with high priority and no fade-in. Use video embeds that load a thumbnail until tapped. Example: a UK furniture store cuts its 3 MB hero photo to 180 KB WebP with no visible loss.

**Judge it by:** Check file sizes in the hand-over folder, the first image's loading attribute, and that mobile LCP is under 2.5 seconds. Weak work hands over 6 MB camera originals and a hero that fades in.

**Breaks when:** A theme or app replaces Shopify's image handling or loads full-size originals; check what is actually served, not what was uploaded.

**From:** SPD-0002, SPD-0006, SPD-0008, SPD-6185, SPD-0010

### 25. Spend about 80% of static production time on research and concept, keep a person at the concept step, and never start from a template

**Why it works:** Starting from a design template makes the layout the idea, so batches come out looking like everyone else's. Language and image models give every brand the same average concepts; people connect ideas from other industries and the brand's own customer words, which is where distinct ads come from.

**How to apply:** Work in order: research (reviews, tickets, surveys, rival ad libraries), concept (persona, angle, offer, eye path), build, variations, review, launch. Ground each concept in a buyer phrase found outside the brand's own marketing. Use AI for research summaries, layout options and resizing, not for picking the concept. Open the design tool only once the words are written. Example: a US jerky brand's concept comes from a review line about checking sugar on labels, and the static is built as a label close-up with the sugar line circled.

**Judge it by:** Does each design trace back to a named customer phrase and a written concept? Weak work is a batch of polished templates with interchangeable headlines.

**Breaks when:** A quick one-off offer static for a live sale, where speed matters more than a new concept.

**From:** MCR-6881, MCR-6876, MCR-6700

### 26. Size the pack to the margin: price the box in freight and postage, drop-test it, and keep the branded moment to 2 to 5% of product cost

**Why it works:** Packaging costs materials, container space and dimensional weight on every parcel. Transit damage returns as refunds and one-star reviews. A branded opening buyers film can earn repeat orders, but only if it costs less than the profit it adds.

**How to apply:** Work out freight per unit from units per container; at one November 2026 Shanghai to Rotterdam rate, a 10% smaller carton cut ocean freight from $0.58 to $0.53 a unit. Check courier dimensional weight (USPS Priority divides cubic inches by 139 above one cubic foot). Specify an ISTA 1A style drop test (760 mm up to 10 kg, 10 drops from the weakest corner). Budget the unboxing at 2 to 5% of product cost: tissue, sticker and card run under $0.50; a printed box set about $1.50 to $3.00. Give inserts one next step.

**Judge it by:** Does the brief show cost per order, units per carton and the drop test? Weak work is a rigid box that doubles postage.

**Breaks when:** Very cheap items where even $0.50 erodes margin; fragile single parcels that need ISTA 3 series tests.

**From:** COG-6377, OPS-6287, COG-6324, CXP-0008, FIN-0008

### 27. Lay out required label text first, legible and where the rules put it, then design around it

**Why it works:** Goods with missing or illegible origin marks are held at the port and relabelled at the seller's cost, and rules often fix where and how big text goes. Adding marks last means cramming and reprints.

**How to apply:** Get the required list from the compliance owner; block it in first. US imports need the English country of origin, legible and permanent; if a US place name appears, 'Made in' plus the country sits near it in similar size. US packaged food puts net quantity in the bottom 30% of the main display panel, at a type height set by panel area. EU food labels need an x-height of at least 1.2 mm (0.9 mm on small packs). EU products need a batch number and maker address; GB needs the importer's address. India-only label rules go to Ishita (ishita-india-commerce).

**Judge it by:** Measure the smallest required text at print size and check each mark's position. Weak work hides the origin line in 4 pt light grey.

**Breaks when:** Rules differ by product and market and change often; the designer lays out, the compliance owner signs off.

**From:** COG-6370, COG-6385, COG-6388, 21 CFR 101.105, https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-G/section-101.105; EU Regulation 1169/2011, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2011/1169/oj

### 28. Specify colour and artwork for production in codes and measurements, and approve on physical samples, not screens

**Why it works:** A factory fills every gap in a spec with its cheapest or easiest choice. One founder's first big order arrived in three shades of grey under one SKU because no colour standard was given. Screens show colour differently from print and fabric, so a design approved on a monitor can ship wrong.

**How to apply:** Give every printed or dyed element a Pantone code, never 'match body'. State artwork positions in exact measurements from a seam or edge ('40 mm below the top fold'), never 'centred'. Approve in stages: print proof or strike-off, pre-production sample, production sample; keep one golden sample and leave one at the factory. Number every artwork revision. Check product photos against the golden sample before ads use them. Example: a UK apparel brand's tech pack lists the neck print as Pantone 19-4052 at 80 mm wide, 60 mm below the collar seam.

**Judge it by:** Could a factory with no questions make the pack or print exactly? Weak work sends a JPG mockup and the word 'navy'.

**Breaks when:** Buying a factory's existing catalogue item with only a logo change, where a short spec and approved sample are enough.

**From:** COG-6407, COG-0007, COG-0011, COG-0005

### 29. Write specific alt text for every informative image and leave decorative images empty

**Why it works:** Search engines read alt text alongside computer vision and the page to understand an image, and screen-reader users hear it in place of the image. Specific alt text helps the image rank and helps a buyer who cannot see it; keyword-stuffed alt text reads as spam and helps no one.

**How to apply:** For each informative image, write a short description of what it shows and the doubt it answers: 'Linen shirt in sage on a 180 cm model wearing size M', not 'shirt' or a string of keywords. Give decorative images (textures, dividers) empty alt text. Hand over an alt text file with the images. Example: a US cookware gallery's scale image reads '10 inch frying pan beside a dinner plate, showing it is slightly larger'.

**Judge it by:** Read only the alt text for a gallery: could a buyer picture the product and its size? Weak work leaves file names like IMG_4032 or repeats the product title on every image.

**Breaks when:** Marketplaces that ignore or overwrite alt text; there, the image itself must carry the information.

**From:** SEO-6463, W3C, WCAG 2.2 (1.1.1 non-text content), https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/

## Working from the org context

- Read the design system first: logo files and clear space, fonts with sizes, hex colours, approved photo style and any do and don't examples. Use the files given; never redraw a logo or guess a font.
- List the brand's distinctive assets (colours, shapes, characters, type) and choose the two or three every piece will carry. Keep them even when you change everything else.
- Pull the buyers' own words from reviews, tickets and surveys before designing. Headlines, overlays and gallery captions use their phrases; the pack label's words are a last resort.
- Read what ran and won. Keep the winning formats and change persona, angle or offer; note which visual styles lost so you do not repeat them.
- Read the top return and complaint reasons. Size, colour and 'not as pictured' problems point straight at missing in-scale images, colour accuracy or contents shots.
- Check the current offers, prices and stock on the day. A design that shows a sold-out colour or an ended offer is wrong, however good it looks.
- Keep the brand's voice and look on every piece, including native formats: native means the format looks organic, not that the brand disappears.
- Where the brand's current design breaks a principle here (headlines too small, accent colour everywhere, text that fails contrast), propose the change on one asset with the reason and a before and after, and let the owner choose. Never quietly rebuild the brand system.
- Write back what you learned: which visual hooks, gallery orders and layouts won, and any palette or type adjustments the owner approved.

## Review checklist

- [ ] Each design has a written persona, angle and offer, and the batch has more than one concept.
- [ ] The first eye stop carries the hook or the broadest message, not the logo or the product name (unless it is a retargeting piece).
- [ ] Each static is clearly at one end: a stranger names product, buyer and offer in 4 seconds, or the curiosity gap leads to a teaching page.
- [ ] Each static names its scroll-stop route (native or pattern interrupt), and in a mock feed it does not look like the rival ads beside it.
- [ ] The main call to action is the obvious target in a blurred screenshot, and the accent colour is used only for it and the announcement bar.
- [ ] The headline is roughly 3 to 4 times body size and wraps to three lines at most on a phone.
- [ ] All text and button colour pairs pass 4.5:1 (body) or 3:1 (large text, buttons), and no meaning relies on colour alone.
- [ ] No word, logo, price or key product detail sits in the placement's safe zones; 9:16 and 4:5 are separate exports.
- [ ] Every gallery image answers a named buyer doubt, thumbnails are visible, and at least one image shows true size.
- [ ] The first mobile screen answers what, why this, why now and why trust, with risk reducers under the button.
- [ ] Proof shows names, photos and sources; press logos carry a quote; two proof types appear above the fold.
- [ ] The product in every image matches the shipped product in colour, contents, label text and scale.
- [ ] Main marketplace and feed images pass the platform's current clean-image rules.
- [ ] Before-and-after and body imagery follows the platform's health and personal-attributes rules.
- [ ] The brand is recognisable with the logo covered.
- [ ] Images are under the size targets (hero under 200 KB, product under 100 KB) and the first image is not lazy-loaded.
- [ ] Informative images have specific alt text; decorative ones are empty.
- [ ] Packs show cost per order, freight per unit and a drop-test spec, and required label text is at legal size and position.
- [ ] Production files give Pantone codes, exact artwork positions and the sample approval stages.

## Common failures

- A pretty product shot with the logo at the top and a tagline: it only reaches people who already know the brand. Fix: put the hook at the first eye stop and design the path to the action.
- Ten 'new' ads that are one concept in ten colours. Fix: change persona, angle or offer on every design and label each artboard with all three.
- A gallery of six studio angles of the same object. Fix: rank buyer doubts from reviews and returns and give each its own image, including one in-scale shot.
- AI-generated packs with garbled label text or garments in colours that do not exist. Fix: composite the real product photo, set type by hand and run a product accuracy check.
- Headlines and prices placed under the Stories call-to-action button. Fix: overlay the safe-zone template on every export and build a separate 4:5.
- Thin grey text over a busy photo, or a sale shown only in red. Fix: check contrast pairs, add a band behind text and pair every colour cue with a word.
- Text, badges or 'sale' stickers on the main marketplace or feed image, leading to disapproval. Fix: keep the main image clean and sell in the secondary slots.
- Polishing three ads for a week while the account starves. Fix: write a 7 out of 10 bar and ship everything that passes it.
- A muddled static that half teases and half explains, sent to a product page. Fix: pick clarity or curiosity, and route curiosity to a page that teaches.
- A full-frame before-and-after or a graphic photo of a health condition. Fix: use a small inset or illustration and check policy first.
- Packaging that looks great in the mockup but doubles postage or breaks in transit. Fix: price cube and dimensional weight and specify a drop test before artwork.
- Legal label text squeezed in last at unreadable size. Fix: block in required marks at legal size and position before the brand artwork.
