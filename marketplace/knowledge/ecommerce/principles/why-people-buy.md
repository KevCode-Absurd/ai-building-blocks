# Why people buy: craft principles

This book is for everyone on an e-commerce team who makes something a shopper sees: ads, pages, emails, offers, chat replies, packaging and post-purchase messages. It explains what makes a stranger notice, believe, want and pay, and what makes them stop. Read it before the job, write to it, and check the draft against the review checklist before hand-over.

## What great looks like

- Every piece is written to one named buyer in one buying moment, and a reviewer can say who it is for in one line.
- The buyer's problem appears in words a real customer used, traceable to a review, ticket, survey or thread.
- The top "what almost stopped you" reason from real buyers is answered where they decide, usually by the button.
- The opening matches what the buyer already knows, and the piece moves them one step, not five.
- Every claim has proof of the right kind sitting next to it, and no claim is bigger than its proof.
- The offer adds value that costs the brand little, and its break-even is worked out before it runs.
- The price is shown next to a reference the buyer accepts, and the full total holds no surprises.
- Every lever of urgency, scarcity or "was" price is true and could be shown to a regulator.
- The piece reads as help, not as an ad, until the buyer is ready for the ask.
- The win shows up in profit and repeat buying, not only in clicks or first orders.

## The principles

### 1. Sell to one person with one problem in one moment, never to a demographic

**Why it works:** People buy when they see their own situation, not their age bracket. On Meta the creative is the targeting: the system reads the spoken words, copy and images to pick viewers, so a vague buyer gets vague delivery.

**How to apply:** Write each persona as a problem, a desire and a buying trigger. Women 25 to 45 is a label; a consultant who flies twice a week and needs one carry-on outfit for boardrooms and bars is a persona. Build the first list by hand from your last 20 buyers, then let AI expand it and cut to the best five. A self-label repeating in reviews (as a nurse) marks a segment worth its own ad. Hook, body, proof and page must all speak to that person. Once a script wins, swap only the persona, each with its own page.

**Judge it by:** You can name the person, problem and trigger from the work alone. Weak work opens with "hey everyone".

**Breaks when:** The pool is too small; one agency sees a narrow callout saturate after perhaps $10,000, then delivery drifts to worse audiences. Keep a broad belief hook running too.

**From:** AVT-0030, AVT-0031, MCR-6872, AVT-0016, MCR-6675, MCR-6630, MCR-6811, MCR-6612

### 2. Find the desire written down by buyers before you write a word

**Why it works:** Buyers trust language that sounds like their own head. Copy built from what the brand thinks sounds like marketing and gets skipped. AI asked to guess objections returns the average of the internet, not your buyer.

**How to apply:** Rank sources by yield: calls and support tickets, return reasons, reviews, post-purchase surveys, then forums and Reddit. Read reviews by hand and paste exact phrases; automated summaries strip the emotion. Mine 2 to 4 star reviews for objections, and 1 and 5 star reviews for people who spent money on failed fixes. Translate labels into lived words: hair thinning becomes I can see my scalp. If you cannot find the desire outside your own marketing, you have a product description, not a concept. Around 20 conversations usually reach saturation, where themes start repeating; confirm at scale with a survey.

**Judge it by:** Key lines trace to a named source. Weak work uses words like "elevate" that no customer wrote.

**Breaks when:** A new brand has no customers; use competitor reviews and forums, and mark the language as borrowed until buyers confirm it.

**From:** AVT-0028, AVT-0007, AVT-0026, AVT-0027, AVT-0009, MCR-6731, MCR-6700, AVT-0017

### 3. Find the last thing that nearly stopped real buyers, and answer it where they decide

**Why it works:** From Bob Moesta and Clayton Christensen's jobs to be done work: wanting the product is rarely the blocker. Anxiety about choosing wrong and the pull of the old habit stop people who already want it.

**How to apply:** Add a two-question post-purchase survey: what made you buy today, and what almost stopped you? One practitioner sees 85 to 90% completion with no incentive. Add a product page friction survey at about 60% of average session length. Rank near-miss reasons monthly; answer the top one above the fold and by the button, with 3 to 4 reviews. A sandal brand whose buyers doubted the leather rewrote its hero around craftsmanship, which its agency credits with about $77,000 a month. For retargeting, give each non-buyer reason (price, trust, quality, not ready) its own ad; one cookware brand phoned checkout leavers and built an ad on its lifetime warranty.

**Judge it by:** The top near-miss is answered above the fold and by the button. Weak work buries guesses in a bottom FAQ.

**Breaks when:** Too few buyers to survey; a handful of answers misleads, so start from competitor reviews and call it a guess.

**From:** CRO-0018, LPG-0042, AVT-0027, LPG-0005, MCR-6845, MCR-6674, MCR-0043

### 4. Open with what the buyer already knows, and move them one stage at a time

**Why it works:** From Eugene Schwartz's idea of market awareness: a buyer unaware of the problem cannot hear a pitch, and a buyer ready to buy is bored by education. Mixing stages confuses viewer and delivery alike.

**How to apply:** Tag every piece with one stage: unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware or most aware. Keep one stage per ad, or move downward steadily. Put most creative in the first three stages and about 20% in the last two; an account of only bottom-funnel ads stays warm and frequency climbs. Route traffic to match: unaware ads to an advertorial or quiz, persona ads to a persona page, retargeting to the product page. One large supplement brand sent about 137 of 708 ads to advertorials and quizzes, 150 to persona pages and 280 to product pages.

**Judge it by:** The brief states the stage, and hook, body and page all fit it. Weak work pairs a product-aware image with solution-aware copy.

**Breaks when:** A famous brand harvests demand made elsewhere and can assume product awareness, or buyers already understand the category, where solution-aware ads can carry most spend.

**From:** MCR-0056, MCR-6719, MCR-6772, MCR-0057, LPG-6607, LPG-0070, MCR-6620, MCR-6751

### 5. State the promise first, then the mechanism that makes it believable

**Why it works:** People buy an outcome and believe it when they understand why it happens. Leading with jargon loses them; a promise with no reason reads as hype. The mechanism also explains why past fixes failed, turning disappointment into a reason to try again.

**How to apply:** Write the promise in one sentence. Then link each feature as feature, which means the benefit, because of the mechanism: 320 mg saw palmetto, which means you stop losing hair at the part, because it blocks the enzyme. Even a t-shirt has one: merino traps odour in the fibre, not on your skin. Name a root cause the buyer's past fixes ignored, and map each part of the product to that cause, or the page reads as a scam. Avoid four failures: no promise, five promises, a feature posing as a promise, and a promise bigger than the proof.

**Judge it by:** The reviewer finds one promise, one named mechanism and a line on why other fixes failed. Weak work lists ingredients with no "why".

**Breaks when:** The product is simple and already understood, such as a basic tee bought on price; a short benefit line is enough.

**From:** MCR-6711, MCR-6704, MCR-6695, MCR-6706, LPG-0028, MCR-6619, MCR-6819, LPG-6621

### 6. Read how tired the market is, then write one stage beyond it

**Why it works:** Buyers in crowded categories have heard every claim. From Schwartz's idea of market sophistication: the first claim works because it is new, then claims inflate, then buyers stop believing claims and want a mechanism, then bigger mechanisms, and finally they respond to who they become.

**How to apply:** Scan 20 to 30 competitor ads and tag each by stage: plain claim, bigger claim, mechanism, bigger mechanism, identity. Write one stage above the crowd. In a stage 3 market, the strongest formula is dead and it is the follicle, and this hormone shrinks it, is alive. Ads that name what the buyer uses now, show why it fails and offer the switch work well on category-aware buyers. Study the longest-running ads of brands one or two steps bigger than you, not giants; a rival's winner proves it works with their built-up trust, not yours.

**Judge it by:** The brief names the market's stage with examples, and the lead is one stage above it. Weak work repeats the category's tired claim louder.

**Breaks when:** The category is truly new and buyers do not yet understand the basic claim; plain education comes first.

**From:** MCR-6696, MCR-6749, MCR-0034, MCR-6682, MCR-6683, ADC-0001

### 7. Match the proof to the doubt your claim creates, and keep claims smaller than proof

**Why it works:** Proof only closes the gap it is aimed at. A visual promise needs seeing, a medical promise needs someone qualified, and a lifestyle product needs someone admired. Generic badges answer no doubt at all.

**How to apply:** Write down the boldest thing you say, then pick proof by strength: demonstration, third-party verification (lab tests, clinical results, press), volume of specific social proof, named testimony from someone like the buyer, mechanism explanation, and founder assertion last. Replace adjectives with checkable specifics: dry in 40 seconds, we timed it, beats fast absorbing. Replace customers love it with counts from reviews, such as how often the word finally appears. Under each press logo, show the outlet's actual line. Keep proof numbers identical across all live ads.

**Judge it by:** Each claim has proof of the matching type beside it. Weak work makes a skin claim and backs it with a padlock icon.

**Breaks when:** A claim is not allowed whoever says it; change the claim, not the proof. In the US every objective claim needs a reasonable basis before the ad runs, and "tests prove" needs at least that test.

**From:** MCR-6754, MCR-6712, MCR-6713, MCR-6714, LPG-6620, LPG-0037, MCR-6609, MCR-6022

### 8. Handle objections in the order the buyer thinks them

**Why it works:** Doubts arrive in sequence. A guarantee means nothing to someone who thinks the product is not for them, and urgency before belief feels like pressure. Answering in order keeps each answer landing on a ready mind.

**How to apply:** Run the six common objections in this order. Not for me: persona language. It won't work: mechanism plus proof. It won't work for me: proof from people like this buyer, not general proof. Not worth it: the offer, perceived value or the cost of not buying (the medical route for acne means appointments and years of prescriptions). What if I'm wrong: a guarantee. I'll do it later: a true reason to act now. Choose each testimonial to answer one objection, from a different customer type. Pre-empt a known downside through an experience the buyer has had: you will react in week one, everyone does, so the first tube is a low dose.

**Judge it by:** Each section can be labelled with the objection it answers, in order. Weak work opens on the guarantee and a countdown.

**Breaks when:** A 15-second ad has room for only one or two; pick the ones your research shows matter most.

**From:** MCR-6698, LPG-0053, LPG-0027, EML-0033, MCR-6709

### 9. Answer four questions in the first screen: what is it, why this one, why now, why trust you

**Why it works:** A visitor decides in seconds whether to stay. In one practitioner's audits about 60% of visitors never scroll past the hero; in another, a brand's main selling points sat where only about 6% scrolled.

**How to apply:** Replace the product name headline with the outcome (firmer skin by morning, not face serum 30ml). Add a subtitle naming the mechanism, one reason only this product can claim, a true reason to act now, and the rating with review count. Keep price, button and risk reducers within the first mobile screen, with two kinds of proof, such as a rating at the top and a quote by the button. Open the scroll map and move key proof sitting below 50% reach. Fix clarity and hierarchy first, then copy, offer, proof and friction.

**Judge it by:** Cover everything below the first screen; a stranger can still answer all four questions. Weak pages open on a lifestyle image and a brand name.

**Breaks when:** A well-known brand gets visitors who arrive already sold; a plain retail layout can convert fine there.

**From:** LPG-6617, LPG-0041, CRO-0018, LPG-0051, LPG-0034, LPG-0036, LPG-0038, CRO-6615

### 10. Make the deal feel close to cost while you keep the margin

**Why it works:** Shoppers judge an offer by how close it feels to the seller's cost. A deep cut feels real because it hurts the seller. Add-ons with high perceived value and low real cost give that feeling without the loss.

**How to apply:** Before any straight discount, recompute break-even: 30% off a $100 item costing $60 cuts gross margin from $40 to $10 and lifts break-even ROAS from 2.5 to about 7. Then list add-ons that cost a few dollars and are valued highly: a $30 travel set costing $4 to $5 unlocked at a cart threshold, or a $4 bag that turns a one-towel order into two at nearly the same break-even. Pick gifts that help use the product. Frame value in the form that reads biggest: one high-ticket brand's $25 credit pop-up drew three times the opt-ins of a free gift and lifted placed orders from 3.3% to 10.7%.

**Judge it by:** The offer names its perceived value, real cost and break-even. Weak work leads with 10% off.

**Breaks when:** Buyers respond only to price, or nobody wants the add-on. In the US a free-with-purchase offer must rest on the paid item's normal price.

**From:** OFF-6681, OFF-6625, OFF-6684, OFF-0039, OFF-0028, OFF-6633, OFF-6500, OFF-0035

### 11. Protect the price buyers learn from you; constant discounts teach them to wait

**Why it works:** Buyers learn a brand's real price from how often it is on sale. Weekly promotions train people to wait for the code, and full price stops feeling fair.

**How to apply:** Count last year's promotion weeks. Keep straight sitewide discounts to once or twice a year, on high-margin or slow stock. Do the arithmetic first: 25% off a $100 order costing $40 drops gross profit from $60 to $35, so the sale needs ad efficiency up about 50% just to hold profit. Forecast the lift from your own past sales; one agency models 70 to 80% for luxury brands that rarely discount and about 5% for known discounters. Weekly promotions can drag gross margin from 60% to about 45% for good. Set the anchor price at launch; brands that trained buyers on 70 to 80% off lost volume at 25 to 30%. Aspirational brands keep price out of ads.

**Judge it by:** The plan states the discount history and the lift the sale needs. Weak work answers every slow week with a new code.

**Breaks when:** Low price is the positioning and costs are built for it, as in true discount retail.

**From:** OFF-6645, OFF-6685, OFF-6661, OFF-6659, OFF-6667, OFF-6668, OFF-6624, OFF-6697

### 12. Never let the total surprise the buyer

**Why it works:** A shopper who decided at one price and meets a higher one feels tricked. Baymard puts average cart abandonment near 70% across 50 studies; among checkout leavers, extra costs lead (40%), ahead of slow delivery (20%) and card distrust (19%).

**How to apply:** Keep shipping, tax and fees added at checkout under about 10% of order value; one operator's tests found no conversion effect below that. Show the real total before the cart. A monthly price on a plan sold only quarterly, which jumps to $300 to $400 in the cart, gives buyers a reason to leave; judge it on completed orders, not add to cart. US subscription law requires all material terms shown before billing details are taken. Name a subscription in the ad and show how often it ships and bills. On a prepay selector, state the total and timing beside the per-unit price. State free shipping and easy returns in the ad.

**Judge it by:** The ad, page and checkout tell one price story. Weak work reveals fees or billing at the last step.

**Breaks when:** Duties vary by country and cannot be known early; then say so early, with the range.

**From:** CRO-6180, LPG-6614, LPG-0063, LPG-6622, LPG-6624, OFF-6629, OFF-6304, OFF-6669

### 13. Give every price a reference point the buyer accepts

**Why it works:** Nobody judges a price alone. If you do not set the reference (a rival, the cost of the problem, the cost per day), buyers pick the cheapest one they can find.

**How to apply:** Instead of was $89, now $69: anchor to the alternative (one specialist visit costs about $200), reduce to a daily cost (73 cents a day), price the problem (you spent more on two fixes that failed), or make low effort the price (one gummy a day). A premium top tier makes the middle look sensible; show per-serving price on each tier. Show savings as the bigger number: dollars above about $100, percent below. Write buy 2, get 1 free, not 67% off unit three. Thomas and Morwitz found the left digit sways buyers only when leading digits differ: $39 against $40 matters, $47 against $48 barely.

**Judge it by:** The price sits beside an accepted reference, and nobody does maths. Weak work shows a bare price.

**Breaks when:** The reference is not credible (cost per day on luxury). In the US a was price must have been openly offered for a reasonable recent period.

**From:** OFF-6654, OFF-6608, OFF-0017, OFF-0026, OFF-6145, Thomas and Morwitz, the left-digit effect, Journal of Consumer Research 2005, https://doi.org/10.1086/429600

### 14. Offer fewer, finished choices and pre-select the right one

**Why it works:** Each extra option adds work and the fear of picking wrong. A meta-analysis by Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman found choice overload is real when options are complex, the task is hard, preferences are unclear or the buyer wants to decide fast.

**How to apply:** Cut bundle pickers to three. One drinks brand went from five packs (1, 2, 3, 5, 10) to three (1, 3, 5): conversion rose 53% and order value 13.5% (no sample size given, so test it). Pre-select the middle tier, price it per unit against the single, and make it the only offer the ads name. Tie each tier to a real use (one person, the whole family). Space savings so the middle saves as much over the single as the large saves over the middle. For big, anxious purchases, finished options beat a configurator.

**Judge it by:** A buyer can choose at a glance, and the default is the pack you would recommend to a friend. Weak work shows six tiers with small grey percentages.

**Breaks when:** Buyers enjoy choosing, as hobbyists and collectors do, or really need many sizes; cutting options can lose them.

**From:** LPG-0006, OFF-0027, OFF-0018, OFF-0049, LPG-6618, Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, Journal of Consumer Psychology 2015, https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002

### 15. Set every reward threshold just above where orders already cluster, and show the progress

**Why it works:** Once a shopper has decided, a visible next goal turns spending more into progress toward a reward, and small adds priced well below the main item feel trivial. A threshold below the usual basket gives shipping away for nothing.

**How to apply:** Export orders and plot the count at each order value. Explain each peak (an entry product's price or a common unit count). Set the free shipping line to push the main peak up, such as testing $100 when most orders sit at $80. Use a progress bar with two unlocks, free shipping then a gift. Put one-click add-ons and a bundle that lands just over the line beside the bar. Check carrier cost per order by country first; one store lost about $23 on each US order. Read the result on the order value curve and net shipping profit, not revenue before and after, which mixes in seasonality.

**Judge it by:** The brief names the order peak, the new line and the add-on that bridges the gap. Weak work picks a round number.

**Breaks when:** Most orders are one high-priced item far above any line, or rivals all ship free lower down.

**From:** OFF-6614, LPG-0001, OFF-6665, OFF-6625

### 16. Get the first 80 or so reviews fast, then show the ones that prove the advertised claim

**Why it works:** Reviews tell a stranger that people like them took the risk and it worked. The jump from none to some is the biggest; then relevance beats volume, and a perfect score looks managed.

**How to apply:** Northwestern's Spiegel Research Center found purchase likelihood with five reviews 270% higher than with none, peaking at ratings of 4.0 to 4.7. One operator saw conversion roughly double between 0 and about 80 reviews in his own stores, with no visible gain from 80 to 400. Collect a new product's first 80 with photos or video, sorted to the top. Then show reviews proving the ad's exact claim: five about the benefit beat 400 saying great product. On angle pages, filter reviews to that angle's claim. Head the section with the outcome and a true count (helping 12,000 people sleep through the night).

**Judge it by:** The reviews near the button match the ad's claim. Weak pages sort by newest.

**Breaks when:** Incentives break platform or legal rules. Amazon bans them; the US bans fake or sentiment-conditioned reviews and hiding bad ones.

**From:** LPG-0062, LPG-6616, LPG-0049, MCR-6045, CXP-6301, AMZ-6448, Spiegel Research Center, How Online Reviews Influence Sales, https://spiegel.medill.northwestern.edu/how-online-reviews-influence-sales/

### 17. Sell the length of time the product needs to work

**Why it works:** Buyers who expect results in a week quit at day ten, ask for refunds and never reorder. A time frame set up front makes the result arrive on schedule.

**How to apply:** If results need 90 days, lead with a 90-day pack and say a 30-day pack is set up to fail. Walk a results timeline (day 1, day 9, day 21, each with what to notice); one large supplement brand scaled on this script. Where the benefit shows after about 120 days, sell the 90-day plan and add a free unit on the second order, so the saving lands when doubt peaks. Match subscription cadence and quantity to real use; too much product is the most common cancel reason. End a personalisation quiz by plotting the buyer's own goal on a calendar date.

**Judge it by:** Pack size, guarantee length, timeline copy and cadence all agree on one time frame. Weak work sells a 30-day bottle for a 90-day result.

**Breaks when:** The product works at once, such as a cooling stick or a snack; then show the instant result, not a timeline.

**From:** OFF-6655, OFF-6641, MCR-6607, OFF-0040, LPG-0018, MCR-6616, EML-0005

### 18. Make the guarantee specific, sized to the result and placed under the button

**Why it works:** Fear of being wrong is a cost paid before buying. A specific guarantee that would cost the seller real money signals confidence; a vague 30 days says nothing.

**How to apply:** Set the length to the time the product needs, in the ad's own words: use it for 90 days, and if your part line has not changed, send back the empty tubs for a full refund. A proof-of-use condition sends refunds to real triers, but state it in the ad. Put risk reducers (free returns, guarantee, trial) right under the main button. On cheap consumables, one agency finds a 180-day guarantee brings fewer claims than 30 days, because buyers stop tracking the deadline. Put the answer to the main fear on the bigger tier only, such as a lifetime replacement on the two-pack, so it lifts conversion and order size together.

**Judge it by:** The guarantee names what counts as failure, how long, and what comes back. Weak work has a footer badge.

**Breaks when:** Margins are thin or fraud risk high. In the US a money back guarantee means a full refund on request, with any condition stated clearly in the ad.

**From:** OFF-6653, LPG-0038, LPG-0017, OFF-0060, OFF-6690, OFF-6501, OFF-0064

### 19. Make the first three seconds clear, relevant and new to the right buyer

**Why it works:** Roughly 80% of viewers leave during the hook and about 15% reach a video's second half. Meta learns from who keeps watching, so a broad curiosity hook trains delivery toward people who never buy.

**How to apply:** Score each hook on clarity to a stranger in three seconds, relevance to a real problem and novelty first; specificity (a number or name) and visual credibility are bonuses. Treat it as three layers (visual, spoken words, on-screen text); changing only the overlay can double hook rate. Spend effort on openings: for the same hour, two bodies with 15 hooks usually beat two hooks with five bodies. Check the drop from click rate to link click rate; one satisfying-visual ad hit 10.9% CTR and sold almost nothing, while a 6.3% demo sold.

**Judge it by:** A stranger can say who the hook is for and what it promises. Weak work stops the scroll with something unrelated to the product.

**Breaks when:** The ad is retargeting warm buyers, who need the product and offer fast, not a clever opening; or the body scripts are weak, so one body wins alone and the hook gets false credit.

**From:** MCR-6644, MCR-0077, MCR-0026, MCR-6668, MCR-6741, MCR-6743, MCR-6744, MCR-0029

### 20. Remove doubt about when it arrives and whether it fits

**Why it works:** Will it arrive in time, and will it fit? Doubt on either is a reason to wait, and a wrong guess becomes a return.

**How to apply:** Show a specific delivery date ("arrives Thursday"), padded so you usually beat it and accurate above 90% of the time; vendor studies report conversion lifts of about 13 to 25%, so trust the direction more than the size. If slow delivery drives abandonment, add paid express first, then test showing estimates against hiding them for 7 to 10 days. For apparel, Baymard found only 17% of desktop and 13% of mobile sites give enough sizing information: show sizes in inches and centimetres, how to measure, fit notes by product, model measurements and size worn, and a size guide link beside the size selector. A foreign brand selling into the US should look local: site, currency, copy and returns.

**Judge it by:** The buyer sees a date and a fit answer before the button. Weak work says "ships in 3 to 10 business days" and links a generic size chart.

**Breaks when:** Fulfilment is erratic (made to order, pre-orders, cross-border); a missed precise date harms trust more than an honest range.

**From:** CXP-0010, CRO-6611, CXP-6161, MEA-6266, LPG-6612, GAD-0018

### 21. Earn a stranger's trust with signals a rival cannot copy

**Why it works:** A new buyer assumes risk until shown otherwise. Reviews and badges are table stakes, so they prove little. Trust comes from evidence that is costly or impossible to fake.

**How to apply:** List what only you can show: a science stack (advisory board, completed trials), named experts, real stores, real press with the quote, a founder with standing. One brand saw online sales in Canada rise about 20% after it opened stores there, so show stores in ads where shoppers do not know them. In a new market, assume no one knows you: home campaigns lean on years of awareness, and UK or Australian accents underperform in the US, so build trust from zero with local creators. With no reviews, lean on demonstration and send product to about 20 people for honest, detailed reviews. Keep real, organic-looking demos in the mix.

**Judge it by:** The page carries at least one trust signal no direct rival could post tomorrow. Weak work leans on "secure checkout" and "100% satisfaction" badges.

**Breaks when:** A famous brand already holds trust; then the signals matter less than the offer.

**From:** LPG-6606, MCR-6824, BRD-6490, MCR-6628, MCR-6629, MCR-6684, MCR-6753

### 22. Cut every line the product next to yours could also say

**Why it works:** The buyer is choosing between you and something else. A line a rival could say gives no reason to pick you, and shared claims put you in a group where the cheapest wins.

**How to apply:** Test every differentiator with three questions: is it a real claim, can rivals not say it, and would it move a stranger? Published third-party lab results pass; family owned since 1994 does not. Below the fold, shrink table stakes (vegan, 30 ml, fast dispatch) and give room to the one line no rival can claim. Mine reviews for benefits rivals do not claim. If the factory process is common but no rival talks about it, name it; Claude Hopkins took Schlitz from fifth to first by describing steam-cleaned bottles. Avoid naming the category too early ("we sell electrolytes"), which drops you into price comparison before your mechanism lands.

**Judge it by:** Swap your brand name for a rival's in each line; most lines should stop being true. Weak work survives the swap untouched.

**Breaks when:** You stack two generic benefits to look unique; that only works if the pair meets a real desire.

**From:** OFF-6657, MCR-6689, LPG-6615, BRD-6488, MCR-6725, MCR-6691, OFF-6656

### 23. Keep the feeling of being sold near zero until the buyer is ready

**Why it works:** People relax while learning and tense up when pitched. Good ads die at the joins, where story turns to pitch. Marketing language, not production quality, is what makes an ad look like an ad.

**How to apply:** Let product awareness rise slowly. Bridge from the hook into education about the problem, show the product before you name it, and name it near the offer. In story ads, hold on the speaker and delay product cutaways; test B-roll from around 20 seconds. Edit advertorials by marking each sentence where the story stops and the pitch starts, then rewrite it inside the story. Show a sticky buy button only after the reader passes the paragraph that introduces the product. Write to be spoken: one idea per sentence, verb first, read aloud at real pace.

**Judge it by:** Mark where you first felt sold; it should be late and smooth. Weak work names the product in line one and jumps to shop now.

**Breaks when:** The buyer is already product aware; then an early reveal and a fast call to action work better.

**From:** MCR-0027, MCR-6738, MCR-6728, MCR-6693, MCR-6768, LPG-0012, LPG-0013, LPG-0011

### 24. Show the outcome as a scene from the buyer's life, and close on who they become

**Why it works:** People feel scenes and forget claims. A picture of the changed moment plays on emotion and carries less compliance risk than a stated health result. Identity tied to a real mechanism moves people and filters out buyers who would quit.

**How to apply:** Turn each claim into a moment: "joining in with the grandkids instead of sitting and watching" instead of "improves mobility". When a hook names an unfamiliar condition, add how common it is ("4 in 10 have it, most do not know") and call out several symptoms so more viewers include themselves. End on an identity line tied to the mechanism, such as the people who keep their hair started before the loss showed; identity with no mechanism is a lifestyle brand with a product attached.

**Judge it by:** The reviewer can picture one moment and one person. Weak work stacks abstract results (more energy, better focus) with a bare buy now.

**Breaks when:** The identity line is generic brand talk with no link to what the product does, or a scene still implies a claim a regulator would treat as a promise.

**From:** MCR-6613, MCR-0018, MCR-6694, OFF-6662, MCR-6818

### 25. Give a true reason to buy now, never a fake one

**Why it works:** Buyers delay by default, so a real deadline turns intent into an order. A fake one, such as a timer that resets, works once, breaks trust when spotted, and draws regulators.

**How to apply:** Tie urgency to something true: a sale that really ends, stock that is really low, a season, a gift date, or the cost of waiting (each month you wait is another cycle). Plan four demand peaks a year around moments you can own, not one big sale. Keep a small brand's sale to one clear weekend deal. Write the call to action in four parts: the action, what happens next, the effort, and the reason to act now. Keep a record behind every scarcity or time claim. The US FTC's 2022 dark patterns report names fake scarcity and countdowns, and the UK CMA has made urgency claims an enforcement focus.

**Judge it by:** Every urgency line has a fact the store could produce. Weak work runs a timer that restarts on reload.

**Breaks when:** There is no true reason; then lead with value and risk reversal.

**From:** MCR-6681, EML-0055, OFF-6660, US FTC, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, https://www.ftc.gov/reports/bringing-dark-patterns-light, UK CMA, urgency claims letter, https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/64232b153d885d000cdadd30/OCA_business_open_letter_FINAL.pdf

### 26. Win the buyer you can keep, not just the first click

**Why it works:** Pressure and promises the product cannot keep lower the cost of a first sale, then return as refunds, chargebacks and no repeat orders.

**How to apply:** For every new angle, check return and refund rates and 365-day value by angle, not just acquisition cost. Drop an angle with a high return rate even if its first-order numbers look great; support tickets and return reasons show where a claim fails. Never use AI video to fake a product demo or a testimonial; viewers who spot it turn on the brand and believers become refunds. Check no sale makes one-time cheaper than subscribing. Explain pre-ticked add-ons; an explained version can beat a silent tick. Shift spend toward the persona, angle and offer that brought your top 20% of customers, even at slightly worse first-order numbers.

**Judge it by:** The plan reads success on profit over time and returns by angle. Weak work celebrates a cheaper first sale from a claim the product cannot deliver.

**Breaks when:** The category is a one-off purchase with little repeat buying; then watch refunds and chargebacks only.

**From:** CRO-6609, MCR-6727, MCR-6809, OFF-6612, OFF-6628, MCR-6810, MCR-6608

### 27. Make the first use easy; the second order is bought by the first experience

**Why it works:** Low effort builds loyalty more than surprise: in a study of over 75,000 people, 96% with high-effort service grew more disloyal, against 9% with low effort. People repeat habits, so the first weeks of use decide the reorder.

**How to apply:** Fix effort before adding delight: make "where is my order", returns and fixes take one touch, and never try to delight someone mid-problem. Survey buyers on when and how they use the product, compare retention by answer after 60 to 90 days, and make onboarding repeat the one routine that retains best (one creatine brand found buyers who stirred it into morning coffee stayed longest). Add a small peak where it is cheap and memorable, such as unboxing at about 2 to 5% of product cost, because people remember the best moment and the end.

**Judge it by:** The post-purchase plan names the habit to build and the effort removed. Weak work sends a discount on day 3 before the product has arrived.

**Breaks when:** Operations are already low effort and reliable; then gains shift to peaks and community.

**From:** CXP-0009, EML-0005, OFF-0039, OFF-0040, CXP-0008, Dixon, Freeman and Toman, Harvard Business Review 2010, https://hbr.org/2010/07/stop-trying-to-delight-your-customers

### 28. Be easy to think of in the moments people need the category

**Why it works:** Most buyers meet a need or occasion and grab whichever brand comes to mind and is easy to spot. The Ehrenberg-Bass Institute calls these moments category entry points: the more a brand is linked to, the more often it is recalled.

**How to apply:** List the moments that send people to your category (before a trip, after a bad night, a new baby, a gift deadline), pulled from surveys and reviews. Link the brand to several of them across ads, not one slogan. Keep distinctive assets (colour, shape, character, sound) the same everywhere so the brand is known in a second. Count the share of ads whose hook only works if you already know the brand; an account built on those reminds rather than recruits. Keep on-brand comedy statics for recall even when they lose on last click. Branded web mentions also feed what AI assistants recommend.

**Judge it by:** The plan names the buying moments it links the brand to. Weak work speaks to one occasion and changes the look every month.

**Breaks when:** A very small store needs sales this month; then direct response leads.

**From:** MCR-6751, MCR-6622, AEO-0022, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, category entry points, https://marketingscience.info/research-services/identifying-and-prioritising-category-entry-points/

## Working from the org context

- Read the buyers' own words first. Pull each persona's phrases, top complaints, top near-miss reasons and failed past fixes before drafting; if a phrase is missing, find it in reviews or tickets and add it back to the org context.
- Take the persona list as given, then check it against what sold. If the brand's named buyer is not the one the data ranks highest, say so in one line with the number and propose a second persona; do not quietly swap.
- Keep the voice. Use the brand's vocabulary, reading level and tone even when a more aggressive line would test well; pressure written in a calm brand's voice will feel wrong and cost trust.
- Keep the design system and distinctive assets fixed. Recognition builds only when colour, shape and characters repeat; improve layout and hierarchy inside the system, not by changing it.
- Read what ran and what won. Find the market's stage, the stage of the brand's past winners and the proof types they used; start from a winner's persona, angle and offer before inventing new ones.
- Read the discount history and margins before writing any offer. Note how many weeks were on sale, the deepest cut, the price buyers have learned, the gross margin and where orders cluster by value; never propose an offer that resets the reference price without saying so.
- Check every proof point against the claims sheet. Use only approved claims, real review counts and live policies; one figure per fact across every asset.
- Where the brand under-sells, propose the fix as an option beside the brand's current way, with the reason and one number, such as moving the guarantee under the button or naming a mechanism the brand already has but never says.
- For India-only matters such as cash on delivery or refused parcels, hand the question to Ishita (`ishita-india-commerce`).

## Review checklist

- [ ] Can a reviewer name the one buyer, their problem and their trigger from the work alone?
- [ ] Does the main problem line use words a real customer wrote, with the source logged?
- [ ] Is the top "what almost stopped you" reason answered above the fold and by the button?
- [ ] Is the awareness stage stated, and do the hook, body and landing page all fit it?
- [ ] Is there one promise, stated before the mechanism, with a line on why other fixes failed?
- [ ] Does every claim have proof of the matching type next to it, and is no claim bigger than its proof?
- [ ] Do the objections run in the buyer's order, from "not for me" to "I'll do it later"?
- [ ] Does the first screen answer what it is, why this one, why now and why trust you?
- [ ] Is the offer built from perceived value at low cost rather than a straight discount, with break-even worked out?
- [ ] Does any sale plan state the discount history and the efficiency lift it needs to hold profit?
- [ ] Does the total, billing schedule and shipping cost match from ad to checkout with no surprise?
- [ ] Does the price sit next to a reference the buyer accepts, and is every "was" price real?
- [ ] Are there three or fewer choices, with a sensible default pre-selected?
- [ ] Is any free shipping or gift threshold set just above where orders cluster, with progress shown?
- [ ] Do the reviews shown near the button prove the exact claim in the ad?
- [ ] Do pack size, timeline and guarantee length agree on how long the product takes to work?
- [ ] Is the guarantee specific about what counts as failure and placed under the button?
- [ ] Is every urgency or scarcity line backed by a fact the store could show?
- [ ] Would most lines stop being true if a rival's name replaced the brand's?
- [ ] Is success read on profit, returns and repeat buying, not on clicks or first orders alone?

## Common failures

- Writing to a demographic ("women 25 to 45") instead of a person in a moment. Fix: rewrite the persona as problem, desire and trigger from a real buyer.
- Inventing customer language with AI. Fix: use AI only to fetch real quotes with links, and paste exact phrases from reviews and tickets.
- Guessing objections instead of asking buyers. Fix: add the two-question post-purchase survey and answer the top near-miss reason by the button.
- Opening with the product or a feature list for cold buyers. Fix: start with the problem or a belief the buyer holds, then the promise, then the mechanism, and name the product late.
- Backing a specific claim with generic proof (badges, "loved by thousands"). Fix: pick the proof type the claim's doubt needs, and show reviews that prove that exact claim.
- Fake urgency and inflated "was" prices. Fix: tie urgency to a real date, stock level or moment, and keep a record behind every claim.
- Answering every slow week with a new discount code. Fix: work out break-even first, add perceived value at low cost (gifts, bundles, credit) and keep straight discounts to one or two planned moments a year.
- Hiding the real total until checkout. Fix: show the full price, billing schedule and shipping early, and keep added costs under about 10% of order value.
- Too many options and no default. Fix: cut to three, pre-select the one you would recommend, and tie each tier to a real use.
- Guarantees and timelines that contradict the product. Fix: set pack size and guarantee length to how long the product takes to work.
- Copy any rival could publish. Fix: run the name-swap test and replace shared claims with a mechanism, a number or a proof only you have.
- Judging work on clicks or a cheaper first sale. Fix: check returns, refunds and repeat buying by angle before scaling it.
