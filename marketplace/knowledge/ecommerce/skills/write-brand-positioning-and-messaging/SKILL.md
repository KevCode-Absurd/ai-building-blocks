# Brand positioning and messaging

**You get:** a one-page messaging house: a positioning statement, 3 to 4 message pillars each with its proof points and buyer phrases, a list of words and claims to avoid, and one line per channel showing how the main promise is said in an ad, on the product page, in email and on WhatsApp.

## When to use

- A new brand needs to decide what it stands for before ads, pages and packaging.
- Ads, product pages and emails each say something different.
- The store looks and sounds like every rival in its category.
- Before a rebrand, a new product line or a move into a new country.

## Not for

- How the brand sounds sentence by sentence: use `brand-voice-guide` after this.
- The one habit the brand stands against: use `brand-opponent-statement` (a useful input here).
- One campaign idea across channels: use `develop-campaign-big-idea`.
- One product's full claim list with evidence: use `product-claims-profile`.

## What you need

Required:

1. **Buyer evidence:** reviews export (CSV), interview summaries from `customer-interview-script-and-summary`, post-purchase survey answers, or support tickets. At least 50 usable buyer comments.
2. **Product facts with proof:** materials, ingredients, tests, certifications, guarantees, delivery promise, each with its document.
3. **Rival messages:** home page and top product page headlines of 3 to 5 rivals, and their live ads from the Meta Ad Library, with dates.
4. **Gross profit per order** for the main products, so the promise does not commit the store to an offer it cannot afford.

Optional: the founder's story (recorded), past winning ads, existing brand guide.

Access: read only (level 1). Changing live pages or ads is a later job, drafted at level 2 with the client's yes.

## Before you start: the gates

1. **Evidence count.** Pass: 50 or more usable buyer comments, or 12 or more interviews. Fail: run interviews or a survey first, or mark the result as a draft to test.
2. **Proof on file.** Every fact the store wants to claim has a document or test result. Claims without one go on the banned-until-proven list.
3. **Gross profit known** for the hero products [MEA-0037]. A promise like "free returns forever" or "lowest price" must be affordable.
4. **Claims law.** US advertisers need a reasonable basis for objective claims before an ad runs, and "tests prove" needs the tests [MCR-6022]. India's ASCI code requires claims to be substantiated on demand and cited research to show source and date [ADC-6146].

## Steps

### 1. Map what rivals say

List each rival's main promise, the buyer it targets, the proof it leads with and its look. Mark where they all say the same thing. A category where every brand looks and sounds alike, and takes itself too seriously or overcomplicates the choice, is open to a brand that brings in a shift buyers already live with elsewhere [PRD-0001].

### 2. Count what buyers say

Code buyer comments by: who they are, the job they hired the product for, the result they got, what they tried before, what nearly stopped them, and the words they use. Count each theme. Keep exact phrases.

### 3. Choose the buyer and the market stage

Pick one main buyer. Without a clearly better product, start with the narrowest buyer situation that converts, then widen [AVT-0012]. Note how aware the market is: an unaware market needs the felt problem first; an aware market compares options; a saturated market needs a new mechanism or messenger [ADC-0001]. If the product is the same as a big rival's, win on an unserved buyer, not a made-up mechanism [AVT-0014].

### 4. Write the positioning statement

Template:

> For {buyer in a situation}, who {struggle in their words}, {brand} is the {category frame} that {main promise}, because {proof the store can show}. Unlike {the usual option}, it {the one difference that matters}.

Test it:

- **True:** every part has proof on file.
- **Specific:** a rival could not paste its name in and still be right.
- **Non-obvious but true:** an obvious idea is rarely a good one [BRD-0021].
- **One change:** the difference is one big thing buyers notice, not a list [PRD-0002].
- **Affordable:** the promise fits the gross profit.

Write three versions, have someone outside the team pick the clearest, and keep the runner-up.

### 5. Build 3 to 4 message pillars

Each pillar is a reason to buy that supports the main promise. For each:

- Pillar line in plain words.
- 2 to 4 proof points: test, number, guarantee, review count, demonstration. Each linked to its source.
- 2 to 3 buyer phrases from step 2.
- The objection it answers.

Good pillar sets often pair a selfish first reason to buy (look, feel, taste, result) with a reason to keep buying that feels good about the choice (better for the planet, the family, the community); either alone is weaker [BRD-0001]. Write in the buyer's dominant feeling and name the real alternatives they tried [AVT-0010].

### 6. List words and claims to avoid

- Rivals' tag words and category clichés.
- Claims without proof, and health, weight, money or before-and-after claims the platforms restrict.
- Superlatives ("best", "number 1", "clinically proven") without current evidence.
- Fake urgency and invented reviews.

### 7. Write one line per channel

Show the main promise as it would appear in: a Meta ad first line, a Google search headline (30 characters at most [GAD-6140]), a product page headline, an email subject line, a WhatsApp message, and packaging. These are examples of the message, not final copy.

### 8. India and US notes

- India: check the promise reads well in Hindi and the store's regional languages; COD buyers often need trust proof (returns, delivery, real reviews) as a pillar; ASCI rules on claims and comparisons apply.
- US: comparison claims must be fair and provable; "Made in USA" claims have strict rules; reviews and endorsements follow FTC guides.

### 9. Hand over

Send the messaging house to `brand-voice-guide`, `write-product-page-copy`, the ad copy team and `develop-campaign-big-idea`. Plan a cheap test of the statement through ads with two pillars as separate angles.

## Checks before you hand it over

- The statement names one buyer, one promise, one difference and real proof.
- A rival name pasted into the statement makes it false.
- Each pillar has at least two sourced proof points and two buyer phrases.
- Theme counts come from the evidence, not opinion.
- Every claim is on file or on the banned-until-proven list.
- The promise is affordable at current gross profit.
- Channel lines respect character limits and platform rules.

## Common mistakes

- **A statement any rival could use.** Step 4 tests it with a rival's name.
- **Pillars without proof.** Step 5 requires sourced proof points.
- **Inventing a mechanism.** Step 3 moves to an unserved buyer instead.
- **Founder opinion over buyer words.** Step 2 counts what buyers say.
- **Promising what the margin cannot carry.** Gate 3 checks it.
- **Claims that break the law.** Gate 4 and step 6 apply US and India rules.
