# Community building

Channel 19 of 19. Book chapter it updates: Community building. Checked 2026-11-06.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (Portfolio, 2015) [1], count community building among the 19 traction channels. This file restates the idea in our own words and updates it for 2026. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. What this channel is now

Two moves, usually in this order. First, **join** the communities where your buyers already talk (subreddits, Facebook groups, Slack and Discord servers, niche forums) and become useful there. Second, **build** your own room where customers help each other, give feedback and bring friends.

Self-promotion rules as of November 2026, from each platform's own pages:

- **Reddit.** Reddit defines spam as repeated, unwanted or unsolicited actions. Promotion is not spam by itself, but if most of your posts link to a business you run, Reddit says to post less often or use its ads, and each community's moderators decide what counts as spam there [2]. Reddiquette mentions a widely used 9:1 rule of thumb (one in ten submissions your own), a norm rather than policy [3].
- **Discord.** Guidelines effective 29 September 2025 ban unsolicited bulk messages, self-bots, artificially inflating server membership or engagement, and selling servers or invite links [4]. Each server sets its own promotion rules.
- **Slack.** Communities run as Slack workspaces. On the free plan only the last 90 days of messages and files are visible, content older than a year is deleted, and you get up to 10 integrations [5].
- **Facebook groups.** Meta's spam policy bans posting at very high frequency, offering anything of value for likes or shares, hiding content until people engage, and misleading links; clearly labelled branded content is allowed [6]. Group admins can stop Pages from joining, and a Page can only ask to join [7].
- **Forums.** Each forum's pinned rules decide what promotion is allowed. Read them before your first post.
- **Disclosure everywhere.** In the US the FTC expects owners and staff to say they work for the business when they recommend its products; a line on a profile is not enough [8].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

It works when buyers share a problem or identity and already talk to each other, when the product gets better as people share how they use it, and when someone on the team can show up every week for months.

It doesn't work for one-off purchases with nothing to talk about, when nobody owns moderation, or when the "community" is really a broadcast channel for offers. An empty room puts people off, so start small and personal.

## 3. Cheap tests

Set the go and stop numbers before each test, from the client's own figures. Target cost per lead = the most the client can pay for a customer × lead-to-customer rate, with time valued at the client's hourly rate. These are formulas, not industry averages.

**Test 1: Listening fortnight.** Pick five communities where buyers gather. Read the rules, log the questions people ask, and answer the ones you can help with, with no links.
- Cost: no cash; about half an hour a day.
- Time: two weeks.
- Go: at least one person asks what you do, or messages you about your field.
- Stop: zero questions touch what you sell. Pick new rooms.

**Test 2: One useful post per room.** Where the rules allow, share something useful (a guide, a template, a short data summary), say who you are, and use a tracked link.
- Cost: one day of writing.
- Time: two weeks.
- Go: cost per lead at or below the target.
- Stop: the post is removed, or zero tracked visits turn into an enquiry or sign-up.

**Test 3: Seed a small room.** Invite your best customers to a small Discord, Slack or Facebook group and post one prompt a week.
- Cost: no cash; about two hours a week.
- Time: four weeks.
- Go: members post without being asked, and at least one member invites someone new.
- Stop: after week three, you are still the only person posting.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- Active member rate = members who posted or reacted in the last 30 days ÷ all members.
- Peer answer rate = questions answered by members ÷ questions asked.
- Time to first reply = median time from a new question to its first answer.
- Community-sourced leads = sign-ups or enquiries tagged "community" (tracked links or a "how did you hear about us?" field).
- Community conversion = community-sourced customers ÷ community-sourced leads.
- Removal rate = your posts removed ÷ your posts in other people's communities.
- Member retention = customers in the community still buying after a set period ÷ those who joined (compare with non-members; this shows a link, not proof).

## 5. Tools

- Discord (free; optional paid extras) and Slack (free plan with history limits; paid) [5].
- Facebook Groups (free) and Reddit (free to use; Reddit Ads are paid).
- Discourse (open source and free to self-host; paid hosting) for a forum.
- Circle, Skool or Mighty Networks (paid) for membership communities with courses or events.
- Google Forms (free) for member surveys, and Google's Campaign URL Builder (free) for tracked links.
- Access: the lead's brief sets a level per account. Level 1 reads the communities and the client's group data; level 2 prepares drafts where the platform allows them; level 3 posts and replies from the client's own accounts within the client's caps, where each community's rules allow it, and every action is logged.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Links on day one.** Earn standing by answering questions first.
- **The same post in many rooms.** Moderators and spam filters see it as spam [2].
- **Hidden identity.** Staff, friends or family praising the product without saying so [8].
- **Bought members or fake accounts**, banned by Discord [4] and Meta [6].
- **Prizes for likes or shares** on Facebook [6].
- **An empty launch.** Open with a handful of engaged customers, not a public link.
- **No rules or moderator.** Write three rules and name who enforces them.
- **Choosing a platform without checking its limits**, such as Slack's free history [5].
- **Counting members, not activity.** Watch the active member rate.

## 7. By business type

### General

Join before you build. Find where your customers already talk: a local Facebook group, a trade forum, a subreddit for the problem you solve. For a month, be the most helpful person there and say who you are. Note the questions that keep coming up; they become your content and your FAQs. Only then open your own small group, starting with customers who already like you, and give it one clear purpose, such as tips, early access or peer help.

### SaaS

Build a room where users help users: share setups, answer each other's questions and shape the roadmap. dbt Labs runs a Slack workspace, a Discourse forum and city meetups, and its community page says it connects over 100,000 data professionals [9]. Start with a support-and-feedback Slack or Discord for early users, with the founder in it every day. Compare activation and retention for members and non-members, and turn repeated questions into docs and product fixes.

### Ecommerce

Build the community around the use or the identity, not the product. Glossier grew out of the Into The Gloss blog, started in 2010; Emily Weiss used its interviews and reader comments to create Glossier, which launched in 2014 with four products [10]. A store can do the same at small scale with a Facebook group or Discord for early access, product feedback and customer photos shared with permission. Keep giveaways inside Meta's rules [6].

### Local

Local community is part online, part in the room. Take part in neighbourhood Facebook groups within their admin rules [7], and host something regular in person: a run club, a workshop, a class, a tasting. lululemon showed the model at scale when more than 500 North American stores hosted free, ambassador-led events on 10 October 2024 [11]. A single shop can run a monthly event. Ask every attendee for an email with consent, and track attendance to bookings.

### Coaching

For coaches and solo experts, a community sits beside the offer: a free group for your audience and a paid one for clients. Run one live session a week, share member wins with permission, and keep the free and paid rooms separate. Discord, Circle or Skool all work. Track free members who book a discovery call and become clients. Disclose affiliate links and paid partnerships [8], and keep claims about results honest.

## 8. Real examples

- **Glossier.** Into The Gloss launched in 2010; Weiss used what she learned from its interviews and comments to create Glossier in 2014 [10].
- **dbt Labs.** A Slack workspace, a Discourse forum and local meetups, described on its site as connecting over 100,000 data professionals as of November 2026 [9].
- **lululemon.** On 10 October 2024 more than 500 stores in North America hosted free, play-themed events led by hundreds of its ambassadors [11].
