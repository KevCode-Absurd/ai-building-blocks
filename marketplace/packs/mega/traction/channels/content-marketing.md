# Content marketing

Channel 8 of 19. Book chapter it updates: Content marketing. Checked 2026-11-06.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (Portfolio, 2015) [1], gave content marketing its own chapter, separate from SEO and from getting featured on other people's blogs. This file restates the channel in our own words and updates it for 2026. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. What this channel is now

Content marketing means publishing useful material (articles, guides, videos, templates, research, newsletters) that draws buyers in and earns their trust before they talk to you. It runs on your own site and on other people's, through guest posts and syndication. As of November 2026:

- **Original beats volume.** Google asks whether content offers original information and first-hand experience, and lists warning signs: writing to a word count, mostly summarising others, and mass-producing pages on many topics with heavy automation [2]. Many pages made mainly to rank, with little value, break its scaled content abuse policy however they are produced [3].
- **Most pages get no search traffic.** In Ahrefs' 2023 study of about 14 billion pages, 96.55% got no Google traffic, mainly because nobody searched the topic, the page had no links, or it missed what the searcher wanted [4].
- **AI summaries take the easy clicks.** Where Google showed an AI summary, people clicked a normal result on 8% of visits against 15% without one (Pew, March 2025 data) [5]. Tools, original data, worked examples and opinions are what a summary cannot replace.
- **AI writes, results lag.** In CMI's survey published 8 October 2025 (over 1,000 B2B marketers), about 9 in 10 used AI to produce content, but fewer than 4 in 10 said it had improved performance [6].
- **Distribution decides.** Every piece needs a route to readers: your email list, sales follow-ups, communities, or someone else's audience.

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

It works when buyers research before they buy, when the sale is considered or the price is high, when someone in the business knows things customers want to learn, and when you can publish steadily for months and capture readers (email sign-up, demo, booking).

It doesn't work for impulse purchases nobody reads about first, when you need sales within weeks, when nobody can supply real experience or data, or when nobody will distribute the work. Content with no capture step leaks: readers leave and never come back.

## 3. Cheap tests

Set go and stop lines with the client first. Target cost per lead = the most the client can pay for a lead, from the variant playbook. Lines below are sums from the client's own figures, not industry averages. Tag every link with UTM parameters so GA4 can credit each piece.

**Test 1: Answer the questions sales hears most.** Write five honest answers to the questions buyers ask before buying, including price, problems and comparisons, the method Marcus Sheridan describes in *They Ask, You Answer* [7]. Send them in sales follow-ups and to your list.
- Cost: no spend; three to five days.
- Time: 30 days.
- Go: at least one sale or booked call where the buyer read a piece (tracked link or they say so), and sales keep sending them.
- Stop: no tracked reader books or buys in 30 days.

**Test 2: Borrowed audience.** Pitch ten blogs, newsletters or trade sites your buyers read. Publish two or three guest pieces, each linking to one sign-up offer.
- Cost: no spend; about two weeks of pitching and writing.
- Time: four weeks after the first piece runs.
- Go: cost per sign-up (hours × hourly cost ÷ tracked sign-ups) at or under target cost per lead.
- Stop: the first two pieces bring zero tracked sign-ups.

**Test 3: One original-data piece.** Survey your customers or analyse your own anonymised data, publish the findings with a clear chart, and send them to 20 writers or creators who cover the topic.
- Cost: a free survey tool; one to two weeks.
- Time: six weeks.
- Go: at least two independent sites cite or link to it, and sign-ups arrive from those links.
- Stop: no citations and no sign-ups after six weeks.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Key events per piece** = sign-ups, enquiries or purchases in GA4 from sessions that landed on the piece.
- **Cost per piece** = hours × hourly cost + paid help.
- **Content cost per lead** = total content cost ÷ leads that landed on or were sent content.
- **Sign-up rate per piece** = email sign-ups ÷ sessions on that piece.
- **Self-reported share** = new customers who name an article, guide or video ÷ all new customers.
- **Decay** = this quarter's clicks ÷ the same quarter last year, per page, from Search Console.

## 5. Tools

- Google Search Console, Google Analytics 4, Microsoft Clarity and Google Forms: free.
- Google Keyword Planner: free inside a Google Ads account. Ahrefs Free: free for verified sites. Ahrefs and Semrush suites: paid.
- WordPress: free open-source software; hosting is paid.
- Buffer: free plan for a few social channels; paid plans.
- Kit, beehiiv and MailerLite: free plans to capture readers by email; paid plans.
- Access: the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads analytics, Search Console and the CMS; level 2 drafts posts in the CMS and queues social posts, unpublished; level 3 publishes and posts within the client's caps, and every action is logged.

## 6. Common mistakes

- Publishing with no plan for who will see it.
- Topics from keyword tools that nobody in sales recognises.
- AI drafts with nothing first-hand in them [2].
- Dozens of near-identical pages differing only by a place or product name [3].
- Reporting page views instead of key events and sign-ups.
- No way for a reader to come back, such as an email sign-up.
- Stopping after four posts, or publishing for a year without a go or stop decision.

## 7. By business type

### General
Answer the questions most businesses avoid: what it costs, what goes wrong, how you compare with the alternatives [7]. End each piece with one next step (sign up, book, buy) and reuse it in sales emails and replies. Run Test 1 first, because it uses knowledge you already have and shows results inside a month. Only move to a regular publishing schedule once one piece has clearly helped a sale.

### SaaS
Publish what users can act on: templates, how-to guides that use the product, integration walkthroughs and honest comparisons. Guest posts on sites your users read can fill a free trial pipeline before your own blog ranks, as Buffer found [8][9]. Aggregated, anonymised product data, used with permission, makes strong original research for Test 3. Measure trial sign-ups and activated accounts per piece, not visits, and skip "what is X" posts that AI summaries now answer [5].

### Ecommerce
Shoppers decide fast, so content mostly grows the email list and brings people back rather than closing the first sale. Write buying guides, size and fit guides, how-to-use and care guides, and answer the questions in your support inbox. An editorial voice can build an audience before the products do: Glossier grew out of Emily Weiss's blog Into the Gloss [11]. Judge content by email sign-ups and orders from returning readers.

### Local
Write the pages buyers wish existed: what the job costs in your area, what happens on the day, which option suits which home, and write-ups of real jobs with photos. River Pools and Spas, a pool installer, rebuilt its lead flow this way after the 2008 downturn [7][10]. Send pieces before quotes and site visits so buyers arrive informed. One strong page per real service beats many thin ones [3].

### Coaching
Your point of view is the product, so publish one signature piece that explains your method, then turn discovery-call questions into short posts. Case studies need the client's written permission and no invented results. Reuse each piece in your newsletter, on LinkedIn and as a podcast talking point. Measure booked calls where the prospect names a piece of content. If you write about health or money, show your qualifications and cite sources.

## 8. Real examples

- **Buffer.** Co-founder Leo Widrich wrote around 150 guest posts in nine months, Buffer said in 2014 [8]. In a July 2012 interview he credited guest posting alone with about 100,000 users in Buffer's first nine months [9].
- **River Pools and Spas.** Pool & Spa News reported in November 2013 that the owners answered buyers' questions in dozens of blog posts and YouTube videos after the recession hit, found the website was now producing their leads, and gave away an e-book in exchange for a name, state and email address [10]. Marcus Sheridan later set out the method in *They Ask, You Answer* [7].
- **Glossier.** The beauty brand launched online in 2014 with four products, building on Weiss's blog Into the Gloss [11].
