# Creators, newsletters and podcasts in your niche

Channel 1 of 19 in the First traction kit. It updates the "Targeting blogs" chapter of Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015). The book's ideas are summarised in our own words; the authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit. Facts checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed at the end.

## 1. What this channel is now

The book's advice was to get mentioned on, or buy slots in, the small blogs your future customers read. The idea holds; the venues moved. In 2026 the niche voices are mostly newsletter writers, podcasters and video creators who serve one tight audience: Shopify developers, home bakers, finance teams at small firms, new parents in one city.

There are three ways in: **earned** mentions (the creator covers you because it helps their audience), **paid sponsorship** (a newsletter slot, a host-read podcast ad, a sponsored video segment), and **paid recommendations or swaps** between newsletters.

What is true as of November 2026:

- **Podcasts reach most people.** Edison Research's Infinite Dial 2026 (March 2026) found 80% of Americans aged 12 and over have listened to or watched a podcast, and 57% have done both [2].
- **Newsletter ads have marketplaces.** On the beehiiv Ad Network, advertisers run offers that pay newsletters per unique open (CPM) or per verified unique click (CPC). beehiiv vets advertisers, and one post can carry one main ad and up to two smaller ones [3].
- **You can buy subscribers at a fixed price.** SparkLoop, bought by ConvertKit (now Kit) in June 2023, runs paid recommendations: a newsletter pays a guaranteed cost for each new subscriber other newsletters send it [4].
- **Journalists are creators too.** Muck Rack (October 2025) found about one in three journalists now publish on their own, and 41% of these creator journalists use email newsletters [9]. This channel and publicity now overlap.
- **Disclosure is required everywhere.** The US FTC expects any payment, free product or personal tie to be disclosed clearly in the post itself, and in video both said and shown [5]. The UK's ASA and CAP influencer guide reached its fourth edition on 23 October 2026 [6]. The European Commission's Influencer Legal Hub explains that EU consumer law already requires paid content, including gifted products and affiliate links, to be disclosed [7].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

**It works when** your buyers gather around a few trusted voices you can name; the product can be shown or tried in a minute or two; you can give the audience something real (a sample, a longer trial, a template, a discount); and each creator can get a unique link or code so you can count results.

**It struggles when** the audience has no shared niche; the product needs health or money claims you cannot prove; nothing can be tracked; or the order value and the audience are both too small for the cost per customer to work.

## 3. Cheap tests

Weinberg suggests a first test should take no more than about a month or about $1,000 [1]. Each test fits inside that.

### Test 1: Earned mentions from a short list

- **What:** List 30 creators your buyers follow. Send each a short personal note offering something their audience gets (access, a sample, a data point). No payment.
- **Cost:** Time only, plus any samples.
- **Time:** Three weeks.
- **Go:** At least one creator mentions you, and visitors from that mention become customers at or above your site's usual rate.
- **Stop:** No yes after every creator got a personal note and one follow-up. Change the offer before you change the list.

### Test 2: One paid newsletter or podcast slot

- **What:** Book one slot with the best-fit creator. Use a unique link, a unique code and a "How did you hear about us?" question.
- **Cost:** One slot at the creator's posted rate, inside your test budget.
- **Time:** Two to four weeks, including booking lead time.
- **Go:** Cost per new customer is at or below your break-even CAC (section 4).
- **Stop:** Cost per new customer is above break-even, or the zero-sale check fails (section 4).

### Test 3: Paid recommendations or a newsletter swap

- **What:** For businesses that sell through an email list. Buy subscribers through a paid recommendation network [4], or swap recommendations with a newsletter for the same reader.
- **Cost:** A capped number of subscribers at the network's fixed price, or time only for a swap.
- **Time:** 30 days after the last new subscriber arrives.
- **Go:** These subscribers open and click at a rate close to your organic subscribers, and their expected value covers what you paid.
- **Stop:** Their engagement is clearly below your organic subscribers, or their value does not cover the cost.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Cost per click** = slot cost ÷ unique clicks.
- **Cost per thousand reached** = slot cost ÷ (opens or downloads ÷ 1,000), for comparing creators.
- **Visit-to-customer rate** = new customers from the creator ÷ visits from the creator.
- **CAC** = (fee + free product + shipping + your hours × hourly rate) ÷ new customers.
- **Break-even CAC** = the most you can pay for a customer and still profit within your payback window. Your variant playbook sets the formula; for a one-off sale it is the contribution margin of the first order.
- **Attributed customers** = link or code users + customers who name the creator when asked how they heard of you.
- **Zero-sale check:** expected customers = clicks × your usual conversion rate. If that is 3 or more and you got none, stop: zero sales when three were expected happens by chance only about once in 20.
- **Subscriber quality ratio** = 30-day open rate of bought subscribers ÷ 30-day open rate of organic subscribers.

## 5. Tools

- **Finding creators:** YouTube, Apple Podcasts and Spotify search (free); Listen Notes podcast search (free to search on its site); creators' media kits (free).
- **Booking:** beehiiv Ad Network for advertisers (paid, per open or click) [3]; Passionfroot, where brands find, book and pay creators across newsletters, video and podcasts (brands can start free; paid plans); direct email (free).
- **Paid recommendations:** SparkLoop and Kit's paid recommendations (paid, per subscriber) [4].
- **Tracking:** UTM-tagged links in Google Analytics (free); unique codes in your store platform (part of your plan); a "How did you hear about us?" field (free in any form tool).
- **Outreach list:** Google Sheets (free) or HubSpot's free CRM (free tools; paid tiers).
- **Access:** the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads analytics, codes and sales; level 2 drafts outreach in the client's mailbox and sets up codes and links, not yet live; level 3 sends outreach within the client's caps, and every action is logged. Payments and bookings always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Choosing by follower count.** Ask for open rates, downloads per episode, and audience location or job data instead.
- **No way to count.** No unique link, code or survey question means the result is a guess.
- **Over-scripting the host.** Give facts and the offer; let the creator speak in their own voice.
- **Weak disclosure.** "Thanks to" or a tag at the end instead of a clear "ad" or "sponsored" up front [5][6][7].
- **Judging the channel on one creator.** Test more than one before calling it.
- **Counting free product as free.** CAC must include product, shipping and your hours.

## 7. By business type

### General

Start with earned mentions: they cost time, not money, and show which creators your buyers trust. Ask five recent customers which newsletters, podcasts and channels they follow; that list beats any directory. When one creator brings customers at or below break-even CAC, book a second slot with the same creator before trying new ones, so you learn whether the first result repeats. Always send people to a page that names the creator and repeats the offer.

### SaaS

Look for creators who serve a job title: newsletters for developers, finance leads, recruiters or agency owners, and video channels that teach the tools your buyers already use. A creator showing your product doing a real task in a tutorial usually fits software better than a read-out ad, because the viewer sees it work. Offer a longer trial or a free template, and measure trial-to-paid, not clicks. Many niche B2B newsletters sell slots directly or through marketplaces such as beehiiv's [3].

### Ecommerce

Product seeding is the classic move: send product to creators whose audience matches, with no promise of coverage. If you send free product, regulators in the US, UK and EU expect any post about it to carry a clear disclosure [5][6][7]. Give each creator a unique code. CAC must include product, shipping and the discount, so check contribution margin after the code. Go back to the creators whose codes keep getting used.

### Local

The niche is your town. Look for local newsletters, neighbourhood social accounts, local food or family bloggers, and local podcasts. Invite them in to try the service or the menu; a visit beats a press kit. Track with an offer they can quote at the till or a code on your booking page. Location fit matters more than size: a few thousand readers nearby can beat a large account with readers everywhere.

### Coaching

Podcast guesting is usually the main door for coaches and consultants, because a long conversation lets the listener judge you before they book. Pitch shows your ideal client already hears, with two or three specific episode ideas. Newsletter swaps with peers who serve the same client but sell something different are cheap and fast. Paid recommendations can grow your list if a booked call is worth more than the cost per subscriber. Measure booked calls and their show rate, not downloads.

## 8. Real examples

- **The *Traction* book's own launch.** In a September 2015 interview, Weinberg said guest posts on major blogs did not convert into book sales, while podcast appearances did much better, because listeners heard the ideas at length [1].
- **Mailchimp and *Serial* (2014).** Mailchimp sponsored the podcast's first season. In June 2015 the agency McKinney surveyed 6,300 of the show's 137,000 newsletter subscribers: 81% of those who listened during the 2014 run recalled a season-one sponsor, and 92% of that group named Mailchimp [8]. Caveat: this is recall among fans, not sales.
