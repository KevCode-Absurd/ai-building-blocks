# Free tools as marketing (engineering as marketing)

Channel 11 of 19. Book chapter it updates: Engineering as marketing. Checked 2026-11-06.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (Portfolio, 2015) [1], treat building useful free tools as a traction channel of its own. This file restates the idea in our own words and updates it for 2026. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. What this channel is now

You build something small that your future customers find useful, give it away, and let it bring them to you: a calculator, a template, a grader that scores a site or plan, a quiz, a browser extension or a small app. The tool does a real job, and your offer sits next to the result.

What changed by November 2026:

- **Building is cheap.** With AI coding assistants and no-code builders, Ahrefs argued in July 2026, the hard part is choosing the right tool, not building it [2].
- **Tools hold up in AI search.** An AI answer can explain a sum but cannot run it for the reader. That is Ahrefs' view, not a study [2].
- **Thin pages get punished.** Google's spam policies treat mass-produced, low-value pages as "scaled content abuse", however they were made [3].
- **Extensions have store rules.** The Chrome Web Store requires each extension to have a single, narrow purpose [4], and publishing needs a developer account with a one-time registration fee [5].

No-code options (as of Nov 2026):

| Tool | No code? | Usual route |
|---|---|---|
| Calculator | Yes | A shared Google Sheet, or a form builder with logic |
| Template | Yes | Google Sheets, Notion, Canva or a document |
| Quiz or scorecard | Yes | Typeform, Tally or Outgrow |
| Grader that reads a URL or file | Usually not | Needs an API; AI coding assistants help |
| Browser extension | No | Code, then a Chrome Web Store listing [4][5] |
| Small app | Often | Glide or Softr; code for anything custom |

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

It works when:

- your buyer repeats a sum or a task and searches for help with it, so demand is visible before you build;
- the tool's job sits right next to what you sell, like a payroll firm's paycheck calculator [2];
- the result gives a reason to talk: a score with gaps you can fix, or a plan you can deliver.

It doesn't work when the tool is clever but unrelated (traffic without buyers), when nobody searches for or shares it, when you can't keep it current, or when the result drifts into tax, legal, financial or health advice. Label every assumption; have a qualified professional check regulated content.

## 3. Cheap tests

Set the go and stop numbers before each test, from the client's own figures. Target cost per lead = the most the client can pay for a customer × lead-to-customer rate. These are formulas, not industry averages.

**Test 1: Demand check before building.** List ten tool ideas. Check search demand in a free keyword tool and look at what already ranks.
- Cost: free tools, half a day.
- Time: one day.
- Go: at least one idea has measurable search demand, weak current results, and searchers who match the buyer.
- Stop: zero ideas show demand, and customers you ask would not use or share any of them.

**Test 2: Sheet or form version.** Build it as a shared sheet or a form-builder quiz on one page with one next step. Share it with your list, customers and one community whose rules allow it.
- Cost: no cash; one or two days of work.
- Time: two weeks.
- Go: cost per lead at or below the target cost per lead.
- Stop: zero completed uses lead to a reply, booking or sale.

**Test 3: Watch five people use it.** Five customers or prospects try the prototype on short calls while you watch without helping.
- Cost: no cash; five short calls.
- Time: one week.
- Go: more than half finish it unaided and ask to keep it or pass it on.
- Stop: more than half stall or ask what it is for.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- Completion rate = completed uses ÷ started uses.
- Tool-to-lead rate = leads (emails, bookings, trials) ÷ completed uses.
- Lead-to-customer rate = customers tagged "tool" ÷ leads tagged "tool".
- Cost per tool customer = (build cost + monthly upkeep × months live) ÷ customers from the tool.
- Payback months = cost per tool customer ÷ monthly gross profit per customer.
- Search reach = clicks to the tool page (Search Console) and the number of sites linking to it.
- Repeat use = users who come back within 30 days ÷ all users.
- Extensions: weekly active users ÷ installs, and uninstalls per week.

## 5. Tools

- Google Sheets (free) for calculators and templates.
- Typeform and Tally (free plans; paid) for quizzes; Outgrow (paid) for calculators and quizzes.
- Notion (free plan; paid) and Canva (free plan; paid) for templates.
- Glide and Softr (free plans with limits; paid) for small apps.
- Claude or ChatGPT (free and paid plans) as coding assistants for graders and extensions.
- Chrome Web Store developer account (one-time registration fee) [5].
- Google Search Console and Google Analytics (free) for reach and leads; Ahrefs' keyword generator (free; paid plans exist) for demand checks [8].
- Access: the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads Search Console, analytics and the CRM; level 2 builds the tool and its page in the client's builder or repository, unpublished; level 3 publishes the tool page within the client's caps, and every action is logged. Store submissions and spend always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Building before checking demand.** Run test 1.
- **Hiding the result behind an email form.** Show a useful result first; offer a deeper report for an email.
- **No next step.** The result page must point to the offer.
- **Scaled thin pages** that trip Google's spam policy [3].
- **Emails collected without the right consent.** UK and EU marketing email to individuals usually needs consent or a soft opt-in; see the email-marketing specialist.
- **Stale numbers.** Rates and rules go out of date; show a "checked on" date.
- **Scare scores.** A grader that invents problems misleads people.
- **Extensions that do many things**, which breaks the single-purpose rule [4].

## 7. By business type

### General

Find the sum, checklist or document your buyer works through just before they buy from you, and give that away. A shared Google Sheet or a one-page template is enough for the first version. Put it on a page with one next step, send it to your list and customers, and ask partners to share it. Tag every lead "tool" in your CRM to see whether tool users buy as often as other leads. Rebuild it as a proper web page only after the sheet version brings in leads at or below your target cost.

### SaaS

Make the tool a thin slice of the product: run your product's core check on the visitor's own site, file or numbers, and show the result free. HubSpot's free Website Grader scored a site's online marketing, the problem HubSpot's software addressed [6][7]. Ahrefs gives away a backlink checker, a keyword generator and an SEO toolbar extension [8]. The path is result, then "fix this in the product", then a trial. Compare activation of tool-sourced trials with other trials. Build one strong tool before a library, and avoid mass-produced variants [3].

### Ecommerce

Build tools that help people choose and buy the right amount: size and fit finders, "how much do I need" calculators for paint, tile or fabric, routine or gift quizzes, care guides. Every result should end in a product and an add-to-basket link. A quiz built in Typeform or Tally is enough to test. Compare the conversion rate and average order value of tool users against the site as a whole, and watch return rates for fit tools. A quiz that asks about skin, health or children collects sensitive data, so check your privacy notice first.

### Local

The native tool is an honest estimator: "what will this cost?" for a roof, a clean, a boiler service or a course of treatment, shown as a range with what changes the price and "final quote after a visit". Checklists and booking-prep forms work too. Mention your town and service area on the page, because searches are local. End every result with a booking button and a phone number. Clinics and legal or financial services must keep the tool factual and have a qualified person check the content.

### Coaching

Self-assessments are the native form for coaches and solo experts: a short scorecard on where someone is stuck, ending with a personal result and a booking link. Gretchen Rubin's free Four Tendencies quiz shows the reach a good one can have: a PBS page for her show says more than two million people have taken it [9]. Workbooks and planning templates work too. All of it can be built without code in Typeform, Tally or Canva. Measure completions, then booked calls, then new clients. Keep result pages modest: describe patterns, never promise outcomes.

## 8. Real examples

- **HubSpot.** Its 2011 release said Website Grader launched in 2006 and had graded over 4 million websites when Marketing Grader replaced it [6]. Co-founder Dharmesh Shah wrote that the first version was the first code he wrote at HubSpot [7].
- **Ahrefs.** In July 2026 it said its writing-tools section, launched in mid-2023, peaked near one million estimated monthly US search visits (its own estimate), and that its free SEO tools are among its highest-traffic pages [2].
- **Gusto and Shopify.** The same article estimated about 17,800 monthly US search visits to Gusto's hourly paycheck calculator and a peak above 20,000 for Shopify's profit margin calculator [2]. Shopify's free tools page listed 19 tools on 6 Nov 2026, from a logo maker to an invoice generator [10].
