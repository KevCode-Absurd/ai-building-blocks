# Offline ads

Channel 6 of 19. Book chapter it updates: Offline ads. Checked 2026-11-06; platform facts carry their dates. The book is Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (Portfolio, 2015) [1], summarised in our own words; the authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. What this channel is now

Offline ads are print, radio and audio, TV, outdoor (billboards, transit, digital screens) and direct mail. In a 2015 interview Weinberg said online companies underuse offline channels, and DuckDuckGo's billboard came up as both an offline ad and a publicity move [2]. Less competition for attention is the reason to look here.

As of November 2026:

- **Direct mail without a list.** USPS Every Door Direct Mail sends flyers or postcards to every address on chosen carrier routes, addressed to "Postal Customer". Its free online tool filters routes by census data such as age, income and household size; the Retail option takes at least 200 pieces and up to 5,000 a day per ZIP Code [3]. In the UK, Royal Mail Door to Door delivers unaddressed mail to areas chosen by postcode or distance from the business [4].
- **Addressed mail to people needs a lawful basis.** The UK ICO lists post as a likely fit for legitimate interests (the electronic marketing rules don't cover post), but a person's objection to direct marketing must always be honoured [5]. EU GDPR works on similar lines; check local rules and the client's adviser.
- **Audio is partly self-serve.** Spotify Ads Manager lets a business buy audio, video and podcast ads, target by age, location, genre and podcast topic, and go live in as little as a day [12]. For print, local radio, TV and outdoor, ask the seller about short runs and unsold space.
- **The same truth rules apply offline.** In the US the FTC expects ads in mail, on billboards, buses, radio and TV to be truthful and backed by evidence [6]. In the UK the CAP Code covers non-broadcast ads and direct marketing, and the BCAP Code covers TV and radio [7]. In the EU the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive applies, and its update on green claims (Directive 2024/825) has applied since 27 September 2026 [8].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

It works when buyers live or travel in a place you can reach (a town, a commuter route, a trade magazine's readers), when the audience spends less time online, when online channels are crowded, and when a placement can also earn press.

It struggles when buyers are spread thinly across a country, when the budget buys only one or two exposures, when there is no way to tell which responses came from the ad, and when the business needs answers in days (print and outdoor bookings can take weeks).

## 3. Cheap tests

Every test needs its own tracking: a unique code, short web address, QR code or phone number. Set target and break-even cost per customer first. The client sets the budget cap and approves the copy and the action list once. At level 3 the specialist books within the cap; anything outside it needs a new yes.

### Test 1: Neighbourhood mail drop

- **How:** Pick a few routes (US) or postcode sectors (UK) near the business or its best customers. One clear offer with a deadline and a unique code [3][4].
- **Cost:** Print plus postage for a small run near the minimum quantity.
- **Time:** Book and print in a week or two; count responses for three to four weeks after delivery.
- **Go:** Cost per new customer at or below target.
- **Stop:** Cost per new customer above break-even when the response window closes.

### Test 2: Holdout version of the mail drop

- **How:** Codes miss people who search for you instead. List comparable routes or areas, randomly mail half and hold back half, then compare new customers per household in each group. This is the geo experiment design Google researchers described in 2011 [9].
- **Cost:** The same as test 1, spread over more areas.
- **Time:** Four to six weeks.
- **Go:** Extra customers per household x gross profit per customer is more than the cost per household mailed.
- **Stop:** No clear difference between mailed and held-back areas.

### Test 3: One local print or radio placement

- **How:** A single slot in the paper, newsletter, magazine or station the buyers use, with a dedicated web address or phone number. Ask for unsold space.
- **Cost:** One placement, within a cap the client sets.
- **Time:** Two to four weeks after it runs.
- **Go:** Cost per new customer at or below target.
- **Stop:** Above break-even, or too few responses to judge.

### Test 4: Self-serve audio

- **How:** A short Spotify campaign targeted to the client's area and audience, measured with a promo code and the platform's pixel [12].
- **Cost:** A small cap. Weinberg's guide for any channel test was about a month and around a thousand dollars [2].
- **Time:** Three to four weeks.
- **Go:** Cost per customer at or below target.
- **Stop:** Above break-even at the end.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Response rate** = responses / pieces delivered (or calls and visits / estimated audience).
- **Cost per response** = total cost / responses. **Cost per new customer** = total cost / new customers.
- **Break-even response rate** = cost per piece / (gross profit per customer x response-to-customer rate).
- **Lift** = (customer rate in mailed areas - rate in held-back areas) / rate in held-back areas.
- **Incremental customers** = (mailed-area rate - held-back rate) x households mailed.
- **CPM** for outdoor and audio = cost / impressions x 1,000. Treat seller impression figures as estimates.

## 5. Tools

- USPS EDDM Online Tool (free to use; postage paid) [3]; Royal Mail Door to Door (paid service) [4].
- Lob and PostGrid (paid) for printed mail sent through an API.
- Vistaprint (paid) for printing; Canva (free plan; paid) for design.
- Spotify Ads Manager (free to open; paid ads) [12].
- CallRail (paid) for unique tracking phone numbers.
- Google Analytics 4 with UTM-tagged short links and QR codes (free).
- A spreadsheet of areas, codes, costs and responses.
- Access: the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads response and order data; level 2 sets up an unbooked campaign or an unplaced print or mail-drop order in a self-serve tool; level 3 books self-serve placements within the client's budget cap, and every action is logged. Contracts with media sellers always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **No tracking device on a placement,** so nobody knows what it did.
- **One drop and a verdict.** Without a holdout, a busy month looks like a win [9].
- **Buying wide reach on a small budget,** so nobody sees the ad more than once.
- **Claims the ad codes forbid,** such as green or health claims without evidence [6][7][8].
- **Mailing bought lists of named people** without a lawful basis, or ignoring opt-outs [5].
- **A weak or vague offer.** A reader may glance once, so give one clear action.
- **Counting only coupon codes,** which misses people who searched for the name later.

## 7. By business type

### General

Pick the one place the buyers gather offline and test it once with a code and a holdout. If the business already sells online, look for regions where it is weak and test there, so the comparison is clean. Ask sellers for unsold space and short runs. Write the go and stop lines down before booking. If one placement pays back, repeat it before adding new media, because one good result can be luck.

### SaaS

Mass offline ads rarely suit a young software company, but two forms can: a small run of addressed mail to named target accounts with a reason to reply (a short audit, a useful report), and sponsorship of a podcast or newsletter print edition the buyers read. In the UK and EU, check the lawful basis before mailing named people [5]. Track with a dedicated page and judge on qualified meetings booked.

### Ecommerce

Start with people who already know the brand: an insert in every parcel and a postcard to lapsed customers with a code. These cost little and show whether print moves this audience at all. Then test a neighbourhood drop near the best-selling postcodes in the order data. Judge on contribution margin per order after the discount, not on code use. Give each piece its own code so inserts and postcards can be told apart.

### Local

Local businesses are the natural fit. Test a mail drop around the shop or service area, a local paper or radio slot, and posters where customers wait. Use a different code or number for each, and a holdout of routes for the bigger drops [3][9]. Judge on cost per booked job or visit within the response window, and repeat seasonal offers that pay back. At the till and on calls, ask every new customer how they heard, and log it beside the code counts.

### Coaching

Broad offline ads rarely pay for a solo expert. Better tests: an ad in the niche's trade magazine or association newsletter, a postcard to past clients and warm contacts, and posters at events where the buyers gather. Point every piece to a free session or workshop with its own page. Track cost per booked call and keep any results claims provable [6][7]. Ask each new client where they first saw the coach, and log it.

## 8. Real examples

- **DuckDuckGo.** In January 2011 Gabriel Weinberg paid about 7,000 US dollars for a San Francisco billboard questioning Google's privacy practices. Wired, TechCrunch and others covered it, and Google responded publicly [10]. MIT Technology Review reported it faced Bay Bridge commuters and pointed to a privacy explainer site; whether it worked, it said, remained to be seen [11]. Much of its reach came from the press it earned, which is why the 2015 interview treated it as publicity as much as advertising [2].
