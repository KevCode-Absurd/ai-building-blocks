# Offline events: meetups, workshops and pop-ups

Channel 17 of 19 in the First traction kit. It updates the "Offline events" chapter of Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015). The book's ideas are summarised in our own words; the authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit. Facts checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed at the end.

## 1. What this channel is now

Offline events means running your own in-person gatherings, or showing up in a planned way at other people's: small meetups, hands-on workshops, classes, dinners, pop-up shops and market stalls. The book's point still stands: a room of the right people who meet you and the product face to face can start word of mouth that online channels struggle to match. The work is in getting the right people into the room and following up afterwards.

What is true as of November 2026:

- **Starting is cheap.** Meetup introduced a free Starter plan in 2025 for first-time organisers, and its 2026 plans include QR-code check-in; Meetup says 75% of its organisers started as members [2]. Eventbrite charges no fees on free events [3]. Luma's free plan allows unlimited events and guests; its paid plan is Luma Plus [4].
- **Free events lose more people.** PheedLoop's Event Data Lab (April 2026), using check-in data from more than 1,070 live events on its own platform, found a median no-show rate of about 20%. Free events had a median of about 28% against about 17% for paid events, and the smallest events (10 to 49 expected) had about 32% [5]. This is one vendor's data, so treat it as a rough guide, not a benchmark.
- **Online brands still go offline.** Warby Parker took a renovated school bus, fitted out as a showroom with stylists and its frames, on a planned nine-city, six-month tour that set off in 2012 [6].
- **Big events can tip a product.** Twitter marked its fifth birthday by noting that daily tweets went from about 20,000 to 60,000 during South by Southwest in 2007 [7].

Rules differ by place: venue capacity, food and alcohol licences, insurance, accessibility, street-trading permits for pop-ups, and consent for photos and for adding people to a mailing list. Check local rules before each event.

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

**It works when** buyers cluster in one city or community; the product is better understood by trying, tasting or talking; trust is the main barrier (coaching, local services, B2B); there is a community that wants to meet; and you need deep feedback early.

**It struggles when** buyers are spread thinly across many places; the product is cheap and the cost per attendee is high; you can run one event but not a regular series; or there is no plan to follow up with attendees.

## 3. Cheap tests

Weinberg suggests a first test should take no more than about a month or about $1,000 [1].

### Test 1: One small workshop or meetup

- **What:** Teach one useful thing your buyers want to learn, for a small room. Use a free venue (a partner's space, a library room, a café's back room). List it on Meetup, Luma or Eventbrite and promote it to your own list and a partner's.
- **Cost:** Time, snacks and printing.
- **Time:** Three to four weeks, including two to three weeks of promotion.
- **Go:** Attendees take a next step (a trial, a booking, a purchase) at a cost per customer at or below your break-even CAC, or most ask for the next session.
- **Stop:** Few sign-ups after promoting to your list and a partner's, and no next steps from those who came.

### Test 2: Co-host with a partner who has the audience

- **What:** Run one event with a complementary business or community group whose members are your buyers. They bring people; you bring the content.
- **Cost:** Time, and a share of costs.
- **Time:** Four weeks.
- **Go:** New contacts from the partner's audience become customers at or below break-even CAC.
- **Stop:** The partner's audience shows up but does not match your buyer, or takes no next step.

### Test 3: A one-day pop-up or market stall

- **What:** A stall at a market, a corner of a partner's shop, or a short-let space. Collect sign-ups with consent as well as sales.
- **Cost:** The stall or space fee, stock, display and time.
- **Time:** One day, plus two weeks of preparation.
- **Go:** Gross profit from the day covers the costs, or sign-ups come in at a cost per sign-up below your other channels.
- **Stop:** Neither.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Show-up rate** = attendees ÷ registrations. No-show rate = 1 − show-up rate [5].
- **Cost per attendee** = full event cost ÷ attendees.
- **Attendee-to-customer rate** = attendees who buy within your follow-up window ÷ attendees.
- **Cost per customer** = full event cost ÷ new customers from the event.
- **Repeat attendance** = returning attendees ÷ attendees. Rising repeat attendance is the sign a community is forming.
- **Partner share** = attendees who came through a partner ÷ attendees.
- **Pop-up:** gross profit per hour open; sign-up rate = sign-ups ÷ people who stopped at the stall (count them with a tally).

## 5. Tools

- **Listing and RSVPs:** Meetup (free Starter plan; paid plans) [2]; Eventbrite (no fees on free events; fees on paid tickets) [3]; Luma (free plan; Luma Plus paid) [4].
- **Venues:** partners' spaces, libraries and community rooms (often free); Peerspace and Appear Here for short-term spaces (paid, per booking).
- **Taking payments at a pop-up:** Square (free app; fees on each payment); Shopify POS (paid, with a Shopify plan).
- **Sign-ups and feedback:** Google Forms (free) behind a QR code; Typeform (free plan; paid plans).
- **Follow-up:** your email tool or HubSpot's free CRM (free tools; paid tiers).
- **Access:** the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads lists and past sign-ups; level 2 builds unpublished event pages and unsent invitations; level 3 publishes listings and sends invitations, reminders and follow-ups within the client's caps, and every action is logged. Venue bookings and deposits always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Promoting only on the event platform.** Do not rely on the listing alone; invite your own list, your partners' members and people one by one.
- **Free with no reminders.** Free events lose more people [5]. Send reminders, ask people to confirm, or try a small ticket price.
- **Selling hard at a learning event.** Teach first; make one clear offer at the end.
- **Not capturing contacts with consent.** Without a way to follow up, the event ends at the door.
- **One-off events.** A community grows from a regular rhythm, not a single night.
- **The wrong room size.** Twelve people in a room for twelve feels full; twelve in a hall feels like a failure.
- **Skipping permits and insurance** for pop-ups and public spaces.

## 7. By business type

### General

Pick a format your buyers already like: a talk, a hands-on class, a tasting, a dinner. Start small and run it on a regular schedule, because repeat attendance is how a community forms. Co-host with partners who already have the audience. Capture contacts with consent at the door and follow up within days with the one next step you want. After three or four events, compare cost per customer with your other channels before you commit to a full calendar.

### SaaS

User meetups work in cities where you already have customers; workshops work where you teach the job your software helps with, not the software itself. For developer tools, hack nights and small hands-on sessions suit the audience. Co-host with complementary tools that serve the same buyer. Ask attendees to bring a real problem and solve it live with the product. Measure activations and trials from attendees, not attendance.

### Ecommerce

Pop-ups, market stalls, sampling days and in-store events at your stockists let people touch, try and taste. Warby Parker's 2012 school-bus showroom is the classic example of an online brand going on the road [6]. Collect email sign-ups with consent, not just sales, and give a code to use online later so you can count what the day really produced. Work out gross profit per hour before booking a paid stall.

### Local

Your own premises are your best venue: classes, open days, demos, tastings and seasonal evenings. Partner with nearby businesses that serve the same people, and with local clubs, schools and charities. Offer a first-visit booking at the event, and track bookings in the following weeks against the same weeks before. Keep a regular calendar so regulars bring friends. For a clinic, gym or salon, a free taster session or a "meet the team" evening lowers the first-visit worry; for a trade, a short how-to at a local hardware shop or community hall shows skill before anyone asks for a quote.

### Coaching

Small in-person workshops and masterclasses let people experience your method before buying. In PheedLoop's data, paid events had fewer no-shows than free ones [5]; a small fee may also screen for serious buyers. Invite past clients to bring a guest. End with one clear next step, usually a booked call, and follow up within days. Track the share of attendees who book a call, and how many of those become clients.

## 8. Real examples

- **Twitter at South by Southwest (2007).** Twitter's own fifth-birthday statistics, reported by The Next Web in 2011, said the festival was its tipping point, with daily tweets rising from about 20,000 to 60,000 during the event [7]. The figures were widely repeated; the original birthday post is not linked in the report.
- **Warby Parker's Class Trip (2012).** The online eyewear company refitted a school bus as a library-style showroom staffed by stylists and planned a nine-city, six-month tour, recruiting a local brand ambassador in each city [6].
