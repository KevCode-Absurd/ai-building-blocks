# Publicity: press and journalists

Channel 2 of 19 in the First traction kit. It updates the "Publicity" chapter of Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015). The book's ideas are summarised in our own words; the authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit. Facts checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed at the end.

## 1. What this channel is now

Publicity is earned coverage: a journalist, editor or producer chooses to write or talk about you because it serves their readers. The book's core advice still works: start with smaller outlets that cover your niche, because bigger outlets read them and pick up stories that already have proof. Ryan Holiday called this "trading up the chain" in *Trust Me, I'm Lying* (2012).

Weinberg said in 2015 that DuckDuckGo should have pursued press much sooner than it did [1].

What is true as of November 2026:

- **Journalists rely on pitches but ignore most of them.** Muck Rack's State of Journalism 2026 surveyed nearly 1,100 journalists [2]. An agency summary of it reports that 86% say at least some of their stories start with a PR pitch, 50% seldom or never respond to pitches, and 43% seldom receive pitches that match their beat [3]. The full report sits behind a form, so treat these as a summary.
- **Many journalists now publish on their own.** In a separate Muck Rack study (October 2025), about one in three journalists self-published. These creator journalists prefer pitches under 200 words; 82% said some of their stories start from a pitch, but 72% called most pitches irrelevant [4].
- **Expert-source requests changed hands.** Cision rebranded HARO (Help a Reporter Out) as Connectively, then discontinued it, and sold HARO to Featured.com on 15 April 2025 [5]. HARO now sends free daily media queries and says it is powered by Featured [6]. Qwoted offers experts a free Basic plan [7].
- **Paid coverage must look like an ad.** US: the FTC's native advertising guide (December 2015) says content whose commercial nature is unclear needs a clear label such as "Ad" or "Paid Advertisement" [8]. UK: CAP Code rule 2.4 says advertorials must be clearly marked, for example as an "advertisement feature" [9]. EU: point 11 of Annex I to the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive bans paying for editorial content that promotes a product without making that clear [10].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

**It works when** you have real news (a launch with a story, new data, a timely angle, a fight with a giant); the founder can explain it simply; readers can try the product today; and trust matters to the buyer, so being covered acts as proof on your site and in sales calls.

**It struggles when** there is no story, only a product; the product or site cannot handle a spike; you expect coverage alone to keep bringing customers (spikes fade); or someone offers "guaranteed coverage" for a fee, which is advertising and must be labelled as such [8][9][10].

## 3. Cheap tests

Weinberg suggests a first test should take no more than about a month or about $1,000 [1]. These cost mostly time.

### Test 1: One angle, a short list of reporters

- **What:** Write one story angle. Find 15 reporters who covered something similar in the last three months. Send each a personal pitch under 200 words [4], with one follow-up.
- **Cost:** Time only.
- **Time:** Two weeks.
- **Go:** At least one reporter asks for more, or a piece runs.
- **Stop:** No replies after every pitch and follow-up. Change the angle, not the list.

### Test 2: Expert-source replies

- **What:** Answer journalist queries on HARO [6] or Qwoted's free plan [7], only where the founder is truly expert. Reply fast, briefly, with a quotable line and credentials.
- **Cost:** Time only, about 20 minutes a day.
- **Time:** Three weeks.
- **Go:** At least one published quote that names you and sends measurable visits or enquiries.
- **Stop:** No quote picked up after answering every relevant query in the period.

### Test 3: A data story for trade or local press

- **What:** Survey your customers or analyse your own data, then pitch the finding to one trade publication or local outlet.
- **Cost:** Time, plus a survey tool on a free plan.
- **Time:** Four weeks.
- **Go:** The piece runs and brings visits or leads at a cost per lead (your hours valued) at or below your other channels.
- **Stop:** It runs but brings no measurable visits or leads. Keep publicity for credibility; do not scale it as a way to get customers.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Reply rate** = replies ÷ pitches sent.
- **Coverage rate** = pieces published ÷ pitches sent.
- **Referral visits per piece** = visits with the outlet as referrer in the 7 days after it runs.
- **Baseline lift** = visits in the 7 days after the piece − average visits on the same weekdays over the previous 4 weeks. This catches people who search for you instead of clicking.
- **Customers per piece** = referred customers + customers who name the outlet when asked how they heard of you.
- **Cost per piece** = (hours × hourly rate + tools) ÷ pieces published.
- **Pick-ups** = later stories by other outlets that cite the first one, the sign that trading up is working.

## 5. Tools

- **Finding the right reporter:** Google News search and Google Alerts (free); the outlet's own tips or contact page (free); LinkedIn (free; paid plans).
- **Media databases:** Muck Rack, Cision and Prowly (paid).
- **Expert-source requests:** HARO (free daily emails) [6]; Qwoted (free Basic plan; paid plans) [7].
- **Press release wires:** PR Newswire, Business Wire and GlobeNewswire (paid). A wire release is distribution, not coverage.
- **Measuring:** Google Analytics and Google Search Console (free) for referral visits and brand searches.
- **Access:** the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads coverage and analytics; level 2 drafts pitches, unsent, in the client's mailbox or media tool; level 3 sends approved pitches and expert replies within the client's caps, and every action is logged. Wire releases and paid placements always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Mass pitching.** One email to hundreds of names. Journalists say most pitches miss their beat [3][4]. Read what each reporter covers first.
- **Pitching a product, not a story.** Ask: why would this reporter's readers care this week?
- **Long pitches.** Creator journalists prefer under 200 words [4]. Put the news in the first line.
- **Not being ready.** The site breaks, stock runs out, or there is no page for new visitors.
- **Asking to approve the story.** Offer facts and help; do not ask to edit.
- **Buying "featured in" placements without a label** [8][9][10].
- **Expecting one piece to last.** Plan what the piece will do after it runs: quote it on the site, send it to prospects, pitch the next outlet up.

## 7. By business type

### General

Find the five outlets your buyers actually read; customer answers beat any media list. Pitch the smallest relevant outlet first and use the piece to pitch the next one up. Have a one-page press kit ready: what you do, a founder bio, real photos, a few verified numbers, and how to reach you the same day. Expert-source replies are the cheapest steady test for any business with a credible founder.

### SaaS

Trade and niche tech publications usually beat general tech press for early traction, because their readers are the buyers. Strong angles are original data from your product (anonymised and with permission), a sharp opinion on a change in the industry, or a customer story with real numbers the customer agrees to share. A funding round alone is rarely news to buyers. The founder as an expert source on the problem you solve builds credibility that also helps in sales calls.

### Ecommerce

Product journalists need samples, clear photos, prices and stock that holds. Gift guides and seasonal round-ups are planned well ahead, so ask each outlet when it plans them and pitch before that. Some outlets sell places in round-ups; if you pay, it is an ad and must be labelled [8][9][10]. Track coverage with a referral link where the outlet allows it, plus the "How did you hear about us?" question at checkout.

### Local

Local papers, radio, local TV and neighbourhood newsletters want local people and local change: an opening, a milestone, a hire, a charity link, a fix for a local problem. A short, friendly email to the right desk with a photo works. Make it easy to visit: the address, the hours, and the offer in one line. Count bookings and walk-ins in the week after, and ask new customers where they heard of you.

### Coaching

Coaches and consultants win publicity as expert sources more than as news. Answer journalist queries in your exact field, pitch trade publications your clients read, and offer specific, useful opinions with examples. Contributed articles can work if the outlet accepts them; paid "contributor" spots are ads and need a label [8][9][10]. Put each piece on your site and use it in proposals, because coverage does most of its work as proof.

## 8. Real examples

- **Airbnb at the 2008 Democratic National Convention.** HuffPost reported in 2012 that Brian Chesky read an article asking how Denver, with about 28,000 hotel rooms, would house about 80,000 convention-goers. Airbnb pitched Denver bloggers, and its fix for the shortage drew news coverage [11]. The news supplied the angle; the product supplied the answer.
- **DuckDuckGo.** Weinberg said the company should have pursued PR much sooner, and later counted print and TV coverage among its efforts [1].
