# Unconventional PR: stunts and customer appreciation

Channel 3 of 19 in the First traction kit. It updates the "Unconventional PR" chapter of Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015). The book's ideas are summarised in our own words; the authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit. Facts checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed at the end.

## 1. What this channel is now

The book splits this channel in two. **Stunts** are remarkable things done in public so people and press talk about you. **Customer appreciation** is small, surprising care for existing customers (a handwritten note, an unexpected gift, a human gesture) that makes them tell others. Both aim for attention you do not pay for per view.

In 2026 most stunts land first in social feeds, and the press picks up what is already spreading. Appreciation travels the same way: one customer's post about a kind gesture can reach far more people than the gesture cost.

What is true as of November 2026:

- **Prize promotions have rules.** In the UK, CAP Code section 8 covers competitions, prize draws, instant wins and free gifts. It asks promoters to state significant conditions (how to enter, closing date, prizes, who runs it), to award prizes as described, normally within 30 days, and to use independent judging or supervision where chance or judgement decides [8]. US sweepstakes and contest rules vary by state, and EU countries set many prize-promotion rules nationally, so check before you launch.
- **Gifts must not buy praise.** The US FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials, announced 14 August 2024, bans giving payment or incentives for reviews that must say something positive (or negative), and requires insiders' reviews to disclose their connection [9].
- **Stunts in public space can backfire badly.** In 2007, light-up devices placed around Boston to promote a Cartoon Network show set off a bomb scare; Turner Broadcasting and the agency Interference agreed to pay $2 million to cover the response and fund other programmes [7].
- **One of the book's authors used it.** In 2015 Weinberg's interviewer cited DuckDuckGo's San Francisco billboard as an unconventional move that mixed an offline ad with PR [1].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

**It works when** the product has a clear point you can make visible in one picture or sentence (privacy, speed, price, craft); the brand can be playful; the business is small enough that personal gestures are possible and noticed; and you have no ad budget but do have ideas and time.

**It struggles when** the stunt has nothing to do with the product (people remember the joke, not you); the sector is regulated (health, finance, alcohol, children); the idea could look like a threat, a hoax or a safety risk; it uses public space without permission; or the business cannot handle a sudden spike in orders or calls.

## 3. Cheap tests

Weinberg suggests a first test should take no more than about a month or about $1,000 [1].

### Test 1: Appreciation sprint with a holdout

- **What:** Take your newest customers. Split them at random into two groups. Send one group a handwritten note or small gift with no ask attached; send the other nothing.
- **Cost:** Cards, small gifts and postage, inside your test budget, plus writing time.
- **Time:** Four weeks of sending, then watch both groups for one buying cycle.
- **Go:** The note group buys again, refers, or posts about you clearly more often than the holdout group.
- **Stop:** No difference between the groups after the window.

### Test 2: Pitch the stunt before you build it

- **What:** Brainstorm ten stunt ideas. Score each for link to the product's main point, cost, and risk. Describe the top one in a short note to five journalists or creators who cover your niche, before spending anything.
- **Cost:** Time only.
- **Time:** Two weeks.
- **Go:** At least one says they would cover it.
- **Stop:** None bite. Rework the idea or drop it.

### Test 3: A small-batch oddity

- **What:** Make a limited, odd version of your product or a themed side product that makes the product's point (Airbnb's 2008 election cereal is the model [5]). Sell it in a small batch with a story page.
- **Cost:** Production and shipping for the batch, inside the test ceiling.
- **Time:** Three to four weeks.
- **Go:** The batch sells out and earns at least one unpaid press or creator mention.
- **Stop:** Unsold stock and no mentions.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Earned mentions** = unpaid articles, posts and creator mentions you can link to.
- **Cost per earned mention** = total stunt cost ÷ earned mentions.
- **Brand search lift** = brand searches in the 14 days after ÷ brand searches in the 14 days before (Google Search Console or Trends).
- **Referral visits** = visits from mention links + the baseline lift in direct visits.
- **Appreciation lift** = repeat-purchase rate (note group) − repeat-purchase rate (holdout). Do the same for referrals and reviews.
- **Gesture payback** = cost per gesture ÷ (appreciation lift × gross profit per repeat order).
- **Spike readiness** = orders or bookings you can fulfil per day vs the busiest day you expect.

## 5. Tools

- **Mention tracking:** Google Alerts and Talkwalker Alerts (free); Brand24 and Mention (paid).
- **Brand search:** Google Search Console and Google Trends (free).
- **Random split for holdouts:** a spreadsheet's random function in Google Sheets (free) on an export from your store or CRM.
- **Notes and gifts:** paper, pen and postage; your store's gift-note field (part of your plan).
- **Story page:** your own site; Canva for images (free plan; paid plans).
- **Access:** the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads sales data and mentions; level 2 drafts notes, pitches and the story page, unpublished; level 3 sends approved messages and publishes the story page within the client's caps, and every action is logged. A public stunt always needs a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **A stunt with no link to the product.** Attention without memory. Ask: what does this make people believe about us?
- **Risk you did not price in.** Public space, emergency services, children, offence. Boston 2007 cost the companies $2 million [7].
- **Tying gifts to praise.** "Leave five stars and get a gift" breaks the US rule [9] and spoils the gesture.
- **Prize promotions without terms.** No closing date, unclear prize, no named promoter [8].
- **Faking it.** Invented scarcity, fake "leaked" stories or staged customer moments. They get found out.
- **Not ready for success.** No stock, a slow site, nobody answering the phone.
- **Copying a famous stunt.** The second person to do it gets nothing.

## 7. By business type

### General

Start with appreciation, because it is cheap, low-risk and measurable with a holdout. For stunts, write the product's main point in one sentence, then look for a picture that proves it. Pitch the idea before building it. Keep a "what could go wrong" list for each idea and have someone outside the business read it. Plan the follow-through: a page that explains the stunt and an offer for new visitors.

### SaaS

Stunts that dramatise the product's promise work best: DuckDuckGo's billboard turned a privacy claim into a sign facing commuters [3][4]. Appreciation for software is often a founder's personal email to new users, a public thank-you to people who report bugs, or a small gift to early champions inside customer companies. Measure activation, retention and referrals, not likes. A public data stunt, such as an honest report on your own numbers, can also earn coverage.

### Ecommerce

Limited editions, odd collaborations and packaging surprises are the usual tools. A small batch keeps the cost inside the test ceiling and makes scarcity real, not invented. Appreciation shows up in the box: a handwritten note, a sample, or a surprise upgrade for repeat buyers. Chewy's habit of handwritten cards, pet portraits and sympathy flowers is a well-reported example [6]. Never tie the gift to a positive review [9].

### Local

A local business can do things a large brand cannot: name a dish after a regular, hold a free service day for a cause, decorate the window around a town event, or thank a local team. Local press and neighbourhood accounts pick these up because they are about the town. Check permits before using pavements, parks or roads. Measure bookings and walk-ins against the same weeks before, and ask new customers where they heard of you.

### Coaching

Appreciation suits coaches well: a handwritten note at the end of a programme, a free alumni session, or a public shout-out (with consent) for a client's win. Stunts should show the method: a public 30-day challenge, an open coaching session, or a bold, useful prediction you are willing to be held to. Keep claims about results honest and typical. Measure referrals and booked calls from past clients.

## 8. Real examples

- **Half.com renames Halfway, Oregon (2000).** The town of just over 360 people agreed to call itself half.com for a year. The company gave it $75,000 and 22 computers for the elementary school, and its marketing director said media calls came in from as far as Holland and Japan [2].
- **DuckDuckGo's billboard (January 2011).** Weinberg paid $7,000 for a San Francisco billboard questioning Google's privacy policies. He said searches had been about 2.5 million a month and spiked after it went up [3]. MIT Technology Review covered it, noting it faced Bay Bridge commuters [4]. The spike is the founder's own account.
- **Airbnb's election cereal (2008).** AirBed & Breakfast made 500 boxes each of "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's" at $39 a box, tied to its idea of hosting campaign volunteers [5].
- **Chewy (2022).** After a customer's dog died, Chewy refunded unopened food, suggested donating it to a shelter, and sent flowers. Chewy's senior director of customer service said handwritten holiday cards, hand-painted pet portraits and sympathy flowers are part of company culture [6].
