# Viral loops and referral programmes

Channel 10 of 19. Book chapter it updates: Viral marketing. Checked 2026-11-06.

Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (Portfolio, 2015) [1], gave viral marketing its own chapter: growth that comes from users bringing in other users. This file restates the channel in our own words and adds referral programmes. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. What this channel is now

Two kinds of loop, often combined:

- **Built-in loops.** The product spreads as people use it: invites to shared work, files or pages others see, a "Made with" badge on free outputs.
- **Referral programmes.** You reward customers for bringing friends, usually with a two-sided reward so both get something.

The math most teams use comes from David Skok [2]. The viral coefficient K = invites sent per user × the share of invites that convert. Above 1, each wave of users brings a bigger one; below 1, growth stalls on its own [2]. A loop below 1 still multiplies: each customer you win brings 1 ÷ (1 − K) customers in total, the sum of a geometric series. Cycle time, the days from a user joining to their invitee joining, can matter even more than K, because a shorter cycle compounds far faster [2].

What changed by November 2026 is the rulebook around rewards:

- **US.** The FTC's rule announced on 14 August 2024 bans rewards conditioned on writing positive or negative reviews [8]. People who get a discount or commission for recommending you should disclose it [10].
- **Google Maps.** Reviews prompted by rewards are removed, and businesses may not ask only happy customers [9].
- **UK.** The Information Commission says that if you encourage customers to forward your marketing to friends, you are instigating those messages, cannot rely on the soft opt-in, and would need each friend's consent; letting customers share a code on their own is lower risk [7].
- **EU.** Marketing emails need prior opt-in consent, so the same caution applies to "invite a friend" emails you send [11].

## 2. When it works and when it doesn't

It works when the product is used with or shown to other people, when customers already recommend you unprompted, when a reward costs less than your other ways of winning a customer, and when a friend sees value quickly, which keeps the cycle short.

It doesn't work when the purchase is private (debt, some health needs), when you have too few happy customers to ask, or when rewards attract deal hunters and fake accounts. Rewards amplify word of mouth; they rarely create it.

## 3. Cheap tests

Set go and stop lines with the client first. Target cost per customer = the most the client can pay to win a customer, from the variant playbook. Lines below are sums from the client's own figures, not industry averages.

**Test 1: Ask your happiest customers.** Personally ask 30 to 50 recent customers who gave good feedback to refer one person, with a two-sided reward paid only when the friend buys.
- Cost: rewards on success only; one day.
- Time: three weeks.
- Go: cost per referred customer ((rewards + hours × hourly cost) ÷ referred customers) at or under target.
- Stop: zero referred customers after the ask and one reminder.

**Test 2: Waitlist or launch referral ladder.** Give everyone who signs up a personal link and two to four reward tiers for bringing friends, as Harry's did before launch [4]. Block repeat sign-ups and confirm each address.
- Cost: reward stock and a referral tool trial; two to three days to set up.
- Time: one to two weeks live.
- Go: (spend on direct sign-ups + rewards) ÷ all sign-ups at or under your target cost per sign-up.
- Stop: referred sign-ups do not cover the rewards, or most rewards go to fake sign-ups.

**Test 3: Built-in share step.** Add an invite step, a share link, or a small "Made with [brand]" link on free outputs, each tagged with UTM parameters.
- Cost: one to three days of build.
- Time: four weeks.
- Go: invited users become paying customers at or above your normal rate, and the loop's customers cost less than target once build time is counted.
- Stop: share rate and invite sign-ups stay flat for four weeks.

**Test 4: Word-of-mouth baseline.** Add "How did you hear about us?" with a "friend or colleague" option at checkout or booking.
- Cost: free; one hour.
- Time: 30 days.
- Go: "friend or colleague" is one of the top three answers, so run Test 1.
- Stop: hardly anyone names a friend; fix the product first.

## 4. The numbers to watch

- **Viral coefficient K** = invites per user × invite conversion rate [2].
- **Customers per customer won** = 1 ÷ (1 − K), when K is below 1.
- **Cycle time** = average days from a user joining to their invitee joining [2].
- **Participation rate** = customers who refer at least once ÷ customers asked.
- **Cost per referred customer** = (rewards + tools + hours × hourly cost) ÷ referred customers.
- **Referred share** = referred new customers ÷ all new customers.
- **Fraud rate** = rejected referrals ÷ all referrals.

## 5. Tools

- ReferralCandy: paid, with a free trial. Shopify, BigCommerce, WooCommerce, Squarespace and Magento stores.
- Smile.io: free plan that includes referrals; paid plans. Shopify and BigCommerce.
- Rewardful: paid, with a free trial. Affiliate and referral programmes for SaaS billed through Stripe.
- GrowSurf: paid, with a free trial. Referral programmes with fraud checks.
- SparkLoop: free newsletter recommendation features; referral programmes are a paid add-on.
- beehiiv and Kit: newsletter referral programmes on paid plans.
- Google Analytics 4: free.
- Access: the lead's brief sets a level per tool. Level 1 reads sales, review and invite data; level 2 sets up the referral programme and emails, switched off; level 3 switches the programme on and sends the ask within the client's caps, and every action is logged. Reward payouts always need a new yes.

## 6. Common mistakes

- Adding rewards before anyone recommends you unprompted.
- Rewarding only the referrer; Dropbox credited a two-sided reward [3].
- Emailing addresses that customers type in for their friends [7][11].
- Paying for reviews, or asking only happy customers to review [8][9].
- Referrers sharing links without saying they get a reward [10].
- No fraud checks; Harry's saw most abuse at its top tier [4].
- Counting sign-ups instead of K and cycle time.

## 7. By business type

### General
Measure word of mouth first with Test 4, because it is free and tells you whether there is anything to amplify. Ask for referrals at the moment a customer gets a good result, not at the next invoice. Keep the reward two-sided, pay it only on a purchase, and keep sharing in the customer's hands: a link or code they send themselves. Start by hand with a spreadsheet; buy a referral tool only once Test 1 shows people will refer.

### SaaS
Build the loop into the product. Invites to shared work, public pages or forms that carry a small badge, and in-product credit for both sides cost little and run without anyone asking. Tally's free forms carry a "Made with Tally" badge that feeds its growth [6]; Dropbox credited a two-sided incentive with lifting sign-ups [3]. Measure K and cycle time for each loop separately, and shorten the time from invite to first value.

### Ecommerce
Use a give-get offer: the friend gets money off a first order and the referrer gets store credit. Prompt after delivery, when the product has arrived and impressed. Before launch, a waitlist ladder like Harry's can build a list fast [4]. Block self-referrals and stacked codes, and cap rewards per customer. Smile.io and ReferralCandy plug into common store platforms. Judge it on referred customers' repeat purchases, not just first orders.

### Local
Hand out referral cards with a first-visit offer for the friend, or run "bring a friend" sessions if you are a gym, salon or class. Ask every customer for a Google review, with no reward and no filtering, because Google removes reviews prompted by rewards [9]. In the UK, let customers pass on a code themselves rather than collecting friends' emails [7]. Track referrals on the booking form.

### Coaching
Past clients are the best source, so ask at the end of an engagement while results are fresh. Offer alumni something useful, such as a bonus session or a discount on the next programme. Partners and podcast hosts who earn a commission must say so [10]. If you run a newsletter, a referral programme like Morning Brew's can add readers steadily [5]; beehiiv, Kit and SparkLoop offer one.

## 8. Real examples

- **Dropbox.** Drew Houston's April 2010 slides say a two-sided referral incentive, inspired by PayPal, permanently raised sign-ups by 60%, and users sent 2.8 million direct referral invites in the 30 days to April 2010. Paid search had cost $233 to $388 per customer for a $99 product [3].
- **Harry's.** Co-founder Jeff Raider wrote in July 2014 that, before the shop opened, Harry's gathered nearly 100,000 email addresses in a week. About 77% came through referrals, from about 20,000 people who referred about 65,000 friends, with rewards at 5, 10, 25 and 50 referrals [4].
- **Morning Brew.** In July 2020 its COO told Digiday the newsletter had over 2 million subscribers, referrals drove 35% of its growth, and more than 225,000 people had referred at least one person [5].
- **Tally.** In October 2025 the form builder described its loop: people discover Tally by filling in a form, free forms carry a badge, and more creators lead to more paid upgrades [6].
