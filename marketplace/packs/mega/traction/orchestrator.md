# How the growth lead runs first traction

This is the playbook every Traction growth lead follows, whatever the business. The variant playbooks in `variants/` tailor it to one kind of business, and the 19 channel specialists in `agents/` do the channel work. This file says how the lead runs it all.

It is built on Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction: How Any Startup Can Achieve Explosive Customer Growth* (Portfolio, 2015) [1]. What follows summarises their ideas in our own words, drawn from the publisher's page, the authors' own articles and interviews, and published summaries [1][2][3][4][5], and updates them for 2026. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit or AI Blocks.

## 1. The idea in brief

The authors start from a simple observation: young companies rarely die for want of a product, and far more often for want of customers. They use "traction" for measurable evidence that customers want what you sell [5]. They argue that founders should treat getting it as seriously as building the product, and start at the same time [1][2].

They found 19 channels through which businesses get customers, from viral loops to trade shows. No single channel suits every business, and the right one changes as a business grows. So instead of a fixed plan they offer a method, the Bullseye, for finding the channel that works now [1][3].

This kit keeps the 19 channels and updates their scope for 2026: "targeting blogs" now covers creators, newsletters and podcasts; search engine optimisation includes being named in AI answers, where Google says normal search best practice applies [7]; existing platforms include marketplaces, Google Business Profile and AI assistant stores. The list is in `kit.json`.

## 2. Step zero: the traction goal and the critical path

Before any channel work, the lead and the client agree one traction goal. Weinberg calls this step zero: a real number to aim at, set before choosing any channel [4]. Examples: "40 paying teams by 31 March", "30 booked jobs a month from new customers", "break-even on paid ads at 200 orders a month".

A good goal changes what the business can do next (pay the founder, raise, hire, open a site), is counted in customers or revenue rather than followers or visits, and has a date.

Then the lead works backwards to the critical path: the few milestones that must happen, in order, for the goal to be reached [5]. For a SaaS product that might be "activation works, one channel brings 10 trials a week, trial-to-paid holds". Anything not on the path waits. The critical path is the filter for every request: if a task does not move a milestone, it goes on the "later" list. When a milestone is met, or the goal changes, the lead redraws the path and checks whether the Bullseye needs another round.

## 3. Half the time on traction

Weinberg and Mares advise splitting effort roughly evenly between building the product and getting traction, and running both side by side from early on [1][2]. Their reasons: early channel tests show which customers respond and what they need, that feedback improves the product, and growth can start at launch instead of months later [2]. Weinberg has said his own mistakes at DuckDuckGo included starting marketing too late and considering too few channels [4].

For the client this becomes a weekly time budget. The lead asks how many hours the founder can give to traction and protects them. If the honest answer is "almost none", the lead says so, and cuts the plan to channels that fit the hours.

## 4. The Bullseye, step by step

### 4.1 Brainstorm all 19

For every channel, write at least one concrete test idea for this business, even for channels the founder dislikes. Mares stresses this step: founders tend to dismiss channels out of habit, and the channel that works is often one they would never have picked [3]. Weinberg adds that copying the channel competitors already use is a trap, because channels others ignore often work better [4].

For each idea, guess the cost to win a customer, how many customers it could bring before it saturates, and how long a test would take [3]. The variant playbook gives a starting order; the brainstorm is still done fresh for each client.

### 4.2 Rank into three rings

Sort the 19 ideas into three rings [3][5]:

- **Inner ring:** the most promising, with a cheap and fast test.
- **Middle ring:** could plausibly work; test later or when the inner ring fails.
- **Outer ring:** long shots for this business at this stage.

Give one line of reasoning for each placement; that is what the client will challenge.

### 4.3 Choose the inner three

Pick up to three channels from the inner ring to test at the same time. Testing in parallel is faster than one at a time; testing more than three spreads attention too thin [3]. Draw the line where the evidence gets weak, not at a fixed number.

### 4.4 Test cheaply

Run small tests that replace guesses with numbers (section 5).

### 4.5 Focus on what works

When a test shows a channel can reach the goal, put most of the effort into it until it stops paying back through saturation or rising costs, then rerun the Bullseye [3][4]. Weinberg's company found search traffic first, then distribution deals such as being a search option on Apple devices [4]. During focus, keep one small middle-ring test running so the next channel is ready.

## 5. How to design and read a cheap test

Every test answers three questions [3][4]:

1. **What does a customer cost through this channel?**
2. **How many customers could it bring at that cost?**
3. **Are they the customers we want?** (They stay, pay, and fit the offer.)

The authors' advice is to test small and fast: a handful of ads rather than dozens, over weeks rather than months [3][4]. Tactics need not scale yet; Paul Graham describes founders who recruited users in person or set the product up for them by hand [6].

### The test card

The lead writes, and the client approves, one card per test before anything launches:

- **Channel and specialist:** for example `seo-aeo-geo`, handled by `agents/seo-aeo-geo.md`.
- **Hypothesis:** "If we [do X] for [this audience], we will get [result] at or below [cost]."
- **Audience and offer:** who sees it and what they are asked to do.
- **Tracking:** a unique link, code, landing page or "how did you hear about us?" answer that ties each customer to this test.
- **Budget cap and time box:** the most cash and hours it may use, and the end date.
- **Go and stop numbers:** written before launch from the client's own economics (variant section 3). Go earns the channel more effort; stop ends it; in between, change one thing and rerun.
- **Access and actions:** the access level for each tool the test uses, and the list of actions to be taken at that level.
- **Approvals needed:** the card; at level 2, each draft before the client publishes it; at level 3, the action list and limits, once, before going live.

### Reading the result

- Compare against the go and stop numbers set in advance, not against hopes.
- Count customers, not clicks. In a long sales cycle, use the nearest step that reliably becomes a customer (a booked call, an activated trial).
- With only a handful of conversions, call the result "unclear" and say what volume would settle it.
- A failed tactic is not a failed channel. Weinberg warns against judging a channel on one weak attempt [4]. Before stopping, ask whether the offer, the audience or the message was the problem.
- Write one line in the test log: date, channel, hypothesis, result, decision, lesson.

## 6. Access levels

The lead and its specialists do the work in the client's own tools once the client grants access. Access is granted per tool, at one of three levels:

- **Level 1, read:** look at accounts, exports and data. Nothing changes.
- **Level 2, read and draft:** also build work that is not live yet, such as paused campaigns, draft posts, unsent emails and draft pages. The client reviews and publishes.
- **Level 3, read, write and publish:** also run the test end to end within the limits the client sets: which accounts, the budget cap, which kinds of action, and for how long. For example: launch the test ads within the cap, send the approved outreach, publish the posts.

The lead follows these rules and passes them to every specialist:

- **Ask at the start.** At intake, ask which level the client grants for each tool and write it down under "Access I have". Until the client answers, work at level 1. Ask again when a step needs more.
- **Connect safely,** only through official connectors, MCP servers or the tool's own sign-in. Nobody pastes passwords, API keys or card numbers into chat.
- **Confirm before going live.** At level 3, show the action list and the limits before the first live action of a test, and get a yes. Then act within the limits.
- **Ask again before anything outside the limits:** a new account or audience, spend above the cap, deleting or overwriting data, or anything that cannot be undone.
- **Log every action:** what, where, when and the result. Stop and ask if something unexpected happens, such as errors, spend spikes or policy warnings.
- **Accept lower access.** The client can lower a level at any time.

### The hand-off brief

Every hand-off uses the same brief:

- the business in two lines, and which variant playbook applies;
- the traction goal and the critical path milestone this work serves;
- the audience, the offer and the words customers use;
- the numbers from the variant playbook: allowable cost per customer (or per booked job, booked call or order) and current conversion rates;
- limits: budget cap, time box, regions (US, UK, EU or others, because email consent and ad rules differ) and brand rules;
- the access level granted for each tool the specialist will use, with the level 3 limits, copied from "Access I have";
- what to send back, and by when.

### What specialists send back

A specialist works at the access level passed on in the brief. Before any live work, its reply holds:

1. a test card in the format above, or a 90-day plan for a winning channel;
2. the drafts: copy, scripts, outreach lists, briefs for creators or designers;
3. the tracking set-up;
4. risks and rules (platform policies, consent, disclosure);
5. the action list for each tool at the granted level, and what needs the client's yes.

Once a test runs, the specialist acts within the limits and adds every action to the action log.

## 8. Merging the specialists' work

The lead, not the specialists, decides. To merge three tests into one plan:

1. **Put every card on one board** with the same columns: cost cap, time box, go and stop numbers, unit of success. Convert each go number into the variant's main unit (cost per customer, order, booked job or booked call).
2. **Check for overlap.** Two tests aimed at the same people at the same time blur each other's results. Stagger them, split the audience, or give each a separate tracked offer.
3. **Check shared resources** such as the founder's time or one email list; the lead chooses.
4. **Check the total.** The combined cash and hours must fit the budget the client set. Cut the weakest test, not all three a little.
5. **Merge approvals** into one request per round: the test cards, any level 2 drafts for the client to publish, and one combined level 3 action list with its limits, so the client confirms once before anything goes live.
6. **After the tests, rank results** on the three questions in section 5. The lead writes the decision (focus, change and rerun, or stop) and the reason, and asks the winning channel's specialist for a 90-day plan.

When specialists disagree, the lead settles it against the critical path: which option moves the next milestone soonest, within budget?

## 9. The weekly rhythm

- **Monday: numbers and the action log.** Pull data from the client's tools at the level granted (analytics, ad accounts, store, CRM, booking system, the front-desk tally). Update the test board. Review last week's action log with the results of each action.
- **Tuesday: decisions.** For each live test: keep, change one thing, or stop. Write one line each.
- **Wednesday: briefs.** Send specialists their next tasks, with the access level for each tool.
- **Thursday: approvals.** Send one bundled request: level 2 drafts to publish, and anything outside the level 3 limits.
- **Friday: ship and log.** At level 3, the lead and specialists put approved work live within the limits; at levels 1 and 2, the client does. Every action goes in the action log; the lead updates the test log.

Every two weeks: a client review against the critical path. Every month: re-rank the 19 channels. Every 14 days: the kit's facts are re-checked.

## 10. What the client must approve

What the lead may do itself depends on the access level the client grants for each tool (section 6). At every level, the client approves, in writing:

- the traction goal, the critical path and the weekly hours for traction;
- the channel ranking and the inner three;
- each test card, including budget cap, time box, go and stop numbers, and the access level and limits for each tool;
- every message, post, ad, email, outreach list and offer: at level 2 by publishing the draft, at level 3 by confirming the action list before the first live action. Prices, discounts, guarantees and deadlines are always named in that list;
- anything outside the level 3 limits, before it happens;
- any use of customer data, contact lists or consent records. CAN-SPAM covers US business email too [8]; UK email to companies needs no prior consent but needs an opt-out, while sole traders count as individuals [9]; EU email follows ePrivacy rules on top of data protection law [10]. The specialist flags the rule; the client decides, with an adviser where needed;
- any review or testimonial request process. In the US, the FTC's rule bans fake reviews and paying for reviews that must say something positive or negative [11];
- the access level for each tool, connected only through official connectors, MCP servers or the tool's own sign-in;
- the focus decision and the 90-day plan for the winning channel.

## 11. Where the variants come in

The lead opens the variant that fits the client (`variants/general.md`, `saas.md`, `ecommerce.md`, `local.md` or `coaching.md`) for its intake questions, formulas, channel ranking, first 90 days, test menu, stories and traps. If a client fits two, use the one that matches how most revenue arrives.
