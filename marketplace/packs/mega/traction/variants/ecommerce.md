# First traction for online stores and DTC brands

Variant playbook for the ecommerce growth lead. It applies the Bullseye method from Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015) [1], in our own words, to brands that sell products online. Read `orchestrator.md` first: it explains the traction goal, the critical path, cheap tests, the channel team and approvals. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. Who this is for

Founders of online stores and direct-to-consumer brands on Shopify, WooCommerce, Etsy, Amazon or TikTok Shop, up to roughly five million in yearly sales. Online sales are a large and growing share of retail: the US Census Bureau put ecommerce at 17.1% of US retail sales in the second quarter of 2026 [2]. That also means every store competes with thousands of others for the same feeds and search results.

Typical situations:

- the first orders came from friends and family, and then stopped;
- ads ran for a few weeks, sales came in, but nobody knows whether they made money;
- the brand is on five channels and doing well on none.

Stores differ from other businesses in three ways that shape everything below. Every order has a cost of goods, shipping and fees, so margin decides which channels you can afford. Stock ties up cash, so a test that sells out can hurt as much as one that flops. And the second order is where most of the profit often sits, so the first order's cost must be judged against what a customer buys over the following months.

## 2. Intake questions

1. What do you sell, at what prices, and what are your three bestsellers?
2. What does one order cost you: goods, packaging, shipping, payment fees, and returns?
3. What is your average order value, and how many orders a month now?
4. What share of customers buy again within 60 and 90 days? What do they buy second?
5. Where do your sales come from today, channel by channel (store analytics, a post-purchase "how did you hear about us?" survey, discount codes)?
6. What traction goal do you need, by when, and why (break even on ads, hit a wholesale minimum, pay yourself)?
7. How much cash can go into tests this quarter, and how much stock is on hand or on order?
8. Have you run Meta, TikTok, Google or Amazon ads? For the store and each ad account, which access level will you grant: 1 read, 2 read and draft, or 3 read, write and publish within limits you set, such as a budget cap?
9. Are you on any marketplaces, and do your margins survive their fees?
10. Which creators, magazines or newsletters do your customers follow? Have any mentioned you?
11. How big is your email and SMS list, how was consent collected, and which automated flows run now?
12. What makes your product different in a way a customer would repeat to a friend?
13. Which countries do you ship to, and are there product rules (cosmetics, food, children's items, supplements) we must respect?

## 3. The numbers that matter for ecommerce

Contribution margin is the base of every ecommerce decision. Work it out per order, before any marketing spend.

- **Net revenue per order** = average order value − discounts − VAT or sales tax collected.
- **Contribution margin per order (before ads)** = net revenue per order − cost of goods − packaging − shipping − payment and platform fees − a returns allowance.
- **Contribution margin %** = contribution margin per order ÷ net revenue per order.
- **Break-even ROAS** = 1 ÷ contribution margin %. Below this, each ad-driven first order loses money.
- **Break-even first-order CPA** = contribution margin per order. The most you can pay for a first order without losing money on it.
- **90-day contribution per customer** = contribution margin on the first order + contribution margin on repeat orders within 90 days, averaged over the cohort.
- **Allowable CAC** = 90-day contribution per customer, if you can wait 90 days for cash back; the first-order figure if you cannot.
- **New-customer CAC** = spend aimed at new customers ÷ new customers in the same period.
- **Blended MER** = total revenue ÷ total marketing spend. Use it to sanity-check platform-reported ROAS, which can over-count.
- **Repeat purchase rate (60 or 90 days)** = customers with a second order within 60 or 90 days of their first ÷ first-time customers in that cohort.
- **Conversion rate** = orders ÷ sessions. **Average order value** = revenue ÷ orders.
- **Email and SMS share of revenue** = revenue attributed to email and SMS ÷ total revenue.
- **Marketplace contribution** = marketplace sale price − marketplace fees − fulfilment − cost of goods − ads on that marketplace.

Worked example (made-up numbers, to show the maths only): net revenue per order 60; goods 18, packaging and shipping 8, fees 2, returns allowance 3. Contribution = 29, or about 48%. Break-even ROAS = 1 ÷ 0.48 ≈ 2.1. Break-even first-order CPA = 29.

## 4. Channel ranking for ecommerce

A starting order before the brainstorm. A low-price, repeat-purchase brand and a high-price, once-a-decade product will differ.

1. **Social and display ads** (`social-and-display-ads`): *usually inner ring*. Meta and TikTok can find buyers by interest, and results read quickly against break-even ROAS.
2. **Affiliate and creator commissions** (`affiliate-programs`): *usually inner ring*. Seeding product to creators with codes and paying per sale keeps cost tied to orders.
3. **Existing platforms** (`existing-platforms`): *usually inner ring*. Amazon, Etsy and TikTok Shop already hold buyers; AI shopping is joining them, with ChatGPT launching in-chat checkout for US Etsy sellers in September 2025 [8].
4. **Email marketing** (`email-marketing`): *usually inner ring*. Welcome, abandoned-checkout and post-purchase flows raise the return on every other channel.
5. **Content marketing** (`content-marketing`): *usually inner ring*. Short videos of the product in use feed organic reach and give ads their creative.
6. **Publicity** (`publicity`): *test when ready*. Gift guides and product round-ups reach people who are about to buy; guides close well before the season, so ask each editor for the deadline.
7. **SEO plus AI answers** (`seo-aeo-geo`): *test when ready*. Product, collection and buying-guide pages compound, but slowly.
8. **Search ads** (`search-ads`): *test when ready*. Shopping ads capture existing demand; inner ring only if people already search for your product type.
9. **Creators, newsletters and podcasts** (`creators-and-newsletters`): *test when ready*. Paid placements in niche newsletters and podcasts, once you know which message sells.
10. **Viral loops and referral** (`viral-and-referral`): *test when ready*. A refer-a-friend offer works once customers love the product enough to talk.
11. **Offline events** (`offline-events`): *test when ready*. Pop-ups and markets give fast feedback and content, but rarely scale on their own.
12. **Unconventional PR** (`unconventional-pr`): *test when ready*. A memorable launch video or a hand-written thank-you can get people talking.
13. **Community building** (`community-building`): *test when ready*. Strong for hobby and identity products; slow for commodities.
14. **Trade shows** (`trade-shows`): *rarely first*. Wholesale fairs suit brands chasing retailers; some consumer expos work, as Gymshark found.
15. **Partnerships and business development** (`business-development`): *rarely first*. Retail and brand collaborations need proof of sell-through first.
16. **Free tools as marketing** (`engineering-as-marketing`): *rarely first*. Fit finders and "how much do I need" calculators help bigger catalogues.
17. **Offline ads** (`offline-ads`): *rarely first*. Hard to track and costly until the brand has a proven offer.
18. **Sales** (`sales`): *rarely first*. Only for wholesale or corporate gifting.
19. **Speaking, podcast guesting and webinars** (`speaking-engagements`): *rarely first*. Works for founder-story brands, but sells few units directly.

## 5. The first 90 days

**Week 1.** Agree the traction goal. Build the unit economics sheet from section 3. Agree the access level for the store and each ad account (level 1 until the client says otherwise). Add a post-purchase "how did you hear about us?" question.

**Week 2.** Brainstorm all 19 channels. Mine reviews and support emails for the words customers use. Check ten AI shopping prompts for your category ("best X for Y") and log who gets named.

**Week 3.** Rank and pick the inner three. Write test cards with go and stop numbers from break-even ROAS and CPA. Brief specialists. Client approves creatives, creator list, offer and budget caps.

**Week 4.** Launch round one. Typical mix: a two-week Meta or TikTok test with three creatives, product seeded to 20 creators with unique codes, and the three core email flows switched on if missing. At level 3 access the lead launches these within the agreed limits and logs each action; at level 2 it builds them for the client to publish.

**Week 5.** Monday numbers against break-even. Pause the weakest creative; add one new angle from the best creator posts.

**Week 6.** Midpoint review. List five bestsellers on one marketplace if it is in the inner ring. Check stock against the pace of sales.

**Week 7.** Read round one. Judge ads on new-customer CAC and blended MER, not only platform ROAS.

**Week 8.** Round two: rerun the unclear test with one change, or bring in a middle-ring channel such as gift-guide pitches or a weekend pop-up.

**Week 9.** Check repeat buying for the first test cohorts. Add or fix the post-purchase flow and the second-product offer.

**Week 10.** Focus decision. The winning channel's specialist writes a 90-day plan with a creative or creator refresh rhythm.

**Week 11.** Raise volume in steps while CAC stays under the allowable figure. Re-order stock in time.

**Week 12.** Cash check: contribution after marketing, stock on hand, and payment terms. Keep one middle-ring test running.

**Week 13.** Review against the goal. Re-run the ten AI shopping prompts. Re-rank all 19 channels.

## 6. A cheap test menu for the top 6

### 1. Two-week paid social test (`social-and-display-ads`)
One campaign, broad targeting, three creatives built on three different customer reasons to buy.
- **Cost:** a budget cap set in advance, sized to buy enough orders to read at your break-even CPA.
- **Time:** two weeks.
- **Go:** new-customer ROAS at or above break-even ROAS, or within reach with one more creative round.
- **Stop:** the cap is spent with ROAS well under break-even and no creative close.

### 2. Creator seeding with codes (`affiliate-programs`)
Send product to 20 creators whose audience matches your buyer, with a unique code and a commission on sales. Ask nothing in return; offer the commission if they choose to post.
- **Cost:** product and shipping for 20, plus commission on sales.
- **Time:** four weeks.
- **Go:** (product + shipping + commissions) ÷ orders at or below break-even first-order CPA.
- **Stop:** posts with no orders from any code. Creators must label posts as ads (FTC in the US [6], ASA guidance in the UK [7]).

### 3. Five bestsellers on one marketplace (`existing-platforms`)
List the five products with the best margin on the marketplace your buyers already use.
- **Cost:** listing time and the marketplace's fees (check its fee page).
- **Time:** four to six weeks.
- **Go:** positive marketplace contribution and steady weekly sales.
- **Stop:** contribution negative after fees and fulfilment, or no sales without marketplace ads.

### 4. The three core flows (`email-marketing`)
Welcome series with a sign-up form, abandoned-checkout series and a post-purchase series that offers the logical second product.
- **Cost:** time on the email tool's free or starter plan.
- **Time:** two weeks to build, four to read.
- **Go:** flows add orders without raising unsubscribes or complaints, and the repeat rate rises in the next cohort.
- **Stop:** no flow orders, which usually means too little traffic yet. Collect consent properly: CAN-SPAM in the US [9]; in the UK, consent or the soft opt-in for existing customers [10]; the EU has a similar customer exception (the email-marketing specialist has the detail).

### 5. Thirty days of short video (`content-marketing`)
Three formats: product in use, a customer problem solved, behind the scenes. Post daily on one platform.
- **Cost:** time and a phone.
- **Time:** 30 days.
- **Go:** tracked visits and orders from posts, and one or two videos good enough to run as ads.
- **Stop:** no orders and no ad-worthy video after 30 days.

### 6. Gift-guide and round-up pitches (`publicity`)
Pitch 20 editors and writers who cover your category, with a short note, photos, price and a sample offer. Ask each for their deadline.
- **Cost:** samples and time.
- **Time:** four to eight weeks, timed to their calendars.
- **Go:** one placement with a tracked link whose orders cover the samples.
- **Stop:** no replies after one follow-up each.

## 7. Real early-traction stories

- **Gymshark.** In 2012 it sent product to its first athletes, among them Lex Griffin, Matt Ogus and Chris Lavado, who liked it and shared it with their followers. In May 2013 it took a stand at the BodyPower expo in Birmingham, and the company says online sales jumped from about £300 a day to £30,000 in 30 minutes [3]. Channels: creators and a consumer expo.
- **Dollar Shave Club.** Its launch video went up on 6 March 2012, cost $4,500 to make, and the company took 12,000 orders that day, according to Entrepreneur [4]. Channel: content that worked as unconventional PR.
- **Warby Parker.** It launched in February 2010 and had a waitlist of 20,000 people within four weeks. Wharton Magazine reported in 2013 that over half of its sales came from word of mouth, and described its "try five" idea as marketing built into the product [5]. Channels: word of mouth and viral design.
- **Glossier.** Emily Weiss started the Into The Gloss blog in 2010. By around 2014 it drew more than two million unique readers a month, and Glossier launched that year with four products shaped by what readers said [11]. Channel: content marketing that built the audience before the product.

## 8. Budget guidance

- Start every budget from contribution margin. If the margin per order is thin, paid channels will struggle, and the first fix may be price, bundle or average order value rather than marketing.
- Size each paid test to buy enough first orders to read at your break-even CPA, and cap it. A test too small to produce a handful of orders tells you nothing.
- Count product given away (creator seeding, gift-guide samples) at cost as test spend.
- Judge paid tests on new-customer numbers and blended MER as well as platform ROAS, which can over-count.
- Keep cash for stock. A winning test that sells out before re-order wastes the momentum.
- When a channel wins, raise spend in steps and recheck CAC at each step. Creative usually tires, so plan a refresh rhythm before scaling.
- Pay creators and affiliates on results where you can; fixed fees are for creators whose past posts you can verify.

## 9. Common traps for ecommerce

- **Trusting platform ROAS alone.** Compare it with blended MER and your own order data.
- **No margin sheet.** Running ads without knowing break-even ROAS is guessing.
- **Discounting to buy first orders.** Deep first-order discounts attract bargain hunters who do not come back. Watch the repeat rate of discounted cohorts.
- **Spreading thin.** Five channels at once, each underfunded, all looking like failures.
- **Undisclosed creator posts.** Gifted or paid posts must be labelled [6][7].
- **Fake or bought reviews.** The FTC's 2024 rule bans fake reviews and paying for reviews conditioned on sentiment [12], and UK law bans commissioning fake reviews [13].
- **Ignoring returns.** A channel that sells a lot of returns is not working. Track returns by channel.
- **Stock-outs mid-test.** Plan stock before you scale a test.
- **Leaving AI shopping to chance.** Clear product data, honest reviews and specific product pages help assistants describe you; check what they say each month.
