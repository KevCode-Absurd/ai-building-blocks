# First traction for any business

Variant playbook for the general growth lead. It applies the Bullseye method from Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015) [1], in our own words, to businesses that do not fit the SaaS, ecommerce, local or coaching playbooks. Read `orchestrator.md` first: it explains the traction goal, the critical path, cheap tests, the channel team and approvals. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. Who this is for

Any business the other four variants do not fit: B2B service firms, manufacturers and distributors, consumer apps, two-sided marketplaces, franchises, hardware makers and social enterprises. Also established firms taking a product into a new market, where past channels may not carry over.

This variant leans hardest on the Bullseye's first step. With no template to fall back on, the lead brainstorms all 19 channels from a blank page, as the authors intended [1][2]. The default ranking below is only a starting point to argue with.

Three questions sort most of these businesses quickly:

- **Who pays, and how?** A contract signed after meetings, a download from an app store, an order through a distributor, a fee on each marketplace transaction. The way money arrives rules channels in or out.
- **Is there more than one side?** Marketplaces need sellers and buyers, and usually one side is harder. Test channels for the hard side first.
- **How long is the buying cycle?** If a deal takes six months, a two-week test must measure a step that predicts deals, such as qualified meetings.

## 2. Intake questions

1. What do you sell, to whom, and what do they do today instead?
2. Who pays, who uses, and who can say no? Are they the same person?
3. How does a sale happen: online order, app store, contract, distributor, franchise agreement, transaction fee?
4. If you have two sides (buyers and sellers, or users and advertisers), which side is harder to win?
5. How long from first contact to first payment, and what steps sit in between?
6. How did your first customers find you? List them by source if you can.
7. What traction goal do you need, by when, and why that number?
8. What does a customer bring in gross profit, once and over a year?
9. How many hours a week can the founders give to traction, and what skills do they have (selling, writing, speaking, building)?
10. How much cash can go into tests this quarter, and how much can you lose without harm?
11. Which companies already serve your buyers and might partner, resell or refer?
12. Which channels have your competitors used, and which have they ignored?
13. What tracking exists, and which countries do you sell in?
14. For each tool we would use, which access level will you grant: 1 read, 2 read and draft, or 3 read, write and publish within limits you set?

## 3. The numbers that matter for any business

Every test is judged on what a new customer costs, how many the channel can bring, and whether they are the right customers [2][3]. These formulas turn that into numbers.

- **Cost per new customer, by channel** = (cash spent + tools + paid help + founder hours × an agreed hourly value) ÷ new customers from that channel.
- **Gross profit per customer per month** = revenue per customer per month − direct costs of serving them.
- **Expected customer life (months)** = 1 ÷ monthly churn rate, or the average months a customer stays if you know it.
- **Customer lifetime gross profit** = gross profit per customer per month × expected customer life. Use a one-off figure for one-off sales.
- **Payback period (months)** = cost per new customer ÷ gross profit per customer per month.
- **Allowable cost per customer** = gross profit per customer per month × the payback months you can afford.
- **Channel ceiling** = people the channel can reach each month × the conversion rate measured in your test. A cheap channel with a tiny ceiling cannot carry the goal.
- **Days to a readable result** = days until the test produced the minimum number of conversions you set before launch.
- **Step conversion** = people reaching a step ÷ people reaching the step before it. Map every step from first contact to payment.
- **Share of new customers by source** = new customers from a source ÷ all new customers.
- **Marketplace liquidity** = listings or requests that end in a transaction within a set period ÷ all listings or requests in that period.

Worked example (made-up numbers, to show the maths only): a service brings 200 a month in gross profit and clients stay about 10 months, so lifetime gross profit is 2,000. If the business can wait six months for payback, allowable cost per customer is 1,200. If a deal needs four qualified meetings, each meeting is worth at most 300.

## 4. Channel ranking for any business

A starting order to argue with. Re-rank fresh for each client after the brainstorm.

1. **Sales, founder-led** (`sales`): *usually inner ring*. Thirty real conversations teach you the buyer's words and often win the first customers.
2. **Partnerships and business development** (`business-development`): *usually inner ring*. A partner who already serves your buyers can bring many at once.
3. **Search ads** (`search-ads`): *usually inner ring*. A small, capped test reads existing demand within days, whatever the business.
4. **SEO plus AI answers** (`seo-aeo-geo`): *usually inner ring*. If buyers research before buying, useful pages compound and feed AI answers.
5. **Content marketing** (`content-marketing`): *test when ready*. Works when the founder has know-how buyers value and time to share it.
6. **Existing platforms** (`existing-platforms`): *test when ready*. App stores, marketplaces, crowdfunding sites and directories, where buyers already browse.
7. **Publicity** (`publicity`): *test when ready*. For news, data or a story; strong for launches, social enterprises and hardware.
8. **Email marketing** (`email-marketing`): *test when ready*. Once there is a list with clean consent.
9. **Community building** (`community-building`): *test when ready*. When buyers already talk to each other online or in groups.
10. **Creators, newsletters and podcasts** (`creators-and-newsletters`): *test when ready*. When a few niche voices reach most of your buyers.
11. **Viral loops and referral** (`viral-and-referral`): *test when ready*. For apps and marketplaces where using the product involves others.
12. **Social and display ads** (`social-and-display-ads`): *test when ready*. When buyers can be targeted by interest or job and margin allows.
13. **Free tools as marketing** (`engineering-as-marketing`): *test when ready*. When a calculator or template fits a step buyers take before buying.
14. **Speaking, podcast guesting and webinars** (`speaking-engagements`): *test when ready*. For founders who teach well, in industries that gather.
15. **Trade shows** (`trade-shows`): *test when ready*. For manufacturers and B2B firms whose industry meets at a few events.
16. **Offline events** (`offline-events`): *rarely first*. Slow to scale unless buyers are local or tightly grouped.
17. **Affiliate and creator commissions** (`affiliate-programs`): *rarely first*. Needs proven payback before you can promise commissions.
18. **Unconventional PR** (`unconventional-pr`): *rarely first*. High variance; worth it only with a clear, on-brand idea.
19. **Offline ads** (`offline-ads`): *rarely first*. Hard to track to a customer; if you test it, give it its own code, number or web address.

How the order often shifts:

- **Two-sided marketplaces:** sales for the hard side (as Airbnb did with hosts), then SEO or existing platforms for the other.
- **Consumer apps:** existing platforms (app stores) and viral loops move up; sales moves down.
- **Manufacturers and distributors:** trade shows and business development move up.
- **Franchises:** use the local playbook for each site and this one for selling franchises.
- **Social enterprises:** publicity and partnerships move up.

## 5. The first 90 days

**Week 1.** Agree the traction goal and the critical path of milestones to it [8]. Map every step from first contact to payment. Add source tracking to whatever system records customers. Agree the access level for each tool (level 1 until the client says otherwise).

**Week 2.** Brainstorm all 19 channels from a blank page, one concrete test idea each, with guesses for cost, ceiling and time to read. Research how three similar businesses got their first customers.

**Week 3.** Rank into rings and pick the inner three. Write test cards with go and stop numbers from the allowable cost per customer. Brief specialists. Client approves.

**Week 4.** Launch round one. A common mix: a 30-conversation sales sprint, outreach to five possible partners, and a capped search ads test. At level 3 access the lead launches these within the agreed limits and logs each action; at level 2 it builds them for the client to publish.

**Week 5.** Monday numbers. Write down the buyer's exact words from every conversation; they feed every other channel.

**Week 6.** Midpoint review. Follow up partner talks with a simple one-page proposal.

**Week 7.** Read round one against go and stop numbers, on all three questions: cost, ceiling, fit.

**Week 8.** Round two: rerun the unclear test with one change, or bring in a middle-ring channel suited to the business type.

**Week 9.** Fix the weakest step in the path to payment, using what tests showed: the product half of the time split [4].

**Week 10.** Focus decision. The winning channel's specialist writes a 90-day plan.

**Week 11.** Turn the winner into a weekly routine with owners and a tracker.

**Week 12.** Estimate the winner's ceiling from real numbers. If it cannot reach the goal alone, start a middle-ring test now.

**Week 13.** Review against the goal. Re-rank all 19 channels and agree the next goal.

## 6. A cheap test menu for the top 6

### 1. Thirty-conversation sales sprint (`sales`)
Book 30 conversations with people who fit the buyer profile: warm contacts first, then targeted cold outreach. Ask about the problem before pitching.
- **Cost:** time only.
- **Time:** three weeks.
- **Go:** enough qualified next steps (trials, proposals, orders) to meet the critical-path milestone at or below the allowable cost per customer, counting time.
- **Stop:** no buyer will take a next step after 30 conversations; revisit the offer before the channel. Check outreach rules: CAN-SPAM in the US [9], PECR in the UK [10], data protection and ePrivacy in the EU [11].

### 2. Five partner conversations (`business-development`)
List companies that serve the same buyers. Pitch five with a specific, two-sided proposal.
- **Cost:** time only.
- **Time:** four to six weeks.
- **Go:** one partner agrees to a small pilot with tracked referrals.
- **Stop:** no partner sees a gain for them; rework the offer to the partner.

### 3. Capped search ads test (`search-ads`)
Ten to twenty tightly matched keywords, one landing page, conversion tracking on the real goal.
- **Cost:** a budget cap set from the allowable cost per customer.
- **Time:** two weeks.
- **Go:** cost per customer, or per qualified lead with a known close rate, within the allowable figure.
- **Stop:** too little search volume to spend the cap, or the cap spent with no qualified leads.

### 4. Three answer pages (`seo-aeo-geo`)
Pages that answer the three questions buyers ask most before buying, written from sales conversations.
- **Cost:** two to four days of writing.
- **Time:** six to twelve weeks.
- **Go:** rising impressions and the first leads from the pages.
- **Stop:** no impressions after the read period; check indexing.

### 5. Founder content (`content-marketing`)
Thirty days of posts, articles or videos on the platform buyers use, each with a clear next step.
- **Cost:** time only.
- **Time:** 30 days.
- **Go:** conversations or leads traced to content within the allowable cost.
- **Stop:** no conversations after 30 days.

### 6. One existing platform (`existing-platforms`)
List on the one marketplace, app store, directory or crowdfunding site where buyers already look.
- **Cost:** listing time and the platform's fees.
- **Time:** four to six weeks, plus any review time.
- **Go:** customers from the platform at a positive margin after fees.
- **Stop:** no sales, or a margin wiped out by fees.

## 7. Real early-traction stories

- **DuckDuckGo.** Weinberg has said search traffic for search-engine phrases brought DuckDuckGo's first users, but too few to reach the next goal; later, distribution deals such as Apple's inclusion of it as a search option did more [3]. In January 2011 it also tried a billboard in San Francisco; MIT Technology Review noted at the time that it was unclear whether it would work [7]. Weinberg counts starting marketing late and testing too few channels among his own mistakes [3].
- **Airbnb.** Paul Graham describes the founders going door to door in New York to recruit hosts and improve their listings, including taking better photos of their homes [5]. Channel: founder-led sales on the hard side of a marketplace.
- **Mint.** Founder Aaron Patzer wrote that Mint collected emails for nine months before launch and offered early access to people who put an "I want Mint" badge on their blogs, earning about 600 links [6]. Justin Mares writes that blog sponsorships and guest posts brought Mint's first 40,000 customers, before publicity took it to a million users within six months of launch [2].
- **Pebble.** Graham notes that the founders assembled the first few hundred watches themselves, and later sold about $10 million worth on Kickstarter [5]. Channel: an existing platform (crowdfunding) for hardware.

## 8. Budget guidance

- Start with time-based channels while the offer and buyer are still uncertain; cash tests read better once you know whom to target.
- Set each paid test's cap from the allowable cost per customer, sized to produce enough conversions to read, and no larger.
- Give long-cycle businesses a test unit that predicts sales (qualified meetings, trials) and value it with your close rate.
- Count founder hours at an agreed value. A "free" channel that eats the founder's week is not free.
- Commit to contracts, agencies, booths or annual tools only after a channel passes its go number.
- When a channel wins, raise spend in steps and recheck cost per customer and ceiling at each step.

## 9. Common traps for this kind of business

- **Skipping the brainstorm.** Ranking only the channels you know repeats your blind spots [2].
- **Testing the wrong side.** Marketplaces must test the hard side first.
- **Measuring too late in a long cycle.** Pick a leading step you can count within the test.
- **One test per channel.** A weak tactic is not a dead channel; change the offer or audience once [3].
- **Too many tests.** Three at once, maximum.
- **No traction goal.** Without a number and a date, every result looks fine.
- **Copying competitors.** Crowded channels cost more. Look for the one rivals ignore [3].
- **Ignoring the ceiling.** A channel that works but tops out below the goal needs a partner channel soon.
