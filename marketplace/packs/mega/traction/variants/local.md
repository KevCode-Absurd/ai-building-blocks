# First traction for local businesses

Variant playbook for the local growth lead. It applies the Bullseye method from Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015) [1], in our own words, to businesses whose customers live or work nearby. Read `orchestrator.md` first: it explains the traction goal, the critical path, cheap tests, the channel team and approvals. The authors have not reviewed or endorsed this kit.

## 1. Who this is for

Shops, trades, clinics, salons, restaurants, gyms and studios with one to five locations. Owners are busy doing the work itself, marketing happens in gaps, and most of it is guesswork. Competition weighs on them: in the UK government's 2023 small business survey, 48% of employers named competition in the market as an obstacle to success [11].

Local businesses differ from the other variants in four ways:

- **The customer must be able to reach you.** The market is a radius or a postcode list, so every channel is judged on people inside it.
- **The phone and the front desk are the tracking system.** Many customers call or walk in, so the source of each booking must be asked and written down.
- **Reviews and maps decide a lot of choices.** Google Business Profile is a channel in its own right, and Google reports calls, messages, bookings, direction requests and website clicks from it [2].
- **Capacity is fixed.** A test that brings more bookings than staff can handle damages reviews. Check capacity before scaling.

The goal is booked jobs, visits and walk-ins from new customers, and a channel that brings them at a cost the margin can carry.

## 2. Intake questions

1. What do you sell, where, and to whom? What is your service area or the radius most customers come from?
2. What is an average job, visit or basket worth, and what is the gross profit on it after materials and staff time?
3. How often does a good customer come back in a year?
4. How do customers reach you today: calls, walk-ins, booking app, messages, forms? Roughly how many a week?
5. Do you record where each new customer heard about you? Who answers the phone, and are calls missed?
6. What traction goal do you need, by when, and why (fill quiet days, hire, open a second site)?
7. How many more bookings a week could you handle without new staff?
8. Who manages your Google Business Profile? For it, the booking system and any ad account, which access level will you grant: 1 read, 2 read and draft, or 3 read, write and publish within limits you set?
9. How many Google reviews do you have, at what rating, and how do you ask for them now?
10. What marketing have you tried (flyers, ads, Facebook groups, local paper), and what happened?
11. Which nearby businesses serve the same customers without competing (a gym and a physio, a florist and a wedding venue)?
12. How much cash can go into tests this quarter, and what would hurt?
13. Are there rules for your trade on advertising or claims (health, legal, finance, alcohol)?

## 3. The numbers that matter for local businesses

Most of these come from a simple lead source tracker: a sheet or booking-system field where staff note how each new customer found you.

- **Leads by source** = calls + messages + forms + walk-ins, tagged by channel, per week.
- **Cost per lead** = channel spend ÷ leads from that channel.
- **Booking rate** = booked jobs ÷ leads.
- **Cost per booked job** = (channel spend + any paid staff time on it) ÷ booked jobs from that channel. This is the main test number.
- **Show rate** = appointments attended ÷ appointments booked.
- **Gross profit per job** = job value − materials − staff cost of the job.
- **12-month customer value** = gross profit per visit × visits per year by a typical customer.
- **Allowable cost per booked job** = the share of first-job gross profit you are willing to spend, or a share of 12-month customer value if you can wait for repeat visits to pay it back.
- **Review velocity** = new Google reviews per month. **Review ask rate** = new reviews ÷ customers asked.
- **Profile actions** = calls + direction requests + website clicks + messages + bookings, from Business Profile performance [2].
- **Profile action rate** = profile actions ÷ profile views. Google counts each viewer at most once a day [2].
- **Map position** = your place in the map results for ten core searches, checked from inside your area on the same day each month.
- **Repeat visit rate** = customers with two or more visits in a period ÷ customers in that period.

Worked example (made-up numbers, to show the maths only): a boiler service earns 90 gross profit and a typical customer returns once a year for three years. If the owner will spend half the first job's profit to win a customer, the allowable cost per booked job is 45. If they will spend a third of three years' profit, it is 90.

## 4. Channel ranking for local businesses

A starting order before the brainstorm. A restaurant, a plumber and a dental clinic will re-rank differently.

1. **Existing platforms** (`existing-platforms`): *usually inner ring*. Google Business Profile, Apple Business Connect, Yelp and booking apps are where local buyers choose.
2. **Viral loops and referral** (`viral-and-referral`): *usually inner ring*. Reviews and word of mouth drive local choice; a steady, honest review ask is the cheapest test there is.
3. **Search ads** (`search-ads`): *usually inner ring*. Search ads and, where offered, Local Services Ads catch people searching right now; Local Services Ads charge per lead, not per click [3].
4. **SEO plus AI answers** (`seo-aeo-geo`): *usually inner ring*. Service and area pages back up the profile, and Google says AI answers draw on pages that are indexed and eligible for a snippet [10].
5. **Offline ads** (`offline-ads`): *test when ready*. Postcards and flyers to one postcode with a tracked code are cheap to read.
6. **Partnerships and business development** (`business-development`): *test when ready*. Referral swaps with nearby firms that serve the same people.
7. **Community building** (`community-building`): *test when ready*. Local groups and clubs, if you help rather than advertise.
8. **Offline events** (`offline-events`): *test when ready*. Open days, workshops and markets put people through the door.
9. **Email marketing** (`email-marketing`): *test when ready*. Reminders and rebooking messages to past customers lift repeat visits.
10. **Publicity** (`publicity`): *test when ready*. Local press, awards and best-of lists, when there is a real story.
11. **Social and display ads** (`social-and-display-ads`): *test when ready*. Radius-targeted Meta ads with a clear offer.
12. **Unconventional PR** (`unconventional-pr`): *test when ready*. A memorable local gesture or stunt, done with permission.
13. **Content marketing** (`content-marketing`): *test when ready*. Short videos of the work feed profiles and social pages.
14. **Creators, newsletters and podcasts** (`creators-and-newsletters`): *rarely first*. Local creators and newsletters exist, but reach inside the area is hard to check.
15. **Speaking, podcast guesting and webinars** (`speaking-engagements`): *rarely first*. Talks to local groups suit clinics and advisers.
16. **Trade shows** (`trade-shows`): *rarely first*. Home shows and wedding fairs, for trades that sell big jobs.
17. **Sales** (`sales`): *rarely first*. Only for commercial contracts such as office cleaning or facilities work.
18. **Affiliate and creator commissions** (`affiliate-programs`): *rarely first*. Rarely fits local services; referral rewards cover the same ground.
19. **Free tools as marketing** (`engineering-as-marketing`): *rarely first*. A price estimator can help trades, but only after the basics work.

## 5. The first 90 days

**Week 1.** Agree the traction goal in booked jobs or new customers per week. Set up the lead source tracker with staff. Agree the access level for the Business Profile, booking system and any ad account (level 1 until the client says otherwise).

**Week 2.** Brainstorm all 19 channels. Audit the Business Profile (categories, services, hours, photos, booking link). Compare reviews with three nearby rivals. Check ten core searches and two AI assistants for "near me" questions.

**Week 3.** Rank and pick the inner three. Write test cards with go and stop numbers from the allowable cost per booked job. Draft the review-ask script and any ad copy. Client approves.

**Week 4.** Launch round one. Typical mix: Business Profile fixes and weekly posts, the review-ask script on every job or at the till, and capped search ads or Local Services Ads. At level 3 access the lead launches these within the agreed limits and logs each action; at level 2 it builds them for the client to publish.

**Week 5.** Monday numbers from the tracker. Listen to how calls are answered; missed or rushed calls waste every channel.

**Week 6.** Midpoint review. Send a postcard drop to one postcode with a tracked code if offline ads are in the inner ring.

**Week 7.** Read round one against go and stop numbers.

**Week 8.** Round two: rerun what was unclear with one change, or bring in a middle-ring channel such as a referral swap with three nearby businesses or an open day.

**Week 9.** Message past customers who have not been back, with consent, to rebook.

**Week 10.** Focus decision. The winning channel's specialist writes a 90-day plan.

**Week 11.** Make it routine: review asks on every job, weekly profile posts, budget pacing.

**Week 12.** Check capacity. If the winner keeps cost per booked job inside the allowable figure, extend it to the next part of the service area.

**Week 13.** Review against the goal. Re-check the ten searches. Re-rank all 19 channels.

## 6. A cheap test menu for the top 6

### 1. Business Profile tune-up (`existing-platforms`)
Fix categories, services, hours, photos and the booking link; post weekly; answer every review.
- **Cost:** time only.
- **Time:** four weeks.
- **Go:** profile actions and tracked bookings from the profile rise against the four weeks before.
- **Stop:** no change after four weeks, which usually points to reviews or category, not effort.

### 2. Review-ask script (`viral-and-referral`)
Staff ask every customer the same way at the end of the job or at the till, with a short link or QR code. Ask everyone, never only the happy ones, and offer nothing in return: Google bans incentives and selective asking [4].
- **Cost:** printing a card.
- **Time:** one month.
- **Go:** review velocity rises and rating holds.
- **Stop:** staff do not ask; fix the habit before judging the channel.

### 3. Capped search ads or Local Services Ads (`search-ads`)
Two weeks on your core services and area only, with call tracking. Check whether Local Services Ads cover your trade and country, and note that lead credit rules differ by region [3].
- **Cost:** a weekly cap sized to the allowable cost per booked job.
- **Time:** two weeks.
- **Go:** cost per booked job at or below the allowable figure.
- **Stop:** the cap is spent and leads do not book.

### 4. Service and area pages (`seo-aeo-geo`)
One page per main service with prices or price ranges, photos of real work, and the areas you cover.
- **Cost:** a few days of writing.
- **Time:** six to twelve weeks.
- **Go:** better map positions for the ten core searches and calls from the site.
- **Stop:** no movement after the read period; check indexing first.

### 5. Postcards to one postcode (`offline-ads`)
A drop of 500 postcards with one offer and one tracked code or phone number.
- **Cost:** printing and postage or delivery.
- **Time:** three weeks after delivery.
- **Go:** cost per booked job at or below the allowable figure.
- **Stop:** no codes used.

### 6. Referral swap with three neighbours (`business-development`)
Agree with three nearby non-competing businesses to recommend each other, with a card or note each can hand over.
- **Cost:** time and printing.
- **Time:** six weeks.
- **Go:** tracked bookings from partners, and partners still willing.
- **Stop:** partners stop handing cards out.

## 7. Real early-traction stories

Public, sourced stories about trades and clinics are scarce; most published cases come from agencies selling the service, so treat them with care. These three have primary or reputable sources.

- **Shake Shack.** It began in 2001 as a hot dog cart supporting the Madison Square Park Conservancy's first art installation in New York. Lines formed daily through the summers for three years, and the permanent kiosk opened in 2004 [7]. Channel: an offline event tied to a community project, then word of mouth.
- **Drybar.** Before the first salon, Alli Webb did blowouts in friends' homes in Los Angeles for $40 each. With her brother and husband she opened the first Drybar in Brentwood in 2010, offering only blowouts [8]. Channel: a personal network first, then a shop.
- **Franklin Barbecue.** Aaron and Stacy Franklin opened a small barbecue trailer beside an Austin interstate in 2009. The publisher's author note says it went on to be named best barbecue in Texas by *Texas Monthly* and best in America by *Bon Appétit* [9]. Channel: publicity earned by the product.

## 8. Budget guidance

- Fix the free basics first: the Business Profile, the review habit and call answering cost time, not cash, and every paid channel depends on them.
- Set each paid test's cap from the allowable cost per booked job, sized to win enough bookings to read the result.
- Count staff time on a channel, such as handing out flyers or running an open day, as cost.
- Spend inside the service area only. Clicks from outside the radius cost money and never book.
- Check capacity before raising spend. More bookings than staff can serve turns into bad reviews.
- Keep tests short and local: one postcode, one service, one offer. Expand only when the numbers hold.

## 9. Common traps for local businesses

- **No source tracking.** "Word of mouth" becomes the answer for everything. Ask every new customer and write it down.
- **Missed calls.** A busy owner who cannot answer wastes every lead. Fix call handling first.
- **Buying or gating reviews.** Google removes incentivised reviews and bans asking only happy customers [4]. The FTC's 2024 rule bans fake reviews and paying for reviews that must be positive [5]; UK law bans commissioning fake reviews [6].
- **Wrong categories on the profile.** Check that the profile's categories and services describe what you actually sell, in the words customers search for.
- **Ads outside the area.** Check location settings so ads show only where you serve.
- **Judging on clicks.** Cost per booked job is the number, not clicks or likes.
- **Copying the shop down the road.** If every rival advertises in the same place, look for the channel they ignore [1].
- **Scaling past capacity.** Growth that lowers quality shows up in reviews within weeks.
