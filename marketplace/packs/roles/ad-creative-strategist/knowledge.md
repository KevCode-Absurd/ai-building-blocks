# Ad creative strategist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-07. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform facts are dated because they change; re-check them before you rely on them.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires an ad creative strategist when its paid ads have stopped working, never worked, or keep looking the same. The media buyer controls budgets, bids and audiences. The creative strategist controls what people actually see and hear: the idea, the angle, the first seconds, the format, the words on screen and the order in which new ideas get tested.

Who hires it: online shops running Meta and TikTok ads, local services that boost posts and want real ads, app and software firms with a YouTube or Reels budget, founders who film their own videos, and agencies that need a steady supply of tested concepts.

The work comes in four kinds, matching the agents in this role:

- **Direct response copy and angles.** Ads aimed at one buyer, one angle per ad, every claim provable, written to each platform's length limits.
- **Static (image) ad concepts.** One idea per image, with layout, headline, supporting line and safe zones, briefed so a designer can build it.
- **Short video scripts.** A hook that works in the first seconds and with the sound off, shots a founder can film on a phone, on-screen text for every key line.
- **Competitor ad research.** Rivals' public ads tagged by offer, hook, format and claim, so crowded angles and open gaps are visible, then turned into ranked test briefs.

What good work looks like: concepts that differ in idea, not just in colour; hooks that match how much the viewer already knows; proof next to every claim; specs and safe zones respected; tests that change one thing and have a stop rule set in advance; and a written learning after every test.

How it is judged: by cost per result and quality of customers against a control, by how many tests produce a usable learning (not just a winner), by how few ads are rejected for policy, and by whether the account keeps finding new winners before old ones fatigue.

## 2. The canon

**Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) [1].** Copy cannot create desire; it channels a desire that already exists toward a product. Schwartz sorted buyers by awareness (from those who only need the deal to those who do not yet know they have the problem) and markets by sophistication (how many similar claims they have heard). For a creative strategist this decides the hook: a problem-aware viewer needs the problem named in their words; a product-aware viewer needs proof or an offer; a tired market needs a new mechanism or a sharper identity, not a bigger claim.

**Byron Sharp and Jenni Romaniuk, Ehrenberg-Bass Institute [2].** *How Brands Grow* (2010) and *How Brands Grow Part 2* (revised 2021) argue that brands grow mainly by reaching more buyers of the category, so ads must be noticed and linked to the brand by people who are not thinking about it. Romaniuk's *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018) treats colours, characters, taglines, jingles and shapes as cues that let a viewer recognise the brand without reading the name. In practice: show the brand or its distinctive cues early, keep them consistent across tests, and do not let a clever hook hide whose ad it is.

**Les Binet and Peter Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA, 2013) [3].** Across the IPA effectiveness database, the best results came from a mix of roughly 60% brand building and 40% short-term activation; a 2018 follow-up put it at about 62:38. They warn that leaning on short-term online metrics alone erodes long-term growth. For small advertisers this is a caution, not a budget rule: a creative strategist who only optimises for this week's cheapest click will keep producing ads that are easy to forget.

**Orlando Wood, *Lemon* (IPA, October 2019) [4].** Wood, of System1, coded 620 ads from Coronation Street ad breaks between 2004 and 2018 and found right-brain features (a clear sense of place, characters with agency, dialogue, wordplay, music with a melody) declining while left-brain features (flatness, abstracted body parts, monologue, highly rhythmic soundtracks) rose. Using System1's emotional-response testing he argues the flatter, left-brained style is less effective. *Caveat:* the data and the author come from one research firm. The useful habit: a person, a place and a moment usually beat a floating product on a flat background.

**Google's ABCDs (Think with Google, April 2022) [5].** Four principles for video: **Attention** (open mid-action or close up, use sound and bold contrast), **Branding** (brand early, often and in more than one way, including audio), **Connection** (make people think or feel one thing, keep the message focused), **Direction** (ask for a clear next step). Google says they are a guide, not a formula for ideas.

**Testing discipline: Evan Miller and the experimentation literature [7].** Decide the sample size before you start and do not stop the moment a result looks good; checking repeatedly and stopping at the first "significant" reading produced a 26.1% false-positive rate in Miller's example where 5% was intended. Creative tests on small budgets are especially prone to this.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Creative is part of targeting (Meta, as of Nov 2026)

Meta's delivery system starts with a retrieval stage, Andromeda, which narrows tens of millions of candidate ads to a few thousand for each person before ranking [9]. In March 2025 Meta wrote that as advertisers upload more creatives for a diversification strategy, Andromeda uses more complex models to pick the right creative for each person, and credited it with an 8% gain in ads quality (Meta's own figure) [10]. The practical reading: with broad audiences, the creative itself decides who sees the ad. Ten versions of the same idea in different colours give the system little to work with; five genuinely different concepts aimed at different buyers give it more. This is an interpretation of Meta's description, not a published rule; Meta gives no number of creatives to run.

### The planning ladder

Work top-down, and change one level at a time when testing:

1. **Buyer and situation.** Who, in what moment, with what problem or desire. Built from reviews, comments, sales calls and search terms.
2. **Angle.** The argument: pain, desire, proof, comparison, risk removal, timing, identity, mechanism.
3. **Concept.** The one idea that carries the angle, such as "the customer review read aloud by the founder" or "the bill before and after".
4. **Hook.** The first seconds or the first line. Matches the viewer's awareness [1].
5. **Format.** Static, carousel, short video, creator-style talking head, demo, founder story.
6. **Variant.** Small changes inside one concept: headline, first frame, button, length.

### Hooks

A hook earns the next second. Styles that cover most needs: a question in the buyer's words, a bold but provable claim, a visible demonstration, a surprising number the client can prove, a short story moment, a direct call-out of the situation (not of a personal attribute, see policy below). For video, the hook must work with the sound off because many people watch muted, and with the sound on because YouTube is described by Google as almost entirely sound-on [5] and Meta strongly recommends sound for Reels [12]. Show the product or brand cue early [2][5].

### Fair creative tests

- **One question per test.** "Does a founder-to-camera hook beat a product-demo hook for cold buyers?" Change one level of the ladder only.
- **Split audiences.** Meta's split tests divide the audience so nobody sees both versions; its API also documents a Creative Test that compares 2 to 7 creatives with one ad per cell. Meta says to test one variable and to set the confidence level before the test starts; its examples run 7 and 14 days [8].
- **Size it first.** Estimate the results needed per version before launch [7]. If a version cannot reach enough results in the time and budget, test bigger differences (concepts, not button colours), or treat the read as a directional signal and say so.
- **Set the stop rule in advance:** spend per version, end date, the metric that decides, and what counts as "no clear winner".
- **Do not edit mid-test.** Edits, budget changes and overlapping campaigns muddy the result.

### Reading creative results

Read in order, from attention to money:

1. **Attention.** For video: share of impressions that become early views (practitioners call this the hook or thumb-stop rate, built from the platform's short-view count divided by impressions; it is not a platform standard). On YouTube, a TrueView view counts at 30 seconds or the end of a shorter ad, or a click, and view rate is views divided by impressions; Google calls about 10 to 15% average for in-stream but notes wide variation [19].
2. **Hold.** Quartile completion (25, 50, 75, 100%). Google calls quartiles directional, useful for seeing where viewers drop off [19].
3. **Click.** Link click-through rate and cost per click.
4. **Result.** Cost per purchase or lead, and value. This is what decides.
5. **Quality.** Refunds, lead quality, repeat orders, from the client's own records.

A video with a strong hook and weak hold needs a better middle, not a new hook. A video with good hold and poor clicks needs a clearer ask (the D in ABCD) [5]. When an ad that worked starts costing more, check fatigue: Meta describes creative fatigue as an audience having seen the same creative too many times, and Ads Manager flags it with recommendations [11]. The fix is a new concept or a new hook on the winning concept, not a new button colour.

### Competitor and inspiration research

- **Meta Ad Library** shows all active ads across Meta's apps. Ads about social issues, elections or politics stay for seven years; ads delivered in the EU are archived for one year after their last impression [17]. Long-running ads are a clue (not proof) that they pay.
- Under the EU Digital Services Act, very large platforms must keep public ad repositories, and every ad must show who is behind it and why it is shown [29]. That is why EU ad data is fuller than elsewhere.
- Tag each rival ad by offer, angle, hook style, format, main claim, proof and call to action. Angles most rivals use are crowded; angles nobody uses are gaps worth checking against the client's real facts.
- Never copy a rival's ad, brand or unproven claims, and never state a rival's spend or results as fact.

### Policy and the law (the floor)

**Platform policy.**

- Meta reviews most ads within 24 hours and handles appeals through Account Quality [14].
- Meta bans ads that assert or imply a viewer's personal attributes, such as health, financial status, religion or sexual orientation. "Depression counselling" is acceptable; "Depression getting you down?" is not [16].
- Meta's health and wellness policy (version of 23 July 2026) requires 18+ targeting for weight-loss and cosmetic procedure ads, bans side-by-side weight-loss transformations and close-ups pinching fat, and bans ads that imply negative self-perception [15].
- Google groups its policies into prohibited content, prohibited practices, restricted content and editorial or technical rules, and rejects generic or gimmicky text such as "FREE, f-r-e-e" [20]. Its misrepresentation policy covers unreliable claims, clickbait that uses negative life events, misleading ad design and manipulated media [21].

**US.**

- FTC endorsement guidance: an endorsement must reflect the endorser's honest experience. A paid creator's disclosure belongs in the video itself, near the endorsement, ideally both said and shown; a disclosure only in the description or at the end is unlikely to work. Results shown are read as typical unless the ad clearly says what people generally get [25].
- The FTC's rule on fake reviews and testimonials (announced 14 August 2024) bans fake testimonials, including AI-generated ones attributed to people who do not exist or had no experience of the product [26].
- So a creator-style ad with an actor is fine only if it does not pretend the actor is a real customer with real results.

**UK.**

- The ASA ruled in February 2021 against Instagram Stories for Skinny Tan and We Are Luxe (Tanologist) because filters darkened the influencers' skin and exaggerated what the tanning products did [28].
- The ASA's 2025 advice: filters and retouching are not banned, but must not exaggerate a product's effect, a disclaimer does not fix a misleading image, and advertisers should keep records of production techniques [27].

**EU.**

- The DSA requires ad labelling, bans ads based on sensitive data and targeted ads to children, and bans dark patterns [29].
- The AI Act's transparency rules come into effect in August 2026: deepfakes and certain AI-generated content must be clearly labelled, and a voluntary Code of Practice offers icons for doing so [30].
- An AI-generated person presented as a real customer is a problem under all three regimes.

This is general practice, not legal advice. For a specific claim in a regulated category, the client should check with the platform's policy pages and a qualified adviser.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets.

- **How much creative matters.** Project Apollo (2006) attributed 65% of a brand's advertising sales lift to creative. Nielsen's 2017 summary of NCSolutions data on nearly 500 consumer-goods campaigns (2016 to early 2017) found media's share of the effect had risen to 36% from 15% over 11 years [6]. *Caveats:* consumer packaged goods, mostly TV-era media, two studies that are not directly comparable.
- **Video best practice.** Google and Kantar (2021) found ads following the ABCDs averaged a 30% lift in short-term sales likelihood and 17% in long-term brand contribution [5]. *Caveat:* Google's own study of YouTube ads, scored by Kantar's AI tool.
- **YouTube view rate.** Google calls about 10 to 15% average for in-stream, with wide variation [19].
- **Brand and activation balance.** About 60:40 (2013) and 62:38 (2018) brand to activation in the IPA data [3]. *Caveat:* drawn from large advertisers' award entries.
- **Platform system gains.** Andromeda +8% ads quality on selected segments; GEM up to 5% more conversions on Reels [9][10]. *Caveat:* Meta's own figures, not independent.
- **False wins.** Peeking can turn a 5% false-positive rate into 26.1% [7].

No independent public source gives a trustworthy "good hook rate", "good CTR" or "number of creatives per ad set" for small advertisers. Use the account's own history as the baseline and compare like with like (same placement, objective and audience).

## 5. Decision rules

- If the viewer does not know the problem yet, open with a situation or story they recognise, because a product-first hook means nothing to them [1].
- If every rival makes the same claim, do not make a bigger one; show a mechanism, a demonstration or a sharper buyer identity [1].
- If a claim cannot be shown on camera or proved from the client's facts, cut it or narrow it, because unproven claims break platform policy and consumer law [21][25].
- If a hook works but the ad loses people in the middle, keep the hook and rebuild the middle.
- If an ad has many views and few clicks, fix the ask and the offer, not the hook [5].
- If a winning ad starts costing more, make a new concept or a new hook on the same concept, because the audience has seen it too often [11].
- If a test cannot reach enough results per version in the planned time, test larger differences or label the result as a directional read [7].
- If anyone edits a test while it runs, restart it or call it inconclusive.
- If the account uses broad targeting, plan concepts for distinct buyers, because the creative now does much of the targeting [10].
- If a creator or actor appears, decide before filming whether they are a real customer; if not, do not present them as one, and disclose any paid relationship in the ad itself [25][26].
- If filters, retouching or AI images show the product working, do not use them unless real use gives the same result [27][28].
- If the ad is for weight loss, cosmetic procedures or health, read the platform's current health policy before writing a line [15].
- If the copy says "you" next to a condition, debt or identity, rewrite it as a statement about the service [16].
- If an image or voice is AI-generated and realistic, label it where the AI Act or platform rules require it [30].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Variants pretending to be concepts.** Spot it: ten ads that a stranger would describe in the same sentence.
- **A hook that does not match the buyer.** Spot it: a discount code as the first frame for people who have never heard of the brand.
- **The brand arrives too late.** Spot it: cover the last three seconds; can you tell whose ad it is?
- **Text in the dead zones.** Spot it: the offer sits behind the Reels caption or the TikTok buttons [12][22].
- **Sound-only meaning.** Spot it: mute it; the story falls apart.
- **Fake customers.** Spot it: actors, AI faces or staff presented as buyers, invented reviews on screen [25][26].
- **Before-and-after that the policy bans.** Spot it: side-by-side weight-loss shots, filtered skin in a skincare ad [15][27].
- **Personal-attribute call-outs.** Spot it: "Struggling with your debt?" or "Are you diabetic?" [16].
- **Calling winners too early.** Spot it: a test stopped after three days because one version "looked better" [7].
- **Changing several things at once.** Spot it: the new ad has a new hook, new offer and new format, so nobody knows what worked.
- **Copying a rival.** Spot it: same layout, same words, same claim, with a different logo.
- **No learning log.** Spot it: the team cannot say what last month's tests taught.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can I name the buyer, their awareness and the angle for each ad in one line?
2. Are the concepts genuinely different from each other and from what rivals run?
3. Does the hook make sense in the first seconds, with and without sound?
4. Is the brand or a distinctive cue visible early, and again at the end?
5. Does every claim trace to the client's proof list, and can anything shown on screen really be achieved?
6. Are specs, lengths and safe zones right for each placement?
7. Have I checked personal attributes, health rules, before-and-after rules, creator disclosures and AI labelling?
8. Is there one clear ask?
9. Does the test change one thing, with budget, stop rule and decision metric written down before launch?
10. Is there a place in the log for what we learn, whatever the result?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Creative brief:* buyer and situation, awareness, angle, proof allowed, offer, mandatory lines, formats and placements, what the test will teach.
- *Concept card:* one idea in one sentence, hook options, the first frame described, body beats, ask, proof used, policy flags.
- *Hook bank:* hooks grouped by style, each tagged with angle and awareness.
- *Video script:* two columns, "what we see" and "what we hear", with on-screen text for every key line, a shot list with props and who is on camera, and the read-aloud time.
- *Static brief:* headline, supporting line, button, picture idea, layout zones, sizes needed, safe-zone notes.
- *Test plan:* question, the one change, versions, budget per version, dates, decision metric, stop rule.
- *Readout:* was the test fair, what can and cannot be concluded, next test.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain words, the buyer's own phrases, numbers only from the client's facts. Tables for anything with more than three items. Every claim tagged with its source line from the proof list. Notes to the client kept separate from copy so nothing internal ships.

## 9. Glossary

- **Angle:** the argument an ad makes, such as pain, desire, proof or comparison.
- **Concept:** the single creative idea that carries an angle.
- **Hook:** the first seconds of a video or the first line of an ad, which earns the next second.
- **Hold:** how long viewers keep watching after the hook, read from quartile metrics [19].
- **Awareness stage:** how much the viewer already knows about the problem, the solutions and the product [1].
- **Market sophistication:** how many similar claims the market has heard [1].
- **Distinctive brand asset:** a colour, character, sound, shape or line that signals the brand without its name [2].
- **ABCD:** Attention, Branding, Connection, Direction, Google's video principles [5].
- **Andromeda:** Meta's ad retrieval system, which picks a few thousand candidate ads per person [9].
- **Creative fatigue:** falling results because the audience has seen the same creative too often [11].
- **Safe zone:** the part of a placement not covered by buttons, captions or profile details [12][22].
- **Split test:** a test that divides the audience so nobody sees more than one version [8].
- **Stop rule:** the spend, date or result count at which a test ends, set before launch.
- **Peeking:** checking a running test and stopping when it looks good, which inflates false wins [7].
- **TrueView view:** a YouTube view counted at 30 seconds or the end of a shorter ad, or a click [19].
- **View rate:** views divided by impressions [19].
- **Ad Library:** Meta's public database of active ads [17].
- **Personal attributes policy:** Meta's ban on ads that assert or imply things like health or finances about the viewer [16].
- **Material connection:** a link between an endorser and the brand, such as payment or free product, that must be disclosed [25].
- **Deepfake:** realistic AI-generated or altered media of people or events, which the EU AI Act requires to be labelled [30].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Every concept in one look.** Three "different" concepts that share the same style, setting and edit, and differ only in wording or colour. Make concepts differ on named axes: mechanism, proof, structure, visual world or offer.
- **One hook formula reused.** Every variant opens the same way ("Stop scrolling if...", "POV:", a question). They read as one ad repeated, so vary the hook type, not just its words.
- **Product on a gradient.** The product floating on a soft purple or blue gradient as the main image. It shows nothing about use or proof; show the product working in a real setting.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "High quality", "fast results", "loved by thousands" as the core message. If a rival could run the line unchanged, find the mechanism or proof only this client has.
- **Fake urgency.** Countdowns, "last chance" or "selling out" with no real deadline or stock limit behind them. It reads as a template and breaks trust; use a real reason or none.
- **Stock AI phrasing.** Scripts and captions built on "Unlock", "Elevate", "Seamless" or "Game-changer". These words mark the ad as machine-written; use the buyer's own words from reviews and research.
