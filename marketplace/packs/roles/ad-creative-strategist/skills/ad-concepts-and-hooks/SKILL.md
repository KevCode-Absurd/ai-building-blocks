# Ad concepts and hooks

This skill produces a creative batch where each ad makes a different argument to a named buyer, every claim traces to the client's proof, and the copy fits the placement. It covers both written ads (primary text, headline, button) and static image concepts.

## When to use

- A new account or product needs its first creative batch.
- Results are slipping and the live ads are all versions of the same idea.
- The client asks for a set of ad texts, image concepts or hooks for one offer.
- A winning ad needs new concepts around it, not just new colours.

Not for: reading test results (use creative-test-plan-and-readout) or full video scripts (use short-video-ad-script; this skill gives the hook and concept only).

## What you need

- **The offer:** product, price, any deal and its real end date, guarantee.
- **The buyer:** who, in what situation, and how aware they are of the problem and the product.
- **The proof list:** reviews (with permission to quote), numbers the client can show, awards, press, guarantees. If it is thin, say so and write around it.
- **Assets:** photos and footage that exist, what can be filmed, who can appear.
- **Placements:** Meta Feed, Reels, TikTok, YouTube, Pinterest.
- **Restricted areas:** health, weight loss, finance, housing, jobs, alcohol and similar.
- **Access level:** this skill needs level 1 (read) at most. Uploading drafts to an ad account needs level 2.

## Steps

1. **Write the proof list as a table** with an ID for each item (P1, P2...). Copy review lines word for word. Nothing outside this table may be claimed.
2. **Mine the buyer's words.** From reviews and comments, list phrases for the pain, the desired outcome, the doubts and what they used before. These become hook raw material.
3. **Set the awareness.** Infer awareness from the actual query, prior interaction and customer evidence; cold or retargeting delivery alone does not establish awareness. The hook style follows: situation or story for the unaware, the problem in their words for the problem aware, proof or a demonstration for the solution aware, the offer for the most aware.
4. **List four to six angles:** pain, desire, proof, comparison, risk removal, timing, identity, mechanism. Each ad uses one angle only.
5. **Turn each angle into one concept.** Write it as one sentence a stranger could repeat ("The founder reads a one-star review out loud and shows what changed"). If two concepts would be described the same way, merge them.
6. **Write hooks for each concept,** three or more styles: question in the buyer's words, provable bold claim, visible demonstration, short story moment, situation call-out. A call-out names the situation, never the viewer's health, money, body or identity.
7. **Write the ad in five parts:** hook, problem, proof (with its ID), offer, ask. Replace vague words such as "best" or "premium" with a fact from the table, or cut them.
8. **Fit the placement.** Use the requested placement and current official size, text and safe-zone specifications; record the specification source and check date. Count characters and distinguish hard limits from optional style guidance. Preview readability at the delivered size.
9. **For static concepts,** add: the picture idea, headline, one supporting line, button, logo position, and a layout note keeping text out of each placement's safe-zone margins. One message per image.
10. **Run the policy pass** on every line: personal attributes, before-and-after, health and weight-loss rules, implied results, fake urgency, actors or AI faces shown as customers, filters that change what the product does. Flag and rewrite.
11. **Rank the batch** by fit with the buyer, strength of proof and production effort, and say which three to test first.

## From concept to a usable creative

For each concept, record the customer tension and supporting evidence, one distinct argument, the proof to show, actual asset ID/path and usage permission, exact frame/shot or static crop, destination URL, and the test question. Map the ad promise to the destination hero, offer, SKU/variant availability and CTA outcome. Mark matched, drift or broken and resolve material mismatches before launch.

A script saying "watch the demonstration" needs an actual demonstration asset. Tag each concept **ready with supplied assets**, **needs production**, or **blocked by missing proof**. Do not call a brief a finished creative. When images or videos are requested and the available tools support production, produce the files, inspect the renders for product/brand fidelity and text legibility, and deliver the asset plus editable copy and source references. Otherwise state precisely what is missing and provide the feasible alternative. Generated imagery must not invent product features or imply a fabricated customer testimonial.

Build the first test to answer one useful question: identify what changes and what stays fixed, the primary business outcome and creative diagnostic, the guardrail and outcome-maturity rule. Concept exploration can vary several creative elements, but its result applies to the whole concept, not to one hook or format in isolation. Do not declare a winner on CTR alone when the objective is profitable purchases.

## Checks

- Every claim has a proof ID; nothing is invented.
- Concepts differ in idea, not only in wording or colour.
- Each hook matches the buyer's awareness.
- The brand or a distinctive cue appears early in each ad.
- Character counts are within the placement's recommended limits.
- No "you" plus a condition, debt or identity.
- No timer, stock or deadline claim that is not true.
- Any creator or customer shown is real and has agreed, or is clearly not presented as a customer.

## Output format

1. **Proof list** table: ID, proof, source, permission.
2. **Batch table:** concept, angle, awareness, hook, primary text, headline, button, proof IDs, format, characters.
3. **Static briefs** (if asked): one block per concept with picture, headline, line, button, layout, sizes.
4. **Policy flags:** line, risk, safer rewrite.
5. **Production and test ledger:** asset paths, proof/permission IDs, ready/needs-production/blocked state, destination match, test question, changed variables, primary outcome and guardrail. Include finished renders when requested and feasible.
6. **Test order:** the first three to run and the question each answers.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a refill shop selling dried foods by weight, with a 4.8 rating from 212 reviews (P1). Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

| Concept | Angle | Hook | Headline | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The weekly shop on one scale | Mechanism | "We weighed a family's weekly dry goods. Here's what the packaging alone weighed." | Bring a jar. Pay by weight. | P2: in-store weigh-in, filmed |
| A regular reads her review | Proof | "I came for lentils. I stayed because nobody judged my mismatched jars." | 4.8 from 212 local reviews | P1, P3 (review quoted with permission) |
| Buy two spoons, not a bag | Risk removal | "Never cooked with sumac? Buy two spoonfuls and find out." | Try any spice from 10g | P4: price list shows 10g minimum |

**Policy flag:** "Cut your plastic waste in half" has no proof. Rewrite: "Bring your own jars and skip the packaging on everything you buy here."

**Test first:** "The weekly shop on one scale" against "A regular reads her review", same offer and audience, to learn whether a demonstration or social proof opens better for cold local buyers.
