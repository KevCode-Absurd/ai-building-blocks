# Advertising copywriter: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-07. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Rules and platform limits are dated because they change; re-check them before you rely on them.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires an advertising copywriter when it has a few seconds of someone's attention and needs one idea to land: a poster across a street, a 30-second radio spot, a postcard on a doormat, a jingle, a book's back cover, a search ad, a line under a social image. The job is short, memorable, true copy written for the medium and the moment people meet it.

This role is different from its neighbours. A conversion copywriter builds long pages and email sequences around research and testing. A media buyer runs budgets and bids. An ad creative strategist plans concepts and tests for paid social. The advertising copywriter writes the words, and the ear and eye they are written for, across print, outdoor, audio, direct mail, book marketing and short digital ads.

Who hires it: local services buying radio time or letterbox drops, shops and clinics that need flyers and brochures, trades and estate agents mailing postcards, podcast sponsors, businesses that want a jingle or sonic logo, independent authors and small presses, and marketers who need search and social ad lines that obey each platform's limits.

The agents in this role cover:

- **Print:** brochures, flyers, posters, press ads, leave-behinds, written to the size and fold.
- **Direct mail:** postcards and letters with an offer, a tracking code per version and a break-even response rate.
- **Audio:** radio, streaming and podcast ads timed to the second, with voice direction.
- **Jingles and sonic logos:** lyrics with syllable counts and briefs a composer can use.
- **Book marketing:** back-cover blurbs, store descriptions, taglines and author bios.

What good work looks like: one idea per piece; the point understood at a glance or in the first line heard; the brand clear; a specific, true reason to act; one way to respond that can be counted; and nothing that a regulator, a station's clearance team or a platform reviewer would reject.

How it is judged: by response that can be counted (calls, codes, scans, bookings, sales), by recall of the brand and message, by clearance and approval first time, and by whether every claim holds up if checked.

## 2. The canon

**Rosser Reeves, *Reality in Advertising* (Knopf, 1961) [1].** Reeves argued each ad should make one proposition to the buyer, one competitors do not or cannot make, strong enough to move people: the unique selling proposition. The habit to keep: before writing, say in one sentence what this business offers that rivals do not, and make every line serve it.

**David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983; Vintage paperback 1985) [2].** Do the research first, put the benefit and the brand in the headline because many more people read headlines than body copy, prefer facts to adjectives, and write to one reader. Ogilvy is the source of much print craft still in use: specific headlines, captions under pictures, and long copy only when every line earns its place.

**James Webb Young, *A Technique for Producing Ideas* (first published by Advertising Publications, Inc., 1940s) [3].** An idea is a new combination of old elements. Young's process: gather specific material about the product and general material about life; work it over; set it aside so the mind can combine it; catch the idea when it comes; then shape it to fit reality. Copywriters use this as a defence against the blank page and the first obvious line.

**Luke Sullivan, *Hey, Whipple, Squeeze This* (Wiley, 1998; six editions) [4].** A working copywriter's guide to concepting: write many ideas, kill the clichés, let the headline and visual do different jobs, and remember the work exists to sell. It is the modern craft book most agencies hand to juniors.

**Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (Oxford University Press, 2018) [5].** Taglines, jingles, characters, voices and colours can become cues that let people recognise a brand without reading or hearing its name, but only if they are used consistently and linked to the brand. For jingles and slogans this means the brand name must sit inside the memorable part, and a good asset is repeated for years, not replaced every campaign.

**Radio craft: the US Radio Advertising Bureau and the UK's Radiocentre [18][19][20].** The opening line of a radio ad is its headline; write for the ear, one idea per spot, read it aloud against a timer at a normal pace, and cut until it fits [18]. Know the spot length, including any legal copy, before writing; keep the message single-minded; a consistent voice and recognisable audio cues help listeners link the ad to the brand [19]. Do not read out long phone numbers or web addresses, spoof DJs, or have actors repeat the brand name in unnatural conversation [20].

## 3. How the work is done now

### Print: brochures, flyers, posters and press ads

- **One glance first.** A reader decides in a glance whether to keep reading. The headline carries the point on its own; the picture and headline work together.
- **Plan reading order:** what the eye hits first, second and last. On a fold, plan panel by panel in the order the piece opens.
- **The headline must not mislead.** The FTC's deception policy notes that accurate text in the body may not cure a false headline, because reasonable consumers may only glance at the headline; fine print and disclaimers may not correct a misleading main claim [6]. The FTC's small-business guide adds that qualifiers should sit close to the claim they modify and be clear and conspicuous [7].
- **One way to respond, counted:** a phone number, a code or a QR code unique to this piece, so replies can be traced.

### Outdoor and digital out-of-home

- OAAA's guidance: one message, bold sans serif type, strong contrast, seven words or fewer, and a message that is understood in a few seconds or lost [22].
- Digital billboards show static messages that typically rotate every six or eight seconds, with no flashing or full motion [23].
- ABX's 2024 guide, drawing on testing of 16,000 outdoor ads, adds: few elements, readable at the real viewing distance, the brand as the hero, benefits said quickly, no insider language, and an easy next step [24].
- Real examples from that guide (against an ABX norm of 100):
  - High scorers: Amazon Prime's digital billboard in Australia, with vivid colour and giant text, scored 135. Comfort's UK six-sheet "100 Days of Freshness & Fragrance", with a clear product shot and logo, scored 126.
  - Low scorers: a Qantas credit card digital billboard with too many messages and small logos scored 72. An AMEX stadium display with a tiny grey logo scored 47. CMC Markets' "Zero. Zilch. Nada." board, with an almost invisible logo and message, scored 40 [24].

### Radio, streaming and podcast audio

- **Spot length sets the word budget.** Write to the length, including legal lines. None of the sources checked for this kit gives an official words-per-second standard, so read the script aloud at a natural pace with a timer, as the RAB advises, and cut until it fits with room for music and pauses [18][19].
- **Write for the ear:** short sentences, the key message and the call to action said clearly, numbers and web addresses kept short or replaced by a memorable name [20].
- **Legal lines cost attention.** A 2013 UK Radio Advertising Bureau study found long terms and conditions had little effect on ad awareness but lowered listeners' involvement and recall of who the ad was for, with attention falling in the final seconds [21]. Keep legals short, plain and true.
- **UK clearance.** Broadcast ads follow the BCAP Code, enforced by the ASA [11]. Radiocentre clears commercial radio ads; ads in special categories (gambling, alcohol, finance, health and beauty, slimming, environmental claims and others) must be pre-cleared [13].
- **US sponsorship identification.** A station must announce paid matter and who paid for it; for ordinary product ads, naming the sponsor or product is enough [9].
- **Podcast host reads.** An endorsement must reflect the speaker's honest opinion and experience. The FTC treats a talk show host's paid positive opinion as an endorsement that should be disclosed, and an audible endorsement needs at least an audible disclosure [8]; apply the same care to podcast host reads. A host who has not used the product must not say they have.

### Jingles and sonic logos

- Build the hook around the brand name, count syllables and stresses so it sings naturally, and keep the same hook across 15, 30 and 60 second versions so it becomes a distinctive asset [5].
- Music already on the radio cannot be used in an ad without the rights from the publisher [18]. Radiocentre notes that clearing commercial tracks takes time and is not guaranteed, and bespoke composition is often cheaper than licensing a famous track [19].
- Never write lyrics that copy or parody a known song or slogan, or brief a sound-alike of a famous voice or tune.

### Direct mail

- **Mail that can be counted:** one offer, a real deadline, a different code, phone number or landing page per version, and staff who log responses.
- **Break-even first.** The response rate needed to pay back = cost per piece / profit per response. Show the sum, then the same with repeat value if the client has it.
- **US:** USPS Every Door Direct Mail reaches every address on chosen carrier routes without names, addressed to "Postal Customer"; Retail EDDM has a minimum of 200 pieces and a maximum of 5,000 per day per ZIP Code [29].
- **UK:** the Mailing Preference Service lets people opt out of unsolicited addressed mail. Licensed mailers screen their lists against it, registration can take up to three months to take effect, and it does not cover unaddressed mail or mail to "The Occupier" [30]. The CAP Code covers direct marketing and use of data for marketing [10]. Data protection questions go to the client's own adviser; the copywriter does not rule on them.

### Book covers and store descriptions

- A blurb gives the hook, the trouble and the question, and stops before the climax. Match the genre's tone.
- Amazon KDP limits the description to 4,000 characters including HTML. It bans reviews, quotes or testimonials, contact details, time-sensitive information, prices, spoilers, emojis and keyword lists in the description [28].
- Never invent praise or call a book "bestselling" or "award-winning" unless it is true and the author can show it [7][8].

### Search and social ad lines

- **Google responsive search ads:** up to 15 headlines of 30 characters and 4 descriptions of 90, with display paths of 15. Any headline may show with any other, so each must make sense alone and in combination. Pin only text that must always show, such as a required disclaimer, because pinning cuts the combinations [25].
- **Google's editorial policy** bans gimmicky capitals, punctuation, symbols and repetition, phone numbers in ad text, and ads that do not name the business [26].
- **Meta Feed:** Meta's ads guide recommends primary text of 50 to 150 characters and a headline of about 27 [27].

### The legal floor (dated)

- **US:**
  - The FTC Act requires ads to be truthful, not deceptive and backed by evidence before they run. Express and implied claims both need proof; comparisons must be truthful; "free" offers and "Made in USA" have specific rules; bait advertising is illegal [7].
  - Deception is judged by the net impression on a reasonable consumer [6].
  - Testimonials must reflect honest experience and typical results, and material connections must be disclosed [8].
- **UK:**
  - The CAP Code covers non-broadcast ads, promotions and direct marketing [10]; the BCAP Code covers radio and TV [11]; both are enforced by the ASA.
  - CAP's copy advice service gives free, non-binding advice on non-broadcast ads, with paid faster options [12].
  - The CMA's guidance CMA207 (April 2025, updated November 2025) explains unfair practices under the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 [14].
  - The Green Claims Code sets six principles and warns that unexplained terms like "green" or "eco-friendly" are likely to mislead [15].
- **EU:**
  - The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive covers business-to-consumer advertising across member states; the comparative advertising rules of Directive 2006/114/EC also apply [16].
  - Directive 2024/825 applies from 27 September 2026 [16]. It bans generic environmental claims such as "eco-friendly", "green" or "climate friendly" unless the trader can show recognised excellent environmental performance, and bans claims that a product is carbon neutral or has reduced emissions based on offsetting. A claim is not generic if its specifics appear clearly on the same medium, such as the same advertising spot. The Commission's September 2026 Q&A repeats that if a poster or billboard has no room to specify an environmental claim, the claim should generally not be made [17].

This is general practice, not legal advice. For a claim in a regulated area, the client should use the regulator's copy advice service or a qualified adviser.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets.

- **Billboard word count:** OAAA recommends seven words or fewer (2019 guidance, not a controlled study) [22].
- **Digital billboard dwell:** static messages typically rotate every six or eight seconds [23].
- **Outdoor creative scores:** ABX's 2024 guide reports wide gaps between named ads, from 135 down to 40 against a norm of 100, with clutter, small logos and missing messages common among low scorers [24]. *Caveat:* a vendor's survey-based index; scores are not sales.
- **Radio legal copy:** in 150 UK scripts analysed in 2013, consumer-credit terms averaged 24 words and could add more than 10 seconds; across 239 campaigns with terms in the RAB's radioGauge database, long terms (over 5 seconds or 14 words) went with lower involvement, brand identity and impression scores [21]. *Caveat:* UK, 2013, the RAB's own database.
- **Search ads:** Google reports that advertisers who raised Ad Strength from Poor to Excellent saw 15% more clicks and conversions on average, and that adding a second responsive search ad to an ad group went with 6.6% more conversions (internal data, August 2025) [25]. *Caveat:* Google's own figures.
- **Mail preference lag:** UK MPS registration can take up to three months [30].
- **Clearance times:** Radiocentre targets 24 hours during office hours [13]; CAP copy advice standard replies take 3 to 5 working days [12].

No free, authoritative public source gives a current "average direct mail response rate" or "average radio response" for small businesses. Use the break-even sum and the client's own past results.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the piece is seen in passing (poster, billboard, van), keep one message and as few words as the idea allows, because it must be understood in a few seconds [22].
- If the headline would mislead without the body copy, rewrite the headline, because people may only read the headline [6].
- If a qualifier is needed, put it next to the claim and make it readable, or change the claim [7].
- If a radio script does not fit when read aloud at a natural pace, cut words, never speed up the read [18].
- If a phone number or long web address is the call to action on radio, replace it with a short memorable name or search term where you can [20].
- If the ad is for a special category in UK radio, plan Radiocentre clearance time before booking airtime [13].
- If the legal lines run long, simplify the offer instead of reading faster, because long terms cost attention and brand recall [21].
- If a host reads the ad, write it in their voice from their real experience, and disclose the paid relationship audibly [8].
- If a jingle or slogan is meant to last, put the brand name inside the hook and keep it unchanged across versions [5].
- If a client wants a famous song, a parody or a sound-alike, refuse and offer original music, because it needs rights the client usually does not have [18][19].
- If a mail piece has more than one version, give each its own code, number or page, or the test cannot be read.
- If the mailing goes to UK addresses by name, ask the client to confirm their list is screened against MPS [30].
- If a book description includes review quotes or a launch date, move them out: KDP bans both [28].
- If an environmental claim cannot be specified in full on the same poster or spot in the EU, drop it [17].
- If a search headline only makes sense after another headline, rewrite it so it stands alone [25].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Two ideas in one piece.** Spot it: the poster needs "and" to describe what it says.
- **Clever over clear.** Spot it: a stranger cannot say what is sold or who sells it after one look or one listen [24].
- **Hidden brand.** Spot it: cover the logo; is the advertiser still obvious? Tiny logos sank the AMEX and CMC Markets boards [24].
- **Body text that fixes the headline.** Spot it: the headline says "free" and the small print says otherwise [6].
- **Radio written for the eye.** Spot it: long sentences, brackets, a web address with slashes, a phone number read twice.
- **Over-length scripts.** Spot it: nobody read it aloud against a timer.
- **Legal copy as an afterthought.** Spot it: terms added after the script was approved, pushing the spot over length [19][21].
- **Undisclosed or invented endorsements.** Spot it: a host praising a product they never used, a quote with no source [8].
- **Borrowed tunes.** Spot it: "make it sound like" a named song.
- **Untrackable mail.** Spot it: three versions, one phone number.
- **Spoilers and fake praise in blurbs.** Spot it: the blurb reveals the twist, or calls the book "bestselling" without proof [28].
- **Vague green words.** Spot it: "eco-friendly packaging" with nothing specific beside it [15][17].
- **Search headlines that depend on each other.** Spot it: "Our Prices" means nothing alone [25].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can I say the single message in one sentence, and does every line serve it?
2. Does it work at the real distance, speed or volume: across the street, at a glance, on a car radio, on a phone speaker?
3. Is the brand obvious without hunting for it?
4. Is every claim true, provable and not misleading in the headline alone?
5. Are qualifiers next to the claim, readable or clearly spoken?
6. For audio: read aloud against a timer, legal lines included, with the right voice direction and pronunciation guide?
7. Is there one way to respond, and can it be counted per version?
8. Are music, lyrics, voices, quotes and images original or licensed, with permission for any real person?
9. Does it meet the medium's rules: KDP description rules, Google editorial and character limits, BCAP or CAP Code, Radiocentre clearance where needed, US sponsorship identification?
10. Are environmental, health, price and comparison claims specific and supported?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Print copy deck:* laid out by panel or section in reading order, with headline options, body, proof, offer, contact block, terms, tracking code and image notes.
- *Radio script:* a header (client, title, length, version, voice), then lines with cues in brackets such as [MUSIC: light acoustic] or [SFX: door], voice direction, a pronunciation guide, the read-aloud time and the word count.
- *Jingle lyric sheet:* lines with syllable counts and stresses, rhyme scheme, the hook marked, tempo, mood and melody shape in words, and 15, 30 and 60 second versions.
- *Direct mail pack:* front and back or letter copy per version, what each version tests, code or number per version, break-even sum, list rules supplied by the client.
- *Book copy set:* blurb, store description with formatting notes, taglines and bios in three lengths, with a spoiler check.
- *Search and social lines:* table of headlines and descriptions with character counts.

**Tone and conventions.** Short words, concrete nouns, active verbs, the customer's own phrases. Numbers as the reader will see or hear them (on radio, "nine-three-seven" style where a number must be said). No exclamation marks doing the work a fact should do. Notes to the client kept apart from the copy so nothing internal is printed or read on air.

## 9. Glossary

- **Unique selling proposition (USP):** one benefit the brand offers that rivals do not, strong enough to move buyers [1].
- **Headline:** the line most people read first and often the only one.
- **Net impression:** the overall message a reasonable person takes from the whole ad [6].
- **Substantiation:** the evidence a business must hold before making a claim [7].
- **Material connection:** a link between an endorser and the brand, such as payment or free product, that must be disclosed [8].
- **Sponsorship identification:** the US rule that broadcast stations must announce paid matter and who paid [9].
- **CAP Code and BCAP Code:** the UK rule books for non-broadcast and broadcast ads, enforced by the ASA [10][11].
- **Clearance:** pre-approval of a broadcast ad; Radiocentre for UK radio [13].
- **Copy advice:** CAP's free, non-binding pre-publication check [12].
- **Generic environmental claim:** a broad green claim like "eco-friendly" with no specifics on the same medium [17].
- **Spot:** one radio or audio ad, sold by length.
- **Legals:** the required terms read at the end of an ad [21].
- **Host read:** a podcast or radio ad read by the show's own presenter.
- **Sonic logo:** a short sound or note pattern that signals a brand.
- **Distinctive asset:** a cue such as a jingle, tagline or colour that signals the brand without its name [5].
- **DOOH:** digital out-of-home, screens in public places that rotate static messages [23].
- **Six-sheet:** a common small poster format used at bus shelters and in streets in the UK.
- **EDDM:** USPS Every Door Direct Mail, mail to every address on a carrier route [29].
- **MPS:** the UK Mailing Preference Service opt-out register [30].
- **Responsive search ad (RSA):** a Google search ad assembled from up to 15 headlines and 4 descriptions [25].
- **Pinning:** fixing an RSA headline or description to one position [25].
- **Blurb:** the short selling copy on a book's back cover or store page.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock AI phrasing.** "Unlock", "Elevate", "Seamless", "Game-changer" or "In today's fast-paced world" in headlines, scripts or body copy. These words mark copy as machine-written; use the client's and buyers' own words.
- **Rule of three everywhere.** Every line a list of three adjectives or benefits ("fast, simple, reliable"). The rhythm reads as default; say the one thing that matters.
- **Headline formula clichés.** "The X you didn't know you needed", "Say goodbye to X", "X, reimagined". They could head any ad; write a line only this brand could run.
- **Fake urgency.** "Hurry", "ends soon" or "limited time" on a poster, spot or mailer with no real deadline. It reads as filler and can break advertising rules; state the true reason or drop it.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** Lines like "quality you can trust" or "service that cares". If a rival could run it unchanged, it is not this client's ad.
- **One hook formula reused.** Every version of a campaign opening with the same structure, for example a question then a twist. Vary the idea across versions, not just the words.
