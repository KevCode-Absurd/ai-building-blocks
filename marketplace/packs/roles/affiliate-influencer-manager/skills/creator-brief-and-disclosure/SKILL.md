# Creator brief and disclosure

A good brief gives a creator the facts and the freedom to say them in their own voice, and makes the disclosure part of the job from the start. In the US, UK and EU the brand shares responsibility for clear labelling, so the brief and the post check are where most risk is removed. This is general practice, not legal advice: the client's lawyer writes the contract, and specific questions go to the official guidance or a qualified adviser.

## When to use

- Any paid, gifted, affiliate or commission-based creator post or video.
- An affiliate partner kit's content rules.
- Checking posts before a creator is paid or content is reused in ads.

## What you need

- **The offer:** product, the one thing the audience should take away, the proof the client can back.
- **The deal:** fee, gift, commission, timeline, number and type of posts, and whether the brand may reuse the content (where, how long).
- **Markets:** where the creator's audience is.
- **Platforms:** where the content will run.
- **Tracking:** the creator's link and code.

**Access:** level 1 to read posts. Level 2 to draft the brief in the client's tools. Sending a brief that is part of a binding agreement, or confirming a paid creator, needs the client's fresh yes. Payment stays with the client.

## Steps

1. **State the story in one line** and the three key points. Keep them about the viewer, not the brand.
2. **List approved claims** with their proof. Anything else, the creator does not say. Health, money or results claims need evidence the client holds.
3. **Set must-haves and must-nots.** Product shown in use; link and code; no competitor put-downs; no claims beyond the list. Keep the list short; over-scripted posts read like ads and lose trust.
4. **Give creative freedom.** Say what the creator decides: format, angle, words.
5. **Set the timeline and review step.** Draft by date, brand feedback within a set number of days, post window. Review covers facts and disclosure, not the creator's voice.
6. **Write usage rights.** Organic repost only, or paid ads too; which platforms; how long. If content will run as an ad on Meta, the ad must tag the business partner with Meta's branded content tool. Rights are agreed in writing before use.
7. **Write the disclosure for each market:**
   - **US (FTC):** a clear label such as "Ad" or "Sponsored" with the endorsement, hard to miss; in video, said and shown, and repeated in live streams. Avoid "sp", "spon", "collab", "ambassador" or a lone "thanks". "Affiliate link" alone is not enough; "paid link" next to the link is. On YouTube, a description-only disclosure is not enough.
   - **UK (ASA and CMA):** "Ad", "Advert", "Advertising" or "Ad Feature", upfront, before people click or watch; on YouTube, an identifier such as "Ad" in the title and/or thumbnail. "Gifted", "aff", "spon" and "thanks to" are likely not enough, and a tag, code or affiliate link alone is not enough.
   - **EU:** brand partnerships and affiliate links must be disclosed; check the national guidance through the Commission's Influencer Legal Hub.
   - **Platform settings:** YouTube's paid promotion setting; Meta's paid partnership label or branded content tool; TikTok's content disclosure setting. Use them as well as the words, not instead.
8. **Tell the creator how to disclose AI.** If any realistic person, voice or scene is made or changed with AI, use the platform's AI disclosure.
9. **Check what comes back.** For each post: label present, upfront and visible on mobile; platform setting on; claims within the approved list; link and code correct; for video, disclosure spoken and shown at the start or before the mention. Ask for fixes promptly. If a creator keeps failing to label, recommend ending the partnership, because the CMA warns that continuing puts the brand at risk.
10. **Hold payment until the check passes,** where the agreement allows. The client pays.

## Checks

- One-line story, three points, approved claims with proof.
- Disclosure wording for every market the audience is in, plus the platform setting.
- Usage rights written, with platform and duration.
- Review step covers facts and labels, not the creator's voice.
- Post check done before payment or reuse.
- The brief says the contract is for the client's lawyer.

## Output format

1. **One-page brief:** story, points, approved claims, must-haves, freedom, timeline, review, usage rights, link and code, disclosure by market and platform.
2. **Disclosure card:** the exact words and settings, per platform.
3. **Post check table:** creator, post, label, placement, setting, claims, link or code, pass or fix.

## Example of good output (short)

*Northfield Oats is a made-up business used only to show the format: an oat milk brand sending a paid brief to a UK and US cooking creator. All details are invented.*

**Story:** "A creamier flat white at home without a barista jug."

**Approved claims:** "Froths at home with a hand frother" (client's test video [check: share]); "No added sugar" (label). Not approved: anything about health benefits.

**Disclosure card:**

| Platform | Words | Setting |
|---|---|---|
| Instagram Reel | "Ad" as the first word of the caption; "Ad for Northfield Oats" on screen in the first seconds | Paid partnership label |
| YouTube video | Title starts "Ad:" for the UK audience; spoken "This video is sponsored by Northfield Oats" before the mention, with on-screen text | Paid promotion setting |
| Link in bio or description | "Paid link" next to the link | n/a |

**Post check:** Reel 1: label upfront, setting on, claims fine, code correct: pass. YouTube video: disclosure only in the description: fix before payment.

*Why this works:* one story, claims tied to proof, and disclosure written per platform and market rather than left to the creator to guess.
