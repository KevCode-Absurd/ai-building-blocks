# AI skills workshop

## When to use

- A team has AI licences and low or careless use.
- Office, admin, marketing or customer service staff are nervous about data going into AI tools.
- An EU business wants to show steps taken on the AI Act's AI literacy duty (no certificate is required; training suited to the staff and the context is the point).

Not for building AI tools, and never with real customer or staff data in practice tasks.

## What you need

1. **The AI tools staff have,** the plan for each, and how they sign in.
2. **Roles and three to five real tasks per team** that happen every week.
3. **The business's AI rules,** if any. If there are none, draft a one-page safe-use sheet and mark it for the owner's sign-off before the session.
4. **Group size, format** (in person or remote), and how comfortable people are with the tools.
5. **Access I have:** the level the client grants in each tool (1 read, 2 read and draft, 3 read, write and publish). Steps 1 to 7 need level 1 only. Creating the skills-check form or course pages in the client's tools needs level 2; sending them to staff needs level 3. Until the client answers, work at level 1.

## Steps

1. **Run a short skills check first.** Five to eight questions plus one small practical task done in the team's own tool, with a marking guide. Cover: what the tool is good and bad at, what data may go in, how to give context, how to check an answer, what to do when it is wrong. Use the same check a month later to measure change.
2. **Build practice tasks from real work,** using made-up data that looks like the real thing. For each task: the goal, a starting prompt, what a good result looks like, and what to check (facts, numbers, names, tone, anything invented).
3. **Plan the two-hour workshop.**
   - **10 minutes:** a short demo on one real task, showing a weak prompt and a strong one.
   - **Practice rounds (about 20 minutes each),** each adding one habit:
     1. **Give context:** who it is for, the goal, the background.
     2. **Show an example:** paste a good past example (anonymised) and ask for the same style.
     3. **Ask for a format:** a table, a checklist, a three-sentence summary, a reply under 120 words.
     4. **Check the result:** ask the tool for its assumptions, compare with the source, fix what is wrong.
   - **Safe-use segment (15 minutes):** what never goes in (passwords, keys, card numbers, personal or client data on unapproved tools), how to spot made-up facts, when a person must check, and who to ask.
   - **Wrap-up (10 minutes):** each person writes down one task they will use AI for this week and how they will check it.
   Adjust timings to the group; keep practice as most of the session.
4. **Write facilitator notes:** what to say, common problems and fixes, how to help people who are stuck or sceptical, and how to handle "it got it wrong" moments (use them to teach checking).
5. **Describe tool features in general terms** or mark them "[check in your tool]". Plans and features change often; confirm on the vendor's own pages before the session.
6. **Write the follow-up micro-course:** short lessons by email or chat over two to four weeks, each with one tip and one task based on the team's work, and a reply-to question so the facilitator can see who is stuck.
7. **Repeat the skills check a month later** with the same questions, task and marking guide. Report the change by question, not just an average, and list what to cover next.
8. **Act only at the right level.** Level 1: deliver the plan, tasks, notes, check and course. Level 2: create the skills-check form, the course pages and the prompt library page in the client's tools as drafts that are not shared. Level 3: after the client confirms the action list and limits (which staff list, which channel, which dates), send the check and the lessons to the named staff. Log each send. Ask again before contacting anyone not on the list.

## Checks

- Every practice task is based on a real task and uses made-up data only.
- The safe-use segment matches the business's rules, or the draft sheet is marked for sign-off.
- Tool features are general or marked "[check in your tool]".
- No promise of time saved, in the session or the materials.
- The before and after checks use the same questions and marking guide.
- Any level 2 or 3 work is in the action log.

## Output format

1. Skills check: questions, practical task, marking guide.
2. Workshop plan: timed agenda, facilitator notes.
3. Practice tasks: goal, starting prompt, what good looks like, what to check.
4. Safe-use sheet (or a link to the business's rules).
5. Micro-course: lesson titles, tips and tasks.
6. After-check report template.
7. Action log (if any level 2 or 3 work).

## Example of good output (short)

*Hollin Housing Co-op is a made-up 20-person housing association used only to show the format: staff have Microsoft 365 with Copilot Chat. Access I have: level 1 (task list and the co-op's draft AI rules).*

**Practice task 2 (round 2, show an example).** Goal: reply to a tenant asking when a repair will happen.
- **Starting prompt:** "Here is a reply we sent last month that tenants found clear [paste anonymised example]. Write a reply in the same style to this message: [made-up tenant message]. Do not promise a date. Keep it under 120 words."
- **What good looks like:** warm, plain, says what happens next and who to call, no date promised.
- **What to check:** no invented dates or names, the right phone number, nothing the co-op has not agreed.

**Skills check question 4:** "A colleague pastes a tenant's full name and medical note into a free chatbot to draft a letter. What should they have done instead?" Marking guide: 2 points for "use the approved work tool or remove personal details", 1 point for "ask the data lead", 0 otherwise.
