# Product opportunity research

Most failed Amazon products fail before launch: the gap was not real, the margin vanished after fees and ads, or a rule nobody checked got the listing removed. This skill tests an idea against all three before any money goes to a supplier. It works only from the material the client shares (reviews, rival listings, tool exports, costs). It does not browse marketplaces or pull live data, and it says so.

## When to use

- A founder has one to twelve product ideas and wants to know which to pursue.
- A seller wants a variation or new product in a niche they already sell in.
- A client asks for a "product gap" report for one niche.
- A monthly scan of new ideas and changes in a category the client watches.

Not for: predicting sales, picking stocks, or copying a protected design.

## What you need

- **The decision:** what the client will do with the answer (order a sample, place a first order, drop the idea) and their budget for a first order.
- **Rival evidence:** the top three to five rival listings (title, bullets, price, rating, review count) and 50 to 100 reviews per rival, weighted to one, two and three stars.
- **Demand evidence:** Product Opportunity Explorer screenshots or exports for the niche, or a keyword export from a research tool such as Jungle Scout or Helium 10. Mark every tool figure as an estimate.
- **Costs:** supplier quote per unit, freight, duty, prep and packaging; dimensions and weight of the packed unit; target price.
- **Fees:** the referral fee rate for the category and the FBA fee for the size tier, from the Revenue Calculator or the client's own fee reports, dated.
- **Constraints:** stores (US, UK, EU), cash available, any category the client will not touch.
- **Access:** level 1 (read) is enough for every step. This skill analyses and changes nothing in any account.

If reviews or costs are missing, stop at the step that needs them and list exactly what to send.

## Steps

1. **Pin the question.** One sentence: "Should [client] order [quantity] of [product] for [store], given [budget]?"
2. **Code the reviews.** Read each low-star review. Tag the complaint theme (breaks, wrong size, hard to clean, smell, missing part), the job the buyer hired the product for, and the situation ("on a commute", "for a toddler"). Keep the buyer's exact words in a column. Count each theme and note how strongly it is put (mild, angry, returned it, safety worry).
3. **Read the praise too.** Four- and five-star reviews show what must not be lost in any improved version.
4. **Rank the gaps.** Order themes by count and strength. For each of the top three, check whether any rival already fixes it (look at their bullets, images and their own recent reviews). A theme that the best rival already fixes is not a gap.
5. **Check the fix is buildable.** For each real gap, write what the supplier must change and what it might cost. If the client has not asked the supplier, write the question to ask.
6. **Check demand evidence.** Note each demand signal, who produced it, when, and how strong it is. One tool estimate or one viral post is a rumour. Look for agreement between at least two kinds of evidence (search volume trend, review count growth on rivals, Product Opportunity Explorer niche trends).
7. **Work the unit profit.** Price minus: referral fee, FBA fulfilment fee, monthly storage per unit, inbound placement fee (state which split option was assumed), returns allowance, ad cost per sale, landed cost. Show the margin before ads and after ads. The margin before ads is the break-even ACOS: ads that cost more than this per sale lose money.
8. **Check rules and risks.** Product safety and labelling (EU and Northern Ireland need a Responsible Person and local-language warnings), restricted or gated categories, patents and trademarks on rival designs, seasonality, breakable or oversize items, high return rates. Flag anything that needs a qualified adviser.
9. **Score.** Score each idea 1 to 5 on demand evidence, margin after ads, risk (5 is lowest), effort to launch and fit with the client's brand and cash. Show every score with its reason so the client can challenge it. Do not weight the scores unless the client gives weights.
10. **Give the verdict.** Go, test small (a sample order or a small first batch with a capped ad budget) or stop, with the one reason that decides it.
11. **Say what would prove you wrong** and what you could not see (live search demand, the supplier's real quality, future fee changes).

## Checks

- Every complaint theme has a count and at least one quoted line.
- Each gap is checked against what rivals already offer.
- Every fee in the profit sum is named, dated and sourced; nothing is "approximately Amazon's fees".
- Margin is shown before and after ads, and break-even ACOS is stated.
- Tool estimates are labelled as estimates.
- Safety, labelling and IP risks are named for every store in scope.
- The verdict follows from the scores, and any override is explained.
- No idea depends on bought, swapped or rewarded reviews.

## Output format

1. **Question** (one sentence) and **assumptions**.
2. **Review themes** table: theme, count, strength, example quote, rival that fixes it (or none).
3. **Gap** chosen, with the supplier change needed.
4. **Demand evidence** list: signal, source, date, strength.
5. **Unit profit** table: each line item, amount, source and date; margin before and after ads; break-even ACOS.
6. **Risk notes** by store.
7. **Scorecard**: idea by idea, five scores with reasons.
8. **Verdict** and the reason, then **what would prove me wrong**.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: an outdoor gear seller weighing a new product for its US Amazon store. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape; they are not real Amazon fees.*

**Question:** Should the seller order 500 collapsible camping kettles for the US store with a $9,000 first-order budget?

**Review themes (240 low-star reviews across four rivals)**

| Theme | Count | Strength | Example quote | Fixed by a rival? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Silicone smell or taste | 61 | Many returns | "Water tasted like a pool toy for a week" | No |
| Hinge leaks after a month | 44 | Angry | "Leaked all over my stove bag" | Partly (rival C, but 3 stars) |
| Handle too hot | 18 | Mild | "Need a glove to pour" | Yes (rival A) |

**Gap:** taste-free kettle with a stainless hinge. Supplier question: cost of food-grade platinum-cured silicone and a steel hinge, with a test certificate.

**Unit profit (invented figures)**

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Price | $34.99 |
| Referral fee (example 15%) | $5.25 |
| FBA fulfilment (example tier) | $6.10 |
| Storage, placement, returns allowance | $1.90 |
| Landed cost | $9.80 |
| Margin before ads | $11.94 (34%) |
| Ad cost per sale (assumed) | $6.00 |
| Margin after ads | $5.94 (17%) |

Break-even ACOS: 34%.

**Scores:** demand 3 (two signals agree, both estimates), margin 3, risk 3 (food contact rules), effort 3, fit 4.

**Verdict: test small.** Order 150 units after a taste test of three supplier samples; cap launch ads at the break-even ACOS for eight weeks.

**What would prove me wrong:** samples still taste of silicone; rival C fixes its hinge; ad cost per sale above $11.94 after the first month.
