# Overdue invoice chase

## When to use

- Weekly credit control: who to chase, how, and when.
- Month end: the aged debt report has grown and needs a plan.
- One large customer is late and the client is worried about the relationship.
- The client hates chasing and wants it done politely and on time.

Do not use it to threaten, to decide on legal action, or to collect personal or household debts without checking the rules first. Chasing consumers is more tightly regulated than chasing businesses in many places: check this with local rules, and ask a qualified accountant or solicitor before any interest claim, compensation claim or legal step.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** open invoices, aged debt report, payment history, customer notes and the accounts inbox.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save reminders in drafts, set up reminder schedules switched off, draft payment plan offers and statements.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send approved reminder templates and statements to known billing contacts on the approved schedule, record promises and receipts, and update the tracker, within the limits the client set. A fresh yes every time for: final notices that mention legal steps, interest or compensation claims, putting an account on stop, payment plans outside the approved range, referrals to a collection agency, formal dispute replies and write-offs.

## What you need

1. The open invoice list or aged debt report: customer, invoice number, date, due date, amount, amount paid, days overdue.
2. Payment terms as agreed with each customer, and where they are written.
3. Each customer's history: usual payment days, past promises, disputes, and how much they buy.
4. Notes on customers to handle with care, and any the client always calls personally.
5. The client's voice: two or three past emails, or words they like and dislike.
6. Approved limits: which messages may go without review, the payment plan range the client accepts, and when an account goes on stop.

## Steps

### 1. Clean the list

- Remove invoices already paid but not matched (check receipts and remittances first).
- Mark invoices in dispute; they follow the dispute route, not the reminder ladder.
- Check each overdue invoice was received by the right contact. If not, resend it before chasing.

### 2. Group and rank

Age bands: due in the next 7 days, 1 to 30 days late, 31 to 60, 61 to 90, over 90. Within each band, rank by amount, then by risk: a customer who has gone silent ranks above one who has promised a date.

### 3. Choose one next step per invoice

- **Nudge** (due soon or a few days late, good payer): a friendly heads-up with the invoice attached.
- **Reminder** (late, no reply): the facts and one easy way to pay.
- **Call** (large amount, second reminder ignored, or a customer the client knows well).
- **Payment plan** (the customer says they cannot pay in full): dates and amounts within the approved range.
- **Hold new work** (as the client's stop rules say; needs a fresh yes the first time for each customer).
- **Refer to the client** (anything that might need interest, a legal letter or an agency).

Give each step a date.

### 4. Write the messages

Each message:
- Under 100 words for email; one screen on a phone.
- Names the invoice number, amount and original due date.
- Offers one way to pay (payment link or the bank details already on file) and one way to talk (a name and number).
- Rises one step on the tone ladder at a time: friendly nudge, clear reminder, firm notice, final notice.
- Never threatens a step the client has not approved. Mentions statutory interest or compensation only if the client and their adviser agree it applies [UK and EU rules allow it between businesses; check this with local rules].

### 5. Prepare calls

For each call: an opening that names who you are and the invoice; one question ("Can you tell me where this invoice is in your process?"); calm answers to common replies (not received, in the approval queue, waiting for a purchase order, cash is tight, dispute); and how to agree a date. After the call, confirm the date in writing the same day.

### 6. Handle disputes and promises

- Disputes: log the date, what is disputed and the amount. Chase the undisputed part. Fix the disputed part with evidence or a credit note the client approves.
- Promises: record the date and amount, confirm by email, and check the bank on the day.

### 7. Build the 90-day cash-in view

Expected receipts by week for the next 13 weeks, from due dates, payment habits and promises, with low and likely columns. Show how much is at risk.

### 8. Send (level 3) and log

Send only the templates and customers in the approved limits. Log each send, call, promise and dispute. Stop and ask on a bounce, a complaint, or a reply that suggests the invoice is wrong.

## Checks

- Every amount comes from the client's list; totals tie to the aged debt report.
- Every message names invoice, amount and due date; no message goes to a disputed invoice without the dispute note.
- No threat of a step the client has not approved; no false claim about the law.
- Payment plans stay inside the approved range.
- Disputes and promises logged with dates.
- The output carries the line: *General practice, not legal advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or solicitor before claiming interest or taking legal steps.*

## Output format

1. Summary: total overdue by age band, top five accounts by amount, change since last week.
2. Chase list: customer, invoice, amount, days late, next step, date, owner.
3. Messages ready to send, labelled by tone step.
4. Call sheets for the calls this week.
5. Dispute and promise log.
6. 90-day cash-in view.
7. Decisions for the client: stops, plans outside the range, interest, referrals, write-offs.

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a sign maker selling to shops and builders. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.
