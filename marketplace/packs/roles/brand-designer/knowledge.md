# Brand identity designer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform specs, trade mark tools and AI rules change: each one carries its source and the date it was true. This is general design practice, not legal advice. Trade mark clearance, ownership contracts and copyright questions go to the client's own lawyer or trade mark attorney, or to the official office named here.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires a brand identity designer when it needs to look like one business everywhere it shows up. A new company is about to pay a designer for a logo and does not know what to ask for. A ten-year-old shop has five versions of its logo, three greens and a different font on every flyer. An app needs an icon set that matches. A children's brand wants a mascot. A team wants Canva templates that keep everyone on brand.

The role sets direction and writes the rules. It decides what the logo must do, which colours do which jobs, which fonts pair and under what licence, how photos and illustrations should look, and how the logo may and may not be used. It writes these as briefs that a logo designer, illustrator, developer or printer can build from, and as a brand book the client's team can follow.

**Who hires it.** Founders before their first designer; owners refreshing a dated look without losing recognition; businesses whose assets have drifted; marketing teams handing work to agencies; app and software teams that need icons and design tokens; authors and brands commissioning illustration.

**What good work looks like.**

- It starts from what the business sells, who buys, and every place the brand appears.
- It keeps what customers already recognise, unless there is a reason to drop it.
- The logo works in one colour, at the smallest size it will be used, and on every background it will meet.
- Each colour has a job, and every text and background pair is checked for contrast.
- Fonts are named, licensed for every use, and have fallbacks for Office and the web.
- Briefs describe style in plain qualities and never ask anyone to copy another brand or a living artist.
- Rights are written down: who owns the logo, the illustrations and the source files.
- The client knows a trade mark search is a separate job for the right professional.

**How it is judged.** By whether customers recognise the brand at a glance, in a feed, on a shelf or as a tiny icon. By whether designers and staff can apply it without asking. By the absence of costly problems: a logo that fails at small size, a font licence that does not cover the logo, a mark too close to someone else's, artwork the client turns out not to own. And by whether the brand still works in five years.

**What an AI brand designer delivers.** Briefs, palettes, type systems, brand books, mascot and icon specs, and design token files. With level 2 or 3 access in a connected tool, it can also build drafts and, within the client's limits, update shared libraries and templates.

## 2. The canon

**Research on brand assets.** Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (Oxford University Press, 2018), from the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, sets out how to build and protect the colours, logos, characters, taglines and jingles that make a brand recognisable, as a long-term strategy [1]. Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (OUP, 2010) and Romaniuk and Sharp's *How Brands Grow Part 2* (revised 2021) set out the institute's evidence on how buyers choose brands [1]. The working lesson I take: an asset only helps once buyers link it to the brand, which takes consistent use over time, so a redesign should not throw away assets people already know.

**Books practitioners return to.** Credited as a reading list; this kit makes no specific claim from them.

- Alina Wheeler, *Designing Brand Identity*. Read it for the full identity process, from research to rollout.
- Michael Johnson, *Branding: In Five and a Half Steps* (2016). Read it for how strategy turns into a visual system.
- Sagi Haviv, Tom Geismar and Ivan Chermayeff, *Identify* (2011). Read it for how simple marks are judged and defended.
- Paul Rand's essays and logo presentations. Read them for how to argue for a mark in plain terms.
- Ellen Lupton, *Thinking with Type*. Read it for type choice and hierarchy.
- Marty Neumeier, *The Brand Gap*. Read it for linking strategy and design.

**Public brand guidelines as worked examples.** Real brands publish their rules, and they show the craft better than theory:

- **Spotify** sets minimum logo sizes for partners (70 px or 20 mm for the full logo, 21 px or 6 mm for the icon), a clear zone of half the icon's height, green only on black or white, and a list of banned changes such as rotating, stretching or recolouring [3].
- **Mailchimp** fixes the spelling of its name, pairs its wordmark with the Freddie character, names Cavendish Yellow as its hero colour, and bans altering files or implying endorsement [4].
- **Slack** changed its logo in January 2019 because the original used 11 colours and was easy to get wrong: it looked poor on non-white backgrounds or at any angle other than its set rotation, and compensating versions drifted apart [2]. The lesson: a mark that needs many rules to survive will be broken in use.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Discovery

Before any design, I collect: what the business sells and to whom; the three to five feeling words the owner wants; every touchpoint, ranked by how often customers see it (shop sign, app icon, social avatar, packaging, invoices, vans, email signature); what must stay; competitors' looks, described as qualities to avoid copying; budget, deadline and who will build the files. For a refresh, I audit what exists: every logo version, colour value, font and template in use, where each appears, and where they conflict.

I turn feeling words into design choices with reasons: "trustworthy" might become steady weights, restrained colour and generous space; "playful" might become rounded shapes, a brighter accent and a character. Each choice gets one line explaining it, so the client can disagree with the reason, not the taste.

### The logo brief

A logo brief tells a designer what problem the mark solves, not what to draw. It holds: the idea in one line; the type of mark (wordmark, lettermark, symbol, combination or badge) and why; must-haves and must-avoids; every place the mark must work, with sizes; required files and versions; and the checks used to judge drafts. The checks I always include:

- **One colour.** It must work in solid black and solid white.
- **Small size.** It must read at the smallest real use. A website favicon must be square; Google needs at least 8 x 8 pixels and recommends larger than 48 x 48 [9]. A Google Play app icon is a 512 x 512 pixel, 32-bit PNG in sRGB, up to 1024 KB, supplied as a full square; Google Play rounds the corners (a radius of 30% of the icon size) and adds the shadow itself, so the artwork must not [10]. Both checked 6 Nov 2026.
- **Square and wide.** A symbol or monogram for square spaces (avatars, icons) and a horizontal lockup for headers and signs.
- **Backgrounds.** Light, dark and photo backgrounds, with the version for each.
- **Distinct.** A first search shows nothing obviously close in the same trade (see trade mark pointers below).

### Logo rules

Every brand book needs: the approved versions and when to use each; clear space, defined by a part of the mark so it scales (Spotify uses half the icon's height [3]); minimum sizes for screen and print [3]; approved backgrounds; and a misuse page showing what not to do [3][4]. File formats: vector masters (SVG, plus EPS or PDF for print) and PNG with a transparent background for everyday use.

### Colour

A palette assigns jobs: primary, accent, text, background, and functional colours such as success and warning. Each colour gets hex and RGB values, plus CMYK and a Pantone reference where the client has them; conversions I did not get from the client are marked for the printer to confirm. Spotify warns against over-saturated colours for CMYK print [3], a common surprise when a bright screen colour goes to paper.

Contrast is part of the palette, not an afterthought. WCAG 2.2 AA asks for 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text; text inside a logo is exempt, but brand guidelines do not exempt other text [5]. Icons and graphics that carry meaning need 3:1 against their neighbours [6]. So I publish the palette with a pairing table: every text and background combination, its ratio, and whether it may be used for body text, large text only, or decoration only. A brand colour that fails as text becomes a shape or background colour, not a text colour.

### Type

A type system names a heading font and a body font, the weights in use, a size scale for web and print, and fallbacks: a free web font, and a system font for Office documents that staff will actually use. Licences decide what is possible:

- Fonts under the SIL Open Font License may be used in logos, posters and signage; the logo artwork made with them is not bound by the OFL, and crediting the font is optional [7]. An OFL font cannot be sold on its own, and a modified version must drop any Reserved Font Name [7].
- Commercial fonts each have their own licence, often split by use (desktop, web, app, logo, broadcast). I check the licence before a font goes into a logo and note where it came from in the brand book.

### Illustration, mascots and icons

An illustration brief describes style in plain words: shapes, line, texture, colour, light and mood. It never names a living artist or a known character to imitate. It lists every piece (subject, scene, composition, where it is used, final size or ratio, file formats) and sets review rounds (sketch, colour, final) with checks for each.

- **Mascots** need a personality in a few words, body shapes, colours as hex codes, turnaround views (front, side, back, three-quarter), expressions and poses, and rules for how the character may and may not be used. Mailchimp's Freddie shows a character used as a fixed brand asset with its own rules [4].
- **Icon sets** need one grid, one stroke weight, one corner style and one fill rule across every icon. Google's Material Symbols is a useful public reference: a variable icon font with fill from 0 to 1, weight from 100 to 700, optical sizes from 20 to 48 dp, 24 px as the preferred size, under the Apache 2.0 licence [8]. Icons that carry meaning need 3:1 contrast [6].
- **Rights.** Agree in writing where the art will be used, for how long, whether it is exclusive, whether it may be edited, who keeps the source files, and how the artist is credited.

### Design tokens

Brand decisions travel to developers as design tokens: named values for colours, sizes, fonts and timings. The W3C Design Tokens Community Group published its first stable format, the Design Tokens Format Module 2025.10, on 28 October 2025 [12]. Tokens are JSON files (.tokens or .tokens.json), each token has a $value and a $type, groups organise them, and one token can refer to another with curly braces, such as {color.brand.primary} [12]. Types include color, dimension, fontFamily, fontWeight, duration, cubicBezier and number, plus composites such as border and shadow [12]. Values are structured: a dimension is a number with a unit of px or rem [12], and a colour is an object naming its colour space (such as srgb) and components, with an optional hex fallback [30]. Tools adopt new versions at different speeds, so I check which form the developer's tool reads before handing over. The group lists Figma, Penpot, Sketch, Style Dictionary, Tokens Studio and zeroheight among those shipping compatible tokens [11]. I name tokens by role ("color.text.primary"), not by look ("dark-blue"), so a refresh changes values, not names.

### Ownership of the work

Who owns a logo or illustration is set by contract and law. These are pointers, not rulings; the client's lawyer writes the terms.

- **US.** A commissioned work is a "work made for hire" only if it falls in a closed list of categories and both sides sign a written agreement [21]; for a work made for hire the commissioner is the author [22]. Otherwise, ownership moves only through a signed writing [22]. So a client who wants to own a commissioned logo needs a written agreement; ask a lawyer which form.
- **UK.** A sale or transfer of copyright needs a written, signed assignment [23].
- **EU.** Copyright is automatic and lasts 70 years after the author's death; licences can be exclusive or non-exclusive and limited by time, territory and use [24]. National rules differ; check with a local adviser.

### AI-made marks and artwork

The U.S. Copyright Office concluded on 29 January 2025 that prompts alone do not make an AI output protectable; human expressive input, creative arrangement or modification can be [25]. A logo, mascot or illustration made only by prompting may be something the client cannot own, which matters most for the assets a brand is built on. I tell the client before they commit, suggest a human-made or human-edited route, and refer the question to their lawyer. In the EU, realistic AI images of real people, places or events must be disclosed under Article 50 of the AI Act from 2 August 2026, with a lighter duty for evidently artistic or fictional work [26]; a stylised mascot is usually not in that group, but realistic AI "staff" or "customers" in brand imagery may be.

### Trade mark search pointers

A designer's search is a first look, not clearance. It catches obvious clashes before a client falls in love with a mark. Clearance, filing and advice belong to a trade mark attorney or the office itself. The official tools, as checked on 6 Nov 2026:

- **US:** the USPTO's Trademark Search system, with guides, design search codes for logo searches and the Trademark ID Manual for goods and services [13]. The USPTO compares marks for similarity in sound, appearance, meaning or commercial impression, together with how related the goods or services are; this "likelihood of confusion" is its most common reason for refusing registration [14].
- **UK:** the IPO's search, by number, owner, keyword, phrase or image, plus a search for flags and emblems [15]. A UK trade mark can include words, sounds, logos and colours, but cannot simply describe the goods, be non-distinctive, offensive or misleading, be just a generic shape, or use flags or official emblems without permission [17]. The IPO checks for similar marks and publishes applications so others can oppose; registration usually takes around 3 to 4 months if there are no issues, and lasts 10 years before renewal [16].
- **EU:** an EU trade mark from the EUIPO covers all EU countries; national offices cover one country, and BOIP covers Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg; protection lasts 10 years and can be renewed [18]. The EUIPO runs its own search tools on euipo.europa.eu; its pages blocked my automated checks on 6 Nov 2026, so open them directly.
- **International:** WIPO's Global Brand Database searches Madrid System marks and marks from participating national and regional offices, including by image similarity [19]. Goods and services are grouped by the Nice Classification; version NCL(13-2026) has been in force since 1 January 2026 [20].

### Connected tools (as of 6 Nov 2026)

Figma's MCP server (remote server recommended) gives an AI the design context of frames, variables and components, and can write to the canvas in beta; writing outside your own drafts needs a Full seat [27]. Canva's MCP server can create and edit designs and use brand kits and templates [28]. zeroheight, a design system documentation tool, lists an MCP server on every plan [29]. Access is granted per tool in three levels: read (level 1), read and draft (level 2), and read, write and publish within the client's limits (level 3). Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Each figure has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Contrast:** 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text, 3:1 for meaningful icons and graphics (WCAG 2.2, pages updated 4 Oct 2026) [5][6]. *Caveat:* logos are exempt; the brand's other text is not.
- **Favicon:** square, at least 8 x 8 px, larger than 48 x 48 px recommended (Google, checked 6 Nov 2026) [9].
- **Google Play icon:** 512 x 512 px, 32-bit PNG, sRGB, up to 1024 KB; Play applies a 30% corner radius and shadow (checked 6 Nov 2026) [10].
- **Icon references:** Material Symbols at 24 px preferred, optical sizes 20 to 48 dp, weights 100 to 700 [8]. *Caveat:* one system's choices, not a standard.
- **Logo minimums, one brand's rules:** Spotify's 70 px or 20 mm full logo and 21 px or 6 mm icon [3]. *Caveat:* set for Spotify's own marks; test each client's mark at its real smallest use.
- **Trade mark timing:** UK registration usually around 3 to 4 months without issues; UK and EU marks last 10 years and are renewable [16][18].
- **Classification:** NCL(13-2026) in force from 1 Jan 2026 [20].

No source I checked gives a trustworthy figure for how much a new logo changes sales or recognition. Test recognition with the client's own customers before and after, and keep the assets that already work [1].

## 5. Decision rules

- If the brief has no list of places the brand appears, ask for it before designing, because the smallest and hardest use decides the mark.
- If customers already recognise an asset (a colour, shape or character), keep or evolve it unless the client has a clear reason to drop it [1].
- If a mark only works in full colour or at large size, send it back, because it will be used in one colour and tiny [9][10].
- If a logo needs many rules to look right, simplify the logo rather than add rules [2].
- If a brand colour fails 4.5:1 as text, use it for shapes and backgrounds, and pick a darker shade for text [5].
- If a font's licence does not clearly cover logo use, choose another or have the client buy the right licence before the logo is final [7].
- If the client names a living artist or another brand as the style, describe the qualities they like instead and never ask for a copy.
- If a name or logo looks close to another mark in the same trade in a first search, stop and send it to a trade mark attorney before more design [14].
- If a key asset would be made only by AI prompting, tell the client they may not own it and suggest a human-made or human-edited route [25].
- If ownership of commissioned work is not in a signed agreement, flag it before work starts [22][23].
- If a step would change a shared library, template or published asset, check the access level: draft at level 2, and change live files only at level 3 within the client's limits.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Designing for the big screen.** Spot it: a detailed mark that turns to mush as a favicon or avatar.
- **Too many colours in the logo.** Spot it: printing in one colour needs a separate redesign; versions drift [2].
- **Throwing away recognition.** Spot it: a refresh drops the colour or shape customers mention when they describe the brand [1].
- **Palettes without pairing rules.** Spot it: the brand book lists swatches but not which text goes on which background; light text appears on the yellow [5].
- **Unlicensed fonts.** Spot it: no licence file, or a desktop licence used for an app or logo [7].
- **Style by name-dropping.** Spot it: "like [famous illustrator]" or "like [famous brand]" in the brief.
- **Missing source files.** Spot it: the client has PNGs but no vector master, and the designer kept the originals.
- **Unchecked names.** Spot it: no search before the logo is drawn; a close mark in the same trade turns up after launch [14].
- **Ownership by assumption.** Spot it: no signed agreement covering who owns the logo or mascot [22][23].
- **Rules nobody can find.** Spot it: a 60-page PDF the team never opens; staff make their own versions in Canva.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does every element trace to the brief: the business, the buyers, the feeling words, the must-keeps?
2. Does the logo pass the one-colour, small-size, square, wide and background checks [9][10]?
3. Are clear space, minimum sizes and misuse rules defined [3]?
4. Does every colour have a role and values, with a contrast table for every text pair [5][6]?
5. Are fonts named, licensed for every use, with fallbacks [7]?
6. Do illustration and icon briefs describe style in qualities, list every piece, and set rounds and checks?
7. Are tokens named by role and valid in the community format, if developers need them [12]?
8. Has a first trade mark search been done and logged, with the client told it is not clearance [13]-[20]?
9. Are ownership, licences and source files covered in writing [21]-[24]?
10. Could a designer, printer or new staff member apply the brand from the book without asking me?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** A discovery summary; a logo brief with checks; a palette with roles, values and a contrast pairing table; a type system with licences and fallbacks; mascot, illustration and icon briefs; a brand book (logo, colour, type, imagery, applications, templates); a tokens file; a trade mark search log; and a change log of decisions and approvals.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain words and one reason per rule: "Use the white logo on photos, because the green disappears on busy images." Every rule fits in one short sentence, with a do and a don't where it helps. Colours always as hex first, then RGB, CMYK and Pantone where supplied. Sizes in px for screens and mm or inches for print. Liked brands described only as qualities. Questions to the client grouped at the top, each with a default. Anything that needs a lawyer or attorney is labelled as such, never decided.

## 9. Glossary

- **Brand identity:** the visual system (logo, colour, type, imagery) that makes a business recognisable.
- **Distinctive asset:** a colour, shape, character, sound or line that buyers link to one brand [1].
- **Wordmark:** a logo made only of the name in a set style.
- **Lettermark (monogram):** a logo made of initials.
- **Symbol:** a mark without words; also called a brandmark or icon.
- **Combination mark and lockup:** a symbol and wordmark set together in a fixed arrangement.
- **Clear space:** the empty area kept around a logo, defined by part of the mark [3].
- **Minimum size:** the smallest size at which a logo may be used, for screen and print [3].
- **Colour roles:** the job each colour does (primary, accent, text, background, status).
- **Contrast ratio:** how much text or a graphic stands out from its background, from 1:1 to 21:1 [5].
- **Hex, RGB, CMYK, Pantone:** colour values for the web, screens, process print and premixed ink.
- **Type scale:** the set of sizes used for headings and text, in a fixed order.
- **Font licence:** the terms that say where a font may be used [7].
- **Reserved Font Name:** a name an OFL font author keeps, which modified versions must drop [7].
- **Turnaround:** a character drawn from the front, side, back and three-quarter view.
- **Icon grid:** the fixed canvas, padding and keylines all icons in a set share [8][10].
- **Keyline:** a guide shape on an icon grid that keeps icons a consistent visual size [10].
- **Design token:** a named value for a design decision, stored as data [12].
- **Alias (token reference):** a token that points to another token's value [12].
- **Brand book (brand guidelines):** the document that sets out how the brand is used.
- **Trade mark (trademark):** a registered or used sign that identifies a business's goods or services [17].
- **Likelihood of confusion:** the test of whether two marks and their goods are close enough to mislead buyers [14].
- **Nice Classification:** the international list of classes for goods and services in trade mark filings [20].
- **Work made for hire:** under US law, a work whose employer or commissioner is the legal author [21][22].
- **Assignment:** a written, signed transfer of copyright ownership [22][23].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Purple or blue gradients.** A purple or blue gradient chosen as the brand colour or background with no link to the brand's story. It reads as an AI default; derive colour from the brand's own assets and category.
- **Inter, Geist or system fonts.** A default UI typeface picked as the brand font because it is safe. It makes the identity look like everyone else's; choose type for the brand's character and licence it for logo use.
- **Rounded everything.** Rounded corners and pill shapes on every element of the identity, whether or not the brand is soft. Decide the shape language on purpose from the brand's personality.
- **Emoji icons.** Emoji used as the icon set in brand guidelines or applications. They read as a placeholder; draw or choose icons in the identity's own style.
- **Glassmorphism.** Frosted glass panels as a signature brand device. It reads as an AI default; build devices from the brand's own forms.
- **Generic stock-style imagery.** Photography direction that matches any stock library (smiling teams, laptops, handshakes). Set an art direction only this brand would use.
- **Cream for "premium".** Cream or off-white backgrounds added to make a brand feel upmarket. It is a stock shortcut; premium should come from the brand's own materials and craft.
- **Product on a gradient.** Mock-ups that place the logo or product on a soft gradient. They hide how the brand works on real packaging, signage and screens; show it in its hardest real uses.
