# Illustration and mascot brief

An illustrator can only match a brand if the brief says what "our style" means in shapes, lines and colours. This skill writes that brief, lists every piece, sets fair checks for each round, and puts the rights questions on the table before anyone draws.

## When to use

- A brand wants a mascot or character.
- A website or app needs a set of spot illustrations or icons that match.
- A self-published author or brand needs picture book or editorial spreads briefed.
- A client wants to test a mascot idea cheaply before commissioning.

Not for: drawing the art, copying a living artist's style or a known character, or writing the legal contract (this skill lists the questions to agree).

## What you need

- What the art is for, the audience, where it will be used and at what sizes.
- Brand colours (hex), fonts and logo; any existing illustrations.
- Style words from the client, and examples they like (to describe, not copy).
- The list of pieces needed, budget and dates.
- **Access I have.** Steps 1 to 8 need level 1. Step 9 (setting up a draft board in the client's Figma or Canva with the brief and reference qualities) needs level 2. Adding approved art to the client's live library or brand kit needs level 3 within the client's limits.

## Steps

1. **Write the goal in one line** and the style in plain words: shapes (round, angular), line (none, thin, thick, uneven), texture (flat, grain, paper), colour (brand palette only, or extended), light and mood. Turn each liked example into qualities ("flat colour, no outlines, rounded hands") and drop the names.
2. **List every piece.** Number, subject, scene or pose, composition, where it is used, final size or ratio, and file formats (vector SVG for icons and spot art; layered source files for everything).
3. **Mascots and characters.** Personality in three words; body shapes and proportions; colours as hex codes from the palette; turnaround views (front, side, back, three-quarter); a set of expressions and poses tied to real uses (welcome, error message, celebration); what the character never does. Mailchimp's Freddie is a public example of a character treated as a fixed brand asset with its own rules.
4. **Icon sets.** Each icon's meaning in words; one grid size and padding; one stroke weight; one corner style; one fill rule; how icons pair with text. Google's Material Symbols is a useful reference for a system: 24 px preferred size, optical sizes from 20 to 48 dp, weights from 100 to 700. Icons that carry meaning need 3:1 contrast against their background (WCAG 2.2). For app store icons, follow the store's spec (a Google Play icon is a 512 x 512 px full square, checked 6 Nov 2026).
5. **Picture book or editorial spreads.** Spread by spread: the text, what we see, where the words sit (leave clear space for type), page-turn moments, and character continuity notes.
6. **Rights questions to agree in writing.** Where the art may be used and for how long; exclusive or not; whether the client may edit it; who owns the copyright and the source files; credit. Note the basics without ruling: in the US, ownership of commissioned art moves only by a signed writing unless it qualifies as a work made for hire under a signed agreement; in the UK an assignment must be written and signed. The client's lawyer writes the terms.
7. **Rounds and checks.** Sketch, colour and final rounds, each with at least four pass or fail checks: on brief, on palette, consistent with the set, readable at the smallest size, no likeness to existing characters, files as specified.
8. **Optional AI test prompts.** If the client wants to test the idea first, write a few prompts showing the mascot in different poses, using only the qualities from step 1, never artist or character names. State clearly: AI-made images from prompts alone may not be protectable by copyright (U.S. Copyright Office, January 2025), so use them only to test the idea; commission or substantially create the final character with human authorship, and check with a lawyer.
9. **Level 2: set up the board.** With level 2 access, create a draft board in the client's design tool with the brief, the piece list and the checks, for the illustrator to work against. Log it.

## Checks

- Style is described in qualities; no living artist or known character is named.
- Every piece is listed with size, use and formats.
- Mascot briefs include turnarounds, expressions and poses tied to uses.
- Icon briefs fix grid, stroke, corners and fills, with contrast.
- Rights questions are listed for written agreement; nothing is assumed.
- Each round has pass or fail checks.
- Any AI test prompts carry the ownership caveat.

## Output format

1. **Goal and style** in plain words.
2. **Piece list** table.
3. **Character sheet spec** or **icon system spec** or **spread plan**, as needed.
4. **Rights questions.**
5. **Rounds and checks.**
6. **Optional test prompts** with the caveat.
7. **Open questions** and an action log for any level 2 or 3 work.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a savings app for teenagers. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Goal:** a friendly guide who makes saving feel like a small win, used in onboarding and empty states.

**Style:** flat colour from the brand palette, no outlines, soft rounded shapes, slight paper grain, warm light, calm and encouraging rather than silly.

**Mascot:** a young badger. Personality: patient, curious, quietly proud. Shapes: pear-shaped body, short legs, big round eyes. Colours: #3B3F5C body, #F2E8D5 face stripe, #F28C38 scarf. Turnarounds: front, side, back, three-quarter. Expressions: welcome, thinking, celebrating, sorry (for errors). Never: handles cash, wears brand logos of others, appears sad about a user's balance.

**Rights questions:** exclusive use in all media? Edits allowed by our team? Copyright assigned in writing? Source files delivered? Credit line?

**Round 1 checks (sketch):** reads as a badger at 64 px; personality visible in the welcome pose; no likeness to existing cartoon badgers; three-quarter view consistent with front view.
