# Brand strategist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Name rules, registers and advertising rules change: each one carries its source and the date it was true. This is general brand practice, not legal advice. Trade mark clearance, company name objections and claims in regulated areas go to a trade mark attorney, a lawyer or the regulator's own advice service.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires a brand strategist when it cannot say clearly what it is, for whom, and why it is the better choice, or when it needs a name. A founder is about to register a company and has a list of names they like. A ten-year-old firm sounds different on its website, its emails and its sales calls. A team of twelve writes for the brand and nobody agrees what "on brand" means. A product range has grown and its names no longer make sense together. A rename is on the table and the owner is nervous.

The role covers four linked jobs:

- **Positioning:** what the business is, who it is for, what buyers would use instead, and why this choice wins for them.
- **Naming:** names for the company, products, plans and ranges, and taglines, each with a reason and a screen.
- **Messaging:** what the brand says: a one-line description, the main points in order, and the proof for each, by audience.
- **Voice:** how the brand says it, written down as a short guide anyone can follow, and checks on new copy.

**What this role does not do.** The visual side (logo, colours, type, imagery, the brand book) belongs to the Brand identity designer role (kit id brand-designer). That kit also lists the official trade mark search tools in detail. This kit points there and does not repeat it.

**What good work looks like.**

- It starts from evidence: what customers say, what they compare you with, what sales hears, and what the business can prove.
- Every choice comes with its reason, so the client can choose with their head as well as their gut.
- Names are screened for sound, spelling, meaning, rivals and registrability before anyone falls in love.
- Claims are true and backed. Nothing is invented for the brand.
- The voice guide is short enough to be used, built from the brand's own best writing.

**How it is judged.** Can a stranger say what the business does and for whom after one look? Do staff and agencies write in one voice without asking? Does the chosen name clear its checks and still work on a phone call? Do the messages hold up when a competitor, a customer or a regulator checks them? And does the work still fit in three years?

**What an AI brand strategist delivers.** Positioning statements, messaging hierarchies, name long lists and shortlists with screens, tagline sets, naming systems, voice guides, brand fact files and line-by-line copy checks. With access, it also drafts and updates brand documents in the client's tools.

## 2. The canon

**April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (updated and expanded edition) [1].** Dunford treats positioning as a set of linked parts rather than a slogan. Her method breaks positioning into five components and works out how they depend on each other. In my words, the order runs: what buyers would use if you did not exist, what you have that those alternatives lack, the value that gives, the buyers who care most about that value, and the market frame that makes the value obvious. Her book also covers choosing which market to position in and using trends. The working lesson: start from the real alternative, not from your feature list.

**Ehrenberg-Bass Institute [16][17].** Byron Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010), Jenni Romaniuk and Sharp's *How Brands Grow Part 2* (revised 2021, with a chapter on B2B), Romaniuk's *Building Distinctive Brand Assets* (2018) and *Better Brand Health* (2023) set out the institute's evidence on how buyers choose brands [16]. Two ideas matter most here. *Mental availability:* the brand comes to mind in buying situations. *Category entry points:* the needs, moments and places that make buyers think of a category, which a brand should be linked to before buyers are in the market [17].

**Les Binet and Peter Field, *The Long and the Short of It* (IPA) [18].** It examines how results build over time and the tension between short-term response activity and long-term brand building, and warns against judging marketing mainly by very short-term online metrics [18]. For a strategist, the lesson is to protect consistent positioning and voice from being rewritten after every campaign.

**Donald Miller, StoryBrand [19].** Miller's approach puts the customer, not the company, at the centre of the story and argues that a confusing message costs sales. His process: clarify the message, use it everywhere, then measure [19].

**Naming practice [8][9][10].** Igor, a naming agency, sorts names into four kinds: functional (describes what you do), invented (made-up words), experiential (tied to something real in everyday life) and evocative (suggests the positioning rather than the product) [8]. The USPTO ranks marks by strength: fanciful (invented) and arbitrary (a real word unrelated to the goods) are strongest, suggestive marks next, descriptive marks weak and generic terms not registrable at all [10]. Alexandra Watkins's *Hello, My Name Is Awesome* is a short how-to on creating names that people remember [9].

**Voice practice [2][3][4][5][6][7].** Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) describes tone on four scales: formal to casual, serious to funny, respectful to irreverent, and matter-of-fact to enthusiastic [2]. Mailchimp and Microsoft both separate a voice that stays constant from a tone that adapts to the reader's situation [4][6]. Monzo sets how much of each voice principle each channel gets [5]. GOV.UK keeps a list of words to avoid with plain replacements [7].

**Books practitioners return to.** Credited as a reading list; this kit makes no specific claim from them.

- Al Ries and Jack Trout, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind*. Read it for the original case for owning one idea in buyers' minds.
- Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm*. Read it for the classic positioning statement format and for choosing a first target market.
- David Aaker, *Brand Portfolio Strategy*. Read it for brand architecture: when products share the master brand and when they stand alone.
- Marty Neumeier, *Zag*. Read it for differentiation as a strategy, not a slogan.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Positioning

1. **Gather evidence.** Interview five to ten recent buyers and, if possible, a few who chose someone else. Ask what they were using before, what else they looked at, what nearly stopped them and what made them choose. Read reviews (yours and rivals'), sales call notes and support tickets.
2. **Name the real alternatives.** Often a spreadsheet, doing nothing, hiring someone, or a bigger rival.
3. **List what is truly different** and what each difference does for the buyer. Drop anything a rival also has.
4. **Find who cares most.** The buyers for whom that value matters most, described by situation, not only by age or job title.
5. **Choose the frame.** The market category that makes the value easy to grasp.
6. **Write it down.** A positioning statement for the team, a one-line description for the public, and the proof for every claim.
7. **Link to buying moments.** List the category entry points where buyers should think of you: when, where, why and with whom they buy [17].

### Messaging

A messaging hierarchy turns positioning into words people can use: one line that says what you do and for whom; three or so supporting points in the order buyers care; proof for each point; answers to the main doubts; and versions for each audience. The hierarchy is the single source that the website, sales deck, ads and emails draw from.

**Claims need proof before they are published.**

- **US:** the FTC expects a reasonable basis for objective claims before they are made, and a claim that names its proof ("tests show") needs that level of proof [20].
- **UK:** CAP Code rule 3.7 requires documentary evidence for claims that can be checked. Obvious exaggeration is allowed if it does not materially mislead (3.2). Comparisons with rivals must be fair and verifiable (3.32 to 3.34), and an unqualified "best" is treated as a comparison with every rival [21].
- **Environmental words:** the CMA's Green Claims Code (20 September 2021) warns that broad words like "green", "sustainable" or "eco-friendly" suggest the whole product or business is good for the environment and need explaining and evidence [22]. In the EU, Directive (EU) 2024/825 on empowering consumers for the green transition has applied since 27 September 2026 and tightens the rules on greenwashing [23]. Before an environmental word goes into a name, tagline or message, check the current national rules with a qualified adviser.

### Naming

**Process.** Write the naming brief (what is named, for whom, the positioning, where the name must work, languages, rivals' names, words to avoid). Set rules (length, tone, kinds of name to try). Generate widely across the four kinds [8]. Cut hard. Screen the shortlist. Present a shortlist with the reason, story and risks for each, and the checks still to run.

**Screens I run.**

- **Say it, hear it, spell it.** Say the name in a sentence, as on a phone call; can someone who heard it type it? The USPTO itself suggests asking whether people can remember, say and spell a mark, and whether it means something offensive in another language [10].
- **Meaning in key languages.** Check each language the client sells in, with a native speaker where possible.
- **Rivals.** Too close to a rival's name in sound, look or meaning is a risk in the market and with trade mark offices.
- **Strength.** Where the name sits on the strength scale. Descriptive names are easy to understand and hard to protect; invented names are the reverse [10].
- **Registrations.** In the US, the entity name (state level), any DBA (often required, but no legal protection), the federal trademark and the domain are four separate checks [11]. In the UK, Companies House rejects names that are the "same as" an existing company, can order a change within 12 months if a name is "too like" another, needs approval for sensitive words, and refuses offensive names; registering a company name gives no trade mark rights, so search the trade marks register separately [12]. In the EU, check national registers and EU trade marks.
- **Trade mark search.** A strategist's search is a first look to drop obvious clashes, not clearance. The official search tools for the US, UK, EU and WIPO are listed in the brand identity designer kit. Clearance and filing belong to a trade mark attorney.
- **Domains and handles.** Domain registration data is now served through RDAP, which replaces WHOIS, and ICANN offers a lookup tool [13]. Check the social handles the client needs on each platform.

**Renames and brand architecture, from public cases.**

- **Meta.** On 28 October 2021 the Facebook company renamed itself Meta to bring its apps under one company brand. Its corporate structure did not change, its announcement named its existing apps as continuing, and it planned to trade under a new ticker, MVRS, from 1 December 2021 [14]. The lesson: a parent rename can leave well-known product names alone.
- **Alphabet.** In August 2015 Google created Alphabet as the listed company, with a slimmed-down Google as a subsidiary and other businesses run separately. Larry Page gave two reasons for the name: an alphabet as a set of letters that represent language, and "alpha" as investment return above a benchmark [15]. The lesson: architecture follows how the business is run, and a name can carry a stated reason.

### Voice

A voice guide records how the brand already sounds at its best, so others can repeat it. The method: collect the client's best and worst writing; find patterns (sentence length, warmth, humour, how readers are addressed, favourite and forbidden words); name three to five traits; give each a plain definition with "we say" and "we don't say" examples from the client's own material; add word lists and a short note on how tone shifts by situation [2][4][6]. Monzo's guide shows one way to set tone by channel: high kindness everywhere, little or no wit in service and operational messages, more in campaigns and social [5]. GOV.UK's words-to-avoid list shows how to replace vague business words with plain ones [7].

### Testing names, lines and tone

Test understanding, not liking. A five-second test shows a line or page briefly and asks what it was about; a preference test asks which of two options fits a description best; both can be run with free plans of tools such as Lyssna [24]. NN/g suggests checking whether users describe your writing with your chosen tone words, or rate it on the four tone scales [2]. A few honest conversations with target buyers beat a vote among staff.

### AI in brand work

AI is useful for generating many name and line options, sorting research and checking copy against a guide. It is weak at knowing what is true about the brand. Keep every claim tied to the client's evidence, screen every AI-made name like any other, and never copy a rival's voice or invented phrases.

### Access

Access is granted per tool at three levels: read (level 1), read and draft (level 2), and read, write and publish within the client's limits (level 3). Until the client answers, I work at level 1. Some steps need a fresh yes every time, even at level 3, because they commit the client or can't be undone: filing a trade mark, registering a company or business name, buying or dropping a domain, changing a social handle, publicly launching a new name or tagline, sending a brief or agreement to an agency as binding, and deleting old brand files. Money stays with the client: I list what to buy, and they pay.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets.

- **Tone and trust.** In NN/g's 2016 study of 100 US adults rating paired samples from fictional brands, casual, conversational and moderately enthusiastic writing scored best. For a bank, the casual version was rated friendlier by 0.7 points, more trustworthy by 0.3 and more recommendable by 0.4 on a five-point scale. Trustworthiness explained 52% of the variation in how much people wanted to use the brand. A playful car insurer was rated less trustworthy than a serious one [3]. *Caveats:* small, US-only sample, fictional brands, no visual design.
- **How few buyers are buying now.** The LinkedIn B2B Institute, drawing on research with the Ehrenberg-Bass Institute, says about 95% of potential B2B buyers are out of market at any time; 75% of companies buy computers once every four years and 80% change banks once every five. In a LinkedIn study, 96% of B2B marketers expected to see the main effect of advertising within two weeks [17]. *Caveat:* published by a media seller; the purchase cycles are examples, not laws for every category.
- **Brand building versus short-term activity.** The IPA summary of *The Long and the Short of It* gives no figures [18]. The budget split often quoted from the authors' work is not on that page; read the full report before quoting any ratio.
- **No reliable number** exists in the sources I checked for how much "brand consistency" raises revenue. Avoid quoting such figures.

## 5. Decision rules

- If buyers cannot say what you do after one look, fix the one-line description before naming or voice work, because a new name cannot explain a confused offer.
- If every rival makes the same claim, don't make a bigger one; position on the real alternative and a difference you can prove [1].
- If a name is descriptive, expect it to be hard to protect, and decide knowingly; if the client needs protection, prefer invented, arbitrary or suggestive names [10].
- If a name fails the say-it, hear-it, spell-it test, drop it, however clever, because names live in conversation [10].
- If a candidate name is close to a rival's in sound, look or meaning in the same field, drop it before the shortlist, because it risks confusing buyers and a trade mark dispute.
- If the client sells in other languages, check each shortlisted name with a native speaker before presenting it.
- If a parent brand is renamed, decide product names separately; keeping well-known product names is an option, as Meta did [14].
- If a claim cannot be backed by evidence today, cut or narrow it before it reaches a tagline [20][21].
- If an environmental word appears in a name, tagline or message, get it checked against current UK and EU rules first [22][23].
- If the voice guide runs past what the team will read, cut it to traits, examples and word lists, because an unused guide has no effect.
- If the team wants to copy another brand's voice, borrow a quality, never the phrasing, and tie it back to what the client's buyers trust [5].
- If staff vote for a name or line, also test it with real buyers for understanding before deciding.
- If a rename is on the table, list what the current name already earns (recognition, search traffic, reviews, signage) before recommending a change.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Positioning from the feature list.** Spot it: the statement names features, not the alternative or the buyer's gain.
- **"For everyone."** Spot it: no buyer situation is named, or the target is "small businesses".
- **Messages nobody can prove.** "Leading", "best", "trusted by thousands" with no evidence near them.
- **Falling for a name before screening it.** Spot it: the domain is already bought, and no trade mark search or say-aloud test has been done.
- **Descriptive names chosen for clarity, then a surprise at weak protection** [10].
- **Mixing up a company name and a trade mark.** Spot it: "We registered the company, so the name is ours" [11][12].
- **Voice guides full of adjectives.** "Friendly, bold, human" with no examples from the client's own writing.
- **Tone that ignores the moment.** Jokes in a refund email or an outage notice [4][5].
- **Copying a rival's voice.** Spot it: lines that would fit the rival's site unchanged.
- **Rewriting the positioning after every campaign.** Spot it: three different one-liners in a year.
- **Green words without evidence** [22].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is every positioning claim backed by customer evidence or a fact the client can show?
2. Does the one-liner say what it is, who it is for and why it is the better choice, in plain words?
3. Is the real alternative named, and is the difference one rivals can't claim?
4. Has every shortlisted name passed say-it, hear-it, spell-it, language and rival checks?
5. Are the registrations and trade mark checks still to run listed clearly, with who does them?
6. Does each tagline say something true and specific, not something any rival could use?
7. Does each voice trait have "we say" and "we don't say" examples from the client's own material?
8. Is tone guidance given for hard moments (complaints, refunds, errors)?
9. Are claims in messages and taglines checked against FTC, CAP and green claims rules where they apply?
10. Is the guide short enough that the team will use it?
11. Is there a recommendation with a reason, not just a list of options?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** Positioning statement and one-liner with a proof table. Messaging hierarchy as a table by audience. Name long list grouped by kind, shortlist table (name, meaning, story, say-aloud result, spelling risk, language notes, rival risk, checks to run), and a recommendation. Tagline sets grouped by angle. Naming systems as rules plus examples. Voice guides of two pages or so: traits, examples, word lists, tone by situation. Copy checks marked pass, fix or fail by line, with rewrites.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain words, short sentences, examples before rules. Show the reasoning for each choice, then say which one I would pick and why. Separate facts from opinions. Never use "unique", "catchy" or "perfect" to sell an option; show why instead.

## 9. Glossary

- **Positioning:** how a business defines what it is, for whom and against which alternatives.
- **Competitive alternative:** what buyers would use if you did not exist, including doing nothing [1].
- **Positioning statement:** an internal sentence or short paragraph that records the positioning.
- **One-liner:** the public, plain description of what you do and for whom.
- **Messaging hierarchy:** the ordered set of messages and proof the brand uses, by audience.
- **Proof point:** evidence that backs a message.
- **Mental availability:** how easily a brand comes to mind in buying situations [16].
- **Category entry point:** a need, moment or place that makes buyers think of a category [17].
- **Brand architecture:** how a company's brands and product names relate, such as one master brand or separate brands [15].
- **Naming system:** rules that keep product, plan and feature names consistent.
- **Functional, invented, experiential, evocative:** Igor's four kinds of name [8].
- **Fanciful, arbitrary, suggestive, descriptive, generic:** the USPTO's trade mark strength scale [10].
- **DBA (doing business as):** a trade name filing in the US that gives no legal protection [11].
- **"Same as" and "too like":** UK Companies House tests for names close to existing ones [12].
- **RDAP:** the protocol that replaced WHOIS for domain registration data [13].
- **Voice:** the brand's constant personality in writing [4][6].
- **Tone:** how the voice adapts to the reader's situation [4][6].
- **Tone scales:** formal or casual, serious or funny, respectful or irreverent, matter-of-fact or enthusiastic [2].
- **Tagline:** a short line used with the brand name to state its promise or attitude.
- **Puffery:** obvious exaggeration that is not taken literally; allowed in the UK if not materially misleading [21].
- **Substantiation:** the evidence needed before a claim is made [20][21].
- **Five-second test:** a quick test of what people take from a line or page at a glance [24].

- **CAP Code and ASA rulings** on claims and comparisons [21]; FTC guidance [20].
- **Domain data rules and tools:** ICANN's RDAP pages [13].
- **Public voice guides** used as examples (Mailchimp, Monzo, Microsoft, GOV.UK), which are revised without notice [4][5][6][7].

- **Testing and writing tools,** their free plans and AI features: check vendors' own pages.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock AI phrasing.** Positioning lines, values and taglines built on "Unlock", "Elevate", "Seamless" or "Game-changer". They mark the work as generic; use the words buyers use about the real problem.
- **Rule-of-three values.** Three brand values or pillars as single abstract words ("Innovation, Integrity, Impact"). Any brand could own them; name the few behaviours that make this brand different.
- **Headline formula taglines.** Taglines like "X, reimagined" or "The future of X". They fit any category; write a line tied to the brand's provable difference.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** Positioning on "quality", "service" or "trust" that every rival also claims. Position on the real alternative and a difference the client can prove.
- **Every territory in one tone.** Strategic routes that differ only in wording or style label and share one voice. Make routes differ on mechanism, proof, audience or offer, and say how each departs from the category default.
