# Name generation and screening

A name has to be said on the phone, typed after hearing it, found in search, registered and defended. Clever is optional; workable is not. This skill generates widely and then screens hard, so the client chooses from names that can survive.

## When to use

- A new business, product, plan or range needs a name.
- The client has a list of favourites and wants them checked.
- A rename is being considered.
- A range has grown and needs naming rules (also see the product-naming-system prompt).

Not for: trade mark clearance or filing (a trade mark attorney does that), logo design (see the brand identity designer kit).

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the brief, the positioning and rivals' names, and to run free searches. Level 2 to write the long list, shortlist and screen in the client's docs. No step here files, registers or buys anything: filing a trade mark, registering a company name, buying a domain or claiming a handle needs a fresh yes every time, and the client pays.

- The positioning and one-liner (or run the positioning-and-messaging skill first).
- What is being named and where the name must work: signs, packaging, app stores, phone calls, voice assistants, other languages.
- Rivals' names and names the client loves or hates.
- Words, sounds or meanings that are off limits.
- Countries where the business trades now and soon.

## Steps

1. **Write the naming brief.** One page: what is named, for whom, the positioning in one line, the feeling the name should carry, where it must work, languages, length limits, rivals, words to avoid, and the kinds of name to try.
2. **Generate widely across four kinds.** Functional (says what it does), invented (a new word), experiential (tied to something real in daily life) and evocative (suggests the positioning). Also try founder or place names, compounds and metaphors if they fit the brief. Aim for a long list big enough that cutting hurts.
3. **First cut.** Drop names that are hard to say, hard to spell after hearing, obviously descriptive and generic, close to a rival, or carrying an unwanted meaning.
4. **Say-it, hear-it, spell-it test.** Put each survivor in a spoken sentence ("Hi, I'm calling from ___"). Could someone type it after hearing it once? Note likely misspellings.
5. **Meaning check.** Check each shortlisted name in every language the client sells in, ideally with a native speaker. Note slang, unfortunate sounds and existing brands.
6. **Strength check.** Place each name on the trade mark strength scale: fanciful (invented) and arbitrary are strongest, suggestive next, descriptive weak, generic not registrable. Say what that means for protection.
7. **First-look searches (level 1).** Search the official trade mark registers for the countries that matter (US, UK, EU and WIPO tools listed in the brand identity designer kit), the company register (Companies House name checker in the UK; the state business register in the US), domain lookups (registrar search or ICANN Lookup) and the social handles needed. Record what was searched, when and what came up. This is not clearance.
8. **Write the shortlist.** For each name: meaning, the story it tells, how it sounds, spelling risk, language notes, rival risk, strength, search notes, and checks still to run.
9. **Recommend one.** Say which I would pick, why, and what would change my mind. Name the runner-up.
10. **List the next steps** with who does them: trade mark attorney clearance in each country, registrations (entity name, DBA where required, trade mark, domain) and handles. In the UK, remember a company name gives no trade mark rights; in the US, the entity name, DBA, trademark and domain are four separate things.

## Checks

- Every shortlisted name passed say-it, hear-it, spell-it.
- Languages checked for every market the client sells in.
- No shortlisted name is close to a known rival's in the same field.
- Strength is stated honestly, including for descriptive names.
- Searches are logged with date and result, and labelled "first look, not clearance".
- A recommendation with reasons, not just a list.
- Nothing filed, registered or bought without the client's fresh yes.

## Output format

1. Naming brief.
2. Long list grouped by kind (names only).
3. Shortlist table: name, kind, meaning and story, say-aloud result, spelling risk, language notes, rival risk, strength, search notes, checks to run.
4. Recommendation and runner-up, with reasons.
5. Next steps with owners: attorney clearance, registrations, domains, handles.

## Example of good output (short)

*Kōwhai Kitchen is a made-up fresh dog food delivery business in Auckland, used only to show the format. Search notes are examples of the format, not real results.*

**Brief (extract):** warm, honest, a bit playful; must work on a van, on a phone call and as an app name; English and te reo Māori considered; avoid "paws" and "pet" (every rival uses them).

| Name | Kind | Story | Say-aloud | Spelling risk | Strength | Checks to run |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fettle | Evocative | "In fine fettle": healthy, lively | Clear on a call | Low ("Fettel") | Arbitrary for dog food: strong | Trade mark search NZ and AU; domain; handles |
| Bowlful | Functional and playful | A full, fresh bowl | Clear | Low | Suggestive: medium | Rival check: similar names in pet food? |
| Kai Hound | Compound | Kai (food, te reo Māori) plus hound | Clear in NZ; may puzzle overseas | Medium | Suggestive | Check use of te reo with a Māori language adviser |

**I'd pick Fettle:** short, warm, easy to say and spell, and not descriptive, so it should be easier to protect. Runner-up: Bowlful, clearer but weaker to protect.

**Next steps:** trade mark attorney to clear Fettle in NZ and Australia before any spend; then company name, domain and handles, each with the owner's go-ahead.
