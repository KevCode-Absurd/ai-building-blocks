# Positioning and messaging

Positioning decides what the business is, for whom, and why it wins against what buyers would otherwise do. Messaging turns that into words everyone can use. Get these right first; names, taglines and voice all depend on them.

## When to use

- A new business or product needs its position before naming or launch.
- Buyers, staff or the website describe the business in different ways.
- The message sounds like every rival's.
- Before a name, tagline or voice project, as the brief for it.

Not for: visual identity (see the brand identity designer kit) or ad copy for one campaign (a copywriter's job, using this as the brief).

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the site, documents, reviews and sales notes. Level 2 to write the positioning and messaging documents as drafts in the client's docs or wiki. Level 3 to publish the approved version to the team space and update approved page copy on the pages the client names. A public change of name or tagline needs a fresh yes every time.

- What the business sells, prices and who buys today.
- Customer evidence: recent reviews, five to ten interview notes or call transcripts recorded with consent, survey answers, reasons for lost deals.
- Rivals: names, sites, the lines they use.
- Facts the business can prove: results, numbers, certificates, awards, guarantees.
- Constraints: claims the business may not make, regulated areas.

## Steps

1. **Collect what buyers say.** From reviews and interviews, pull exact lines about what they used before, what else they considered, what nearly stopped them and why they chose. Tag each line with its source.
2. **Name the competitive alternatives.** Usually a few: a bigger rival, a cheaper rival, doing it in-house, a spreadsheet, or doing nothing.
3. **List differences against those alternatives.** Only things the business truly has and can prove. Drop anything a rival also offers.
4. **Turn each difference into value.** "Because we [difference], buyers [gain]." Use the buyers' words where possible.
5. **Find who cares most.** Describe the best-fit buyers by situation ("owners of two to five cafes who do their own rotas"), not just by demographics.
6. **Choose the frame.** The market category in which that value is obvious. If the frame needs a long explanation, try another.
7. **Write the positioning statement** for internal use: for [best-fit buyers] who [situation], [brand] is the [frame] that [main value], unlike [main alternative], because [proof].
8. **Write the one-liner** for public use: what it is, who it's for, the main gain, in plain words. Test it on someone who has never heard of the business.
9. **Build the messaging hierarchy.** One core message; three or so supporting messages in the order buyers care; proof for each; the top doubts with answers; a short version for each audience (buyers, users, partners, hires).
10. **List category entry points.** When, where, why and with whom buyers start looking for this kind of product, so marketing can link the brand to those moments.
11. **Check claims.** Every message must be backed by evidence the client holds before it is published (FTC in the US; CAP Code rule 3.7 in the UK). Unqualified "best" or "number one" is a comparison with every rival and needs proof. Environmental words need explaining and evidence, and checking against current UK and EU rules.
12. **Recommend and explain.** Say which frame and core message I'd choose and why, and what would change my mind.

## Checks

- Every difference is something a rival cannot claim, and each has proof.
- The real alternative is named, not only rivals.
- The one-liner passes a stranger test: they can say what it is and for whom.
- Every message in the hierarchy has a proof point, or is marked "needs proof" and kept out of public copy.
- No banned or risky claims: unproven superlatives, vague green words, invented numbers.
- The best-fit buyer is described by situation.

## Output format

1. Evidence summary: top buyer quotes by theme, with sources.
2. Alternatives and differences table: alternative, our difference, value, proof.
3. Positioning statement (internal).
4. One-liner (public), with two alternatives and why I chose the first.
5. Messaging hierarchy table: message, proof, doubt it answers, audience versions.
6. Category entry points list.
7. Claim check notes and anything needing proof.

## Example of good output (short)

*Rotawise is a made-up rota app for small cafe groups, used only to show the format. Quotes are invented examples.*

**Alternatives:** a shared spreadsheet; a big workforce app built for chains; the owner doing rotas by text on Sunday nights.

| Alternative | Our difference | Value | Proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spreadsheet | Staff swap shifts in the app, owner approves with one tap | Sunday nights back | Interview C3: "I used to spend two hours every Sunday" |
| Big workforce app | Set up for two to five sites in an afternoon, no training | Live this week, not next quarter | Average set-up time from onboarding logs [NEED: export] |

**Positioning statement:** For owners of two to five cafes who build rotas themselves, Rotawise is the rota app made for small groups that lets staff swap shifts without endless texts, unlike spreadsheets and chain-sized software, because swaps and approvals happen in one place.

**One-liner:** Rotas and shift swaps for small cafe groups, sorted on your phone.

**Why this frame:** "rota app for small cafe groups" is instantly clear; "workforce management" invites comparison with chain-sized software on features we don't need to win.

**Needs proof before use:** set-up time claim; any "most popular" line (no evidence held).
