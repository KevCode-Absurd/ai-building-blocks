# Account plan and MEDDICC review

A good account plan says who to talk to, about what, and what you still do not know. A MEDDICC review says whether a live deal is real. This skill does both from facts, and turns every gap into the next question.

## When to use

- Choosing which companies, councils, schools or property managers to pursue.
- Before a first meeting or site visit with a target account.
- Each month for the top accounts, to update the plan.
- When a deal has gone quiet, slipped a close date, or the forecast feels like hope.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the CRM, past deals and the pages and notes the client shares. Level 2 to create draft accounts, plans, notes and tasks in the CRM. Level 3 to update accounts, deal stages, labels and next steps, and log activity, within the client's limits.

- The client's best current contracts, to write the rules for a good account.
- A list of candidate accounts, or the market and area to search.
- Public pages for each account: website, published contract notices, annual reports, news.
- Notes from any calls, meetings or emails, as written.
- For deal reviews: the deal record, the people involved and what each has said.

## Steps

### Rules and list

1. **Write the rules for a good account.** From the best existing contracts: size, sites, contract length, location, how they buy (quotes, tender, framework, direct award), who signs, and red flags. If there are no good contracts yet, mark the rules as a first guess.
2. **Score and tier.** Apply the rules to each candidate. Top tier gets a full plan; the next tier gets a brief and lighter outreach; the rest wait.

### Account brief and plan

3. **Research.** From the pages and notes supplied, and public registers such as Companies House for UK companies. Three columns: fact (with source and date), what it might mean, and guesses (marked). Never guess personal details about anyone.
4. **Map the people.** Who buys, who uses, who checks (procurement, health and safety, IT), who signs. What each one cares about. Mark unknown roles as unknown.
5. **Name the problems we could solve.** In the buyer's terms, with the evidence for each.
6. **Choose the way in.** A small job, a trial site, a framework or approved list, a partner who already works there, or a published tender. Say why.
7. **Set the next three actions.** Each with an owner and a date. [L3] Create them as CRM tasks.
8. **List the unknowns.** And the question that would answer each.

### MEDDICC review for a live deal

9. **Score each letter on evidence.** For each, write what we know, the evidence (who said it, when), and the next question.
   - **Metrics:** what the buyer would measure, in their numbers.
   - **Economic buyer:** who controls the money and signs. Have we met them?
   - **Decision criteria:** what they will judge on, written down if possible.
   - **Decision process:** the steps, people and dates to a decision.
   - **Paper process:** supplier set-up, insurance checks, purchase orders, contract review, signature.
   - **Identify pain:** the problem in their words and what it costs them.
   - **Champion:** who inside wants this and acts on it. A friendly contact is not a champion until they act.
   - **Competition:** the incumbent, other bidders, doing it in-house, or doing nothing.
10. **Call the deal.** Qualified, at risk (name the letters), or not qualified. Never fill a letter with a guess to make the deal look healthy.
11. **Set the next actions.** The questions for the weakest letters become the next meeting's agenda. [L3] Update the deal's fields, labels and next step in the CRM. In HubSpot, teams often require key fields before a stage, and may use association labels (Professional or Enterprise) to mark the champion and economic buyer on the deal.

## Checks

- Every fact has a source and a date; guesses are labelled.
- No personal details beyond the person's work role.
- Each MEDDICC letter shows evidence or says "unknown".
- The way in is realistic for the firm's size and paperwork.
- Next actions have owners and dates.
- Nothing in the plan suggests sharing prices or bids with a competitor, or offering gifts to a public buyer.

## Output format

1. Rules for a good account and the tiered list.
2. Account brief (one page per account): what they do, sites, people, problems, sources, unknowns.
3. Account plan: brief plus way in, next three actions, MEDDICC table.
4. Deal review: MEDDICC table (letter, what we know, evidence, next question), the call, next actions.

## Example of good output

*Made-up business for illustration: Brindlecote Cleaning, reviewing a deal with a made-up property management firm, Quarrow Estates.*

| Letter | What we know | Evidence | Next question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metrics | Complaints about common areas; count not known | Facilities manager, site walk, 3 Nov | "How many cleaning complaints did tenants log last quarter?" |
| Economic buyer | Unknown; FM says "head office signs" | FM, 3 Nov | "Who at head office approves contracts of this size?" |
| Decision criteria | Price, response to complaints, staff vetting | FM email, 4 Nov | "Which matters most if two quotes are close?" |
| Decision process | FM shortlists, head office decides; date unknown | FM, 3 Nov | "When does the current contract end?" |
| Paper process | Supplier form and insurance check; unknown after that | Supplier form received | "Who reviews the contract, and how long does it take?" |
| Pain | Tenants complain about stairwells at two blocks | FM, site walk | "What happens when complaints rise?" |
| Champion | FM is friendly but has not introduced us to head office | n/a | "Would you bring head office to the next meeting?" |
| Competition | Incumbent on a rolling contract | FM, 3 Nov | "What would make you change supplier now?" |

**Call:** at risk. No economic buyer, no champion action yet, end date unknown. **Next action:** ask the FM to introduce head office before quoting (owner: Dev, by 14 Nov).
