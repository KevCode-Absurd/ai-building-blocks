# Interview prep from your stories

## When to use

- An interview is booked and the person wants to be ready.
- The person freezes on "Tell me about yourself" or "Why should we hire you?"
- A competency, values-based or public sector interview is coming, scored against criteria.
- The person has been invited to an AI-led or recorded video screening.
- The employer has set a presentation task.

Do not use it to invent experience, to coach answers that mislead, or to take part in a live interview for the person. This is general career advice. If the person is worried an interview question is unlawful, check this with local rules and ask a qualified employment lawyer or official helpline.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the invite, the job ad, the CV and the person's notes and stories.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** create the prep sheet and mock script in the person's documents; add tentative calendar holds for practice sessions.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** accept the interview slot inside the times the person allows, and send approved replies about logistics to the named recruiter. Anything about pay, and any message to a new contact, needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. The job ad and any assessment criteria, competency framework or values the employer publishes.
2. The interview details: format (panel, video, phone, AI-led), length, who is on the panel, any task.
3. The person's CV and a list of stories: things they did, fixed, built, changed or learned.
4. The questions they fear, and anything they want to explain (a gap, a dismissal, a change of field).
5. The employer's rules on AI use, if any.

## Steps

### 1. Find the criteria

From the ad and the employer's own pages, list what will be scored: skills, behaviours, values. Public sector and large employers often publish frameworks; use their headings.

### 2. Predict the questions

At least twelve, grouped:
- Core skills from the ad, as "Tell me about a time…" (behavioural) and "What would you do if…" (hypothetical).
- Values and culture.
- Motivation: "Why this role?", "Why us?", "Why now?"
- The person's story: "Tell me about yourself", "Walk me through your CV".
- Tough spots: the gap, the change of field, a weakness, a failure.

### 3. Build the story bank

Ask the person for real examples. For each, write it as STAR: one or two lines of situation and task, most of the words on what they personally did, and a result with a number or a change, plus a line on what they learned. Under 250 words each. Tag each story with the criteria it proves. If no story fits a question, write a prompt to help them find one ("Think of a time a deadline moved. What did you do first?"), never an invented answer.

### 4. Match stories to questions

A table: question, story to use, the criterion it proves. Use each strong story for no more than two or three questions, so the person has variety.

### 5. Write the openers

- A 60-second "Tell me about yourself": present role, two proofs, why this job.
- "Why us?": three specific reasons from the employer's own work or values.

### 6. Prepare the worry answer

Honest, brief, then forward: what happened in one sentence, what the person did or learned, why they are ready now. No blame, no over-explaining.

### 7. Questions to ask them

Five questions about the work, the team, success in the first six months and next steps. None that a quick look at the website answers.

### 8. Format notes

- Panel: speak to everyone, not just the person who asked.
- Video: test camera and sound; notes off-screen.
- AI-led screening: treat it like a structured panel. On LinkedIn's version (as of November 2026), answers are recorded and rated on how closely they match the hirer's ideal answers, their supporting detail and their clarity, and the hirer decides; use the practice option if offered.
- Presentation: a timed talk with speaker notes and likely questions.

### 9. Mock interview script

A script a friend can read: the questions in order, one follow-up for each ("What exactly did you do?", "What would you do differently?"), and what a strong answer includes.

## Checks

- Every story is the person's own and every number is theirs.
- Each answer is under 250 words and ends with a result.
- Each criterion has at least one story.
- The worry answer is honest and short.
- The employer's AI rules are followed in preparation and in the interview.
- The output carries the line: *General career advice, not legal advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser if a question may be unlawful.*

## Output format

1. Criteria list.
2. Question and story map.
3. Story bank in STAR form.
4. Openers and the worry answer.
5. Questions to ask.
6. Format notes.
7. Mock interview script.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real person: a nurse going for a unit manager role, worried about questions on managing a poor performer. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Question:** "Tell me about a time you managed someone who was not meeting the standard."
> **Story (STAR):** On a 24-bed ward, a newly qualified nurse was missing medication rounds on night shifts (S). As shift lead I had to raise it and protect patients (T). I met her privately, asked what was happening, learned she was struggling with the drug chart system, and set up two buddy shifts and a weekly ten-minute check-in for a month (A). Missed rounds stopped within three weeks, and she later trained two new starters on the system (R). I learned to ask before I assume (learning).
> **Proves:** people management; patient safety.
>
> **Mock follow-up:** "What would you have done if nothing changed after a month?"
