# LinkedIn profile and bios

## When to use

- The person is job hunting and their profile does not match the roles they want.
- A consultant or freelancer sounds like everyone else.
- A speaker needs bios and an introduction the host can read.
- Someone is changing direction and needs a new story.
- A freelancer needs a profile for a marketplace such as Upwork or Contra.

Do not use it to invent titles, clients, credentials, endorsements or recommendations, or to post or message from the person's account. This is general career advice: check this with local rules, and ask a qualified adviser before naming clients covered by a confidentiality agreement.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the current profile (a link or PDF export), CV, website and past bios.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** write the exact text for each section in a document, ready to paste, and save bio files.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** update the person's website bio or speaker pages in tools they have connected, and send bios to named event organisers they approved. Their LinkedIn profile is changed by them, from their own account: I do not sign in to it.

## What you need

1. The current profile and CV.
2. Who the person wants to reach: recruiters for a role, or clients of a certain kind.
3. Results they can share, and clients or employers they may name.
4. Topics they want to be known for.
5. Whether the search is private from their current employer.
6. Examples of their own writing (posts, emails) to catch their voice.

## Steps

### 1. Find the line that sets them apart

Who they help, with what, and one proof. If no proof is in the brief, leave [add a result] and ask for one.

### 2. Write three headline options

Plain words, the role or service, who it is for, and a proof or specialty. Keep within the limit LinkedIn's editor shows. No buzzwords.

### 3. Write the About section

- Open with the people they help and the problem they solve.
- Then what the person does and how.
- Then proof: two or three results.
- Then how to get in touch.
- Short paragraphs that read well on a phone, in first person.

### 4. Rewrite experience entries

Two to four bullets per recent role, each a result. Tag skills to the jobs and projects that show them, since LinkedIn's old skill quizzes have been withdrawn and skills now sit on evidence.

### 5. Plan the featured section

Two to four items that prove the headline: a case study, a talk, an article, a portfolio piece.

### 6. Set privacy and status

- Open to Work: explain the three options (everyone with the photo frame, recruiters only, or only the person) and that recruiters-only cannot guarantee privacy from the current employer. The person chooses.
- Career break: if relevant, suggest adding it as its own section with one honest line.

### 7. Write bios

About 25, 75 and 150 words, in first and third person if needed. Same facts, different lengths. The 25-word version names the role, who they help and one proof.

### 8. Speaker sheet or marketplace profile (if asked)

- Speaker: talk titles, a one-line promise for each, and a 30-second introduction the host can read aloud.
- Marketplace: headline, overview, portfolio blurbs and the services list, following that platform's own rules.

### 9. Check the voice

Read it against the person's own writing. Cut anything they would not say.

## Checks

- Every title, client, date and number comes from the person.
- Named clients are ones the person may name.
- No buzzwords without proof ("guru", "results-driven", "thought leader").
- Privacy choices explained, not assumed.
- The person pastes and publishes the LinkedIn text themselves.
- The output carries the line: *General career advice, not legal advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser before naming clients or past employers.*

## Output format

1. The line that sets them apart.
2. Three headline options.
3. About section.
4. Experience bullets by role.
5. Featured section plan.
6. Privacy and status notes.
7. Bios at three lengths (and person).
8. Speaker sheet or marketplace profile, if asked.
9. Questions for the person.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real person: an HR consultant who sets up HR basics for small charities. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Headline options:**
> 1. HR consultant for small charities | HR basics set up in 30 charities
> 2. I set up HR for small charities: policies, contracts and training your team will use
> 3. Freelance HR for charities with under 50 staff | 15 years in HR
>
> **About (opening):** "Small charities rarely have an HR person, but they still hire, manage and sometimes let people go. I help them get the basics right: contracts, policies and the training to use them. In five years I've set up HR for 30 charities."
>
> **25-word bio (third person):** "Rhiannon Vell is an HR consultant for small charities. She has set up HR basics for 30 charities and spent 15 years in HR."
>
> *General career advice, not legal advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser before naming clients or past employers.*
