# Offer and pay talk

## When to use

- An offer has arrived and the person wants to ask for more, or for something other than salary.
- An application or interview asks for salary expectations.
- The person wants a pay rise or promotion and needs a case and a script.
- Two offers are on the table and the person wants to compare them fairly.

Do not use it to invent rival offers, to pressure anyone, or to judge whether a contract term is lawful. This is general career advice: check this with local rules, and ask a qualified employment lawyer before signing a contract with terms you are unsure about.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the offer email or letter, the job ad, and the person's notes.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** create the pay brief and script, and save the follow-up email in the person's drafts.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** update the job tracker. Every message about pay, and accepting or declining an offer, needs the person's fresh yes each time, even at level 3, because it commits them.

## What you need

1. The offer in writing, or what was said, with the date: pay, bonus, leave, pension or retirement plan, hours, place of work, start date, notice, probation, any conditions.
2. The pay range in the job ad, if one was published.
3. Where the job is (country, state, city) and whether it is remote.
4. Public pay data for the role and place, if the person has looked (or we look together).
5. The person's alternatives: staying where they are, other processes and how far along they are, and what they would do with no deal.
6. What matters most to the person beyond salary: flexibility, start date, title, training, leave.

## Steps

### 1. Get it in writing

If the offer was verbal, the first step is a short email thanking them and asking for the written offer. Do not accept on the call.

### 2. Read the whole offer

List every element of total reward. Mark anything unclear or unusual (a long notice period, a clawback, a non-compete) as [ASK AN ADVISER] rather than explaining what it means legally.

### 3. Gather the facts

- The published range for this job, if any. Where pay range laws apply, the employer's own range is the strongest anchor.
- Public pay data for the role and place, with the date and source.
- The person's evidence of value: results, skills in short supply, the size of the job compared with the ad.

### 4. Set the numbers with the person

The person chooses three numbers: a target (ambitious but defensible from the facts), an acceptable figure, and a walk-away point tied to their real alternative. Write down the alternative in one line. If it is weak, they keep it private.

### 5. Pick the asks

Salary first if it matters most; then one or two others from the list the person cares about (start date, flexibility, title, training budget, leave). Fewer asks, clearly reasoned, land better than a long list.

### 6. Write the script

Short lines the person can say aloud:
1. Thanks and enthusiasm for the role.
2. The ask: one number or a narrow figure near the target.
3. The reason: one or two facts (the range, the scope, a result).
4. A pause.
5. If they say no: ask what is possible ("Is there flexibility on the start salary or a review at six months?"), or move to another ask.
6. Close: thank them, confirm next steps, and ask for any change in writing.

### 7. Prepare for likely replies

"That's the top of the band", "We don't negotiate", "What are you on now?" (where pay history questions are not allowed, the person can decline politely; elsewhere they can choose to answer with their expectations instead), "We need an answer today".

### 8. Follow-up email

A short email confirming what was agreed, or restating the ask after the call.

### 9. For a pay rise

Build the case from results since the last review, the size of the job now, and market data. Script it the same way, ask for a meeting rather than raising it in passing, and plan a follow-up date.

## Checks

- Every number comes from the offer, a dated source or the person.
- No invented or exaggerated rival offer.
- Legal terms marked for an adviser, not explained as settled.
- The person approves every message about pay before it goes.
- The output carries the line: *General career advice, not legal or tax advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified employment lawyer about any contract term.*

## Output format

1. Offer summary table: element, offered, notes.
2. Facts: range, pay data (with dates), the person's evidence.
3. Target, acceptable and walk-away figures, and the alternative.
4. The asks, in order.
5. Call script and replies to likely pushback.
6. Follow-up email draft.
7. Questions for an adviser.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up person for illustration: an offer for a marketing coordinator job at 58,000 a year; the published range was 55,000 to 64,000.

> **Ask:** 62,000, or 60,000 with a pay review at six months.
> **Reason:** "The ad's range goes to 64,000, and I'll be running the email programme on my own from day one, which I did at my current job, where I grew our list by 40% in a year."
>
> **Script:** "Thank you, I'm excited about this role. Based on the range in the ad and the email work I'll own from day one, I was hoping for 62,000. Is there room to get closer to that?" Then stop and listen.
>
> **If they say no:** "I understand. Would you consider 60,000 with a review at six months, against goals we agree now?"
>
> **Walk-away:** below 58,000 with no review, because staying in the current job is an acceptable alternative for now (kept private).
>
> *General career advice, not legal or tax advice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified employment lawyer about any contract term.*
