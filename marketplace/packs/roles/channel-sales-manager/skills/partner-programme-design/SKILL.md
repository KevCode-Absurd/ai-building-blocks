# Partner programme design

## When to use

- A software, service or product business wants partners to refer or resell it.
- A programme exists but most partners never sell.
- Partners argue over who gets credit for a customer.
- The owner asks "what commission can we afford?"

Not for: hiring a sales team (see the `sdr` kit) or writing the contract itself (that is the lawyer's job; this skill writes the plain summary the lawyer works from).

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** pricing, margins, CRM deals with source, current partner list and payouts.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the programme page, partner properties in the CRM, and partner groups and offers in the partner tool, none of it live.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the agreed programme page and set up the agreed partner groups. Signing agreements, any commission or discount outside the agreed range, and paying partners need the client: a fresh yes for the first two, and payments stay with the client.

## What you need

1. Product, price and how customers buy (one-off, subscription, project).
2. Margin per sale after delivery, support, refunds and payment fees.
3. Who the buyer is and who already reaches them (bookkeepers, agencies, IT firms, shops).
4. Current partners and what they were promised, if any.
5. The client's own sales team, if any, and the customers they own.
6. Countries and US states where partners will sell.

## Steps

### 1. Work out what a partner can earn

Start with margin per sale, not with a percentage. For each partner type, show the client's margin left after the partner's share over the customer's expected life, and what the partner earns per customer and per year with a realistic number of customers. If the margin cannot pay a partner enough to bother, say so and suggest a simpler deal (one-off referral fee, a services role instead of resale).

### 2. Choose partner types

Partners who already reach the buyer and do not compete with the client. For each: what they do (refer, resell, implement), why they would want to, and what they need from the client (training, materials, a demo account).

### 3. Design the deal for each type

- **Referral:** who pays whom, how much, for how long, and when (on payment received, not on signature). Real examples to compare with: HubSpot pays 20% for 3 years on partner-sourced deals (see `knowledge.md`, section 3).
- **Reseller:** the wholesale or partner price, who bills the customer, and who supports them. The reseller sets its own resale price. Write a recommended retail price only, marked "recommended".
- **Tiers (optional):** levels earned by results or certification, with benefits that cost the client little and matter to partners (directory listing, leads, training). Xero's five levels are a public example (see `knowledge.md`, section 3).

### 4. Write the rules

- What counts as a referral: tracked link, registered deal, or introduction email, and within what time.
- Deal registration: what evidence, how long protection lasts, who wins when two partners claim one customer, and how the client's own sales team is treated.
- Refunds and clawbacks: what happens to a commission when the customer refunds or cancels.
- Market development or co-op funds, if any: qualifying activities, pre-approval, proof, claim deadline. For resellers of goods in the US, offer them to competing resellers on proportionally equal terms (see `knowledge.md`, section 3).
- Disclosure: partners who promote the client publicly label it clearly ("Ad", "I get commission") near the link.
- Prices: the client never requires a resale price, restricts discounting, or asks partners to report each other's prices. In the UK these count as resale price maintenance; in the US some states are stricter than federal law (see `knowledge.md`, section 3).
- No rewards for recruiting other partners, no buy-in and no stock quotas.

### 5. Write how the deal ends

Notice period, what happens to commissions on existing customers, return of materials and logins, and how customers are handed over. Write this section first; it is the one partners ask about.

### 6. Plan onboarding and launch

The first 30 days for a new partner (use the `partner-onboarding-and-scorecard` skill), the launch announcement, a how-to-join page and an FAQ.

### 7. Research (both modes)

With web search connected, check the current partner programmes of two or three competitors and similar vendors, with dates, and the official competition guidance pages in `sources.json`. Without it, ask the client for any competitor terms they know and mark comparisons as unchecked.

### 8. Mark the lawyer and accountant points

Every clause that becomes contract language, every price list for competing resellers, tax forms for partner payouts, and anything in a country not covered here.

## Checks

- The margin working is shown and the deal leaves the client a profit per sale.
- Every rule answers who, what, when and how much.
- How the deal ends is written.
- No term sets, restricts or monitors resellers' prices.
- Funds and allowances follow one written rule.
- No reward for recruitment, no buy-in, no stock quota.
- Disclosure rules are included.
- Lawyer and accountant points are marked; signing and payments are left to the client.

## Output format

1. Summary in five lines.
2. Margin working by partner type.
3. Partner types and why each would join.
4. The deal for each type, with tiers if any.
5. Rules: referrals, registration and conflicts, refunds and clawbacks, funds, disclosure, prices.
6. How the deal ends.
7. Onboarding and launch plan.
8. Points for the lawyer and accountant.
9. Decisions for the client.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Ledgerly, bookkeeping software at USD 50 a month, designing a programme for bookkeepers.*

> **Margin working.** Ledgerly keeps USD 41 a month per customer after hosting, support and payment fees. A referral fee of 20% for 24 months pays the bookkeeper USD 240 per customer and leaves Ledgerly USD 31 a month during those two years. Eight referred clients earn a bookkeeper USD 1,920 over the two years of fees (up to USD 960 in the first year if all eight join in January). Worth their time: yes, if onboarding takes them under an hour per client.
>
> **Conflict rule.** The first partner to register a customer with an introduction email gets 90 days of protection. If the customer signs up through Ledgerly's own website first, no partner fee applies unless the partner registered them earlier.
>
> **Prices.** Bookkeepers who resell set their own price to clients. Ledgerly publishes a recommended price of USD 50 a month and never asks partners to match it.
>
> **For the lawyer:** clawback wording, termination notice, data handover on exit.
