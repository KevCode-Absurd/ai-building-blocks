# Chief of staff: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. Tool features change often; each one says when it was true. This kit covers how a leader's office runs: goals, decisions, reviews, board and investor updates, cross-team projects and meetings. It is not legal, tax, investment or company secretarial advice. Where company law, a shareholder agreement or the numbers decide the answer, I say who to ask: the company secretary or a lawyer for board formalities, a qualified accountant for the figures.

Inbox, diary and travel are covered in the executive assistant kit (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/executive-assistant/knowledge.md`); process maps, SOPs and weekly operations reviews in the operations manager kit (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/operations-manager/knowledge.md`); project plans, estimates and change control in the project manager kit (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/project-manager/knowledge.md`); finance packs and big money decisions in the fractional CFO kit (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/fractional-cfo/knowledge.md`). This kit links to them instead of repeating them.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a chief of staff.** Founders and owner-managers of small and mid-sized firms whose own attention has become the limit. Typical signs: ten "top priorities", decisions made in a hallway and forgotten, a board or investor update written at midnight before the deadline, three teams working on the same thing without knowing it, and a diary full of meetings that end without a decision. In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into two jobs: goals and OKRs (choosing the few goals that matter, measuring them and checking in), and the leader's office (decision briefs, monthly reports, board and investor updates, strategy memos and the operating rhythm).

**What good work looks like.**
- The leader can name the few things that matter this quarter, and so can the team.
- Every big decision has one written page: the question, the real options (including doing nothing), the trade-offs, a recommendation with a confidence level, and who decides.
- Decisions are logged with the date, the decider and the reason, so nobody relitigates them by accident.
- Reviews happen on a fixed rhythm (weekly, monthly, quarterly), each with a set agenda and a short written pre-read.
- Board and investor updates go out on time, lead with what changed, and put bad news on page one.
- Cross-team projects have one owner each and one shared list of what is blocked.
- Meetings exist to decide things. Updates move to writing.

**How the work is judged.**
- Fewer, clearer priorities, and visible progress against them.
- Speed and quality of decisions: how long a decision waits, and how often it is reopened.
- No surprises for the board, investors or the team.
- Leader hours returned: fewer meetings, shorter ones, better pre-reads.
- Trust: the chief of staff is neutral on options, shows sources and never speaks for the leader beyond what the leader agreed.

**What this role never does here.** Hide bad news, invent a figure, or present a guess as a fact. Make the leader's decisions. Send anything in the leader's name outside the written limits the leader set (see `access.md`). Give legal, tax or investment advice. Tie goals to pay or performance ratings: that is a people decision for the leader and an HR adviser.

## 2. The canon

**Andy Grove and John Doerr on OKRs.** Objectives and key results come from Andy Grove at Intel, who adapted Peter Drucker's management by objectives; John Doerr took the method to Google's founders in 1999 [1]. Compared with older annual objectives, OKRs run quarterly and are kept apart from pay [1]. An objective says what you want to achieve; a key result says how you will know, and it is either met or not met [1]. Doerr's site separates committed OKRs (expected to be fully met) from aspirational ones (stretch goals whose path is not yet proven) [1].

**Google's OKR guide (re:Work, first published 2016, now archived).** Three to five objectives with about three key results each; key results describe outcomes, not activities; each key result is graded from 0.0 to 1.0, and for ambitious goals an average of 0.6 to 0.7 is the aim, because always scoring 1.0 means the goals were too safe [2]. OKRs are visible across the company, and Google says plainly that they are not employee evaluations [2].

**Amazon on decisions and memos.** Jeff Bezos's 2015 letter splits decisions into Type 1 (one-way doors: hard or impossible to reverse, so decide slowly and consult widely) and Type 2 (two-way doors: reversible, so a small group with good judgement should decide fast), and warns that growing companies wrongly use the heavy process for both [3]. His 2016 letter adds that most decisions should be made with about 70% of the information you wish you had; that a leader can ask a team to "disagree and commit"; and that real misalignment should be escalated early, not settled by whoever has more stamina [4]. His 2017 letter explains Amazon's narrative six-page memos, read in silence at the start of meetings instead of slides; a great memo can take a week or more to write and rewrite [5].

**Netflix, informed captains.** Netflix names one "informed captain" for each significant decision, who seeks out dissent, weighs views by expertise, then decides; everyone then commits, including those who disagreed [6]. Managers give context rather than control, but step in on unethical or seriously harmful choices [6].

**Decision roles: DACI and RAPID.** Atlassian's DACI play names a Driver who runs the process, one Approver, Contributors who have a voice but no vote, and people who are Informed after the decision; the decision document holds the background, data, decision factors, options with pros, cons and costs, actions and the outcome [7]. Bain's RAPID uses Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input and Decide, with one recommender and ideally one decider; "Agree" is kept for hard constraints such as legal rules, and Bain advises starting with high-value or frequent decisions rather than labelling everything [8].

**Decision records.** Software teams keep architecture decision records: one short record per decision with its reasons, trade-offs and consequences, and the collection forms a decision log; Michael Nygard's 2011 post made the practice popular, and its use has spread beyond software [9]. A chief of staff's decision log is the same idea for the whole business.

**Basecamp, Shape Up.** Basecamp works in six-week cycles followed by a two-week cool-down. A short "betting table" meeting of the CEO, CTO, a senior programmer and a product strategist picks the next cycle's work from a few shaped pitches; there is no backlog, and a project unfinished at the end of its cycle does not get more time by default [10]. The lesson for any small firm: a fixed cycle, a short list of bets, and a rule that stops runaway work.

**EOS and Scaling Up.** Two operating systems many owner-run firms use. EOS (Gino Wickman, *Traction*) runs a 90-minute weekly leadership meeting with a fixed agenda: a short check-in, the scorecard, a review of 90-day priorities called Rocks, headlines, last week's to-dos, then an hour to identify, discuss and solve issues, ending with a meeting rating [11]. Scaling Up (Verne Harnish) frames the business around four decisions (people, strategy, execution, cash) and one-page tools such as the One-Page Strategic Plan [12].

**Meetings.** Steven Rogelberg, *The Surprising Science of Meetings* (2019) and *Glad We Met* (on one-on-ones): meetings can be made more efficient and inclusive with research, and you control the meetings you run [13]. Paul Graham's "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (2009) explains why a meeting in the middle of a focus block costs far more than its length; the executive assistant kit covers it.

**Runway as a standing question.** Paul Graham's "Default Alive or Default Dead?" (2015): if costs stay flat and revenue grows at its recent rate, does the company reach profit on the cash it has? Ask it early, and keep a written plan B [14]. A chief of staff puts this line in every monthly report of a loss-making firm.

**Reading list (credited, no claims drawn from them here).** Andy Grove, *High Output Management* (1983). John Doerr, *Measure What Matters* (2018). Gino Wickman, *Traction* (2007). Verne Harnish, *Scaling Up* (2014). Chip and Dan Heath, *Decisive* (2013). Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets* (2018). Gary Klein's work on the pre-mortem. Brad Feld and Mahendra Ramsinghani, *Startup Boards*.

## 3. How the work is done now

### The operating rhythm (as of Nov 2026)
Most small firms that run well use a short set of fixed reviews, each with an owner, a pre-read and an output:
- **Weekly leadership meeting.** Scorecard, priorities on or off track, issues to solve, actions. EOS's fixed 90-minute format is one model [11]; many teams run a shorter version. Output: decisions and actions.
- **Monthly business review.** What changed, the numbers against plan, cash and runway [14], what we learned, decisions needed. Output: a two-page report and, where there are investors, the monthly or quarterly update.
- **Quarterly review and planning.** Score last quarter's OKRs honestly [2], keep, change or drop each goal, set the next few. Output: next quarter's OKRs, a "not this quarter" list, and the board pack if the board meets quarterly.
- **Annual plan.** The year's few goals and the budget, set before the first quarter's OKRs.

Basecamp's six-plus-two-week cycle shows the rhythm need not follow calendar quarters [10]. Pick one and keep it.

### Decisions
- Sort every decision first: one-way door or two-way door [3]. Two-way doors get a fast owner and a short note; one-way doors get a full brief and time to consult.
- Name roles on the brief: one decider, the people who give input, and any party with a veto over legal or financial limits [7][8].
- Decide with enough, not all, of the information [4]. State what would change the recommendation.
- After the decision: log it (date, decider, options considered, reason, review date) [9], tell the people who need to know [7], and turn it into actions with owners.

### Goals and OKRs
- Draft from the year plan and last quarter's numbers. Every key result needs a starting value, a target, an owner and a source for the number.
- Keep tasks out of key results: "launch the new pricing page" is a task; "trial-to-paid rate from 8% to 11%" is a result.
- Mark each OKR committed or aspirational, because they are scored differently [1].
- Keep OKRs out of pay and ratings [1][2].
- Tools change: Microsoft retired its Viva Goals OKR product on 31 December 2025 [16]. Keep OKRs in a format you can export (a sheet, a doc or a database), whatever tool shows them.

### Board packs and investor updates
- A board pack is a written pre-read, sent ahead of the meeting: the leader's letter (what changed, what worries us), the numbers against plan, decisions the board must take, risks, and appendices. Amazon's practice of a narrative memo read before discussion is a useful model even for a two-page pack [5].
- An investor update is shorter and regular (monthly or quarterly): highlights, lowlights, the key numbers, what we learned, and specific asks.
- Formal minutes, resolutions and filings follow company law and the company's own articles and agreements. UK companies must keep records of shareholder votes and resolutions, among others [25]. The UK Corporate Governance Code 2024 applies to companies in the commercial companies and closed-ended funds categories from financial years starting 1 January 2025; private firms sit outside it, though large private companies must report on governance and may use the Wates Principles [24]. In the US and EU, minute and resolution rules come from state or national company law. Check this with local rules and the company secretary or lawyer.

### Meetings
Microsoft's June 2025 data from Microsoft 365 users (to 15 February 2025, EU tenants excluded): 57% of meetings were ad hoc with no calendar invite; meetings starting after 8 pm were up 16% year on year; half of all meetings fell between 9-11 am and 1-3 pm; and PowerPoint edits jumped 122% in the last ten minutes before a meeting [15]. That last figure is the case for pre-reads written a day ahead.

### Working in the leader's name (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Email.** A Gmail delegate can read, send and delete, and mail they send shows the delegate's own address [17]. In Outlook, Author and Editor delegates can send on behalf; "send as", which looks like the leader wrote it, can only be set by an admin [18].
- **AI connectors.** Claude's Gmail, Calendar and Drive connectors ask for approval before sending, replying, forwarding, sharing, moving or trashing by default; on Team and Enterprise plans the owner decides whether members may switch that off [19].
- **Chat.** Slack's own MCP server can search, read, draft, schedule and send messages and edit canvases, acting through the connected user's permissions, with admin approval and audit logs [20]. A message sent through the leader's connection reads as the leader's.
- **Rule.** Any message that goes out in the leader's name, or reads as the leader's view, needs a fresh yes unless it falls inside written limits the leader set (which channels, which kinds of message, which people). See `access.md`.

### Cross-team projects
One owner per project, one shared tracker, one weekly line per project: on track, at risk or off track, and what is blocked. Asana, Notion and Atlassian (Jira, Confluence and Goals) publish their own MCP servers, which can read and update tasks, pages and goals within the user's existing permissions [21][22][23]. The project manager kit covers plans, estimates and change control.

## 5. Decision rules

- If there are more priorities than the team can name from memory, cut to the few that move the year most and park the rest on a "not this quarter" list, because a long list spreads effort thin.
- If a key result has no baseline, ask for one before accepting a target; if none exists, make the first key result "measure the baseline".
- If a key result is a task, rewrite it as the change the task should cause.
- If an OKR could affect someone's pay or rating, take it out of the OKR set and send it to the leader, because Google and Doerr both keep them apart [1][2].
- If a decision is a two-way door, name an owner and a date and move on; if it is a one-way door, write the full brief and consult [3].
- If a brief has only one real option, add "do nothing" and at least one alternative before recommending.
- If two people think they decide, write down one decider before any more debate [6][7][8].
- If a disagreement has run for more than one meeting, escalate it with both views written side by side [4].
- If a decision has been made, log it the same day with the reason and a review date [9].
- If a meeting has no question to answer, replace it with a written update.
- If a board or investor update contains bad news, put it on page one with the cause and the plan, because finding it later makes every other number doubtful.
- If a figure comes from a guess, label it as an estimate, and name the source of every other figure.
- If the company is loss-making, show cash, monthly burn and whether it is default alive in every monthly report [14].
- If a message would go out in the leader's name and is not inside the written limits, draft it and ask.
- If a question touches minutes, resolutions, filings, tax or securities rules, check this with local rules and ask the company secretary, a lawyer or a qualified accountant.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Ten priorities.** Spot it: nobody can list them without looking.
- **Key results that are tasks.** Spot it: verbs like launch, build, hire, with no number that moves.
- **Targets with no baseline.** Spot it: "grow revenue 30%" with no starting figure or date.
- **Sandbagged OKRs.** Spot it: every key result scores 1.0 every quarter [2].
- **Decisions that never die.** Spot it: the same topic on three agendas in a row with no logged decision.
- **Two deciders.** Spot it: "we all agreed" and then two people act differently.
- **Briefs that argue for one answer.** Spot it: one option gets a page, the others a line.
- **Last-minute packs.** Spot it: slides edited in the ten minutes before the board meets [15].
- **Bad news on page six.** Spot it: the cash line appears only in an appendix.
- **Status meetings.** Spot it: people read their updates aloud.
- **Speaking for the leader.** Spot it: a team quotes "the CEO wants" for something the CEO never said.
- **Tool lock-in.** Spot it: goals live only in a tool that is about to change or close [16].

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything goes to the leader, I check:
1. Is the question, decision or message stated in one sentence at the top?
2. Does every option get the same space, including doing nothing?
3. Is every number traced to its source, and every estimate labelled?
4. Is bad news on page one?
5. Is there one decider, one owner per action and one date per action?
6. Do key results have a baseline, a target, an owner and a data source?
7. Is it short enough to read and act on in one sitting?
8. Is anything going out in the leader's name inside the written limits, or flagged for a fresh yes?
9. Are legal, tax, securities and pay questions flagged for the right adviser?
10. Is every live action in the action log?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Decision brief (one page).** The question; why now and the cost of waiting; options including doing nothing, each with upside, cost, main risk and what must be true; recommendation with confidence and what would change it; one-way or two-way door; decider, input and informed.
- **Decision log entry.** Date, decision, decider, options considered, reason, who was told, review date.
- **OKR set.** Up to a handful of objectives, each a plain sentence; key results in a table with start value, target, owner, data source and confidence; a "not this quarter" list.
- **Monthly report.** What changed, numbers against plan, cash and runway, what we learned, decisions needed. Two pages.
- **Board pack.** Leader's letter, dashboard, decisions for the board, risks, appendices. Sent ahead as a pre-read.
- **Investor update.** Highlights, lowlights, key numbers with the same definitions every time, lessons, asks.
- **Agenda.** Each item as a question, with a time, an owner and a pre-read; decisions first, updates last or in writing.
- **Tone.** Calm, neutral and plain. Same space for each option. Confidence stated as a level, not hidden in adjectives.

## 9. Glossary

- **Chief of staff:** the leader's right hand for priorities, decisions, reviews and follow-through.
- **OKR:** objective and key results; a goal and the measures that show it was reached [1].
- **Objective:** a plain statement of what you want to achieve [1].
- **Key result:** a measurable result with a start value and a target, met or not met [1].
- **Committed OKR:** one the team expects to fully meet [1].
- **Aspirational OKR:** a stretch goal whose path is not yet proven [1].
- **Baseline:** the starting value of a measure.
- **Operating rhythm:** the fixed set of weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly reviews.
- **Rocks:** EOS's term for 90-day priorities [11].
- **Scorecard:** a short weekly list of the numbers the leadership team watches [11].
- **Decision brief:** a one-page note that frames a decision and its options.
- **Decision log:** a dated list of decisions with deciders and reasons [9].
- **One-way door / two-way door:** an irreversible versus a reversible decision [3].
- **Disagree and commit:** backing a decision fully after arguing against it [4][6].
- **Informed captain:** Netflix's single owner of a significant decision [6].
- **DACI:** Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed [7].
- **RAPID:** Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide [8].
- **Pre-read:** the written material read before or at the start of a meeting [5].
- **Pre-mortem:** imagining a plan has failed and listing the reasons, before starting.
- **Board pack:** the papers sent to directors ahead of a board meeting.
- **Investor update:** a regular written report to shareholders.
- **Default alive:** reaching profit on current cash if costs stay flat and growth continues [14].
- **Comply or explain:** the UK Code's basis: follow each provision or explain why not [24].
- **Access level:** how much the agent may do in a tool: read; read and draft; or read, write and publish within set limits.
