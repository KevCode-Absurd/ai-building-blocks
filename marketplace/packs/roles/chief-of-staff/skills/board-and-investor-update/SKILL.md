# Board and investor update

## When to use

- Monthly: a business report for the leader and leadership team.
- Monthly or quarterly: a written update to investors.
- Before each board meeting: the board pack, sent ahead as a pre-read.
- When a board or investor asks a hard question and the answer needs one clear page.

Not for drafting formal board minutes, written resolutions, filings or anything that offers shares. Those follow company law, the articles and any shareholder agreement; the company secretary or a lawyer handles them. Check this with local rules.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the accounts, scorecard, CRM, OKRs, last updates and board papers.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** build the report, update or pack in a working folder; save the investor email as an unsent draft.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved monthly report to the leadership folder and post it in the agreed channel. Sending to investors or the board, or sharing the board folder with anyone new, needs a fresh yes every time, even at level 3.

## What you need

1. This period's numbers and the plan or budget they compare to.
2. Last period's update, so definitions and layout stay the same.
3. The leader's notes: what went well, what went badly, what worries them.
4. Decisions the board must take, with any papers behind them.
5. Who reads it, and when it must go.

## Steps

### 1. Fix the definitions
List each number the update uses and how it is defined (for example: "revenue = invoiced in the month, excluding VAT"; "active customer = ordered in the last 90 days"). Use the same definitions as last time. If one must change, say so and show both versions for one period.

### 2. Pull the numbers and check them
Take each figure from its source, note where it came from, and compare with the plan and last period. Any figure that does not reconcile goes on a list for the leader or bookkeeper; never smooth it over. Mark estimates [ESTIMATE].

### 3. Find what changed
Write three to five lines on what is materially different since last time: up, down or new. Explain the cause of each where known, and say "cause not yet known" where not.

### 4. Put bad news first
If cash, a key customer, a target, a person or a legal matter has gone wrong, it goes on page one with the cause, the effect and the plan. Readers forgive problems more readily than surprises found later.

### 5. Add cash and runway
For any loss-making company: cash at the end of the period, monthly burn, months of runway on current spend, and whether the company is default alive (reaches profit on current cash if costs stay flat and growth continues). Have a qualified accountant confirm the cash figures if the board relies on them.

### 6. Write lessons and asks
What did we learn? Then the asks: specific, with a name and a date where possible ("an intro to a buyer at a national garden-centre chain"; "approval of the Q1 hiring plan at the 14 Jan meeting").

### 7. Separate decide from note
For a board pack, split the agenda into "for decision" (each with a one-page brief and a proposed resolution wording left [FOR COMPANY SECRETARY]) and "for information". Put decisions early on the agenda.

### 8. Shape the format
- **Monthly report:** what changed, numbers against plan, cash, lessons, decisions needed. Two pages.
- **Investor update:** highlights, lowlights, key numbers table, lessons, asks. Short enough to read on a phone.
- **Board pack:** leader's letter (one or two pages), dashboard, decisions with briefs, risks, appendices. Sent ahead so directors can read it before the meeting.

### 9. Final read
Read it as the most sceptical reader. Is anything implied that the numbers don't show? Is any good news overstated? Is every claim about the future marked as a plan or forecast, not a promise?

## Checks

- Every number has a source and the same definition as last time.
- Bad news is on page one with cause and plan.
- Cash and runway are shown for loss-making companies.
- Forecasts are labelled as forecasts.
- Asks are specific.
- Decisions are separated from information.
- Formal resolutions and minutes are flagged for the company secretary or lawyer; figures the board relies on are confirmed by a qualified accountant.
- Nothing goes to the board or investors without a fresh yes.

## Output format

1. The report, update or pack in the chosen format.
2. A list of figures that need checking, with their source.
3. Questions the readers are likely to ask, with draft answers for the leader.

## Example

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a 22-person software company selling scheduling tools to dental practices in the UK and Ireland. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. October investor update. Figures only show the shape.*

> **Lowlight first:** we lost Customer A, a dental group (11% of recurring revenue) on 18 Oct after their merger; their new owner uses a rival system. Monthly recurring revenue fell from £96k to £88k. Plan: we have paused the second sales hire and moved one engineer onto the integration two prospects asked for.
>
> **Highlights:** 9 new practices signed (plan: 7). Churn excluding Customer A: 1.2% [definition unchanged].
>
> | | Oct | Sep | Plan |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Monthly recurring revenue | £88k | £96k | £101k |

> | Net burn | £92k | £85k | £90k |

> **Lesson:** our top accounts need a named contact above practice-manager level.
