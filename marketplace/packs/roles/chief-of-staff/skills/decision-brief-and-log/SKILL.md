# Decision brief and decision log

## When to use

- A leader faces a choice with real trade-offs: hire A or B, raise prices, open a second site, switch a core tool, cut a product.
- A disagreement has run across two meetings without an answer.
- A board or investor asks "why did you decide that?" and nobody wrote it down.
- To set up a decision log for a team that keeps reopening old decisions.

Not for legal, tax or investment decisions on their own merits: the brief can frame those, but the answer comes from a qualified adviser, and the brief says so.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the numbers, notes, past decisions and the documents behind the choice.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the brief as a draft in the working folder; draft the decision-log entry.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** file the final entry in the shared decision log, tell the "informed" list in the agreed channel, and create follow-up tasks, within the confirmed limits. Any message to the board, investors or the whole company needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. What the decision is, who raised it and by when it must be made.
2. The facts and numbers you have, with where each came from.
3. The options people are already arguing for, and who holds each view.
4. Any hard limits: cash, contracts, legal or regulatory constraints, people.
5. Who decides today, if anyone knows.

## Steps

### 1. Frame the question in one sentence
Write it as a choice with a date: "Do we hire a salesperson or an operations lead in Q1, given cash for one?" If you cannot write one sentence, the decision is really two decisions; split it.

### 2. Sort it: one-way or two-way door
Ask: if we choose wrong, can we reverse it cheaply within a few months? If yes, it is a two-way door: keep the brief to half a page, name an owner and a date, and decide fast. If no (a long lease, a senior hire, a price rise to all customers, a market exit), it is a one-way door: full brief, wider input, more time. This split comes from Amazon's 2015 shareholder letter.

### 3. Name the roles
One decider. The people whose input is needed (by expertise, not seniority). Anyone with a veto over a hard constraint, such as the finance lead on cash or a lawyer on a contract. The people to inform afterwards. Use DACI or RAPID labels if the team knows them. If two people believe they decide, stop and get the leader to name one.

### 4. List the real options
Include doing nothing, and at least two real alternatives. Look for a hybrid or a smaller test ("hire a part-time ops lead for three months"). Drop options nobody would choose, and say why in one line.

### 5. Weigh each option the same way
For each: the upside, the cost (money, time, people, attention), the main risk, and what must be true for it to work. Give each option the same space. Mark every figure with its source or as an estimate.

### 6. Check what you don't know
List the gaps that matter. For each, say whether it can be found before the deadline and how. Do not wait for everything: most decisions are made with less than full information, and the brief says what is missing.

### 7. Recommend
Pick one. Give a confidence level (low, medium, high, or a rough percentage) and the one or two facts that would change the recommendation. Add the cost of waiting.

### 8. Run a quick pre-mortem
Imagine it is a year later and the recommended option failed. Write the three most likely reasons. If one is likely and serious, add a guard to the plan.

### 9. After the decision, log it
Fill in the decision-log entry the same day: date, decision, decider, options considered, reason, who was told, review date. Turn the decision into actions with one owner and one date each. If people disagreed, note that they committed.

### 10. Review
On the review date, check what happened against what the brief expected. Note what you would change in how you decide, not only in what you decided.

## Checks

- The question is one sentence with a date.
- Doing nothing is an option, and every option has the same headings.
- Every number has a source or is marked [ESTIMATE].
- One decider is named.
- The recommendation has a confidence level and "what would change my mind".
- Legal, tax and investment points are flagged for the right adviser.
- Bad news (a risk, a cost overrun, a missed target) is in the brief, not left out.

## Output format

1. Decision brief (one page; half a page for a two-way door): question, why now and cost of waiting, door type, roles, options table, gaps, recommendation with confidence, pre-mortem.
2. Decision-log entry.
3. Actions with owners and dates.

## Example

*Made-up business for illustration: Harbour & Pine, a 14-person furniture maker in Bristol, UK.*

> **Question:** Do we hire a sales lead or an operations lead in January, given cash for one salary for a year?
> **Door:** one-way (a senior hire). **Decider:** Maya (MD). **Input:** Tom (finance), Priya (workshop). **Informed:** whole team after the decision.
>
> | | Sales lead | Ops lead | Do nothing |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Upside | Trade orders could grow [ESTIMATE] | Lead times back under the promise on the website | Cash kept |
> | Cost | One salary plus commission | One salary | Maya keeps doing both jobs |
> | Main risk | More orders into a workshop already late | Sales flat | Late orders keep growing; Maya burns out |
> | Must be true | Workshop can take more orders | Delays are caused by planning, not machines | Q1 orders stay flat |
>
> **Gap:** we don't know how much of the delay is planning. Priya can check six weeks of job cards by 12 Dec.
> **Recommendation:** ops lead, medium confidence. I'd switch to sales if the job-card check shows machines, not planning, cause the delays.
> **Pre-mortem:** the hire fails because the role is vague; guard: a written 90-day plan before advertising.
