# Operating rhythm and meetings

## When to use

- The leader's diary is full, yet decisions wait.
- Weekly meetings run as people reading out updates.
- Teams start overlapping projects without knowing it.
- The company has grown past the point where the founder can hold it all in their head.
- After a planning day, to make the plan run week by week.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the calendar, recurring meeting list, past agendas and notes, project tracker and channels.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the rhythm calendar, agendas and project list; propose changes to recurring meetings as drafts.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** create or change recurring meetings, send agendas and pre-read reminders, update the project list and post the weekly status in the agreed channel, within the confirmed limits. Cancelling a meeting someone else owns, or any message to the whole company, needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. A calendar export or list of every recurring meeting the leader attends: name, length, frequency, attendees, purpose.
2. The current goals or OKRs.
3. The project list, or the main pieces of cross-team work.
4. When the board meets and when investors get updates.
5. The leader's protected focus time, if any.

## Steps

### 1. Audit the meetings
For each recurring meeting, write: its purpose as a question it answers, the decisions it made in the last month, the people who needed to be there, and hours used per month (length times attendees times frequency). Then mark each:
- **Keep** if it makes decisions only that group can make.
- **Shrink** if it could be shorter or smaller.
- **Merge** if another meeting covers the same ground.
- **Replace with writing** if it is mainly updates.
- **Stop** if nobody can name what it decides.

### 2. Design the rhythm
Set one review per level, each with an owner, a day, a pre-read and an output:

| Review | Purpose | Pre-read | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly leadership | Scorecard, priorities on or off track, issues to solve | Scorecard and written updates by the day before | Decisions and actions |
| Monthly business review | What changed, numbers against plan, cash | Monthly report | Decisions; investor update draft |
| Quarterly review | Score OKRs, set the next few | Quarter report and draft OKRs | Next quarter's OKRs and "not this quarter" list |
| Annual plan | The year's goals and budget | Draft plan | The plan |

Fit the days to the business: a retailer avoids peak trading days; a team across time zones picks overlapping hours. Some firms run fixed cycles instead of calendar quarters, as Basecamp does with six-week cycles and a two-week cool-down.

### 3. Write the weekly agenda
Keep the same order every week. One proven layout is EOS's: a short check-in, the scorecard, priorities on or off track, headlines, last week's to-dos, then most of the time on the top issues, ending with new actions and a rating. Each issue is a question: "Do we pause the Leeds launch until the new hire starts?"

### 4. Set meeting rules
- No agenda, no meeting.
- Pre-reads go out the day before, written, not slides.
- Decisions first, updates in writing.
- Every decision goes into the decision log; every action has one owner and one date.
- Meetings are placed next to each other, not in the middle of focus time.
- Anyone may decline a meeting that has no question for them.

### 5. Build the cross-team project list
One row per project: name, owner, goal it serves, status (on track, at risk, off track), what is blocked, the next decision and its date. Each owner updates their row before the weekly meeting. Projects that serve no current goal go on the "not this quarter" list or stop.

### 6. Write the rhythm calendar
Put every review on the calendar for the next two quarters, with its pre-read deadline, owner and attendees. Add board and investor dates and the work back from them (pack draft, numbers locked, leader review).

### 7. Run it and tune it
After the first month, re-audit: did each meeting decide things, did pre-reads arrive, did actions close? Adjust lengths and attendees. Keep the meeting rating if the team uses one and act on low scores.

## Checks

- Every meeting left on the calendar answers a named question.
- Hours freed are counted and shown to the leader.
- Each review has an owner, pre-read, agenda and written output.
- Board and investor deadlines are worked back into the calendar.
- Every project has one owner and a status line.
- At level 3, no meeting owned by someone else is cancelled without a fresh yes.

## Output format

1. Meeting audit table with keep, shrink, merge, replace or stop, and hours per month before and after.
2. Rhythm table and calendar.
3. Weekly agenda template.
4. Meeting rules (one page).
5. Cross-team project list template.

## Example

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a group of four physiotherapy clinics in Glasgow, UK, with 40 staff. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Audit (founder's diary, per month):**
>
> | Meeting | Decides | Hours (length x people x times) | Call |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Monday all-managers | Nothing logged in October | 1 h x 6 x 4 = 24 | Replace with written updates by Friday |
> | Clinic lead 1:1s | Staffing, patient issues | 0.5 h x 2 x 16 = 16 | Keep |
> | Marketing catch-up | Campaign sign-off | 1 h x 4 x 4 = 16 | Shrink to 30 min, two people |
> | Finance review | Budget calls | 1.5 h x 3 x 1 = 4.5 | Merge into monthly business review |
>
> **Freed:** about 40 person-hours a month (24 + 12 + 4.5).
> **New weekly leadership meeting:** Tuesday 8:30-9:30, founder and four clinic leads (1 h x 5 x 4 = 20 hours a month), scorecard, clinic issues, actions. Pre-read: each clinic lead's three-line update by Monday 4 pm. **Net saving:** about 20 person-hours a month, and every remaining hour decides something.
