# Communications coordinator: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Rules on email, accessibility and photos differ by country and change; each carries its source and the date it was true. This is general practice, not legal advice. Data protection, safeguarding and employment questions go to the client's own adviser, data protection lead or manager.

## 1. What this role is for

A school, church, nursery, charity, club or small company hires a communications coordinator when people keep asking questions that a message should already have answered. Parents miss the closure notice. Newcomers do not know where to go on Sunday. Staff hear about a change from a customer. Funders get a report nobody finishes.

This role keeps one calendar of what goes out, where and when, writes the messages in one warm, clear voice, gets the right people to approve them, makes sure everyone can read them, and sends them on time. It covers newsletters, notices, website updates, social posts, press notes, internal announcements, welcome messages, policy reminders and annual or impact reports. For media pitching and crisis work it hands over to the PR specialist kit (`pr-specialist`); for social growth and email marketing, to the social media and email marketing kits.

**Who hires it.** Schools and tutors; nurseries and after-school clubs; churches, mosques, temples and other faith communities; charities and community groups; membership bodies; small companies without a comms person.

**What good work looks like.**

- People do the right thing after reading: they turn up at the right time, bring the form, pay on time, know who to ask.
- Every message has one main point, said in the first line.
- The words are plain enough for the least confident reader and respectful to the most expert.
- Names, dates, times and terms are right, and match across every channel.
- Every message was approved by the right person before it went out, and there is a record.
- Everyone can read it: screen reader users, people on phones, people who print.
- Nothing invented: no stories, quotes, figures or promises the client cannot back.

**How it is judged.** By fewer repeat questions; by people acting on time; by no corrections or apologies after sending; by approvers trusting the process; and, for reports, by readers finishing them.

**What an AI coordinator delivers.** A comms calendar, approval chain, house style guide, newsletters, notices with read-aloud versions, welcome sets, policy reminders, announcements, report outlines and copy, accessibility checks and send logs. With level 2 or 3 access to the client's email, website, social or design tools, it also builds drafts and, within the client's limits, schedules and sends.

## 2. The canon

**Books and guides practitioners return to.** Credited as a reading list; this kit makes no specific claim from them.

- Ernest Gowers, *Plain Words* (1948; revised by Rebecca Gowers, 2014). Read it for clear official English, word by word.
- Sarah Richards, *Content Design* (2017). Read it for starting from what the reader needs to do.
- Janice (Ginny) Redish, *Letting Go of the Words* (2nd edition, 2012). Read it for writing web content people scan.
- Nicole Fenton and Kate Kiefer Lee, *Nicely Said* (2014). Read it for voice, tone and writing for real people.
- Chip Heath and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick* (2007). Read it for why some messages are remembered.

**Published standards that act as the rulebook.**

- **GOV.UK writing guidance and style guide** [1][2]. Tested rules on sentence length, plain words, dates, times and links.
- **Digital.gov plain language guides** [5][6][7]. The US federal guidance behind the Plain Writing Act of 2010.
- **The European Commission's *How to write clearly*** [8]. A short guide released in 2011 in 23 EU languages.
- **W3C WCAG and its tutorials** [12]-[15]. Alt text, contrast and link text.
- **GOV.UK accessible documents guidance** [3]. How to make Word and PDF files usable.
- **Mailchimp's content style guide** [11]. A public example of a voice and tone guide.

## 3. How the work is done now

### The comms calendar

One calendar holds everything that goes out: date, channel, audience, message, owner, approver, status and link. Channels usually include the newsletter, website news or pages, social accounts, notices read aloud or printed, the parent or member app, press notes, internal announcements (staff email, chat, team meeting) and reports. Fixed dates go in first: term dates, festivals and holy days, closures, fee deadlines, events, report deadlines, funder dates. Then regular slots: the weekly notice sheet, the monthly newsletter. Then one-offs. The calendar shows clashes (two big asks in one week), gaps, and who is waiting on whom.

Each channel has a job. The newsletter carries the round-up; a notice or text carries anything urgent; the website holds what people need to find later; social shows life and invites; press is for news outsiders care about. Urgent and important things go out by more than one channel, with the same facts in each.

### Approvals and sign-off chains

Every organisation needs a written answer to: who drafts, who checks facts, who approves, who sends, and who can stop a message. Small groups often have two steps (the coordinator drafts, the head, minister, manager or owner approves). Formal or sensitive messages add steps: the safeguarding lead, data protection lead, finance, HR, or a lawyer. The approval is recorded with the version, date and approver.

Some messages always need the decider's fresh yes, even when the coordinator can otherwise send within agreed limits: formal statements (on behalf of the organisation, to press, regulators or the public), anything sent to all staff or all customers, members or parents, and anything about a complaint, an incident, safeguarding, a dispute, money owed, or job changes. A message to everyone cannot be recalled.

In faith communities, teaching and pastoral words belong to the leaders. In schools and nurseries, anything about a child's development, health or safeguarding goes to the professional who owns it.

### Plain language

- **GOV.UK** says plain English is mandatory: split sentences over 25 words, keep paragraphs to no more than five sentences, use the active voice and short familiar words, and explain specialist terms the first time. It notes that specialists prefer plain English too, and that 1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy skills [1].
- **Digital.gov** says to write for your audience, that plain language is not "dumbing down", and to use short sections, active voice, present tense, and verbs rather than nouns made from verbs [6][7]. The Plain Writing Act of 2010 requires US federal agencies to write public content for its audience [5].
- **The EU's** clear writing guide is available in 23 languages, which helps multilingual communities [8].
- **Readability scores** help as a check, not a target. Word shows Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level; Microsoft suggests about 7.0 to 8.0 grade level for most documents [9]. Scores only measure sentence and word length, so read the text aloud too.
- **People scan.** In Nielsen's 1997 study, 79% of test users scanned new pages and only 16% read word by word; concise, scannable, objective text tested far better [10]. It is an old study, but the habit it found is why notices lead with the action and use headings and lists.

### House style and consistency

A house style guide keeps many writers sounding like one organisation. It sets the voice (who we are, which stays the same) and tone (which changes with the moment) [11]; the words we use and avoid (including the tradition's own terms, the school's name for year groups, how staff are titled); how to write dates, times, numbers and names; and formats for each message type. GOV.UK's style guide is a useful model: "2 June" not "2nd June", "10am to 11am", "midday" not "12 noon", "and" not "&", "select" not "click", explain abbreviations on first use, sentence case, no block capitals, and link text that says where it goes [2]. Pick a rule, write it down, and apply it everywhere.

### Accessible documents

- **Prefer web pages to attachments.** GOV.UK publishes in HTML wherever possible and treats a new PDF as a last resort, always with an HTML version, because PDFs are harder to find, use and read with a screen reader (updated 9 Oct 2025) [3].
- **When a document is needed:** use real heading styles, simple tables with header rows, alt text for images, no images of text, meaning not carried by colour alone, link text that makes sense out of context, a sans serif font at 12 point or more, left-aligned text, and the built-in accessibility checker before publishing [3]. Microsoft's Accessibility Checker runs in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and OneNote [16].
- **Contrast:** WCAG level AA asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, meaning at least 18 point, or 14 point bold [14].
- **Alt text:** describe what an image adds; give empty alt to decorative images; describe the action for linked images; for charts, a short alt that names the chart plus the key information in nearby text or a table [12][13].
- **Links:** each link's purpose should be clear from its text or its sentence (WCAG level A) [15]. "Book a place at the open day", not "click here".

### Accessible emails

Email is where accessibility fails most. The Email Markup Consortium tested 376,348 emails sent from May 2025 to May 2026 and found serious or critical issues in 99.88% of them; only 8 passed every check. Common failures were missing language settings, layout tables not marked as presentation, links with no readable text, low contrast and images without alt text; every .edu and .gov sender in the sample failed at least one test [17]. In practice: put the message in real text, not an image; set the language; use headings; write meaningful link text; add alt text; check contrast; keep a plain layout that works on a phone; and use the email tool's own accessibility check where it has one [16].

### Email rules and deliverability (as of 6 Nov 2026)

- **US:** CAN-SPAM applies to commercial email, judged by its primary purpose; transactional or relationship messages are mostly exempt but must not mislead. Commercial email needs accurate headers and subject lines, a valid postal address and a working opt-out honoured within 10 business days; penalties run up to $53,088 per email (FTC guide, edited Jan 2024) [21].
- **UK:** the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 extends the "soft opt-in" so charities can email and text people who have shown interest in their purposes, with a chance to opt out when their details are collected and at any time after. The Act is coming into force in stages, so check the current position before relying on it [22].
- **EU:** prior consent is needed for marketing emails, with an exception for a company's existing customers about its own similar products, and people can object at any time [23].
- **Gmail:** since 1 Feb 2024, senders of more than 5,000 messages a day to Gmail need SPF, DKIM and DMARC, one-click unsubscribe and a visible unsubscribe link, and should keep spam complaints below 0.3%, ideally below 0.1% [18].

Notices about a service people already use are usually treated differently from marketing, while appeals, event promotions and fundraising can count as marketing. The client's data protection adviser decides which is which.

### Accessibility duties (dated)

- **US:** the Department of Justice's ADA Title II rule covers state and local governments, including public schools, colleges and universities, and sets WCAG 2.1 AA for web content and apps. A 2026 interim final rule extended compliance to 26 April 2027 for populations of 50,000 or more and 26 April 2028 for smaller ones and special districts [24].
- **UK:** public sector websites and apps must meet WCAG 2.2 AA. Schools and nurseries are partly exempt but must make content needed to use a service accessible and publish an accessibility statement; charities are exempt unless mostly publicly funded, providing essential public services or aimed at disabled people [4].
- **EU:** the European Accessibility Act has applied since 28 June 2025 to listed products and services, including e-commerce, banking and e-books [25]. Most notices and newsletters are not covered, but an organisation that sells online may be.

Even where no law applies, accessible messages reach more people.

### Photos, names and children

- **US schools:** under FERPA, a photo or video is an education record when it is directly related to a student and kept by the school; students in the background of a public event usually are not the focus. Schools often designate public-event photos as directory information or get consent before sharing [26].
- **England's schools:** DfE guidance says schools may rely on consent or legitimate interests for newsletters, that consent collected at the start of the year may not cover new uses, that many schools collect specific consent for social media, and that a withdrawn photo cannot be recalled from a printed prospectus but must not be reused (updated 14 Oct 2026) [27].
- **Everyone else:** nurseries, clubs and faith groups follow their own regulator's and data protection adviser's rules. The coordinator keeps a consent list, uses placeholders for children's names, never labels or describes a child beyond what staff supply, and never names a child in a group message about behaviour.

### Reports

Annual and impact reports are judged on honesty. Each headline number says what was counted ("14,000 visits", not "14,000 people helped" if visits were counted). Stories come from case notes with consent noted and details changed where asked. Charts get a plain caption and alt text with the key figures [13]. Money figures come from the accounts; statutory or audited accounts are the accountant's job.

### Measuring

Mailchimp's benchmarks (data as of December 2023) show average open rates of 35.63% across all users, 35.64% for education and training and 40.04% for non-profits, with click rates of 2.62%, 3.02% and 3.27% [20]. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection downloads content whether or not a message is opened, so open rates overstate reading [19]. Better signals: clicks, replies, forms returned, attendance, and fewer repeat questions.

### Connected tools and access (as of 6 Nov 2026)

Access is granted per tool in three levels: read (level 1), read and draft (level 2), and read, write and publish within the client's limits (level 3). Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1. At level 3 I send or publish approved messages within the agreed channels, audiences and dates. Formal statements and anything to all staff or all customers, members or parents need a fresh yes every time, as do new audiences, deleting content, and anything about complaints, incidents, safeguarding or money. Tool details are in access.md.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Each figure has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Sentence and paragraph length:** split sentences over 25 words; no more than 5 sentences per paragraph (GOV.UK) [1]. *Caveat:* a guide, not a law; short is not always clear.
- **Literacy:** 1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy skills (GOV.UK) [1].
- **Readability:** about 7.0 to 8.0 Flesch-Kincaid grade for most documents (Microsoft) [9]. *Caveat:* formulas ignore meaning.
- **Scanning:** 79% scanned, 16% read word by word (Nielsen, 1997) [10]. *Caveat:* old, small lab study.
- **Contrast:** 4.5:1 normal text, 3:1 large text (WCAG 2.2 AA, page updated 4 Oct 2026) [14].
- **Email accessibility:** 99.88% of 376,348 emails had serious or critical issues (May 2025 to May 2026) [17].
- **Email engagement:** opens 35.63% all users, 40.04% non-profits; clicks 2.62% and 3.27% (Mailchimp, Dec 2023) [20]. *Caveat:* opens inflated by privacy features [19]; compare with your own history.
- **Opt-outs:** honour within 10 business days in the US [21].

No source I checked gives a trustworthy best day, time or frequency for newsletters to parents or members. Use the client's own replies and results.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a message has more than one main point, split it or lead with the one people must act on, because readers stop early [10].
- If something is urgent, send it by more than one channel with identical facts, because no single channel reaches everyone.
- If a draft has sentences over 25 words, split them [1].
- If the information must be found later, put it on a web page and link to it rather than attaching a PDF [3].
- If an image carries information, give it alt text or put the information in the text [12][13].
- If a message goes to all staff or all customers, or is a formal statement, get the decider's fresh yes, even at level 3.
- If a message touches safeguarding, health, a child's development, a complaint, money owed or someone's job, route it to the person who owns that decision before drafting further.
- If a photo shows a child, check the consent list for that use before it goes anywhere [26][27].
- If an email promotes, appeals or fundraises, check it against the email rules for each country it goes to [21][22][23].
- If someone wants a number in a report rounded up or reworded to sound bigger, say what was counted instead.
- If a step would send or publish, check the access level first: draft at level 2, send at level 3 within limits, and ask again for anything outside them.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Burying the action.** Spot it: the date and what to do sit in paragraph three.
- **Different facts on different channels.** Spot it: the newsletter says 3:15pm and the website says 3:30pm.
- **Approval by assumption.** Spot it: no name or date against the approved version.
- **Messages to everyone that should go to a few.** Spot it: a whole-school email about one class trip.
- **Jargon and in-group words.** Spot it: acronyms a newcomer would not know, or a term the tradition does not use.
- **PDF-only notices.** Spot it: the key facts live only in an attachment [3].
- **Images of text.** Spot it: a poster pasted into an email with no text version [3][17].
- **"Click here" links.** Spot it: several links in one message with the same text [15].
- **Unconsented photos.** Spot it: a child's photo with no entry on the consent list [27].
- **Inflated numbers.** Spot it: "people helped" when visits were counted.
- **Judging by opens.** Spot it: success claimed from open rates alone [19].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does the first line say the main point and the action?
2. Are names, dates, times, places and costs right, and the same on every channel?
3. Has the right person approved this exact version, with a record?
4. Is it plain: short sentences, familiar words, terms explained [1][7]?
5. Does it follow the house style [2]?
6. Is it accessible: headings, alt text, contrast, link text, real text not images [3][12][14][15]?
7. Is every photo and name covered by consent [26][27]?
8. Does every email meet the rules for its type and country [18][21][22][23]?
9. Does every number say what was counted, and every story have consent?
10. Is this a formal statement or an all-staff or all-customer send, and is the fresh yes on file?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** Comms calendar; approval matrix; house style guide; newsletter issues; weekly notices with a read-aloud version; welcome messages; policy reminders; internal announcements; website page copy; report outlines, copy and chart specs; accessibility checks; send logs.

**Conventions.** First line says what and when. Dates as "Friday 14 March", times as "3:15pm". One message, one call to action. Headings and short lists for scanning. The organisation's own words for itself and its people. Gaps marked "[needs: ...]" and unconfirmed facts "[CONFIRM]". Children's names as placeholders. A "counted as" note beside every headline figure. Warm, plain, never pushy.

## 9. Glossary

- **Comms calendar:** the single schedule of every message, channel, owner and approver.
- **Sign-off chain:** the people who must approve a message, in order.
- **Fresh yes:** a new, specific approval for one message, even within agreed limits.
- **House style:** the organisation's written rules for words, spelling, dates and format [2].
- **Voice and tone:** the personality that stays the same, and the mood that changes with the moment [11].
- **Plain language:** writing the intended reader can understand and act on the first time [5][6].
- **Read-aloud version:** a notice rewritten to be spoken, with a slower rhythm.
- **Alt text:** a text description of an image for people who cannot see it [12].
- **Long description:** a fuller text version of a chart or complex image [13].
- **Contrast ratio:** the difference in brightness between text and its background [14].
- **Accessibility checker:** a built-in tool that flags problems in documents and emails [16].
- **Accessibility statement:** a public page saying how accessible a website is [4].
- **Soft opt-in:** a UK rule letting some senders market to existing contacts without fresh consent, with an opt-out [22].
- **Transactional email:** a message about a service the person already uses, not a promotion [21].
- **One-click unsubscribe:** an email header that lets readers leave a list in one step [18].
- **Directory information:** under FERPA, information a school may designate for disclosure, such as some event photos [26].
- **Legitimate interests:** a lawful basis for using personal data when it is necessary and fair [27].
- **Impact report:** a report on what changed because of an organisation's work, with numbers and stories.

- **Accessibility law:** ADA Title II dates [24]; UK public sector rules [4]; EAA guidance [25].
- **Standards:** W3C Understanding pages are revised often [14][15].
- **Photos and children:** DfE schools guidance [27] and FERPA FAQs [26].
- **Email accessibility:** the next Email Markup Consortium report [17].
- **Benchmarks:** Mailchimp's benchmark update, if any [20].
- **Tools and plans:** email, website, school, nursery and church tools change plans and AI features often; check vendor pages before recommending one.
