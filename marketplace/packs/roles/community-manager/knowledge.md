# Community manager: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform and legal facts come from the platforms' and regulators' own pages, and each says when it was true. This kit is reviewed every 30 days.

This kit overlaps with the social media manager kit on public comment replies and ad disclosure. For posting on a brand's own social profiles, use that kit; this one is about groups where members talk to each other.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires a community manager to build a place where its customers, members or fans help each other, and to keep that place friendly, useful and alive. The role has four jobs:

- **Build and run:** the community's purpose, its welcome, the weekly rhythm of prompts, threads and events, and replies that get members talking to each other.
- **Moderate:** house rules with reasons, a ladder of steps for each kind of problem, reply templates, moderator rotas, and fair handling of reports and appeals.
- **Membership:** for paid communities, the levels, the path from joining to a first win to renewal, actions for quiet members, and an honest cancel flow.
- **Outside communities:** taking part in Reddit, Discord servers, Facebook groups and forums that the business does not own, as a helpful member who says who they are, not as a hidden advertiser.

Who hires it: creators, coaches and course sellers; software and developer tools; clubs, studios and trade associations; brands with a customer group; owners who started a free group that has gone quiet or turned unpleasant.

What good work looks like:

- One line says what the community is for and who it is for, and every rule, prompt and event serves it.
- New members get a welcome, a first small thing to do, and a reply to their first post.
- Members answer each other; the manager hosts more than broadcasts.
- Rules are few, written down, explained, and applied the same way to everyone.
- Moderation is calm, specific and logged, with a way to appeal.
- Paid members reach a first win early, and leaving is easy and honest.
- Outside, the business adds value first, discloses its connection, and follows each community's rules.

How it is judged: new members who post, members who come back, questions answered by other members, reports handled and appeals upheld or overturned, cancellations for paid communities, and no avoidable incidents (hidden promotion, unfair bans, data lost by deleting the wrong thing).

**How this agent acts.** It works at the access level the client grants for each tool (see access.md). At level 3, moderation actions follow the client's written rules. Bans and deletions need a fresh yes unless the written rules list that action for that case. Erasing a member's account or data always needs a fresh yes. Personal accounts, such as someone's own Reddit or Discord profile, are used only by their holder.

## 2. The canon

**Jakob Nielsen, "The 90-9-1 Rule for Participation Inequality" (Nielsen Norman Group, 2006) [20].** In most online communities, a small minority creates most of the content, a slightly larger group contributes now and then, and most people only read. Nielsen's point is that this cannot be removed, only shaped: make contributing easier, let people edit or react rather than start from a blank page, reward contribution moderately so the loudest few do not take over, and surface good posts. He also warns that the vocal few are not a fair sample of members, which matters when you read feedback threads.

**Richard Millington and FeverBee [21][22].** FeverBee describes a community in four layers: a shared interest, a way to engage, relationships between members, and a purpose. A community manager acts as a host who gets members talking to each other, where a social media manager mostly broadcasts [21]. FeverBee's ROI guide splits value into customer lifetime value, acquisition, cost reduction (such as support), efficiency and social return, set against technology, staff and process costs [22]. Use it to agree with the client, before launch, which of these the community is meant to move.

**The Santa Clara Principles 2.0 (2021) [19].** A coalition of human rights groups and academics set out what fair moderation looks like: rules people can understand, with examples; telling a person what was actioned, under which rule and how to appeal; an appeal reviewed by someone not involved in the first decision; and telling people when automated tools are used. These were written for large platforms, but the habits scale down to a 300-member group.

**The platforms' and regulators' own rules.** Discord, Slack, Circle and Facebook publish what server owners and group admins control and what the platform enforces itself [1][2][6][9][13]. The EU Digital Services Act describes notice-and-action and statements of reasons [14][15]. These pages, not third-party "growth hacks", set the frame.

**Reading list (credited, no claims drawn from them here):** Robert Kraut and Paul Resnick, *Building Successful Online Communities* (MIT Press, 2012); Charles Vogl, *The Art of Community* (2016); Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990); Patrick O'Keefe, *Managing Online Forums* (2008); Jono Bacon, *People Powered* (2019).

## 3. How the work is done now

### Platforms (as of Nov 2026)

**Discord.** The Community Guidelines (effective 29 September 2025) apply to every server [1]. Rules that touch community managers directly: every account must belong to a human, so self-bots and user-bots are banned; artificially inflating membership or engagement is banned; buying or selling servers, permissions or invite links is banned; evading a ban, including with a new account or server, is banned; and owners must label channels with adult sexual content as age-restricted [1]. Owners and admins set server permissions and extra rules, but stay bound by Discord's policies [2]. Discord's own Trust and Safety team reviews reports from users and moderators and can warn, remove content, restrict features, block a server from inviting new members, remove a server, or suspend accounts [1][4]. Discord advises every server to write behaviour rules that add to its guidelines [3]. AutoMod (described in a September 2022 post) can block widely reported spam, mention spam and harmful links, and moderators can pause invites during a raid [5]; check the current options in Server Settings, as only that 2022 post was reachable.

**Slack.** On the free plan, members can see 90 days of messages and files, and content older than one year is deleted; free workspaces allow 10 app integrations [6]. That makes the free plan a poor home for a community whose old answers matter. Deactivating a member signs them out, removes them from channels and keeps their messages; they are not told, and it can be reversed [7]. Slack publishes an official MCP server; workspace admins approve which AI clients may use it [8].

**Circle.** Members, moderators and admins can report posts; a report threshold can hide content automatically; the profanity filter and flagged members send content to a review queue, hidden while "In review"; approving keeps it, rejecting removes it, and decisions can be reversed (page updated June 2025) [9]. Moderators act only in the spaces they are assigned; settings, analytics and billing stay with admins [10]. Circle separates three removals (page updated October 2026) [11]:

| Action | What happens | Can it be undone? |
|---|---|---|
| Deactivate | Content stays; profile hidden; course progress, tags and activity score removed | Yes, by reactivating |
| Delete | Profile and content permanently erased | No |
| Ban | Erased, and IP addresses from the last 30 days blocked | Only through Circle support |

Members are not notified of any of the three, and each appears in the activity log [11]. The Circle MCP lets admins on the Business plan and higher connect an AI tool with read-only or full access, chosen at sign-in [12].

**Facebook Groups.** Admins have Admin Assist (automatic management), membership questions, preapproval of members and posts, rule tagging on posts and comments, and remove, ban, suspend and limit actions [13].

**Reddit.** Reddit's policy pages could not be opened by my fetch tool on 6 November 2026, so this kit states no Reddit rule as fact. Before any Reddit work, read Reddit's Rules, its Moderator Code of Conduct and the subreddit's own rules and wiki on the day, and treat the subreddit's moderators as the final word on what is allowed there.

### The weekly rhythm

1. **Purpose line.** "A place for [who] to [do what] together." Every prompt and rule should pass this test.
2. **Welcome.** A welcome post or message, a prompt to introduce themselves with a question that is easy to answer, and one small first task. Reply to every first post by name.
3. **Rhythm.** A small set of repeating formats: a weekly question, a share or show-your-work thread, a member spotlight, an event or office hour. Same day each week, so members learn the pattern.
4. **Replies.** Answer the question asked, link to past answers, and invite another member in ("Priya solved this last month; Priya, any tips?"). Lowering the effort to take part is how Nielsen suggests shaping the 90-9-1 curve [20].
5. **Superusers.** Recognise helpful members in proportion; do not hand the room to the loudest few [20].
6. **Monthly health read.** New members, first posts, returning members, questions answered by members, reports, removals and appeals.

### Moderation

**House rules.** A small number of rules, each with a one-line reason and an example. People follow rules they understand, and understandable rules with examples are the first Santa Clara principle [19].

**The ladder.** For each kind of problem (spam, off-topic, self-promotion, heated argument, harassment, misinformation, illegal content), write the steps: reminder, warning, hidden post, removal, time-out, ban. Serious harm skips steps. Keep the ladder short enough to use mid-argument.

**Prefer reversible actions.** Hiding beats deleting; deactivating beats deleting (Slack and Circle both keep content when you deactivate) [7][11]. A Circle ban can only be lifted by Circle support [11].

**Notice and appeal.** When you remove or restrict, tell the member what was actioned, which rule, and how to appeal; have someone not involved review appeals [19]. This mirrors the EU DSA, which requires platforms to give a statement of reasons and a free internal complaint route [14].

**Evidence.** Before removing anything serious, save a screenshot or export with date, time, link and usernames, in the place the client chooses, and log the action.

**Serious harm.** For threats of violence, self-harm or child safety, act first, then report. Do not download, forward or re-share child sexual abuse material; report it to the platform and, in the US, to NCMEC's CyberTipline [27]; in the UK, to the Internet Watch Foundation, which takes anonymous reports [28]; and tell the owner, who decides on police contact. Anything that may need legal or police advice goes to the owner.

**AI help.** If an AI tool sorts reports or drafts moderation messages, say so in the rules, as the Santa Clara Principles ask for automated tools [19]. A person, or the agent within written rules at level 3, makes the call.

### The law in outline (US, UK, EU)

This is general background, not legal advice. Whether a law applies to a given community depends on how it is run; the client should check the official guidance or ask a qualified adviser.

- **EU, Digital Services Act.** Applies to all regulated online services since 17 February 2024 [14]. The basics of notice and action: an easy way to report illegal content; prompt handling; telling both the person who reported and the person who posted what was decided; a statement of reasons when content is removed or restricted; a free internal complaint route handled by qualified staff in a timely, non-discriminatory way; and out-of-court dispute settlement [14]. Small and micro firms are spared some rules [14][15]. Platforms also may not use deceptive dark-pattern designs [15]. A business running its own forum may be a hosting service; a group on Discord or Facebook sits inside that platform's service. Even where the DSA does not apply, its steps make a fair moderation process.
- **UK, Online Safety Act.** Covers services where users post or interact, including online forums and small services, with duties scaled to risk and size [16]. Illegal content duties have applied since 17 March 2025, and services likely to be used by children had to finish a children's risk assessment by 24 July 2025 [16]. Ofcom can fine up to £18 million or 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue, whichever is greater [16].
- **US, Section 230.** A provider or user of an interactive service is generally not treated as the publisher of what others post, and is protected when it removes objectionable material in good faith; federal criminal law and intellectual property are carved out [17].
- **US, copyright (DMCA Section 512).** Hosts that want the safe harbour follow notice-and-takedown, accept counter-notices (material can be restored after 10 to 14 business days unless the owner sues), register a designated agent with the Copyright Office and renew it every three years, and have a repeat-infringer policy [18].

### Taking part in communities you do not own

- **Map first.** For each community: what it is for, how active it is, how welcome brands are, its self-promotion and disclosure rules, and who moderates.
- **Give first.** Answer real questions with real know-how; link to the business only when it truly answers the question and the rules allow.
- **Disclose.** In the US, employees and owners must disclose their connection in each post, not only on a profile page; disclosure belongs in the post itself; terms like "#ambassador" alone are not enough [24]. Brands running ambassador programmes must train members on what to claim and how to disclose, and monitor what they post [24]. For UK and EU disclosure rules, see the social media manager kit.
- **No manipulation.** No fake accounts, sock puppets, bought members, coordinated votes or seeded "customer" posts. Discord bans artificial inflation of membership or engagement [1].
- **AMAs and launches** only with the moderators' written approval.

### Paid memberships

Define the member promise (the result or belonging people pay for), one to three levels, and the path: join, welcome, a first win in week one, a monthly rhythm, renewal. Plan benefits the team can deliver every week. Track active members, cancellations and time to first win. For quiet members, reach out with one useful thing, not a guilt trip. Offer a pause option in the cancel flow, and keep cancelling as easy as joining.

Rules on cancelling are moving. In the US, the FTC's October 2024 click-to-cancel rule was revised by a Federal Register notice on 12 February 2026 to conform to federal court decisions, and the FTC asked for comment on new negative option rules in March 2026 [25]. In the UK, the government said on 2 April 2026 that it will bring forward secondary legislation for the subscription contracts regime, covering pre-contract information, renewal reminders and easy exit; the page gives no start date [26]. Check both before designing a renewal or cancel flow, and ask a qualified adviser for anything binding.

### AI in the work

CMX's 2026 report says 93% of community professionals use AI tools, up from 81% the year before [23]. AI is useful for sorting a queue of posts, drafting replies and prompts, summarising long threads and reading a member export. It must not invent members, testimonials, activity or quotes, and it must not pose as a member. Members should know when a reply or moderation step came from an AI tool.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets.

- **Participation inequality:** roughly 90% of members read, 9% contribute now and then, 1% create most content (Nielsen, 2006) [20]. *Caveat:* a pattern seen across many communities, not a measured average; Nielsen reports some platforms far more skewed.
- **AI use by community teams:** 93% of community professionals use AI tools, up from 81% the previous year (CMX 2026 report) [23]. *Caveat:* the page gives no sample size or method.
- **Slack free plan:** 90 days visible, deletion after one year, 10 integrations [6]. *Caveat:* a plan limit, not a benchmark; the page is undated.
- **Circle ban:** blocks IP addresses used in the 30 days before the ban [11].
- **DMCA counter-notice:** material may be restored after 10 to 14 business days [18].

No official or large published study gives a trustworthy "healthy engagement rate" or "normal churn" for small communities. Use the client's own last three to six months as the baseline, and compare like with like (members who joined in the same month).

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client cannot say in one line what the community is for, write that line before any rule, prompt or channel, because every later choice tests against it.
- If a new member posts for the first time, reply by name, because welcoming newcomers is core daily work for a community manager [21] and an ignored first post gives no reason to post again.
- If a choice is between hiding and deleting, hide, because hidden content can be restored and deleted content often cannot [9][11].
- If a removal is needed and the platform offers deactivation, prefer it to deletion unless the rules say otherwise, because deactivation keeps the record and can be undone [7][11].
- If an action is a ban or a deletion and the client's written rules do not list it for this case, prepare it and ask for a fresh yes, because these are hard or impossible to undo [11].
- If a request would erase a member's account or data, always ask first, even at level 3, and check the client's data duties.
- If content may involve child sexual abuse, a credible threat or self-harm, act to stop the harm, save only what the rules allow, report through the platform and the official route [27][28], and tell the owner the same day.
- If a member asks why a post was removed, give the rule, the reason and the appeal route, because notice and appeal are the basics of fair moderation [14][19].
- If the community is on Slack's free plan and old answers matter, tell the client about the 90-day view and one-year deletion [6].
- If the business wants to post in a community it does not own, read that community's rules first and get moderator approval for anything promotional or any AMA.
- If an employee, owner or paid ambassador posts about the business, disclose the connection in the post [24].
- If the client asks for fake members, sock puppets, bought votes or seeded posts, refuse and offer an honest alternative, because it breaks platform rules [1] and destroys trust.
- If a paid community is losing members after the first month, look at time to first win before changing price.
- If a rule is broken by a friend of the owner, apply the same step as for anyone else, or change the rule for everyone.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **No purpose line.** Spot it: channels for every topic, few posts in any.
- **Broadcasting instead of hosting.** Spot it: the manager's posts get likes, but members never reply to each other [21].
- **Unanswered first posts.** Spot it: introductions with no replies.
- **Too many rules, or rules without reasons.** Spot it: a wall of "Do not" with no examples [19].
- **Rules applied unevenly.** Spot it: regulars get a pass that newcomers do not.
- **Deleting when hiding would do.** Spot it: a removed thread that later turns out to be fine, and nothing to restore [11].
- **Silent removals.** Spot it: members asking in public where their post went [14][19].
- **No evidence kept.** Spot it: an appeal arrives and nobody can show what was posted.
- **Hidden promotion outside.** Spot it: staff answering on Reddit or in forums without saying who they work for [24].
- **Fake activity.** Spot it: new accounts with no history praising the product; sudden member jumps [1].
- **Free Slack for a knowledge community.** Spot it: members asking for an answer that has scrolled out of view [6].
- **Hard cancellations.** Spot it: "email us to cancel", or a cancel flow longer than the sign-up [25][26].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before handing over

1. Is there a one-line purpose, and does every rule, prompt and event serve it?
2. Does every house rule have a reason and an example, and is the list short?
3. Does the moderation ladder cover each common problem, with reversible steps first?
4. Does every removal message name the rule and the appeal route?
5. Is it clear which actions the agent may take at level 3, and which need a fresh yes?
6. Is there an evidence and action log, with who, what, when and why?
7. Is there a route for serious harm, with the official reporting links and the owner's contact?
8. Are disclosures in every post where the business or its people have a connection?
9. For memberships: is the first win defined, and is cancelling as easy as joining?
10. Are all numbers from the client's own data, with the period stated?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Purpose and house rules:* the purpose line, five to eight rules each with a reason and an example, and how they are enforced.
- *Moderation ladder:* a table by problem type with steps, who may act, at what access level, and what needs a fresh yes.
- *Reply templates:* friendly first, firm when needed, never sarcastic; each one names the rule and the next step.
- *Weekly rhythm:* a repeating schedule with eight weeks of prompts written out.
- *Welcome flow:* welcome message, introduction prompt, first small task.
- *Moderator rota and handover log:* busy hours covered, open issues, decisions made.
- *Health report:* this month against last month, from the client's numbers, with one or two actions.
- *Community map (outside):* each community's rules in plain words, fit, activity, and whether brands are welcome.
- *Membership plan:* promise, levels, path to first win, quiet-member actions, cancel flow.

**Tone and conventions.** Warm, plain and specific. Use members' names. One idea per message. Rules in the second person ("Keep it kind"), with "because" lines. Moderation messages are calm, short and private where possible. Never mock, never argue in public, never promise what the owner has not agreed.

## 9. Glossary

- **Community manager:** the person who hosts a group where members talk to each other [21].
- **House rules:** the community's own rules, on top of the platform's [3].
- **Moderation ladder:** the set of steps from reminder to ban for each kind of problem.
- **Statement of reasons:** a note telling a member what was removed or restricted, and why [14].
- **Notice and action:** a process for reporting illegal content and acting on the report [14].
- **Appeal:** a member's request to review a moderation decision [19].
- **Hide, remove, deactivate, delete, ban:** escalating actions; only some can be undone [7][11].
- **Report threshold:** the number of reports after which content is hidden automatically (Circle) [9].
- **AutoMod:** Discord's automatic filters for spam, mentions and harmful links [5].
- **Raid:** many accounts joining at once to spam or harass [5].
- **Ban evasion:** coming back under a new account after a ban [1].
- **Self-bot:** a person's account run by software; banned on Discord [1].
- **Participation inequality (90-9-1):** most members read, a few contribute, very few create most posts [20].
- **Superuser:** a member who contributes far more than most.
- **First win:** the first useful result a new member gets.
- **Churn:** members who cancel or stop taking part in a period.
- **Disclosure:** stating a connection to the business in the post itself [24].
- **Sock puppet:** a fake account used to pose as an independent member.
- **AMA:** "ask me anything", a planned question-and-answer session.
- **Safe harbour:** legal protection for hosts that follow a set process, such as DMCA notice-and-takedown [18].
- **Designated agent:** the contact a US host registers to receive copyright notices [18].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock AI phrasing.** Posts, welcome messages and prompts full of "Unlock", "Elevate", "Seamless" or "Game-changer". These read as machine-written; write as the community's own people speak.
- **Rule of three everywhere.** Every announcement or prompt as a three-point list. It makes every post feel the same; say one thing and ask one question.
- **Headline formula hooks.** Openers like "Let's talk about X" or "Hot take:" on every post. They read as a template; start from what members actually asked or did.
- **Fake urgency.** "Last chance to join" or "spots filling fast" for events or memberships with no real limit. It reads as default and breaks trust; state real limits only.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** Describing the community as "supportive", "engaged" or "a place to connect". Every community says it; describe what members get here that they cannot get elsewhere.
- **Format sameness.** The same post template, length and emoji bullets every day. It reads as a template; vary format with the purpose of each post.
