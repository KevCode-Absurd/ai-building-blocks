# Contract plain summary

## When to use

- An owner is about to sign a supplier, software, lease or service contract without a legal team.
- A renewal or notice date is coming and nobody remembers the terms.
- Two drafts or two suppliers' terms need comparing.
- A vendor will handle personal data and the data processing terms need checking against the required list.

Do not use it to decide whether to sign, whether a clause is enforceable or what the law requires. For those, ask a qualified lawyer or auditor.

## What you need

1. The full contract, with every schedule, order form and document it refers to (terms online, policies, a data processing agreement). List anything missing before starting.
2. Which side the client is on, and what they are buying or selling.
3. What matters most to them (price certainty, ending early, data, liability, exclusivity).
4. For a comparison: both versions or both offers in full.

**Access:** level 1 to read the contract folder; level 2 to write the summary, tables and questions, and draft tracker rows; level 3 to update the contract tracker and set renewal reminders within your limits. Sending a contract for signature or any reply that commits the client needs a fresh yes.

## Steps

### 1. Read it all, list what is missing

Note the parties (exact legal names), the type of deal, the start date, the governing law and the documents incorporated by reference. If a schedule or linked policy is missing, say the summary does not cover it.

### 2. Summarise by topic

In plain words, with the clause number for each point:
- What each side must do (scope, service levels, deliverables).
- Money: price, increases, payment terms, late payment, expenses, taxes.
- Length, renewal (automatic or not), and how to end it (notice, for cause, for convenience).
- Liability: caps, exclusions, indemnities, insurance.
- Data and confidentiality: personal data, security, sub-processors, deletion at the end.
- Intellectual property and use of the client's name.
- Disputes, governing law and venue.
- Anything unusual for this kind of deal.

### 3. Pull every date, payment and duty into a table

| Item | Clause | Who | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Include the last day to give notice before an automatic renewal, payment due dates and any reporting duties.

### 4. Check data processing terms (if the vendor handles personal data)

Under the GDPR, a controller-processor contract must cover: processing only on instructions, staff confidentiality, security, sub-processors only with authorisation, help with rights requests, breaches and DPIAs, deletion or return at the end, information to show compliance, and audits. Mark each as present (with clause), unclear or missing. For transfers outside the EU or UK, note the transfer tool named (for example standard contractual clauses) or that none is named.

### 5. Flag clauses

For each flag give the clause, a one-line reason and a category: one-sided, vague, unusual for this kind of deal, missing, or inconsistent with another clause. Do not say whether a clause is enforceable or lawful.

### 6. Write questions for the lawyer

Ranked by risk to the client. Each names the clause and what the client needs to know, for example "Clause 14.2 caps the supplier's liability at one month's fees, including for data breaches. Is that acceptable given we send them customer records?"

### 7. Comparison (if asked)

Clause by clause: version A, version B, what changed, and why it matters to the client.

## Checks

- Every point has a clause number; nothing is summarised from memory or from a different contract.
- Missing documents are listed at the top.
- The summary says what the contract says, not what the client should do.
- No sentence says the contract is fair, standard, safe to sign or enforceable.
- The renewal notice date is calculated from the clause and shown with the calculation.
- The document ends with "Questions for a qualified lawyer or auditor" and the line: this is a reading aid, not legal advice.

## Output format

1. Header: parties, contract type, date, documents read, documents missing.
2. One-page plain summary by topic.
3. Dates, payments and duties table.
4. Data processing checklist (if relevant).
5. Flags table.
6. Questions for a qualified lawyer, ranked.
7. Tracker row: contract, start, end, auto-renewal, notice period, last day to act, owner.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a coffee roaster buying a three-year e-commerce platform contract. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Summary (extract).** The platform hosts the online shop for three years from 1 Dec 2026 (cl. 3.1). It renews for another three years unless either side gives 90 days' written notice (cl. 3.2). Fees can rise once a year by an amount the supplier sets, with 30 days' notice (cl. 6.4). The supplier's liability is capped at fees paid in the previous three months (cl. 14.2).

| Item | Clause | Who | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| Last day to give notice of non-renewal | 3.2 | Roaster | 2 Sep 2029 (90 days before 1 Dec 2029) |
| Annual fee review | 6.4 | Supplier | Each 1 Dec, 30 days' notice |

**Questions for a qualified lawyer or auditor**
1. Clause 6.4 sets no limit on yearly increases. Can we add a cap or a right to leave if fees rise?
2. Clause 14.2 caps liability at three months' fees, including for loss of customer data. Is this acceptable for us?

*This is a reading aid, not legal advice.*
