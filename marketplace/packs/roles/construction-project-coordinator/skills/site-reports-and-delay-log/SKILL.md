# Site reports and delay log

## When to use

- Every site day: turn the site manager's notes into the daily report.
- Whenever the job is held up: log the delay the same day.
- Every week: write the client's progress report.
- Before a dispute starts: tidy the record so dates and causes match.

Not for writing delay or extra-cost claims, extension of time submissions or legal letters. The log is the evidence base; the claim is the client's, prepared with a quantity surveyor, claims consultant or lawyer.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** site notes, photos, the programme, deliveries and the project tool's logs.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the daily log in the project tool or a document; draft the weekly report.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** complete the daily log, file photos, and send the weekly report to the named client contacts, within the confirmed limits. Any notice under the contract (delay notice, early warning, extension of time) needs a fresh yes every time.

## What you need

1. The site notes for the day, however rough, with photos or a photo list.
2. Who was on site (trades, numbers, hours).
3. Deliveries, plant, visitors and inspections.
4. The programme and look-ahead, to compare progress.
5. The contract's notice rules for delays, if any (the client or their adviser points to the clause).

## Steps

### 1. Sort the notes under fixed headings
Every day, the same order:
1. Date, site, report number, author.
2. Weather: morning and afternoon conditions, and whether work stopped. Note where the reading came from (site observation, a site station, or an official record).
3. People on site: by trade and company, with numbers.
4. Work done: by area and task, matching programme task names.
5. Deliveries and plant.
6. Inspections and visitors (building control, client, designers).
7. Safety observations, incidents and near misses: facts only, who was told. Serious incidents follow the site's own reporting rules (in the US, OSHA's 8-hour and 24-hour reporting; in the UK, the duty holder's reporting duties). Check local rules.
8. Delays and issues.
9. Decisions needed: what, from whom, by when.
10. Photos: file names with a one-line caption each.

### 2. Keep it factual
Write what happened, not opinions or blame: "Plasterers stood down 13:00, boards not delivered (supplier called 12:40, new delivery Thu 7:30)." Mark anything you were told but did not see as "reported by".

### 3. Log any delay the same day
For each delay, one row in the delay log:
- Date and time.
- Cause (weather, late delivery, late information or RFI, client change, previous trade late, labour, inspection, access, other).
- Activity affected, and whether it is on the critical path.
- Days or hours lost (estimate marked).
- Who was told, how and when.
- Evidence: photos, delivery notes, emails, weather record.
- Follow-up: what is being done and by whom.

Use the same format every time. A consistent record written at the time is far easier to rely on later than one rebuilt from memory.

### 4. Flag notices
If the contract requires notice of a delay or event, flag it in the report the same day with the clause the client gave you: "May need notice under the contract: client to decide by Fri." Do not send the notice yourself without a fresh yes.

### 5. Write the weekly client report
1. Status in one line: on programme, behind or ahead, by how many days, and why.
2. Progress against programme: tasks done this week, tasks planned but missed with reasons.
3. Delays this week from the delay log, with new dates.
4. Decisions the client must make, with dates and the effect of a late decision.
5. Risks for the next two weeks.
6. Next week's work by trade.
7. Photos to attach.
Bad news goes first. Never call a job "on schedule" if a critical-path task is behind.

### 6. Check against the programme
Every date in the report must match the programme and the delay log. If they differ, fix the record or flag it.

## Checks

- Same headings every day, and nothing left blank: write "none" where nothing happened.
- Weather recorded every day, with its source.
- Every delay has cause, activity, critical yes or no, days lost, who was told and evidence.
- Facts, not blame; hearsay marked "reported by".
- Incidents recorded and passed to the person in control of the site; no judgement on whether something is safe.
- Contract notices flagged, not sent, without a fresh yes.
- The weekly report matches the programme and delay log.

## Output format

1. Daily report under the fixed headings.
2. Delay log rows added today.
3. Weekly client report (one page plus photos).
4. List of contract notices to consider, for the client.

## Example

*Made-up business for illustration: Kestrel Interiors, a shop fit-out contractor in Manchester, UK; café fit-out.*

> **Daily report 014, Wed 11 Mar. Author: site manager (notes), compiled by coordinator.**
> **Weather:** rain from 10:00 (site observation); internal work only, no stoppage.
> **On site:** Kestrel joiners 3; Patel Electrical 2; Northline Plastering 2 (left 13:00).
> **Work done:** counter carcass fixed (task 22); first-fix power to back wall complete (task 18).
> **Deliveries:** none. Plasterboard due 08:00 did not arrive.
> **Visitors:** none. **Safety:** extension lead across fire exit moved 09:15 (reported to site manager).
> **Delays:** plastering stood down 13:00, boards not delivered; supplier rebooked Thu 07:30.
> **Decisions needed:** client to confirm counter top colour by Fri 13 Mar (supplier lead time 10 days).
>
> **Delay log:** 11 Mar | late delivery (plasterboard) | task 24 plaster walls | critical: yes | 0.5 day [ESTIMATE] | site manager told supplier 12:40; client emailed 15:10 | delivery note missing, call log, photo of empty bay | rebooked Thu 07:30.
