# Content marketer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

## 1. What this role is for

A small business hires a content marketer to earn the attention and trust of people who will one day buy, and to turn that attention into subscribers, enquiries and sales. The work lives on channels the business owns (its site, its email list) and channels it borrows (search, social, communities, partners' audiences).

Who hires it: service firms that win work on trust, software and online businesses that need a steady flow of qualified visitors, shops whose buyers research before they buy, and founders who know their subject but have no time to publish.

What good work looks like:

- A strategy that starts from a business goal and says plainly what content is for: attract, convert, keep customers, or help sales close.
- A short list of topics the business can credibly own, drawn from real customer questions, not from a keyword tool alone.
- Pieces that answer a real question better than what already exists, with first-hand experience, original information and a clear author.
- A distribution plan for every piece. Publishing is the start, not the end.
- Measurement of what content earns (subscribers, key events, enquiries, assisted sales), not just page views.

How it is judged: contribution to leads, sales and list growth; visibility for the topics buyers search; how much of the audience comes back; cost per piece against what it earns; and whether every piece would pass Google's own people-first questions [1].

## 2. The canon

**Kristina Halvorson and Melissa Rach, *Content Strategy for the Web* (2nd edition, 2012) [18].** Content is a business asset, so plan its whole life: audit what exists, decide what it is for, create it, deliver it, measure it, and maintain or retire it. Most content problems are planning and ownership problems, not writing problems. The habit to keep: every piece has an owner, a purpose and a review date.

**Ann Handley, *Everybody Writes* (2nd edition, 2022) [17].** Good content marketing rests on good writing: clear, useful, empathetic, and edited hard. Handley's practical rules include writing for one reader, putting the reader's need ahead of the brand's, editing in passes, and building credibility with sources and specifics. The 2022 edition also covers the main content types marketers write [17].

**Marcus Sheridan, *They Ask, You Answer* (2nd edition, 2019) [19].** Sheridan used the method to rebuild a struggling swimming pool company. The idea: answer the questions buyers actually ask, honestly and fully, especially the ones businesses avoid. His "Big 5" topics are cost and price, problems, comparisons, reviews, and best-in-class lists. For small businesses this is often the fastest route to content that sells, because it is the content sales calls already need.

**Google's people-first content guidance [1][4].** Google tells creators to judge content by questions such as: does it offer original information or analysis; does it show first-hand experience; would someone feel they had learned enough; was it made mainly to attract search visits? It frames this as Who (clear authorship), How (how it was made, including any AI use readers would want to know about) and Why (to help people, not to manipulate rankings). E-E-A-T (experience, expertise, authoritativeness, trust) is how Google's systems try to identify helpful content; trust matters most, and it is not a single ranking factor [1]. On AI, Google's position since 8 February 2023: appropriate use is allowed, quality matters rather than method, and using automation mainly to manipulate rankings breaks its spam policies [4].

**Topic clusters (HubSpot) [12].** A pillar page covers a broad topic in depth and links out to cluster pages that each answer one narrower question; every cluster page links back. The structure helps readers move through a subject and helps search engines find related pages. HubSpot itself notes that its topic tool does not change SEO directly; the benefit comes from the links you actually publish [12].

**Jobs to be Done and voice-of-customer research [22][23].** The best topic research starts with why people buy. Moesta and Spiek's four forces (push of the current situation, pull of the new way, anxiety about change, habit of the present) map neatly onto content: content that names the push, shows the pull, and answers the anxiety [22]. Copyhackers' research methods (interviews, surveys, review mining, thank-you polls, analytics, competitor audits) are the same sources a content marketer mines for topics and language [23].

**How people read online (Nielsen Norman Group) [20][21].** People scan; in NN/g's 1997 study 79% scanned and 16% read word by word, and concise, scannable, objective writing scored far better than promotional writing [21]. Eyetracking shows the first lines and first words get the most attention, so put the answer first and make headings carry meaning on their own [20].

**Annual industry research [8][9][10].** The Content Marketing Institute (CMI) and Orbit Media publish yearly surveys of marketers. They are self-reported and skew to engaged respondents, but they are the best public view of what practitioners do and what they say works.

## 3. How the work is done now

**Strategy tied to a goal.** CMI's survey for 2025 (fielded June to August 2024, 980 B2B respondents) found 95% had a content strategy but only 29% rated it extremely or very effective. Among those whose strategy was moderately effective or worse, the top reasons were lack of clear goals (42%), content not tied to the stages a buyer goes through (39%), not grounded in data (35%), poor audience research (29%), unrealistic expectations (23%) and putting quantity over quality (20%) [8]. A small-business strategy fits on one page: the business goal, the audience, the two to four topics to own, the formats and channels, the cadence the team can sustain, and the measures.

**Audience and topic research.** Start from the questions customers already ask: sales calls, support tickets, reviews, the inbox, comments. Add Search Console queries for the pages that exist [13], keyword research tools for demand, and communities where buyers talk. Sheridan's Big 5 is a useful checklist for gaps [19]. Every topic gets a note on search intent (what the searcher wants to do) and on where it sits in buying: learning about the problem, comparing ways to solve it, or choosing a provider.

**Pillar and cluster planning.** Group topics into a few pillars that match what the business sells. Each pillar has one in-depth page and a set of cluster pages answering specific questions, linked both ways [12]. Plan internal links and calls to action at the brief stage, not after publishing.

**Search in 2026 (dated facts).**

- Google's March 2024 update introduced three spam policies: scaled content abuse (many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, including with generative AI, with little value), site reputation abuse (third-party pages riding a host site's reputation; enforced from 5 May 2024) and expired domain abuse [2][3].
- Updates are frequent. Google's status dashboard lists, for 2026, a March spam update (from 24 March), a March core update (from 27 March), a May core update (from 21 May), and spam updates in June, August and September (the last from 24 September) [6].
- AI Overviews and AI Mode use the same index. Google says there are no extra requirements to appear in them beyond being indexed and eligible for a snippet, and their traffic is counted in Search Console's Performance report under the Web search type [5].
- Clicks are falling where AI summaries appear. Pew Research Center's analysis of 900 US adults' browsing in March 2025 found people clicked a traditional result on 8% of visits to a results page with an AI summary, against 15% without one; links inside the summary were clicked on 1% of such visits; 18% of searches produced a summary; and 26% of visits with a summary ended the browsing session, against 16% without [7].
- Most pages earn no search traffic at all. Ahrefs' study of about 14 billion pages (published 1 December 2023) estimated 96.55% get zero Google organic traffic, mainly because nobody searches the topic, the page has no links, or it does not match search intent [11].

The practical result: search still matters, but a content plan that depends on search alone is fragile. Build the email list, earn direct visits, and distribute through channels you can reach without a ranking.

**Briefs and the editorial calendar.** Each piece gets a brief before drafting: reader, question, intent, the angle only this business can bring, sources and experts, structure, internal links, call to action, distribution and success measure. The calendar shows what publishes when, who owns each stage, and which pieces are refreshes. A cadence the team can keep beats an ambitious one it abandons.

**Distribution and repurposing.** Plan where each piece will go before writing it. In CMI's 2024 survey, the B2B channels rated as producing the best results were in-person events (52%), webinars (51%), email (42%), organic social media (42%) and corporate blogs (41%); the content types rated most effective were videos (58%), case studies and customer stories (53%), e-books and white papers (45%), research reports (45%) and short articles (43%) [8]. Repurposing turns one researched piece into an email, social posts, a short video, a sales one-pager and a talk, each rewritten for its channel rather than pasted.

**Email amplification.** Since 1 February 2024 Gmail has required bulk senders (over 5,000 messages a day to Gmail) to authenticate with SPF, DKIM and DMARC, to offer one-click unsubscribe, and to keep reported spam below 0.3% [15]. Apple Mail Privacy Protection downloads remote content in the background when a message arrives, hiding when and how often someone opens it [16], so judge email by clicks, replies and conversions, not opens.

**Measuring what content earns.** In GA4, mark the actions that matter to the business (a sign-up, an enquiry, a purchase) as key events [14]. In Search Console, watch clicks, impressions, click-through rate and average position by page and query [13]. Add a "How did you hear about us?" question to forms, because self-reported answers catch the influence that analytics misses. CMI found 56% of B2B marketers struggle to attribute return on investment to content and only 51% agree their organisation measures content performance well [8].

**Using AI without publishing slop.** CMI's 2024 survey found 81% of B2B content teams used generative AI, but only 19% had it in daily workflows and 4% had high trust in its output [8]. In CMI's survey for 2026 (published 8 October 2025), about 9 in 10 marketers used AI to produce content and fewer than 4 in 10 said it had improved performance [9]. Orbit Media's 2026 survey of 1,042 content marketers found nearly all used AI, no single AI task was linked to strong results, and original research and a formal human editing process were linked to better results [10]. The working rules: use AI for research sorting, outlines, first drafts and repurposing; never let it invent facts, quotes, statistics or experience; add what only the business knows; fact-check every claim; keep a human editor; give pieces a real author; and say how AI was used where readers would expect to know [1][4].

**Ethics in distribution.** Creator and partner content that the business pays for, or gives free product for, needs a clear disclosure in the content itself [24]. Fake reviews, including AI-generated ones, are banned in the US under the FTC's 2024 rule [25].

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Strategy and teams (CMI, published 9 October 2024, 980 B2B respondents, mostly North America):** 76% have a dedicated content person or team; 29% rate their strategy extremely or very effective; 46% of those who knew their budget expected it to rise in 2025 [8]. *Caveat:* B2B only, self-reported.
- **AI and results (CMI, 8 October 2025, more than 1,000 B2B marketers):** about 9 in 10 use AI to produce content; fewer than 4 in 10 say it improved performance; 45% named AI tools a top-three investment area for 2026, while people (salaries, training) ranked last at 9% [9]. *Caveat:* press release figures; the full report has more detail.
- **Blogging results (Orbit Media, 2026 survey, 1,042 content marketers):** about 14% report "strong results", the lowest in the survey's twelve-year run [10]. *Caveat:* self-reported, and Orbit's correlations between practices and results do not prove cause. Orbit's page blocks automated reading; these figures were confirmed through search results.
- **Search traffic (Ahrefs, 1 December 2023):** 96.55% of about 14 billion pages get zero estimated Google organic traffic; 1.94% get 1 to 10 visits a month [11]. *Caveat:* Ahrefs' own estimates, and its index skews to higher-quality sites.
- **AI summaries and clicks (Pew, 22 July 2025):** 8% vs 15% click-through on traditional results with and without an AI summary; 1% on links within the summary [7]. *Caveat:* US adults, one month of 2025 data; AI features have changed since.

No reliable public benchmark gives an ideal post length, posting frequency or "good" traffic per post across small businesses. Use the client's own history as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the business cannot say what content is for, agree one goal and one measure before planning topics, because CMI finds unclear goals are the top reason strategies fail [8].
- If sales or support hear the same question every week, write that piece first, because it has proven demand and an immediate use.
- If a topic has no search demand, publish it only if it serves email, sales or social, because it will earn no search traffic [11].
- If a page ranks but does not convert, fix the call to action and the match to intent before writing anything new.
- If a page is losing traffic, check whether the query changed, a competitor answered better, or the facts aged; then refresh, merge or retire it.
- If you are using AI for drafts, add first-hand experience, original data or expert input to every piece, because pages that only summarise others are what Google's guidance warns against [1].
- If you are tempted to publish many near-identical pages (one per town, one per keyword), stop, because that pattern is what the scaled content abuse policy targets [2].
- If AI summaries answer the query fully, aim the piece at what a summary cannot give: tools, templates, original data, worked examples, a point of view, and a reason to subscribe [7].
- If a piece performs well, repurpose it into three or more channels before making something new.
- If email open rates jump without clicks rising, assume privacy features, not interest [16].
- If paying a creator or giving free product, require a clear disclosure in the content [24].
- If the team can sustain one good piece a fortnight, plan one a fortnight.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **No goal.** Spot it: the plan lists topics and dates but no business outcome or measure.
- **Keyword-first topics.** Spot it: topics nobody in sales or support recognises.
- **Thin, summary content.** Spot it: nothing in the piece that the business learned first-hand; sources are other blogs.
- **AI slop.** Spot it: generic openings, padded lists, invented statistics, no author, and the same structure as every other result.
- **Publish and pray.** Spot it: no distribution plan; each piece got one social post.
- **Vanity metrics.** Spot it: reports show page views and opens, never key events or enquiries.
- **Scaling templates.** Spot it: dozens of pages differing only by a place or product name [2].
- **Cluster without links.** Spot it: a pillar page that no cluster page links to [12].
- **Content decay ignored.** Spot it: pages with falling clicks in Search Console that nobody has touched for a year.
- **Undisclosed paid content.** Spot it: creator posts without a clear ad or partnership label [24].
- **Unsustainable cadence.** Spot it: a burst of posts, then months of silence.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does the piece answer one clear question for one clear reader, and say the answer early?
2. Does it include something only this business can offer: experience, data, examples, an expert?
3. Is every fact, number and quote checked against a source, and linked where useful?
4. Is there a real author with relevant background, and a published or updated date?
5. If AI helped, did a human edit it, check it, and add the parts that matter?
6. Do headings make sense when skimmed alone, with the key point first?
7. Does it link to the pillar page and the next logical piece, with one clear call to action?
8. Is the search title clear and specific, and does the page match the searcher's intent?
9. Is the distribution plan written, with channels, dates and the repurposed versions?
10. Is the success measure set (key event, sign-ups, enquiries) and tracked?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Strategy one-pager:* goal, audience, topics to own, formats, channels, cadence, measures, owner.
- *Topic map:* pillars, cluster topics, intent, buying stage, priority, status.
- *Editorial calendar:* piece, owner, draft date, publish date, distribution dates, refresh date.
- *Content brief:* one page per piece, enough for a writer who has never met the client.
- *Draft:* answer first, scannable headings, sources linked, author and date, call to action.
- *Distribution pack:* email copy, social posts per platform, short video script, sales snippet.
- *Monthly report:* what was published, what it earned (key events, subscribers, enquiries), what is decaying, what to do next.

**Tone and conventions.** Helpful before promotional. Plain words and short paragraphs. The reader's question in the title, the answer in the first lines. Specifics over generalities. Sources named and linked. Each channel version written for that channel. Notes to the client kept separate from publishable copy.

## 9. Glossary

- **Pillar page:** an in-depth page on a broad topic that links to related cluster pages [12].
- **Topic cluster:** a pillar page plus the pages answering narrower questions, linked both ways [12].
- **Search intent:** what a searcher wants to do: learn, compare, buy or find a place.
- **E-E-A-T:** experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust; how Google's systems judge helpfulness [1].
- **YMYL:** "your money or your life" topics (health, money, safety) where Google weighs trust more heavily [1].
- **People-first content:** made mainly to help a reader, not to attract search visits [1].
- **Scaled content abuse:** many pages made mainly to manipulate rankings, with little value [2].
- **Site reputation abuse:** third-party pages hosted to ride a site's ranking signals [2].
- **AI Overviews and AI Mode:** Google's AI-generated answers in search results, with links to sources [5].
- **Zero-click search:** a search that ends without a click to any website.
- **Content brief:** the one-page plan a writer drafts from.
- **Editorial calendar:** the schedule of what publishes when, and who owns each stage.
- **Distribution:** getting a piece in front of readers after publishing.
- **Repurposing:** rewriting one piece for other formats and channels.
- **Key event:** an action marked in GA4 as important to the business [14].
- **Impressions, CTR, average position:** how often a page appeared in Google results, the share that clicked, and its typical rank [13].
- **Content decay:** a page's slow loss of traffic over time.
- **Refresh:** updating an existing page with new facts, depth or structure.
- **Cannibalisation:** two of your own pages competing for the same query.
- **Owned audience:** people you can reach directly, such as email subscribers.
- **Original research:** data the business gathers itself, such as a survey of its customers.
- **One-click unsubscribe:** an unsubscribe that works without logging in; required for Gmail bulk senders [15].

## 10. What changes fast

Re-check at each refresh:

- **Google ranking and spam updates:** the Search Status Dashboard [6] and Search Central blog.
- **Google's guidance on AI features and helpful content:** the AI features page [5] and the people-first content page [1].
- **Click behaviour around AI answers:** new studies from Pew and others [7].
- **Annual surveys:** CMI (usually October) [8][9] and Orbit Media [10].
- **Email rules:** Gmail and Yahoo sender requirements [15] and mailbox privacy features [16].
- **Analytics features:** GA4 and Search Console help pages [13][14].
- **Disclosure and review rules:** FTC pages [24][25], and local equivalents in other markets.
- **Social platforms:** reach, formats and algorithms change often; check each platform's own creator help pages before advising.
