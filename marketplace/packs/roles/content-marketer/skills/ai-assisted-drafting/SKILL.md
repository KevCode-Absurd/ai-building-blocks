# AI-assisted drafting

AI can make drafting faster. It cannot supply the things that make content worth reading: first-hand experience, the business's own data, a real opinion, checked facts. Google's guidance is clear that appropriate AI use is allowed, that quality is what counts, and that mass-producing low-value pages to rank breaks its spam policies. This skill keeps the speed and removes the slop.

## When to use

- Drafting any article, guide, newsletter or post with AI help.
- Editing an AI draft someone else produced.
- Setting the rules for a team that uses AI in content.

Not for: producing many near-identical pages at scale. If the request is "200 location pages", stop and use the content-strategy skill instead.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the brief and source material. Level 2 to save the draft in your CMS or shared folder. Level 3 to publish it, within your limits, after the named human approves it.

- A content brief (use the content-brief skill), including the angle and the must-includes.
- The business's own material: notes, transcripts, data, examples, photos, prices, past content in the brand's voice.
- A named human who will check facts and approve the piece, and the author whose byline it will carry.

## Steps

1. **Check the brief has an angle.** If nothing in it is first-hand or original, do not draft; ask for an interview, data or examples first.
2. **Load only trusted material.** Give the AI the brief and the business's own sources. Tell it to use nothing else for facts.
3. **Draft with placeholders.** Instruct the AI to write [EXPERIENCE], [EXAMPLE], [NUMBER: source needed] or [QUOTE] wherever the piece needs something it does not have. It must never fill these itself.
4. **Fill the placeholders from real sources.** The expert or owner supplies stories, examples and numbers. Every number gets its source and date.
5. **Run the fact pass.** List every factual claim, number, name and date. Check each against a primary source. Remove or fix anything you cannot check.
6. **Run the originality pass.** Ask: what does this piece contain that the top five search results do not? If the answer is nothing, add original material or do not publish.
7. **Run the slop pass.** Cut or rewrite:
   - openings that restate the question or announce what the piece will do;
   - padded lists of three where one point is real;
   - vague claims with no example or number;
   - stock phrases the brand's customers never use;
   - a closing summary that repeats the piece;
   - headings that are clever but do not say what the section contains.
8. **Run the voice pass.** Read it aloud against two pieces the brand is proud of. Adjust sentence length, words and tone to match.
9. **Run the structure pass.** Answer first; headings that make sense alone; short paragraphs; links to the pillar, related pieces and sources; one call to action.
10. **Add who and how.** A real author byline with relevant background. Where readers would reasonably want to know how it was made, add a short note on AI's role. Never list AI as the author.
11. **Final approval.** The named human approves before publishing. At level 3 I then publish it within the client's limits and log it; at level 1 or 2 the client publishes.

## Checks

- No invented experience, quotes, statistics, studies or reviews.
- Every number has a source and a date.
- The piece contains something the top results do not.
- Placeholders are all filled or removed.
- Voice matches the brand's best work.
- Real author byline; AI disclosure where readers would expect it.
- A human approved it.

## Output format

1. **Draft** with any unfilled placeholders clearly marked.
2. **Fact log:** claim, source, date checked, status (verified, fixed, removed).
3. **Edit notes:** what the slop, voice and structure passes changed, and why.
4. **Originality note:** what this piece adds that others do not.
5. **Publishing details:** author, disclosure note if any, approver.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a small online tea shop. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Before (raw AI opening):**
> In today's fast-paced world, many people are discovering the wonderful benefits of loose-leaf tea. In this article, we will explore everything you need to know about brewing the perfect cup.

**After (edited, with owner material):**
> Most bitter green tea is brewed too hot. In our tasting room we brew sencha at 70 to 75°C for one minute, and customers who switch from boiling water usually tell us the bitterness is gone. Here is the method, with the times for each tea we sell.

**Fact log (extract)**

| Claim | Source | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Sencha brewed at 70 to 75°C for one minute | The shop's own brewing card, owner confirmed | Verified |
| "Customers usually tell us the bitterness is gone" | Owner's account of tasting-room feedback; no survey | Kept as the owner's experience, worded as such |
| "Green tea boosts metabolism by 20%" (added by AI) | No source found | Removed |

**Edit notes:** cut the generic opening and the "everything you need to know" promise; replaced with a specific first-hand fact; removed an unsupported health claim; changed headings from "The Art of Brewing" to "Water temperature for each tea".

**Originality note:** the piece gives exact times for each of the shop's teas, from its own tasting notes; no top result does this for these teas.

**Publishing details:** author Priya, owner and tea buyer; note at the end: "Drafted with AI help from our tasting notes; checked and edited by Priya." Approved by Priya.
