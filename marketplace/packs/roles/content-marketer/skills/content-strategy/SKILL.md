# Content strategy

Most content fails before anyone writes it: no goal, no audience research, and a cadence nobody can keep. This skill produces a strategy that fits on one page and can be checked against results every month.

## When to use

- A business wants to start a blog, newsletter, video series or resource hub.
- Content exists but nobody can say what it earns.
- The team argues about topics and needs a way to decide.
- Before an editorial calendar, so the calendar has something to serve.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 for every step, because the strategy is a document. Level 2 to put the calendar and first briefs in your shared folder or project board.

- **The business goal** for the next 6 to 12 months (for example: 30 more qualified enquiries a month, a new service line, more repeat orders).
- **Who buys** and who influences the purchase.
- **Customer questions:** from sales calls, support tickets, reviews, the inbox, comments. Twenty real questions is a good start.
- **What exists:** a list of current content and, if available, a Search Console export (queries and pages, last 3 to 6 months) and GA4 key events by page.
- **Resources:** who can write, edit, film or speak, and how many hours a month.
- **Competitors** or other sources buyers read.

If there is no data at all, start from customer questions and the team's own knowledge, and set up measurement before the first piece goes live.

## Steps

1. **Pin the goal and content's job.** Write one sentence in this shape: *content will help [business goal] by [attracting / converting / keeping customers / helping sales], measured by [key event or number].*
2. **Describe the audience in their situation, not demographics.** What pushes them to look, what they want, what worries them, what they do now instead. Use customers' words.
3. **Collect and sort the questions.** Group the customer questions by buying stage: learning about the problem, comparing ways to fix it, choosing a provider. Check gaps against Sheridan's Big 5: cost and price, problems, comparisons, reviews, best-in-class.
4. **Add demand.** For each group, check search demand (keyword tool or Search Console impressions) and note the intent. Mark topics with no search demand that still matter for sales or email.
5. **Choose the topics to own.** Pick two to four pillars where three things overlap: buyers care, the business has real experience to share, and the business sells something related. Drop topics that fail any one of the three.
6. **Shape pillars and clusters.** For each pillar: one in-depth pillar page and five to fifteen cluster pieces, each answering one question and linking back.
7. **Pick formats and channels.** Choose formats the team can make well (written guides, short videos, email, case studies, webinars). For each pillar, name the owned channel (site, email) and one or two borrowed channels (search, a social platform, a community, a partner).
8. **Set a cadence the team can keep.** Base it on the hours available, with time for refreshes. One good piece a fortnight that is distributed well beats four thin ones a week.
9. **Set the measures.** One primary measure tied to the goal (key events, enquiries, subscribers), two supporting measures (clicks from search, email clicks), and a review rhythm. Add "How did you hear about us?" to forms.
10. **Set rules for AI and quality.** Who checks facts, who edits, what every piece must include that only the business can add, and how AI use is disclosed where readers would want to know.
11. **Write the first 90 days.** The first six to ten pieces, in priority order: proven questions from sales first.

## Checks

- One goal and one primary measure, written down.
- Every pillar passes all three tests: buyers care, the business has experience, it relates to what is sold.
- Topics come from real customer questions, not only a keyword tool.
- Cadence matches the hours available.
- Every piece in the first 90 days has a distribution channel.
- Measurement is set up before publishing.
- No plan to mass-produce near-identical pages.

## Output format

1. **Strategy one-pager:** goal and content's job; audience; pillars (with the reason each passes); formats and channels; cadence; measures; quality and AI rules; owner.
2. **Question map:** table of customer questions by buying stage, with source, search demand and pillar.
3. **First 90 days:** table of pieces with pillar, question, format, channel, owner, target date.
4. **Assumptions and gaps.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Brindle and Moss Garden Design is a made-up business used only to show the format: a two-person garden design studio. All research details are invented for this example.*

**Goal and content's job:** content will help win 8 more design consultations a month by answering the questions homeowners ask before they call, measured by consultation requests from content pages (a GA4 key event).

**Audience:** homeowners with a neglected or new-build garden who want it to feel usable and look good, worried mostly about cost and about choosing someone whose style they like. Today they browse photos and get landscaper quotes that do not include design.

**Pillars**

| Pillar | Why it passes | Example cluster questions |
|---|---|---|
| What a garden design costs | Top question in 14 of 20 enquiry emails; the studio quotes weekly | "How much does a garden designer cost?", "Design fee vs build cost", "Can I do it in stages?" |
| Small and new-build gardens | 60% of recent projects; real before and after photos | "Ideas for a narrow garden", "How to fix clay soil in a new-build garden" |
| Working with a designer | Biggest worry in sales calls is the process and style fit | "What happens at a consultation?", "Designer vs landscaper", "How to brief a designer" |

**Formats and channels:** written guides with project photos on the site; a monthly email with one project story; Instagram for before and after; the guides linked in every quote email.

**Cadence:** two pieces a month (about 10 hours) plus one refresh a quarter.

**Measures:** primary, consultation requests from content pages; supporting, Search Console clicks to the three pillar pages and email click rate. Reviewed on the first Monday of each month.

**AI and quality:** AI may outline and draft; the designer adds real project details, prices from the studio's own quotes, and photos; one person fact-checks every number.

**First 90 days (top three):** 1. "How much does a garden designer cost?" (pillar page, with real price ranges). 2. "Designer vs landscaper: which do you need?" 3. Case study: a narrow new-build garden, with the owner's permission.
