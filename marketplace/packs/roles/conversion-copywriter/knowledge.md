# Conversion copywriter: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

## 1. What this role is for

A small business hires a conversion copywriter when people arrive but do not act. The page gets visits and few bookings. The email gets opens and few clicks. The trial gets sign-ups and few upgrades. The job is to move a specific reader to one specific action, honestly, using words the reader already uses.

Who hires it: founders writing their own site, shops with a product page that stalls, service firms whose enquiry form sits quiet, software teams whose pricing page leaks, and marketers who need ads, landing pages and emails that match each other.

What good work looks like:

- It starts with research, not a blank page. The copywriter can show where each claim, phrase and objection came from: a review, a survey answer, a sales call, a support ticket.
- It picks one reader, one stage of awareness and one action, and every section serves that action.
- Claims are specific and provable. Proof sits next to the claim it supports.
- It reads easily on a phone, at a glance, because most people scan [13][15].
- It comes with a way to judge it: a control to beat, a metric, and a plan for how long to wait before calling a result.

How it is judged: by the action rate against a control (not by opinion), by the quality of what follows (refunds, lead quality, churn), and by whether every claim holds up if a regulator, a competitor or a customer checks it.

## 2. The canon

These are the ideas practitioners lean on. Each is summarised in my own words and credited.

**Claude Hopkins, *Scientific Advertising* (1923) [2].** Advertising is selling in print, so judge it the way you judge a salesperson: by sales you can trace. Hopkins keyed coupons to each ad so he could count which version paid. He argued for specific claims over vague superlatives, for samples that let people try before they buy, and for testing small before spending big. The habit to keep: every piece of copy should have a way to be measured.

**John Caples, *Tested Advertising Methods* (first published 1932) [4].** Caples spent decades comparing ads by their keyed returns. His lessons: the headline does most of the work; headlines that promise the reader a benefit beat clever ones; curiosity alone tends to lose unless it is tied to self-interest; and you test before you roll out. He is the reason copywriters write many headlines for every one they use.

**Eugene Schwartz, *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) [1].** Two tools from this book shape almost every brief.

- *Copy cannot create desire.* It can only take a desire that already exists in the market and point it at your product. So research comes first: find the desire, then channel it.
- *Awareness.* Schwartz described how much a prospect already knows, from fully aware of your product and only waiting for the right deal, down to completely unaware of the problem. Today these are usually labelled most aware, product aware, solution aware, problem aware and unaware. The less aware the reader, the further from the product your opening has to start.
- *Sophistication.* How many similar claims has this market already heard? In a new market a direct claim works. As rivals copy it, claims get bigger, then the winners explain a new mechanism (why their way works), then a bigger mechanism, and finally, when buyers are tired of all claims, the copy wins by showing it understands who the reader is.

**David Ogilvy, *Ogilvy on Advertising* (1983) [3].** Do the research before you write. Put the benefit in the headline, because far more people read headlines than body copy. Facts sell better than adjectives. Long copy can outsell short copy when every line is specific and useful. Respect the reader's intelligence: people see through puffery.

**Joseph Sugarman, *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* (Wiley, 2007 edition) [5].** Every element of copy has one job: get the next line read. The headline gets the first sentence read; the first sentence is short and easy; each paragraph pulls into the next. Sugarman also catalogued the emotional and logical reasons people buy, and he pushed copywriters to know the product better than anyone before writing.

**Robert Cialdini, *Influence* (1984; new and expanded edition 2021) [6].** Seven principles describe why people say yes: reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, scarcity, and unity (shared identity, added in 2021). **The ethical line:** use a principle only when it is true for this offer. Real scarcity, real reviews, real credentials and real deadlines persuade. Faked versions are deception, and in the US and UK many of them are now explicitly unlawful [22][23][27].

**Bob Moesta, Jobs to be Done and *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020) [7][8].** People do not buy products; they hire them to make progress in a specific situation. Four forces decide whether someone switches: the *push* of their current situation, the *pull* of the new way, the *anxiety* about the new way, and the *habit* of the present. Copy that only adds pull often fails because anxiety and habit were never addressed. Switch interviews walk a recent buyer back through the timeline of their purchase to find these forces.

**Joanna Wiebe and Copyhackers [9][10].** Conversion copywriting starts with voice-of-customer (VoC) research. *Message mining* means reading reviews, forums and comments about the solutions your buyers use today, copying their exact words into a table, and lifting the lines that name a pain, a desire or a doubt better than you could. Copyhackers lists the research methods it uses: interviews, surveys, review mining, heatmaps and scroll maps, thank-you-page polls, analytics, and competitor audits [10].

**CXL [11][12].** CXL's conversion research teaching pairs on-site polls and customer surveys with analytics. Survey recent first-time buyers while the purchase is fresh, ask open-ended questions so you get their language, and collect enough answers to see patterns (CXL suggests at least about 100) [12]. Use the findings to write, then test.

**Nielsen Norman Group on how people read online [13][14][15].** People scan. In 1997 NN/g found 79% of test users scanned new pages and only 16% read word by word; copy that was concise, scannable and objective (not promotional) scored 124% better on their usability measures than the promotional version [13]. A 2008 analysis estimated people have time to read at most about 28% of the words on an average page visit, more likely around 20% [14]. Eyetracking shows an F-shaped scan when a page gives no strong cues, plus other patterns such as reading only headings (layer-cake) or hunting for one item (spotted) [15]. The fix is front-loading: the key point first, headings that carry meaning on their own, and descriptive link and button text.

**Experimentation: Ron Kohavi and colleagues; Evan Miller [17][20][21].** Ideas are hard to value in advance, even for experts, so test the ones that matter [17]. Decide the sample size before you start and do not stop the moment a result looks good; peeking inflates false wins [20]. *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* (2020) is the standard practical guide [21].

## 3. How the work is done now

**Research before writing.** The working sequence in 2026 is the same one Hopkins and Ogilvy argued for, with better tools:

1. *Reviews.* Your own reviews, competitors' reviews, and reviews of the alternatives people use instead (including doing nothing). Sort lines into pains, desired outcomes, doubts, and the words people use for the product category [9].
2. *Surveys and polls.* A short open-ended survey to recent buyers ("What nearly stopped you?", "What were you using before?", "What made you choose us?") and a one-question poll on the page that leaks [10][12].
3. *Sales calls and support tickets.* The objections people say out loud, and the confusions that show up after purchase. Ask for recordings or transcripts with the customer's permission.
4. *Behaviour.* Analytics (where people drop), session recordings and heatmaps (what they skip, what they tap that is not a link).
5. *Competitors.* What everyone in the category already claims, which tells you the market's sophistication [1].

**From research to a message hierarchy.** Before any copy, write the order of ideas: the one thing the reader must believe to act, the two or three reasons that support it, the top objections, the proof for each, and the action. The hierarchy is the strategy; the copy is how it sounds.

**Page structure follows awareness.** Use the reader's stage to decide where the page starts [1]:

| Stage | Opening | Body | Typical length |
|---|---|---|---|
| Most aware | The offer, price and action | Terms, guarantee, what happens next | Short |
| Product aware | Strongest difference, with proof | Comparison, reviews, objections | Medium |
| Solution aware | The result, and why this way works | Mechanism, proof, how it works, objections | Medium to long |
| Problem aware | The problem in the reader's words | Cost of the problem, the solution type, then you | Long |
| Unaware | A story, news or identity hook | Earn attention before any pitch | Long, often not a landing page at all |

**Headlines and leads by stage.** Following the same logic, match the opening to the reader. An *offer lead* (the deal, up front) suits the most aware. A *promise lead* (the main benefit, proved fast) suits the product aware. A *how-it-works lead* (the result plus the mechanism) suits the solution aware and sophisticated markets. A *problem lead* (their pain, in their words) suits the problem aware. A *story, news or identity lead* suits the unaware, who must be given a reason to keep reading before any pitch. Caples's tests point the same way for headlines: say what the reader gets, and use curiosity only when it is tied to that benefit [4].

**Offers and risk reversal.** The offer is what the reader gets, what it costs, and what happens if it does not work. Risk reversal (a guarantee, a free trial, pay after results, a sample) moves risk from buyer to seller. Hopkins used samples for exactly this reason [2]. The terms must be honest, plain and honoured.

**Objections and proof.** List the reasons people do not buy, from research, then answer each one where the doubt arises, usually near the action. Proof types, strongest first in most markets: a demonstration or specific result, named reviews from real customers, numbers you can show, third-party ratings, credentials, a guarantee. In the US, testimonials showing unusual results need a clear statement of what people generally get, and connections between the business and a reviewer must be disclosed [24][25].

**Calls to action.** One primary action per page or email. The button says what the reader gets or does next ("Book a free fitting", "See my quote"), not a generic word. Microcopy under the button answers the last doubt (cost, commitment, time).

**Editing for clarity.** Cut every line that does not move the reader toward the action. Prefer concrete nouns, short sentences and the reader's words. Remove promotional filler: NN/g found objective language alone improved measured usability by 27% [13].

**Testing.** Write a hypothesis from research ("Visitors doubt the fit; adding the fit guarantee near the button will raise orders"). Size the test before launch [20]. Run whole weeks. Pick one primary metric and one guardrail (refunds, lead quality). If a page cannot reach the sample size in a reasonable time, test the idea where volume is higher, such as ad headlines or email subject lines, and treat page changes as considered bets, not proven wins.

**AI in the copy process.** If you draft with AI, use it to sort research, propose variants and check for gaps. Do not let it invent proof, quotes, numbers or reviews. Generic phrasing is the main failure: every line should trace back to the research file or the product facts.

**The legal and ethical floor (dated).**

- US, fake reviews: the FTC's Trade Regulation Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (16 CFR Part 465) was announced 14 August 2024 and took effect 21 October 2024. It bans fake reviews and testimonials (AI-generated ones included), buying reviews that must express a particular sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled sites posing as independent reviewers, review suppression by threats, and fake social media indicators. Civil penalties are possible for knowing violations [22][23].
- US, endorsements: the FTC updated its Endorsement Guides on 29 June 2023. Asking only happy customers for reviews when that skews results, delaying or hiding negative reviews, and showing high-star reviews first by default can all mislead [24][25].
- US, dark patterns: the FTC's staff report *Bringing Dark Patterns to Light* (15 September 2022) names disguised ads, countdown timers on offers that are not really limited, hard-to-cancel subscriptions, buried terms and hidden fees, and tricks that push people to share data [26].
- UK: the consumer protection parts of the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 apply from 6 April 2025. The CMA can now decide breaches itself and fine up to 10% of global turnover; fake reviews and drip pricing are explicitly banned [27]. The CMA's fake reviews guidance (CMA208) was published 4 April 2025 [28]. The CMA's 2022 work on online choice architecture explains how page design can harm consumers [30].
- UK ads: CAP/ASA advice (20 April 2023) says a countdown must reflect a real time limit; when it hits zero the offer ends, and timers must not restart [29].

This is general practice, not legal advice. For a specific claim in a regulated area, the client should ask a qualified adviser or the regulator's copy advice service.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations, never as targets. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Landing page conversion.** Unbounce's 2024 Conversion Benchmark Report: median conversion rate 6.6% across more than 41,000 landing pages, 464 million visitors and 57 million conversions. Pages written at a grade 5 to 7 reading level had a median of 11.1%, against 5.3% for professional-level writing. *Caveats:* pages built on one platform only; medians hide huge spread by industry and offer; the reading-level link is a correlation, and Unbounce notes financial services as an exception [16].
- **Reading behaviour.** 79% of users scanned and 16% read word by word (NN/g, 1997, small lab study) [13]. At most about 28% of words read per average page visit (NN/g, 2008, 25 users, data collected 2005) [14]. *Caveat:* old and small samples; the direction of the finding has held up in NN/g's later eyetracking work [15].
- **Published test results.** Real, documented, and useful as reasons to test, not as templates:
  - Copyhackers, Beachway rehab centre (2014): a headline lifted from a book review beat the control with more than 400% more clicks on the home page button and more than 20% more lead form submissions on the next page [9].
  - Obama 2008 campaign splash page (published 2010): a 24-combination test of buttons and media; "Learn More" with a family photo raised sign-ups from 8.26% to 11.6%, a 40.6% lift. The often-quoted $60 million is the author's extrapolation, not measured revenue [18].
  - Bing ad headline display change (HBR, 2017): about 12% more revenue, estimated at over $100 million a year in the US, from an idea that sat unprioritised for months [17].
  - GOV.UK organ donor sign-up (2013, reported 2014): eight calls to action shown to over a million visitors; a reciprocity message ("If you needed an organ transplant, would you have one?") won, projected at about 96,000 extra registrations a year [19].
- **False wins from peeking.** In Evan Miller's worked example, checking a test repeatedly and stopping at the first "significant" reading produced a 26.1% false-positive rate where 5% was intended. His rule of thumb for sample size per variant: n ≈ 16 × p(1 − p) / δ², where p is the current conversion rate and δ the smallest absolute change worth detecting [20].

No source gives a trustworthy "average email click rate" or "good conversion rate" for small businesses across industries. Use the client's own history as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the reader already knows and wants the product, lead with the offer and the action, because extra persuasion only delays a ready buyer.
- If the reader knows the problem but not the solution, open with the problem in their own words, because recognition earns the right to pitch [1].
- If every competitor makes the same claim, do not make a bigger one; explain the mechanism or show you understand who the reader is, because a tired market discounts claims [1].
- If you cannot prove a claim, cut it or narrow it to what you can prove, because unproven claims cost trust and may break advertising rules [24].
- If research shows one dominant doubt, answer it right beside the main button, because that is where the doubt stops people.
- If traffic arrives from an ad or email, repeat its promise in the first screen, because a mismatch reads as a bait and switch.
- If the page has one goal, give it one primary action; move secondary links below or into the footer.
- If a testimonial shows an unusual result, add what customers typically get, or choose a typical testimonial [24].
- If scarcity or a deadline is not real, do not use it. If it is real, state the true reason and honour it [26][29].
- If the required sample size will take more than about six to eight weeks to reach, do not A/B test the page; test the idea in a higher-volume channel, or ship the change as a judgement call and watch the trend [20].
- If a test result looks too good, check for a bug before celebrating, because big surprises are usually errors [17].
- If the client wants copy without research, ask for at least twenty reviews or five customer conversations, because without them the copy is a guess.
- If the reader is unaware of the problem, question whether a landing page is the right tool at all; content usually has to do that job first.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Writing before research.** Spot it: no customer phrases in the copy, and nobody can say where a claim came from.
- **Clever beats clear.** Spot it: a stranger cannot say what is being sold after reading the headline and subhead for five seconds.
- **Features without outcomes.** Spot it: sentences end on a spec, never on what changes for the reader.
- **Vague superlatives.** "Best quality", "world-class", "trusted by many". Spot it: no number, name or comparison anywhere near the claim.
- **Wrong awareness level.** Spot it: a cold audience lands on a price-first page, or a returning buyer has to scroll past a long problem story.
- **Proof far from claims.** Spot it: all reviews sit in one carousel at the bottom.
- **Several competing actions.** Spot it: three buttons of equal weight in the first screen.
- **Fake or inflated urgency.** Spot it: a timer that restarts on reload, "only 2 left" that never changes, a sale that never ends [26][29].
- **Cherry-picked or invented reviews.** Spot it: only five-star quotes, no names, no dates, or reviews written by staff [22][24].
- **Stopping tests early.** Spot it: the test ran four days, or stopped "when it hit significance" [20].
- **Copying someone else's winner.** Spot it: "Big brand X used this button colour" as the rationale.
- **AI sameness.** Spot it: lines that could sit on any competitor's page, rule-of-three padding, and stock phrases that no customer ever said.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can I point to the research behind the main message and the top three objections?
2. Does the first screen say what this is, who it is for and what to do, readable in five seconds on a phone?
3. Does the opening match the reader's awareness and the promise of the traffic source?
4. Is every claim specific, true and backed by proof placed next to it?
5. Are testimonials real, attributed, used with permission, typical or qualified, and any connections disclosed?
6. Is there one primary action, with a button that names the outcome and microcopy that answers the last doubt?
7. Are urgency, scarcity and price claims true and honoured?
8. Is the guarantee or risk reversal stated plainly, with its conditions?
9. Read aloud: does each line pull into the next, with no filler and no jargon the reader would not use?
10. Is there a test or measurement plan with a control, a primary metric, a guardrail and a sample size?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Research file:* a VoC bank in a table (quote, source, date, theme: pain, desire, doubt, trigger, words for the category), plus a one-page summary of what it means.
- *Message hierarchy:* one core promise, supporting reasons, objections with answers, proof inventory, the action.
- *Copy document:* section by section, each labelled with its job ("answers the price doubt") and the research it draws on, written in the order the reader sees it, with headline and button variants.
- *Test plan:* hypothesis, variant, primary metric, guardrail, sample size, run time, decision rule.

**Tone and conventions.** Second person. The reader's words over the company's. Short paragraphs, front-loaded headings that tell the story when read alone, numerals for numbers, buttons that start with a verb. No hype words, no exclamation marks doing the work proof should do. Notes to the client are separate from the copy, so nothing internal ships by accident.

## 9. Glossary

- **Awareness stage:** how much the reader already knows about the problem, the solutions and your product [1].
- **Market sophistication:** how many similar claims the market has already heard [1].
- **Voice of customer (VoC):** customers' own words, gathered from reviews, surveys, calls and tickets.
- **Message mining:** reading reviews and comments to collect exact customer phrases [9].
- **Message hierarchy:** the ordered list of what the reader must believe to act.
- **Value proposition:** the main reason this buyer should choose this offer over the alternatives, including doing nothing.
- **Mechanism:** the explanation of why your way works, used when claims alone are worn out.
- **Lead:** the opening of a piece of copy, after the headline.
- **Call to action (CTA):** the one thing you ask the reader to do, and the button or link that does it.
- **Microcopy:** the small text around buttons and forms that removes doubt.
- **Risk reversal:** terms that move risk from buyer to seller, such as a guarantee or trial.
- **Social proof:** evidence that people like the reader chose this and were glad [6].
- **Objection:** a reason the reader hesitates or says no.
- **Four forces:** push, pull, anxiety and habit, the forces that decide whether someone switches [8].
- **Message match:** the landing page repeats the promise of the ad or email that sent the visitor.
- **Control and variant:** the current version, and the new version you test against it.
- **Minimum detectable effect (MDE):** the smallest change a test is sized to detect.
- **Peeking:** checking a running test and stopping when it looks good; it inflates false wins [20].
- **Guardrail metric:** a metric you watch so a "win" does not cause harm elsewhere.
- **Dark pattern:** a design that tricks people into choices they would not otherwise make [26].
- **Drip pricing:** showing part of the price first and adding unavoidable fees later [27].
- **Material connection:** a link between a reviewer and the business (payment, free product, job, family) that must be disclosed [24].

## 10. What changes fast

Re-check at each refresh:

- **FTC rules and guidance** on reviews, endorsements and dark patterns, and any court rulings on them: ftc.gov press releases and business guidance [22][24][26].
- **UK enforcement** under the DMCC Act, new CMA guidance versions and the first fines: gov.uk CMA pages [27][28].
- **ASA rulings and CAP advice** on urgency, pricing and testimonials: asa.org.uk [29].
- **Benchmark reports**, which are re-issued yearly, such as Unbounce's [16].
- **NN/g articles** on reading behaviour, which are reviewed and updated [15].
- **Tooling:** which testing, heatmap and survey tools exist, their pricing and their AI features. Check vendors' own pages before recommending one.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock AI phrasing.** Headlines and buttons with "Unlock", "Elevate", "Seamless" or "Game-changer". They mark the page as machine-written; use the buyer's own words from reviews and calls.
- **Rule of three everywhere.** Every section as three benefits or three bullet points. The rhythm reads as default; say the point that answers the reader's doubt.
- **Headline formula clichés.** "Say goodbye to X", "The X you've been waiting for", "X made simple". They fit any product; write the headline from the reader's awareness stage and this offer.
- **Fake urgency.** Countdowns, "only 3 left" or "offer ends tonight" with nothing real behind them. It breaks trust and can break the law; state the true reason or drop it.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "Save time", "boost productivity", "trusted quality". If a rival could publish it unchanged, explain the mechanism or the proof instead.
- **The default page skeleton.** Hero, three benefit cards, "how it works in 3 steps", testimonials, FAQ, final CTA, whatever the product. Build the order from the buyer's questions and doubts.
- **"Trusted by" with no logos.** A trust line or logo row with no real names or numbers behind it. Use the real proof the client has, or leave it out.
