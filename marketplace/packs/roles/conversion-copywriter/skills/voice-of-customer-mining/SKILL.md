# Voice-of-customer mining

Copy cannot create desire; it can only point an existing desire at a product (Schwartz). So the first job is to find the desire, the doubts and the words, in the customer's own language. This skill produces the research file every other copy skill reads.

## When to use

- Before writing or rewriting a landing page, product page, sales email, ad or pricing page.
- When copy "sounds like everyone else" and the client cannot say why.
- When a test lost and you need a better hypothesis.
- When a business is entering a new segment and needs to hear that segment's words.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 for every step. I only read the sources you share, and nothing here changes your tools.

At least one of these, ideally three:

- 20 or more reviews: the client's own, competitors', and reviews of whatever people use instead (including doing it themselves).
- Open-ended survey answers from recent buyers (best within a few weeks of purchase).
- Notes or transcripts from sales calls, discovery calls or customer interviews.
- Support tickets, live chat logs, or emails that ask questions before or after buying.
- Comments on posts and ads, forum threads, community posts.

Plus the product facts: what it is, price, terms, who it is for.

If the client has none of this, ask for the fastest route: their last 30 reviews, plus five emails from people who asked questions before buying. If there is truly nothing, say the copy will be a hypothesis and suggest a five-question survey to recent buyers.

**Privacy.** Remove names, emails, phone numbers and order numbers before analysis. Quotes used in public copy as testimonials need the customer's permission and must be real and unedited in meaning.

## Steps

1. **Set the question.** Write one line: "Who is deciding what, and what do we need to learn?" Example: riders aged 35 to 60 deciding whether to pay for a bike fit; we need their triggers, doubts and words.
2. **Log the sources.** Give each source an ID (R for review, S for survey, C for call, T for ticket) and note where and when it came from, and whether it is the client's customer or a competitor's.
3. **Read everything once without coding.** Note first impressions: repeated phrases, surprises, strong emotion.
4. **Code each useful line into one theme** (Moesta's four forces plus two copy themes):
   - *Push:* the pain or moment that made them look ("my knees ached after every long ride").
   - *Pull:* the result they want, in their words.
   - *Anxiety:* doubts about buying ("will it be worth the money", "I'm not a serious cyclist").
   - *Habit:* what they did before or what keeps them stuck ("I just kept moving the saddle myself").
   - *Category words:* what they call the product, problem and outcome.
   - *Proof moments:* specific results they report, with numbers or details.
5. **Count.** Tally how many sources mention each sub-theme. A theme in 12 of 40 sources outranks a vivid one-off.
6. **Pick the standout lines.** For each top theme, pick the two or three verbatim lines that say it better than you could. Trim with "..." but never reword. These become headline and subhead candidates.
7. **Name the core desire and the top three doubts.** One sentence each.
8. **Read awareness and sophistication.** From the words people use: do they know the solution type? Do they name competitors? Are competitors all making the same claim? Write the likely awareness stage and sophistication level, with the evidence.
9. **Write the message brief.** Core promise, three supporting reasons, top objections with the proof each one needs, words to use, words to avoid (company jargon customers never say).
10. **List the gaps.** What you still do not know, and the cheapest way to find out.

## Checks

- Every quote has a source ID. Nothing is invented, merged or "improved".
- Personal data is removed. Quotes flagged for public use are marked "needs permission".
- Counts are real counts. Say how many sources you coded.
- Client customers and competitor reviewers are kept apart; their doubts differ.
- Bias is noted: reviews skew to extremes, surveys skew to engaged customers, sales calls skew to people who were close to buying.
- The brief uses the customers' words, not marketing language.

## Output format

1. **Source log:** table with ID, type, origin, date, count.
2. **VoC bank:** table with ID, quote (verbatim), theme, sub-theme, source.
3. **Theme counts:** sub-theme, number of sources, two best quotes.
4. **Summary:** core desire; top pushes; top doubts; habit and alternatives; words to use; words to avoid.
5. **Awareness and sophistication read**, with evidence.
6. **Message brief:** core promise, three reasons, objection and proof table.
7. **Gaps and next research step.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a one-person bike-fitting studio. Its inputs, 34 reviews (R1 to R34) and 12 survey answers (S1 to S12), only show the shape. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Theme counts (46 sources coded)**

| Sub-theme | Sources | Best lines |
|---|---|---|
| Push: pain on longer rides (knees, back, hands) | 19 | R4 "numb hands after an hour, every time" / S7 "my knee hurt after 40 miles" |
| Pull: ride longer without pain | 15 | R12 "did my first 100 km without stopping to stretch" |
| Anxiety: "fits are for racers" | 9 | S3 "I thought it was only for serious cyclists" |
| Anxiety: price vs. a new saddle | 7 | S9 "I nearly bought another saddle instead" |
| Habit: adjusting it myself from videos | 8 | R21 "years of YouTube tweaks and still sore" |
| Category words | 22 | "bike fit", "set up my bike", "position"; nobody said "biomechanical assessment" |

**Core desire:** ride as far as they want without pain.
**Top doubts:** fits are for racers; a fit costs more than another saddle; will it last if I change bikes.
**Awareness:** problem and solution aware (they know fits exist) but doubt it is "for them". **Sophistication:** local rivals all claim "precision fitting", so a bigger precision claim will not land; show who it is for and the before and after.

**Message brief**

- Core promise: "Ride longer without sore knees, back or hands."
- Reasons: one session, adjusted on your own bike; you leave with your numbers to reuse on the next bike; follow-up tweak included.
- Objections and proof needed: "only for racers" → reviews from leisure riders (R12, R21, with permission); "costs more than a saddle" → cost of the saddles they already bought (S9 pattern) and the follow-up included; "new bike later" → you keep your measurements.
- Words to use: bike fit, set up, numb hands, sore knees, longer rides. Words to avoid: biomechanical, optimise, performance.

**Gap:** no data from people who enquired and did not book. Next step: a one-question email to the last 20 non-bookers: "What stopped you booking?"
