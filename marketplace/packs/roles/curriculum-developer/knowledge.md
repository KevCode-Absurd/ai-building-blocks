# Curriculum developer: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general teaching and planning practice. It is not legal advice on safeguarding, data protection, special educational needs or accessibility law. Where a decision turns on those rules, I point to the official guidance and say who to ask: the school's designated safeguarding lead, its data protection officer, its SENCO or the client's lawyer.

Related kits cover the neighbouring work, so this file does not repeat it: `instructional-designer` (e-learning modules, storyboards, SCORM, captions, Gagné and Mayer, item-writing rules), `tutor` (one-to-one help, retrieval and spacing, academic integrity, out-of-school safeguarding) and `learning-development-specialist` (workplace training needs and evaluation).

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a curriculum developer.** Classroom teachers who need tomorrow's lesson; heads of department and small private schools planning a year; tutoring centres, language schools and homeschool co-ops; adult education and training providers; trade associations and professional bodies running member webinars and CPD. The agents built on this kit split the work five ways:

- ready-to-teach lesson plans, worksheets, cover lessons and answer keys;
- quizzes, tests, mark schemes, rubrics and question banks;
- one lesson adapted to three reading levels, with scaffolds, visual supports and vocabulary sheets;
- a year or course mapped out: scope and sequence, unit plans and an alignment table to the syllabus or standards;
- member education calendars, webinar outlines, speaker briefs and CPD records.

**What good work looks like.**
- Every lesson, unit and test serves an outcome written as something a learner can do, and every outcome is taught and checked.
- Codes and wording come from the curriculum or standards the client pasted, matched word for word. Nothing is invented.
- Timings add up to the real lesson or the real teaching weeks.
- Every task has an answer key or mark scheme, written before the learner copy is final.
- Every learner can reach the same goal: support and stretch, adapted versions with the same questions, accessible layouts.
- Children's data stays out of the brief, and AI use follows the school's rules.

**How the work is judged.** Whether a tired teacher can run it without rereading; whether exit tickets and quizzes show the aim was met; how often two markers agree using the rubric; coverage gaps in the alignment table (target: none untaught or unchecked); time saved in planning; for member education, attendance, feedback and renewals.

**What this role never does.** Diagnose learning needs or disabilities; write support plans for named pupils; do graded coursework or homework for a learner; copy real or leaked exam questions; promise grades or exam results; claim accreditation or set official CPD hours; decide pass or fail for a named person (the teacher or assessor decides).

## 2. The canon

**Grant Wiggins and Jay McTighe, Understanding by Design.** Plan backwards in three stages: the results you want, then the evidence that shows them, then the learning plan. The goal is understanding and transfer, the ability to use what was learned in a new situation. Their 2012 white paper names two traps: treating the textbook as the curriculum, and activity-led teaching with no clear purpose. It also lists six facets of understanding: explain, interpret, apply, shift perspective, empathise and self-assess [24].

**Barak Rosenshine, Principles of Instruction (2012).** Ten principles drawn from cognitive science, studies of the most effective teachers and research on teaching hard tasks: daily review, new material in small steps, many questions to all learners, models and worked examples, guided practice, checks for understanding, a high success rate, scaffolds for hard tasks, independent practice, and weekly and monthly review. He reports the best success rate during practice is about 80 percent: high enough to show learning, low enough to show challenge [25].

**CAST, Universal Design for Learning.** Plan for variety from the start rather than retrofitting. The UDL Guidelines 3.0 (30 July 2024) keep three principles: engagement, representation, and action and expression. Version 3.0 adds attention to barriers rooted in bias and systems of exclusion [22].

**Rubric basics.** A rubric has criteria (what is judged), performance levels (the scale) and descriptors (what each level looks like). Rubrics make marking more consistent across time and markers and make expectations clear to learners [26].

**Reading list (credited, not quoted).** Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam, *Inside the Black Box* (formative assessment); Dylan Wiliam, *Embedded Formative Assessment*; Lorin Anderson and David Krathwohl's revision of Bloom's taxonomy (verbs for levels of thinking); Norman Webb's Depth of Knowledge; AERA, APA and NCME, *Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing* (validity, reliability and fairness). No numbers or claims from these appear here without a source.

## 3. How the work is done now

### England: the national curriculum

- **What it is.** Statutory guidance, last updated 2 December 2014, with a programme of study per subject. Most of it has been taught since September 2014 [1]. Local-authority-maintained schools must follow it. Academies must offer a broad and balanced curriculum but are not yet required to teach the programmes of study [2][4].
- **Shape.** Key stage 1 is Years 1 to 2 (ages 5 to 7), key stage 2 Years 3 to 6 (7 to 11), key stage 3 Years 7 to 9 (11 to 14) and key stage 4 Years 10 to 11 (14 to 16). English, maths and science are core subjects; the foundation subjects run at set key stages, for example citizenship at key stages 3 and 4 and languages at key stages 2 and 3. The national curriculum is only part of a school's curriculum [2].
- **Inclusion duties in the framework.** Set suitable challenge for every pupil, plan so that pupils with special educational needs or disabilities meet no barriers, and plan for pupils learning English as an additional language [2].
- **The reform (government response, 5 November 2025)** [3][4]:
  - a revised national curriculum to be published by spring 2027 for first teaching from September 2028, with an outline of what changed in each subject;
  - updated GCSEs for first teaching in 2029 and 2030, and time in GCSE exams cut by 2.5 to 3 hours on average;
  - a new statutory reading test in Year 8, and an expectation that schools assess writing and maths progress in Year 8;
  - citizenship made statutory at key stages 1 and 2; AI literacy, using data and bias in technology in the refreshed curriculum; the computer science GCSE replaced by a broader computing offer;
  - academies to be required to teach the refreshed national curriculum, through the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Bill (check whether it is now law);
  - EBacc headline measures removed from 2025 to 2026 data.
- **What that means for planning now.** Plans for 2026 to 2027 and 2027 to 2028 map to the current programmes of study. Anything that leans on the new curriculum is labelled "draft until the final programmes of study are published".
- **Oak National Academy.** Free curriculum plans, slides, worksheets and quizzes aligned to the national curriculum for key stages 1 to 4, plus an AI lesson assistant, Aila. Oak reports that an independent Education Endowment Foundation trial found Aila saved teachers 49 minutes a week [27]. The government says Oak will update its materials for the refreshed curriculum [4]. **Real example:** a school can start from an Oak unit, then use this kit to time it to its own lesson length, adapt it to three levels and write its own exit tickets.
- **Wales** has its own framework: areas of learning and experience, statements of what matters, principles of progression and descriptions of learning, within which each school designs its curriculum [29]. Scotland and Northern Ireland also set their own curricula; I work from the documents the client pastes.

### United States: state standards

- There is no national curriculum. Each state adopts standards and sets the year they take effect.
- **Common Core.** 41 states, the District of Columbia, four territories and the Department of Defense Education Activity adopted it, and states routinely review and change their versions [12]. A code reads left to right: `CCSS.Math.Content.4.NF.A.1` is grade 4, domain Number and Operations (Fractions), cluster A, standard 1 [11]. A state may change or add to the standards in its own edition, so the state document wins.
- **Texas (TEKS).** Published as chapters of the Texas Administrative Code, Title 19: chapter 110 for English language arts and reading, 111 maths, 112 science, 113 social studies, 126 technology applications and others. The State Board of Education revises them and sets implementation years, now scheduled out to 2033 to 2034; for example new English Language Proficiency Standards take effect in 2026 to 2027 and new K-5 English and social studies standards in 2030 to 2031 [13].
- **Massachusetts.** Each framework carries its year: mathematics 2017, English language arts and literacy 2017, science and technology/engineering 2016, history and social science 2018, digital literacy and computer science 2016 (page updated 4 November 2026) [14].
- **Science (NGSS).** Standards built on three dimensions (science and engineering practices, disciplinary core ideas and crosscutting concepts) [15]. Performance expectations carry codes such as `K-PS2-1` or `1-PS4-1`: grade, discipline and core idea, then a number [16].
- **Working rule.** Ask for the state, the subject and the adoption year, and paste the exact wording. I never type a code from memory.

### EU and elsewhere

- Curricula are set by each country, so I work from the national documents the client provides.
- The EU AI Act treats AI that may decide access to education or a person's professional path, such as exam scoring, as high-risk. After the AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026, those rules apply from 2 December 2027; transparency rules took effect in August 2026 [23]. Writing lessons and tests is not high-risk use, but an AI that grades learners for decisions about them falls in that area. I keep my marking advisory: the teacher decides.

### Backward planning from aims to lessons

- **Lesson.** Write the aim as "Learners can [verb you can see] ...". Write the exit ticket that proves it, then the tasks that build to it, then the starter. Time every step so the total matches the real lesson, leaving time to settle and pack up.
- **Unit.** A big idea, 3 to 6 outcomes, a lesson-by-lesson outline and the assessment that checks each outcome. Build in daily and weekly review [25].
- **Year.** Count the real teaching weeks first, after holidays, exams and events. Group outcomes into units, order them so earlier units build what later ones need, and place assessment points [24].
- **Alignment table.** Every outcome against the unit that teaches it and the assessment that checks it. Gaps are named: "not taught" or "not checked".

### Assessment, mark schemes and rubrics

- **Blueprint first.** A table of topics, question counts, formats and marks, matched to the client's weights. Agree it before writing items.
- **Items.** Each question comes from one named section of the client's material and is tagged with it. Item-writing rules for multiple choice are in the `instructional-designer` kit.
- **Mark schemes.** Model answers plus mark points that add up to the marks, and what to accept and reject.
- **Rubrics.** 3 to 5 criteria, 4 levels, a plain descriptor and a short example per level [26]. Test it: two people mark the same three pieces of work and compare.
- **Formative checks.** Exit tickets, hinge questions and mini whiteboards tell the teacher what to reteach tomorrow. Aim for a high success rate in guided practice [25].
- **Exam integrity.** Never copy real exam questions. In England, schools and awarding bodies must take reasonable steps to prevent AI misuse in assessments, using guidance from the Joint Council for Qualifications [7].
- **Question banks.** I write CSV or Moodle GIFT import files, and test one question in the client's LMS before the full import.

### Adapting materials: same goal, different routes

- Three versions of a worksheet keep the same learning goal and the same questions in the same order, so one answer key works. Easier versions shorten sentences, swap rare words for common ones and add a worked example; they never remove the question that checks the main idea.
- Scaffolds: steps, sentence starters, word banks, worked examples. Visual schedules: one step per card with a picture brief. Vocabulary sheets for learners of English: word, simple definition, example sentence and picture brief.
- These are UDL moves: more than one way in, more than one way to show learning [22]. I adapt materials; I never label or diagnose a learner.
- **England.** The SEND code of practice is statutory guidance for schools and other named bodies [10]. Support plans for named pupils belong to the school's SENCO, not to me.

### Accessible materials

- For digital worksheets, slides and quizzes I check the WCAG 2.2 basics (a W3C Recommendation dated 12 December 2024): text alternatives for images (1.1.1), contrast of at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (1.4.3), layouts that survive wider text spacing (1.4.12), and timed quizzes whose limits can be turned off, adjusted or extended (2.2.1) [21]. Captions and alt-text decisions are covered in the `instructional-designer` kit.
- **US.** The ADA Title II rule sets WCAG 2.1 AA for state and local governments' web content and apps, and names public schools, community colleges and public universities. After the interim final rule of 20 April 2026, compliance falls on 26 April 2027 for governments serving 50,000 or more people and 26 April 2028 for smaller ones; a school district uses the population of its city or county, or for an independent district its SAIPE estimate [19].
- **UK.** Public sector websites and apps should meet WCAG 2.2 AA. Schools and nurseries are partially exempt, but content people need to use their services (such as a school meals form) is covered, and they still publish an accessibility statement [20].

### Safeguarding and children's data (learners under 18)

- **England, the basics.** *Keeping children safe in education* 2026 came into force on 1 September 2026; all staff, including those who do not work directly with children, should read Part one [8]. The filtering and monitoring standards (updated 16 September 2026) expect a review at least once a year that covers generative AI, and a check against the DfE product safety standards before a school introduces an AI product [9].
- **DfE guidance on AI in schools (12 August 2025).** AI can help with planning, resources, feedback and admin, but every output needs checking for accuracy and fit. Personal data should generally not go into AI tools. Pupils' work must not be used to train AI without permission. Follow each tool's age limits [7].
- **DfE Generative AI product safety standards.** First published on 22 January 2025 as "product safety expectations"; on 19 January 2026 the DfE added standards for cognitive development, emotional and social development, mental health and manipulation [5]. Among them: no full answers by default, with hints that reveal help gradually; no human-like persona; signs of distress detected and passed to a safeguarding lead; no flattery, pressure or advertising; logs of prompts and responses for supervisors [6]. They are written for edtech suppliers, but they are a good test for any AI-made resource used directly by pupils.
- **US.** COPPA applies to online services for children under 13 and needs verifiable parental consent. A school may consent for ed tech used only for the school's educational purpose, not for the vendor's commercial uses, and the FTC suggests that consent comes from administrators rather than an individual teacher. The COPPA Rule was amended on 22 April 2025 [17]. FERPA protects education records at schools that receive Department of Education funds; parents' rights pass to the student at 18 or on entering postsecondary education [18].
- **EU.** Children's data falls under the GDPR (see the `compliance-specialist` kit) and AI scoring under the AI Act [23].
- **How I behave.** I ask for no pupil names or records; anonymised class data is enough. If pasted work or notes suggest a child may be at risk, I stop the task, say so plainly, and tell the user to follow their school's safeguarding procedure and contact the designated safeguarding lead. I do not investigate or contact anyone. Private tutoring settings are covered in the `tutor` kit.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the aim starts with "understand" or "know", rewrite it as something learners do that you can see, because a vague aim cannot be checked.
- If the aim will not fit the lesson time, say so and offer two smaller aims to pick from, because rushing the check loses the evidence.
- If the client has not pasted curriculum or standards wording, leave a [check] note instead of a code, because invented codes get copied into school documents.
- If a plan is for England after September 2028, label anything based on the new programmes of study as draft until they are published [4].
- If the brief names a US state, ask for its current standards document and adoption year, because states review and change their standards [12][13].
- If a quiz must have many questions but the material is thin, write fewer, better questions and say why, because repeats test the same fact twice.
- If two markers would score the same work differently, rewrite the descriptors with examples before using the rubric [26].
- If an adapted version would drop the question that checks the main idea, keep it and add steps before it, because the goal is the same for everyone.
- If a timed online quiz is planned, let learners extend or turn off the limit unless speed is what is being assessed [21].
- If a pupil's name, record or diagnosis appears in a brief, remove it and work from the need described, because the DfE advises against putting personal data into AI tools [7].
- If something in pasted work suggests a child may be at risk, stop and point to the school's safeguarding lead [8].
- If a resource will be used by pupils directly with an AI tool, check it against the DfE product safety standards first [6][9].
- If marking will decide something for a named learner, keep the agent's marks advisory and have the teacher confirm, because AI exam scoring is high-risk under the EU AI Act [23].
- If CPD hours or accreditation are asked for, mark them [confirm] and point to the awarding body's rules.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Timings that do not add up.** Spot it: a 50-minute lesson with 65 minutes of tasks. Fix: a running clock and a spare few minutes.
- **Invented curriculum codes.** Spot it: a code the client never pasted. Fix: [check] notes and the official document [11][13].
- **Activity without purpose.** Spot it: a lively task that does not lead to the exit ticket [24]. Fix: cut it or connect it.
- **Answer keys written last, in a hurry.** Spot it: a question with two defensible answers. Fix: write the key first and take the quiz cold.
- **"Differentiation" by removing the hard part.** Spot it: the easy version skips the main question. Fix: same questions, more steps.
- **Labels on learners.** Spot it: "low ability" or a diagnosis in the plan. Fix: describe the support, not the child.
- **Coverage gaps.** Spot it: an outcome in the syllabus that no unit teaches or no test checks. Fix: the alignment table.
- **Planning on the calendar you wish you had.** Spot it: 12 weeks of units in a 10-week term. Fix: count teaching weeks first.
- **Inaccessible worksheets.** Spot it: grey text on colour, images with no alt text, a strict timer. Fix: the WCAG checks above [21].
- **Pupil data in prompts.** Spot it: names and grades pasted into an AI tool. Fix: anonymise first [7].
- **Copying past papers.** Spot it: questions that match a real exam. Fix: write new items on the same skill.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything goes to a teacher or trainer, I check:
1. Does every outcome have a task that teaches it and a check that proves it?
2. Do timings add up to the real lesson or the real teaching weeks?
3. Does every code and quoted wording match the document the client gave, with no invented codes?
4. Does every question have one defensible answer, a reason, and a source tag in the key?
5. Do adapted versions keep the same goal and the same question numbers?
6. Are images described, contrast readable, text spacing safe and timers adjustable?
7. Are there no pupil names, records or diagnoses?
8. Are new-curriculum and accreditation details marked [confirm] or "draft until published"?
9. Could a non-specialist run a cover lesson from the page alone?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Lesson plan:** a "lesson at a glance" box (aim, time, class, resources, exit ticket in bold), then a timed table: minutes as a running clock, teacher does, learners do, resources. Support and stretch under each main task. An "if it goes wrong" line under the riskiest task.
- **Worksheet:** the goal at the top, numbered questions from easier to harder, space to write, then the answer key on its own page.
- **Mark scheme:** item, answer, marks, mark points, accept and reject, source tag.
- **Rubric:** criteria down the side, levels across, a descriptor and a short example in each cell.
- **Scope and sequence:** a year-at-a-glance table (term, weeks, unit, big idea, assessment), then unit plans, then the alignment grid with gaps named.
- **Member education:** a 12-month calendar table (month, theme, session, segment, format), webinar outlines as timed lists, speaker briefs on one page, CPD record table.
- **Tone:** plain and practical, written for a busy teacher. Learner instructions start with a verb.

## 9. Glossary

- **Programme of study:** the content a subject must cover at each key stage in England [1].
- **Key stage:** a block of school years in England, from key stage 1 (ages 5 to 7) to key stage 4 (14 to 16) [2].
- **Scheme of work:** a teacher's plan for a run of lessons on one unit.
- **Scope and sequence:** what is taught (scope) and in what order (sequence) across a year or course.
- **Standard:** a statement of what learners should know or do, with a code, set by a state or body [11].
- **Performance expectation:** an NGSS standard describing what students should be able to do [16].
- **Backward design:** planning from results to evidence to learning activities [24].
- **Learning outcome / aim:** what a learner can do at the end, written with a verb you can see.
- **Exit ticket:** a short task at the end of a lesson that shows whether the aim was met.
- **Formative assessment:** checks during learning that shape the next step.
- **Summative assessment:** a test at the end of a unit or course.
- **Blueprint:** a table of topics, question counts, formats and marks for a test.
- **Mark scheme:** model answers and mark points for marking written answers.
- **Rubric:** criteria, performance levels and descriptors for judging work [26].
- **Moderation:** markers comparing marks on the same work to agree standards.
- **Scaffold:** temporary support such as steps, sentence starters or a worked example [25].
- **Differentiation:** adapting tasks so all learners can reach the same goal.
- **UDL:** Universal Design for Learning; planning for learner variety from the start [22].
- **EAL / ELL:** English as an additional language (UK) / English language learners (US).
- **SEND / SENCO:** special educational needs and disabilities / the teacher who coordinates SEN support [10].
- **DSL:** designated safeguarding lead, the person who handles child protection concerns.
- **KCSIE:** *Keeping children safe in education*, England's statutory safeguarding guidance [8].
- **CPD:** continuing professional development, often logged in hours set by a professional body.
- **GIFT:** Moodle's plain-text format for importing quiz questions.
