# Lesson plan and class materials

## When to use

- A teacher needs tomorrow's lesson, or a run of lessons for one unit, for a named class.
- A tutor plans a one-to-one or small-group session around a curriculum aim.
- A head of department needs cover work a non-specialist can run.
- A trainer wants a classroom session with worksheets and keys.

Do not use it to diagnose learners, plan for named pupils' support needs, write graded coursework, or promise results. For a whole year, use `scope-and-sequence-with-alignment`. For three reading levels of one worksheet, use `adapted-materials-same-goal`.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the curriculum wording, the teacher's notes and textbook pages, last week's exit tickets with names removed.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the plan and worksheets to the agreed folder; create draft assignments in Google Classroom, Teams or the LMS.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** post the agreed worksheet or quiz to the named class on the agreed date. Deleting existing work or sending to parents needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. Subject, age or year group, class size, and anything the teacher wants planned for (learners of English, a reading range, access needs described without names).
2. The aim in the teacher's words, and the curriculum or standards wording if there is one.
3. Real lesson length after settling and packing up, and how many lessons.
4. What learners could do last week and where they usually go wrong.
5. Resources in the room: mini whiteboards, devices, textbooks, fraction strips.
6. The school's lesson plan template, if it has one.

If the curriculum wording is missing, I plan from the aim and leave a [check] note where a code would go.

## Steps

### 1. Fix the aim

Write one sentence: "Learners can [verb you can see] [what] [how well]." Use verbs such as rank, explain, solve, compare or label. If the aim is too big for the time, say so and offer two smaller aims to pick from. If curriculum wording was pasted, quote it exactly under the aim with its code.

### 2. Write the exit ticket first

One to three questions that prove the aim was met, answerable in about three minutes. Bold it at the top of the plan. Write its answer now.

### 3. Plan backwards

- Main task that builds to the exit ticket.
- Guided practice before it: a worked example, then learners try one with the teacher checking all answers (mini whiteboards, show of hands with a reason).
- Teacher input: small steps, each followed by a quick check.
- Starter: retrieval of last lesson's key point or a question that sets up today's.

Rosenshine's principles fit this order: daily review, small steps, models, guided practice, checks for understanding, then independent work. Aim for most learners getting most guided questions right before they work alone.

### 4. Time it

Write timings as a running clock (0 to 5, 5 to 15). The total must equal the real lesson. Keep a few minutes spare for real life. If it does not fit, cut a task, never the exit ticket.

### 5. Write teacher and learner actions

For each step: what the teacher says and does (commands such as "Hand out the cards."), what learners do, and what they need. Add the likely misconception and how to respond. Put an "if it goes wrong" line under the riskiest task.

### 6. Support and stretch

Under each main task, one support move (sentence starter, worked example, fewer steps) and one stretch move (a harder case, explain why, create an example). The goal stays the same for everyone.

### 7. Worksheets

Questions grouped easier, core and harder, numbered, each starting with a verb. Space to write. The time each section takes.

### 8. Answer keys

Full answers, model answers for written questions, and mark points if it will be marked. Keys go on their own page at the end.

### 9. Cover version (if asked)

A plan a non-specialist can run from the page: what to say word for word for the opening, where everything is, answers for every task, a space for behaviour notes, and what to collect.

### 10. Hand-off

List materials to print or prepare, and anything to check before teaching ([check] notes, facts to confirm against the textbook).

## Checks

- Timings add up to the real lesson length.
- The exit ticket tests the aim, and the main task prepares for it.
- Every fact comes from the teacher's material or is standard for the level; anything else is marked [check].
- Every curriculum code and quote matches the pasted wording exactly; no codes from memory.
- Every learner instruction starts with a verb; read each question aloud to check it makes sense.
- Every task has an answer in the key, with one defensible answer for closed questions.
- No pupil names, records or diagnoses appear.
- Digital worksheets have alt text for images, readable contrast and no strict timer.

## Output format

1. **Lesson at a glance:** aim, curriculum reference, class, time, resources, **exit ticket**.
2. **Timed plan table:** minutes, teacher does, learners do, resources; support and stretch under main tasks; "if it goes wrong" line.
3. **Worksheets:** learner copy, sections graded, time per section.
4. **Answer keys:** on their own page.
5. **Before you teach:** materials list and [check] items.
6. For a unit: an overview table first (lesson, aim, exit ticket, key task).

## Example

*Illustrative structure, not a real organisation: a middle school. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Lesson at a glance:** Grade 4 maths, 26 learners, 45 minutes (40 usable). Aim: Learners can explain why two fractions are equivalent using a fraction strip. Standard: 4.NF.A.1 [teacher's pasted state wording quoted here]. Resources: fraction strips, mini whiteboards.
**Exit ticket:** "Is 2/3 the same as 4/6? Draw strips and explain in one sentence."

| Minutes | Teacher does | Learners do | Resources |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 5 | Starter on board: shade 1/2 of a strip. Check all boards. | Shade and hold up. | Whiteboards |
| 5 to 13 | Model 1/2 = 2/4 with strips, thinking aloud. Ask: "Same size piece of the whole?" | Watch, then answer on boards. | Strips |
| 13 to 22 | Guided: 1/3 and 2/6 together; check every board before moving on. | Fold, compare, explain to partner. | Strips |
| 22 to 34 | Worksheet sections A and B. Support: strips stay out. Stretch: find three fractions equal to 3/4. | Work alone. | Worksheet |
| 34 to 40 | Exit ticket; collect. | Answer alone. | Ticket |

If it goes wrong: if learners say 4/6 is bigger "because the numbers are bigger", lay both strips end to end and compare lengths, not numbers.

**Answer key (extract):** Exit ticket: yes; both strips cover the same length of the whole, because each third has been split into two sixths.
