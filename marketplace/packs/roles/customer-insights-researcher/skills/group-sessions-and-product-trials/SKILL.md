# Group sessions and product trials

## When to use

- A client wants reactions to concepts, names, packs or messages from groups of customers.
- A behaviour unfolds over days or weeks (cooking, budgeting, using a product at home) and needs a diary study.
- A food, drink, cosmetics or household brand must choose between versions, check a recipe change goes unnoticed, or test a product at home before launch.

The client or their moderator runs groups and serves samples. I design, prepare and analyse. Health, nutrition, safety and allergen claims need a qualified professional's sign-off; check this with local rules.

## What you need

1. The decision and what result would change it ("Launch recipe B if it is preferred or not worse than A").
2. Who matters, how many sessions or tasters are possible, the budget and dates.
3. For groups: what will be shown (concepts, packs, prototypes) and who will watch.
4. For trials: the versions, what can be served, portion size, ingredients and allergens, and storage and safety rules.
5. For analysis: transcripts or notes per group, diary entries, or score sheets (exported as a spreadsheet).

Access: level 1 (read) for briefs, transcripts and score exports. Level 2 to build unpublished screeners, diary task schedules and score forms in the client's survey tool, and draft reminders. Level 3, within the client's limits (which participants, dates and messages), to publish forms and send scheduled diary prompts and reminders. Recruiting and consent follow the interview-recruiting-and-consent skill. Paying participants stays with the client. I log every action.

## Steps

### 1. Pick the format

- **Focus group or mini-group:** reactions, language, how people talk about a category. Not for usability or counting. Plan more than one group; typical groups are 6 to 9 people for about 2 hours.
- **Co-creation workshop:** people build their ideal version with cards or materials.
- **Diary study:** behaviour over time, in context. Pick event-based (log each time), interval-based (log daily) or prompted logging to match how often the behaviour happens. Over-recruit for dropouts.
- **Difference test (paired or triangle):** "Can people tell A from B?" Use when a change should go unnoticed.
- **Preference or liking test:** "Which do people prefer, and how much do they like each?"
- **Home-use trial:** how a product performs over several days of real use.

Say why the format fits the decision and what it cannot tell you.

### 2. Write the guide (groups and workshops)

Timed sections: welcome and consent reminder (recording, observers, right to stop), warm-up, broad topics, then narrower ones, stimulus, exercises (card sort, ranking, "build your ideal"), wrap-up. For each section: the main question, probes ("Tell me about the last time...", "What makes you say that?") and timing. Add moderator notes for quiet participants and for anyone dominating.

### 3. Brief the stimulus

Describe each concept or board in neutral words, the same level of finish for each, and the order of showing, rotated between groups so no option always goes first. List what to ask after each.

### 4. Plan the trial (taste tests and home use)

- Screener: real buyers of the category; **allergy and dietary questions first**, with anyone at risk screened out.
- Samples coded with random three-digit numbers; servers do not tell tasters which is which.
- Serving order balanced across tasters; same portion, temperature and container; water or a plain cracker between samples.
- Scoring sheet in plain words: overall liking (a labelled scale), key qualities, "just about right" scales for strength, sweetness, saltiness and similar, and a comments box.
- Say how many tasters the decision needs and what a smaller panel can and cannot show.
- Write the decision rule before anyone tastes.

### 5. Diary tasks

Write each day's task in two or three lines, with an example entry. Plan reminders and the follow-up questions you will ask as entries arrive. Set a minimum and maximum number of entries if pay is per entry, and pay in stages tied to milestones.

### 6. Analyse groups and diaries

Read every transcript or entry. Code passages and build themes (see the interview-and-feedback-synthesis skill). For groups, count how many groups and how many people raised each theme, note who dominated, and separate what was said from what the moderator inferred. Quote word for word.

### 7. Analyse trials

From the score sheets: averages and spread by version, share preferring each, just-about-right results (too little, just right, too much), and whether differences are bigger than chance, with every step shown. For difference tests, compare correct answers with what guessing alone would give. Group comments into themes. Then make the call: launch, change or test again, with reasons.

## Checks

- Format fits the decision; limits stated.
- Guide timed; probes neutral; stimulus order rotated.
- Allergy questions first; at-risk people screened out; no health or safety claims.
- Samples coded and order balanced.
- Decision rule written before the test.
- Every score from the client's data, every sum shown.
- Quotes exact; dominant voices noted.
- Children only with the permissions the research codes and law require.

## Output format

Design: format and reason, screener, guide or diary plan, stimulus brief, scoring sheet, serving plan, decision rule.
Readout: answer first, themes with counts and quotes (groups and diaries) or results tables and the call (trials), limits, next step.

## Example of good output

Made-up business for illustration: Brackenridge Kitchen, testing a lower-sugar granola (B) against the current recipe (A).

> **Decision rule (agreed before tasting):** switch to B if overall liking is not lower than A by more than the smallest difference the panel can detect, and fewer than a third of tasters rate B "not sweet enough".
>
> **Result:** 64 tasters, balanced order, samples coded 318 and 742. Mean liking (9-point scale): A 6.9, B 6.7; the difference is within what chance alone could produce at this panel size (calculation shown in the appendix). "Not sweet enough" for B: 14 of 64 (22%).
>
> **Call:** switch to B. Comments to watch: 9 tasters said B was "drier"; test a version with more honey binder in the next round.
