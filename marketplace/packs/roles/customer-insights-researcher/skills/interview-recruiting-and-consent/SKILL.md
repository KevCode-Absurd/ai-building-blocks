# Interview recruiting and consent

## When to use

- A client wants to talk to customers, lapsed customers or prospects and needs the right people booked.
- A focus group, diary study, home trial or taste panel needs participants.
- A client asks "can we just email our customers and record the calls?"

This skill covers getting people there, informed and willing. The client or their moderator runs the live session. Legal points on privacy, recording, children and incentives: check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser.

## What you need

1. The decision the research feeds and the people who matter (situation, recent experience, region).
2. The method, number of sessions, length, format (video, phone, in person) and dates.
3. Where people can be found: customer list, website visitors, social followers, a participant panel, a recruiting platform.
4. The client's privacy notice and what it says about research.
5. The incentive and how the client will pay it.
6. Who will run and watch the sessions, and whether they are recorded.
7. Whether anyone under 18, or anyone in a vulnerable situation, might take part.

Access: level 1 (read) to review lists, past studies and the privacy notice. Level 2 to build the screener as an unpublished form, draft outreach emails unsent, draft a panel study, and draft a booking page. Level 3, within the client's limits (which list, how many people, which dates, a budget cap for panel studies), to publish the screener, send invitations, launch the panel study and send booking links. Contacting a new audience, anything involving children or sensitive topics, and sharing participants' identities need a fresh yes every time. Paying participants stays with the client. I log every action.

## Steps

### 1. Define who you need

Write the target in behaviour terms: "bought a meal kit in the last three months and cancelled", not "women 25 to 40". Set quotas for a fair mix (new and long-standing customers, light and heavy users, regions). Write exclusions: people who work in the industry, for a rival, in market research or in the client's own company; anyone who took part recently.

### 2. Write the screener

- If children could answer, ask age first and stop under-age people from giving further information until a responsible adult's permission is verified (the MRS Code treats under 16s as children).
- Then qualifying questions about real, recent behaviour, with answer options that do not reveal the "right" answer.
- One or two open questions to check people can describe their experience.
- Availability and format.
- A stop rule on every question that disqualifies, and a thank-you for those screened out.
- Collect only the personal data needed to contact and schedule.

### 3. Write the outreach

Short and honest: who is running the research, the topic and purpose in plain words, how long it takes, the format, whether it is recorded, the incentive (who pays it, what, when, on what conditions), that taking part is voluntary, and a contact. No selling: no discount codes, no product pitch, no sales follow-up unless people opt in separately. Do not use the client's own products or vouchers as the research incentive, and never one that requires spending money (MRS Code). For the client's own customers, check their privacy notice allows research contact; if not, ask a qualified adviser before sending.

### 4. Set the incentive

Fair for the time and the audience; specialist audiences cost more. For panels, follow the platform's pay guidance. Diary studies pay more than single sessions and are best paid in stages. State any conditions (for example, completing all diary days). The client pays.

### 5. Write the consent and privacy text

Give people, before the session and in plain words:
- who is collecting the data and how to contact them;
- the subject and purpose;
- what data is collected, especially any sensitive data (health, beliefs, ethnic origin and similar), and why;
- whether it is recorded or observed, and who will see the recording or watch live (name client observers as clients);
- how long it lasts and any costs;
- the legal basis (under the GDPR or UK GDPR) and, if it is consent, that they can withdraw at any time as easily as they gave it;
- how long data is kept and when it is deleted;
- whether data leaves the country;
- their rights and the right to complain to the regulator;
- whether they may be re-contacted, asked as a separate question;
- whether an AI tool will interview them, transcribe or analyse, said at the start.

Consent by a clear act (an unticked box they tick, or a recorded "yes"), never pre-ticked. Separate tick boxes for the research, for recording, for sharing clips or quotes with names, and for re-contact.

### 6. Protect identities

Give each participant an ID. Share names, faces or voices with the client only for the uses each person agreed to. In business-to-business studies, warn people if observers may know them. Note the deletion date for recordings and contact details.

### 7. Schedule and brief

Book sessions with buffers. Send a reminder with the time, link, length and a copy of the consent text. Brief the client's moderator: the guide, the consent script to read at the start (including the recording notice), and how to stop if someone is upset.

### 8. Track

Keep a recruitment log: invited, screened in, screened out (and why), booked, attended, no-shows, incentive owed. The client pays from this list.

## Checks

- Screener uses behaviour, has exclusions and stop rules, and asks age first where needed.
- Outreach is honest, has no selling and states the incentive terms.
- Consent text covers who, why, what data, recording and observers, retention, rights, withdrawal, re-contact and AI.
- Separate consent for recording, named clips and re-contact.
- Personal data limited to what is needed; IDs used in analysis.
- Children, vulnerable people and sensitive topics flagged for a fresh yes and a qualified adviser.
- Every live action within the agreed list, number, dates and budget.

## Output format

1. Recruiting plan: who, how many, quotas, exclusions, channels, dates.
2. Screener with logic.
3. Outreach messages (email, panel listing, social post).
4. Consent and privacy text, with the moderator's opening script.
5. Recruitment log template.
6. Questions for a qualified adviser, if any.

## Example of good output

Illustrative structure, not a real business: an online grocer studying why subscribers cancel. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Target:** 12 people in the UK who cancelled a [Business] subscription in the last 60 days. Quotas: 6 who subscribed under 3 months, 6 longer; at least 4 outside London. Exclude: anyone working in food retail, delivery or research.
>
> **Outreach (email to cancelled customers, allowed by the privacy notice's research clause):** "We're [Business]'s research team. We'd like to hear what made you cancel, in a 30-minute video call, recorded only if you agree. This is research, not a sales call: we won't try to win you back. As thanks, you'll receive a £30 gift card you can use at a range of shops (not a [Business] voucher) within 7 days of the call. Taking part is up to you. Reply to book, or ignore this email."
>
> **Consent boxes (unticked):** I agree to take part. I agree to the call being recorded (audio and video, deleted after 6 months). I agree to short clips being shown to [Business] staff without my name. I'm happy to be contacted about future research.
