# Onboarding to first value

## When to use

- A new plan, product or service is launching and has no onboarding yet.
- New customers sign up, then go quiet, or cancel in the first months.
- A service firm loses forms, logins and approvals in long email chains, or the client repeats what they told sales.
- Welcome emails list features instead of the next step.

## What you need

1. What the customer bought: plan or service, price, term and what was promised in the sale (proposal, sales notes, contract summary).
2. The customer's goal in their own words, if sales captured it. If not, the most common goals for this plan.
3. The steps between signing and the first useful result, as the team does them today, with who does each.
4. Early support tickets or questions from new customers, and where people stall (usage data or the team's notes).
5. Everything you need from the customer: answers, documents, logins, approvals, data.
6. Your voice: two or three emails you have sent that you liked.

## Steps

### 1. Define the first win

Write it as a result the customer can see, for this plan, in one sentence: "First invoice sent from the new system", "First monthly report reviewed with the client", "First class booked and attended". Lincoln Murphy's idea of the Desired Outcome helps here: the customer's goal plus the experience they expect while reaching it. A self-serve buyer wants to be left alone with clear steps; a first-time buyer of a service may want a call at each stage.

### 2. Map the path

List every step from signed to first win. For each step write the owner (us or the customer), the day it should happen, the tool used and what "done" looks like. Mark steps that only exist because of internal habits and cut them. Time to value is the measure: every step either moves the customer toward the first win or is removed.

### 3. Ask once

Build one intake list grouped by when you need each item (before kick-off, week one, later). For each item say why you need it and in what form. Never ask for passwords to be sent by email or chat; ask the customer to invite you as a user or to connect through the tool's own sign-in.

### 4. Pass the promises on

Write a one-page handoff from sales to the delivery or success team: who the customer is, their goal, what was promised (scope, dates, discounts, special terms), the people and their roles, risks spotted in the sale, and open questions. If a promise is unclear, list it for the salesperson to confirm before the kick-off.

### 5. Write the welcome series

Each message does one job: one step toward the first win, what "done" looks like, how long it takes and where to get help. Time each message to what the customer should have done by then, not to a calendar. Stop the series once the first win is reached and switch to a check-in.

Keep these as service messages. A message that helps someone use what they bought is administrative; adding a promotion makes it marketing in the UK and changes the rules in the US. Put upgrade offers in a separate, later message.

### 6. Set the stuck signals

Name the signals that a customer is stalling at each step (no login after sign-up, intake list half done, set-up step not finished, a second ticket on the same issue) and the action for each: a short nudge, a call or a fix to the guide.

### 7. Book the first check-in

Book a check-in after the first win is due, with three questions: did you get the result, what was harder than expected, what do you need next.

### Access needed

- Level 1: read sales notes, tickets and usage to map the path.
- Level 2: save the welcome series as drafts in your email or CRM tool, and create tasks for owners.
- Level 3: switch the series on for new customers on the agreed plans, send intake requests and book check-ins, within the limits you set.

## Checks

- The first win is a visible result, not "understands the product".
- Every step has an owner, a day and a "done" line.
- The intake list asks for each item once and never asks for passwords.
- Every promise from the sale appears in the handoff, or is listed to confirm.
- Each email has one next step, and no email mixes onboarding with an upgrade pitch.
- Every step in the guide matches the real product or service; steps I could not confirm are flagged.
- The stuck signals use data the business actually has.

## Output format

One markdown document:

1. First win (one sentence per plan or service)
2. Path to first win (table: step, owner, day, tool, done when)
3. Intake list (grouped by when needed, with the reason for each item)
4. Sales-to-delivery handoff sheet
5. Welcome series (for each message: trigger, subject, body, the one action)
6. Stuck signals and actions (table)
7. First check-in questions and date
8. Facts to confirm

## Example of good output (short)

> **Made-up example: Ledgerly, a bookkeeping firm for cafés (not a real business).**
>
> **First win:** the client approves their first monthly profit and loss report, delivered by the 10th of the month after sign-up.
>
> | Step | Owner | Day | Done when |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Kick-off call | Ledgerly | 2 | Goal written in the client's words |
> | Invite Ledgerly as a user in the client's accounting tool | Client | 3 | Invite accepted; no passwords shared |
> | Upload last 3 months of supplier bills | Client | 5 | All bills in the shared folder |
> | Bank feed connected and checked | Ledgerly | 7 | Balances match statements |
>
> **Email 2 (sent when the invite is accepted):** "Thanks, we're in. One thing left before we can build your first report: upload the last three months of supplier bills to the folder below. It takes about 15 minutes. Questions? Reply here and Sam will answer the same day."
>
> **Stuck signal:** invite not accepted after the kick-off plus two working days. **Action:** Sam calls and walks through it.
