# Customer support specialist: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This kit covers support practice and points to official consumer rules; it is not legal advice. Consumer law differs by country and US state, and a business's own policy can give more than the law but never less. Where a reply turns on what the law requires, check this with local rules or ask a qualified adviser.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a support specialist.** Small online stores, service firms and software teams with one shared inbox, a help desk such as Zendesk, Gorgias or Help Scout, and too few hours. In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into four jobs: sorting the inbox and drafting replies, returns and refunds, warranty claims, and the library of reply templates (macros) and tone rules that keeps every answer consistent.

**What good work looks like.**
- Urgent and risky messages (safety, money, account access, legal threats) are seen first.
- Every reply gives the answer first, then the next step, then when the customer will hear back.
- Every reply matches the business's written policy. Nothing is promised that the policy or the facts do not support.
- Customers do not have to repeat themselves or chase.
- Cases outside the policy go to the owner with the options and the cost of each.
- Patterns in the tickets (a bad batch, a confusing size chart, a missing help page) reach the people who can fix them.

**How the work is judged.**
- First response time and time to resolution.
- Repeat contacts: how often the same customer has to come back about the same issue.
- Satisfaction (CSAT after a ticket, sometimes NPS for the relationship).
- Accuracy: replies that match policy and law, with no wrong promises.
- Cost: refunds, replacements, chargebacks lost, and agent hours.

**What a support specialist never does here.** Issue a refund, approve a claim or change an order beyond the access level and limits the owner set (see `access.md`). Say a refund has happened when it has not. Give legal, medical or safety advice. Ask customers for passwords or full card numbers.

## 2. The canon

**Matthew Dixon, Karen Freeman and Nick Toman, "Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2010), and *The Effortless Experience* (Dixon, Toman and Rick DeLisi, 2013).** The research behind them argued that going beyond expectations does little for loyalty, while making customers work hard (repeat contacts, being transferred, repeating information, switching channels) drives disloyalty [1]. The practical rule: remove effort before adding delight. Answer the next question before it is asked, resolve on first contact where possible, and never make the customer repeat their story.

**Fred Reichheld, Net Promoter Score (*HBR*, 2003; Bain & Company).** One question, "How likely are you to recommend us?", scored 0 to 10; the score is the share of promoters (9 to 10) minus the share of detractors (0 to 6) [3]. Bain itself advises tracking it by segment, product or team rather than one company figure [3]. For support, a ticket-level satisfaction question is usually more useful than NPS, because it ties to a specific interaction.

**Knowledge-Centered Service (KCS), Consortium for Service Innovation (v6, 2016).** Capture knowledge while solving the ticket, structure it to a content standard, reuse it ("search early, search often") and improve it every time it is used; then manage content health and measure it over time [2]. Reply templates and help pages are not a separate project; they come out of the daily work.

**The service recovery paradox (McCollough and Bharadwaj, 1992; meta-analysis by de Matos, Henrique and Rossi, 2007).** The idea that a customer whose problem was fixed well ends up happier than one who never had a problem. The meta-analysis found the effect on satisfaction but not on whether people buy again or recommend the business. Fix problems well, but do not count on a good recovery to make up for the failure.

**ISO 10002 (complaints handling guidelines, 2018 edition).** The international standard for complaint handling: make the process visible and easy to use, acknowledge promptly, treat complaints objectively and confidentially, and learn from them. A useful checklist even for a three-person team.

**Voice and tone (Mailchimp's content style guide).** Voice stays the same; tone changes with the reader's situation. Think about the reader's state of mind before writing, choose clarity over cleverness, and only use humour when it fits [4]. An upset customer gets the same voice in a calmer, more direct tone.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Help desks, macros and AI (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Macros (reply templates).** In Zendesk, a macro is a prepared response or set of actions an agent applies by hand: it can set status, priority or assignee and insert placeholders such as the customer's first name, with a preview before applying [5]. Gorgias, Help Scout, Freshdesk and Intercom have equivalents (saved replies, canned responses). A macro is a starting point: an agent checks the facts and edits before sending.
- **AI agents.** Klarna said in February 2024 that its AI assistant handled two-thirds of its customer service chats in the first month (2.3 million conversations), cut resolution time from 11 minutes to under 2, reduced repeat inquiries by 25% and matched human agents on satisfaction. These are the company's own claims [8].
- **Connecting AI to the help desk.** Intercom runs an official MCP server: it can search and fetch conversations, contacts and help articles, can add internal notes, and cannot send customer-visible replies; US and EU regions only [6]. Intercom was among the first Claude integrations in May 2025 [7]. Level 1 (read) is the right start; notes-only access fits level 2 (read and draft).
- **Telling people it is AI.** In the EU, AI systems that talk directly with people must tell them they are dealing with AI unless it is obvious, from 2 August 2026 [19]; the AI Omnibus did not delay this duty (law-firm summary) [20]. In a 2026 vendor survey, 95% of consumers said they expect an explanation for decisions made by AI [25]. Label bot replies, offer a route to a person, and keep bots inside the written policy.

### Returns and refunds: the rules to know (check this with local rules)
- **US.** Change-of-mind returns are mostly set by the store's own posted policy, and some states have rules about posting it. For mail, internet and phone orders, the FTC rule requires shipping within the stated time (or 30 days if none is stated), a delay notice offering the choice to cancel, and refunds within seven working days (or one billing cycle where the seller is the creditor) [10]. Customers can dispute card charges for goods not delivered within 60 days of the first bill showing the charge [26].
- **UK.** Faulty goods, goods not as described or not working must be refunded, repaired or replaced; within six months the fault is presumed to have been there at sale; customers have up to six years to claim (five in Scotland). Online, phone and mail order buyers can cancel within 14 days of receiving the item, return it within 14 more days, and get a refund within 14 days of the item coming back, including standard delivery. A "no returns" sign is illegal [15]. Since April 2025 the CMA can decide consumer law breaches itself and fine up to 10% of global turnover [16].
- **EU.** A minimum two-year legal guarantee from delivery: repair or replacement first, then a full or partial refund; defects that appear within one year (two in some countries) are presumed to have been there at delivery [17]. A 14-day cooling-off period for online purchases, with exceptions such as personalised or perishable goods and dated services; the buyer normally pays return postage unless told otherwise [18].
- **Store policy.** A business may offer more than the law. Zappos sets a 60-day window for items in new condition with free US returns, and may refuse returns that break its rules or charge a restocking fee of up to 50% (as of 6 Nov 2026) [9]. Write the policy down, show it before purchase, and make the support team's replies match it word for word.
- **Platforms.** Shopify lets a store set return rules for eligibility and windows, accept self-serve return requests from the order status page, and refund after inspection [23].

### Warranties
- **US.** Under the Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, a written warranty on a consumer product over $10 must be titled "full" or "limited", and over $15 must be in one clear document available before sale. Coverage generally cannot depend on using a particular brand of parts or service. A business that gives a written warranty cannot disclaim implied warranties, although a limited warranty may limit their length [11].
- **UK and EU.** A commercial guarantee is extra: it cannot reduce the customer's legal rights [17], which in the UK can run up to six years for a claim [15]. A warranty reply never says "your warranty has expired, so nothing can be done" without checking the legal position.

### Payments, disputes and subscriptions
- **Chargebacks.** When a cardholder disputes a payment, the card issuer has already taken the money back. Stripe's response window is usually 7 to 21 days, depending on the card network, with one chance to submit evidence; contacting the customer can resolve the complaint and lead them to withdraw it; a decision can take up to three months [22]. A quick, fair refund is often cheaper than a dispute.
- **Subscriptions.** The FTC's 2024 click-to-cancel rule was revised in February 2026 "to conform" to federal court decisions, and in March 2026 the FTC asked for comment on new rules [12]. In the UK, the CMA said it would consult further before enforcing the newer subscription contract rules [16]. Make cancelling as easy as signing up, whatever the current rule.

### Reviews and data requests
- **Reviews.** The FTC's rule bans fake reviews, incentives tied to positive or negative sentiment, and suppressing reviews with threats [13]. Google bars businesses from offering incentives for reviews, discouraging negative reviews or selectively asking only happy customers [14]. Support may ask every customer for an honest review, never just the happy ones.
- **Data requests.** Under GDPR, a customer asking for their data, or for it to be deleted, must get a reply within one month (extendable by two months for complex requests, with notice) [21]. Route these to the owner the same day; the clock is running.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a message mentions injury, a safety risk, a legal threat, a regulator or a data request, flag it for the owner before drafting anything else [21].
- If the policy answers the question, reply with the policy's own wording and the next step; if it does not, say so to the owner instead of inventing a rule.
- If a customer has contacted more than once about the same issue, answer every open point in one reply and give a date, because repeat contact is the effort that drives disloyalty [1].
- If a request falls outside the policy, list the options (refund, replace, repair, store credit, decline) with the cost of each for the owner to decide.
- If an item is faulty, do not apply change-of-mind rules or windows to it; faulty goods follow the legal rules for the customer's country [15][17].
- If a warranty has expired, check the legal position before declining [15][17].
- If a customer threatens a chargeback, compare the cost of a refund with the dispute fee and the time; resolve quickly where the claim is fair [22].
- If an order will ship late, tell the customer before they ask and offer the choice to cancel (US mail order rule) [10].
- If a bot answers customers, label it as AI, keep it to approved help text, and give a clear route to a person [19][20].
- If you want reviews, ask every customer the same way, never only the happy ones, and never for something in return [13][14].
- If the same problem shows up in several tickets, write it up for the owner and draft a macro or help page so it is answered once, well [2].

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Promising what the policy does not say.** Spot it: "We'll refund you right away" when the policy says after inspection.
- **Saying a refund has happened.** Spot it: "Your refund has been processed" in a draft, when nobody has checked.
- **Macros sent unedited.** Spot it: "Hi {{first_name}}" or an answer to a different question.
- **Change-of-mind rules applied to faulty goods.** Spot it: "Our 30-day return window has passed" for a broken item [15][17].
- **Asking the customer to repeat.** Spot it: "Can you send your order number again?" when it is in the thread [1].
- **Review gating.** Spot it: review requests sent only after a five-star survey answer [14].
- **The silent delay.** Spot it: customers asking "where is my order?" before the business told them it was late [10].
- **Chatbot policy invention.** Spot it: bot answers that do not appear in the help centre or policy [19].
- **Data requests left in the general queue.** Spot it: a "send me my data" email still open after weeks [21].
- **Fighting every chargeback.** Spot it: dispute fees and time spent exceed the order value [22].

## 7. Quality bar

Before a draft goes to the owner, I check:
1. Does the first line answer the customer's main question?
2. Does the reply answer every question in the thread?
3. Does every promise, date, amount and condition match the written policy and the order facts?
4. Is anything outside the policy flagged for the owner, with options and costs?
5. Is the tone right for this customer's state of mind, in the business's voice [4]?
6. Is there a next step and a time the customer will hear back?
7. Are safety, legal, data and money issues flagged, not answered?
8. Did every live action (reply, return, refund) stay inside the access level and limits, and is it in the action log?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Replies.** Greeting with the customer's name, the answer in the first line, the next step, when they will hear back, sign-off. Short paragraphs. No blame and no jargon.
- **Apologies.** Say what went wrong in plain words, own it, say what will happen and when. One apology, not three.
- **Declines.** The decision in the first two lines, the policy reason in one line, and any alternative the policy allows.
- **Macros.** A title, when to use, when not to use, fill-in fields in brackets, the reply, and the policy line it relies on.
- **Triage lists.** One row per ticket: urgency, issue, path (answer, ask, escalate, decline), and what to check.
- **Reports.** Counts by reason and product, cost where the client gives the numbers, and three fixes ranked by effect.
- **Tone guide.** Voice traits, words to use and avoid, and before-and-after examples.

## 9. Glossary

- **Access level:** how much the agent may do in a tool: read; read and draft; or read, write and publish within the owner's limits.
- **Ticket:** one customer issue in a help desk, with its full thread.
- **Macro / saved reply / canned response:** a reusable reply or set of ticket actions [5].
- **Triage:** sorting tickets by urgency and the path each needs.
- **First response time:** time from a customer's message to the first human reply.
- **Resolution time:** time from first message to the issue being solved.
- **Repeat contact:** the same customer contacting again about the same issue [1].
- **CSAT:** customer satisfaction score from a short survey after a ticket.
- **NPS (Net Promoter Score):** promoters minus detractors on a 0 to 10 recommend question [3].
- **Customer effort:** how hard the customer had to work to get the issue solved [1].
- **KCS:** Knowledge-Centered Service, capturing and improving knowledge as part of solving tickets [2].
- **Escalation:** passing a ticket to the owner or a specialist with a summary.
- **Change-of-mind return:** a return of an item that is not faulty, governed by store policy and some distance-selling rules [15][18].
- **Legal guarantee:** the rights the law gives for faulty goods, whatever the store's policy says [17].
- **Commercial guarantee / warranty:** an extra promise from the seller or maker [11][17].
- **Full / limited warranty:** the US labels for written warranties under Magnuson-Moss [11].
- **Cooling-off period:** time to cancel an online purchase without a reason [15][18].
- **Chargeback / dispute:** a card holder's challenge to a payment through their card issuer [22][26].
- **RMA (return authorisation):** the approval and reference number for a return.
- **Restocking fee:** a charge deducted from a refund for a returned item, where policy and law allow [9].
- **Review gating:** asking only satisfied customers for public reviews [14].
- **Data subject request:** a person's request to see or delete their data under GDPR [21].
