# Customer path map and service blueprint

## When to use

- Customers complain about something no single team owns ("nobody told me", "I had to ask three times").
- A business is redesigning onboarding, delivery, support or cancelling and wants to see the whole picture first.
- Leaders disagree about where customers struggle and want evidence.

## What you need

1. Which customer type and which stretch of their experience to map, with a clear start and end ("from free trial to the third month").
2. Evidence: reviews, tickets with tags, survey comments, analytics funnels and drop-off points, call notes, interview notes, onboarding emails and letters.
3. The internal side for a blueprint: who does what, which systems, which hand-offs, which policies.
4. Any known metrics per step: conversion, time taken, contact rate, refunds.

**Access:** level 1 to read exports, analytics and documents; level 2 to build draft maps and blueprints on a whiteboard (Miro, FigJam) or in a document; level 3 to update shared team boards you name.

## Steps

### 1. Agree the frame

Customer type, the goal they are trying to reach, the start and end points, and the decision the map will feed. One map per customer type and goal.

### 2. Inventory the evidence and list the gaps

Tabulate each source: what it is, how many items, dates, and what it can tell you. List what is missing before drawing anything (for example "no evidence from customers who cancel in week one"). Gaps are findings too.

### 3. Draft stages from the customer's side

Name stages for what the customer is doing ("Choosing a plan", "Waiting for the first box"), not for departments. Then list the steps inside each stage.

### 4. Fill each step with sourced points

For each step: what the customer does, what they ask, how they feel, what they need, the touchpoint, the owner, and the source for each point (ticket tag and count, review quotes, funnel numbers). Feelings come only from evidence (words customers used, behaviour such as abandoning); otherwise mark the cell "guess".

### 5. Build the touchpoint inventory

Every email, page, letter, call, text and in-person contact the customer meets in the frame, with owner, purpose, trigger and timing. Mark gaps (long silences), repeats and messages that contradict each other. Give each a keep, fix or cut call.

### 6. Rank pain points

Score each pain point on reach (how many customers hit it, from data), severity (how badly it hurts: delay, cost, giving up) and cost to the business (contacts, refunds, lost customers). Use words or simple bands the client agrees on; do not invent weights. Pick the moments that matter most.

### 7. Blueprint where many teams meet

For the moments that matter, draw a service blueprint: customer actions; frontstage (what staff and systems do in view); backstage (what happens out of view); supporting processes and systems; and evidence (what the customer sees or receives). Draw the line of interaction, the line of visibility and the line of internal interaction. Mark fail points, waits and hand-offs where information is lost.

### 8. Write fixes and the future state

For each top moment: the fix, the owner, the date, and how to tell if it worked (a contact rate, a drop-off rate, a theme count). If asked, draw the future-state map showing what changes at each step and who owns it.

## Checks

- Stages are named from the customer's side.
- Every point has a source or is marked as a guess.
- Evidence gaps are listed before the map.
- Every touchpoint has an owner.
- Pain point ranking uses data for reach and says where it does not.
- Blueprint lanes and lines are all present for each mapped moment.
- Every fix has an owner and a measure.
- No invented quotes, feelings or numbers.

## Output format

1. Frame: customer type, goal, start, end, decision.
2. Evidence table and gaps list.
3. Map table per stage: step, does, asks, feels, needs, touchpoint, owner, source.
4. Touchpoint inventory with keep, fix or cut.
5. Ranked pain points.
6. Service blueprint for the top moments (a table with lanes as rows and steps as columns, or a board link).
7. Fix list and, if asked, the future-state map.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: "Northgate Physio", a four-clinic physiotherapy practice, mapping from first enquiry to the second appointment.*

> **Gaps.** No data on people who enquire and never book; no survey after the first appointment.
>
> | Stage | Step | Does | Asks | Feels | Touchpoint | Owner | Source |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Booking | Picks a time online | Fills the form, gets a holding email | "Is this confirmed?" | Unsure (guess) | Auto email | Front desk | 46 tickets tagged "is my booking confirmed" (Jul to Sep) |
> | Before visit | Waits | Nothing arrives for 3 days | "What do I bring?" | Anxious | None | Nobody | 18 reviews mention not knowing what to wear or bring |
> | Second visit | Rebooks | Told to call back | "Why can't I book now?" | Annoyed | Phone | Clinic manager | Funnel: 31% of first visits have no second booking within 14 days |
>
> **Blueprint fail point.** Confirmation depends on a staff member checking the diary each morning (backstage); bookings made on Fridays wait until Monday, so the customer sees silence above the line of visibility.
