# Mystery shopping programme

## When to use

- A group with several sites wants to know whether every site delivers the same standard.
- Managers argue about which branch is "best" without comparable evidence.
- A round of mystery shop scores has come in and needs reading fairly.

The client's shoppers (staff from another site, a panel, or an agency) make the visits. I design the programme and read the scores.

## What you need

1. The service standards as staff are trained on them, and any brand promises.
2. The channels to test (in person, phone, email, chat, website) and the number of sites.
3. Past scores, if any, and how they were used.
4. Who the shoppers are, and whether visits are ever recorded (audio, video, screenshots).
5. The countries or US states where visits happen, for recording and privacy rules.

**Access:** level 1 to read standards and scores; level 2 to draft the scoring form and briefing in a survey or form tool; level 3 to publish the scoring form to the shoppers you name and share reports with named managers. Sharing results that identify an individual employee needs a fresh yes, and this programme is designed not to.

## Steps

### 1. List what every customer should get

Read the standards and promises. List them step by step through a visit or contact: greeting, needs, recommendation, price and terms, close, follow-up.

### 2. Turn each standard into a test

Rewrite each as something any shopper would score the same way: "Greets within 30 seconds of entering: yes or no", "States the cancellation terms before payment: yes or no". Drop or rewrite anything a shopper cannot judge fairly ("makes the guest feel special"). Give each test a pass example.

### 3. Weight and set the pass mark

Weight standards by how much they matter to customers, using complaint and survey evidence where it exists, and agree the weights and pass mark with the client in advance. Write down why each weight was chosen.

### 4. Write scenarios

One scenario card per channel: what the shopper wants, a believable reason, the questions to ask, timing (day, time window), and what to observe. Use the same scenario at every site so scores compare. Avoid scenarios that set staff up to fail or that waste a lot of staff time.

### 5. Write the shopper briefing

Cover story, the scenario, what to note during and straight after the visit, how to score each test with examples, and a "do not" list written first:
- Do not trick or provoke staff, or claim to be someone in distress.
- Do not record unless the programme allows it and staff have been told it may happen.
- Do not buy beyond the agreed amount; do not keep goods unless agreed.
- Do not name or describe staff beyond a role ("the person at the front desk").

### 6. Check recording and privacy

The MRS Code requires telling participants about recording or observation, and says mystery shopping may be recorded only in a business's own-organisation programme where staff have been told it may happen. Recording laws differ by country and US state, and staff data is personal data: check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser before any recording.

### 7. Read the results

1. Visits per site and channel; flag any site with too few visits to judge, and do not rank it.
2. Average score by site, region, channel and standard, with the spread, not only the mean.
3. A grid of standards by site to show patterns (a standard most sites miss points to training or the standard itself).
4. The biggest gaps against the pass mark, and links to customer complaints or survey themes on the same standards.
5. Check shopper consistency: if one shopper scores much lower everywhere, flag it.

### 8. Coaching notes

For each site: what went well, the one fix that matters most, and how it will be checked next round. Notes go to site managers, not into individual performance files.

## Checks

- Every test comes from the client's standards and has a pass example.
- Scenarios are identical across sites for the same channel.
- The briefing's "do not" list is complete and recording follows the rules.
- Thin samples are flagged and not ranked.
- No result names or judges an individual employee.
- Weights and pass mark were agreed before the round.

## Output format

1. Standards table: standard, test, weight, pass example, source in the client's standards.
2. Scenario cards per channel.
3. Shopper briefing with the "do not" list first.
4. Results: site league table with visit counts and thin-sample flags, standards-by-site grid, gaps and links to complaints.
5. Coaching note per site.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a group of 12 small hotels. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> | Standard (from brand book) | Test | Weight | Pass example |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | "Warm welcome on arrival" | Greeted within 30 seconds of reaching the desk, before the shopper speaks | 3 | "Good evening, are you checking in?" |
> | "Clear about what's included" | Breakfast times and price stated before key handover | 2 | "Breakfast is 7 to 10, included in your rate." |
> | "We fix problems" | Offered a fix or an option within 2 minutes of the planted issue (no towel) | 3 | "I'll bring one up now, or there's a set in the cupboard." |
>
> **Results, round 3.** Site 7 (2 visits) is flagged as too few to judge. Most sites missed "breakfast stated before handover": a training gap, not a site problem.
>
> **Coaching note, site 4.** Went well: every shopper was greeted within 30 seconds. One fix: state breakfast times at check-in. Next round: test the same standard on a weekend evening.
