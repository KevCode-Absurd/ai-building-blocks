# Phishing awareness session

## When to use

- An owner wants staff to stop falling for fake emails, texts and calls.
- Someone nearly paid a fake invoice or typed a password into a fake page.
- New staff need security basics in their first week.
- A quarterly review shows few or no reports of suspicious messages.

Do not use it to shame or rank individuals, to send real phishing emails without written authorisation, or to replace technical controls such as MFA and mail filtering. Training is one layer, not the defence.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** sector, tools, how staff report today, recent suspicious messages (with personal details removed).
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** session slides or script, quiz, staff rules and drill in the agreed folder; an unscheduled training campaign in the client's tool.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the staff rules where the team reads them and schedule the agreed training campaign or reminder. A phishing simulation also needs the owner's written authorisation naming the dates, the staff included and who is told, plus HR's agreement on how results are used.

## What you need

1. Sector, team size and roles (who pays invoices, who handles customer data, who resets passwords).
2. Tools staff use: email, chat, phones, store admin, accounting.
3. How staff report a suspicious message today, and who receives the report.
4. Two or three real suspicious messages the business received, with names and links removed.
5. Any rules already in place (payment approvals, password manager, two-step sign-in).

## Steps

### 1. Set the goal

One sentence: "After this session, staff can spot the warning signs of a phishing message or call, check a payment-change request by phone, and report anything suspicious within minutes." Measures: number of reports in the next month, time to report, and payment changes checked by call-back.

### 2. Build realistic examples

Write four to six made-up examples that fit the sector, each clearly labelled as an example:
- a fake invoice or bank-detail change from a known supplier;
- a fake sign-in page for the email or file service;
- a parcel or payment text message;
- a phone call from "IT support" asking for a code or to approve a sign-in prompt;
- a fake boss asking for an urgent payment or gift cards;
- for stores, a fake platform or payment provider notice.
Never use a real person's name or a real company's branding in a way that could be mistaken for the real thing.

### 3. Teach the warning signs

Urgency and pressure, an unusual request, a changed bank account, a link or sender that does not match, a request for a code or a prompt approval, and a request to keep it secret. Pair each sign with one action.

### 4. Teach the three rules

1. **Stop and check:** any payment change or urgent payment is checked by phone on a number already on file.
2. **Never share codes:** no one, including IT, needs your sign-in code or an approved prompt you did not start.
3. **Report fast, no blame:** report anything odd, even after clicking; quick reports limit damage.

### 5. Show how to report

The exact route: a report button in the mail client if there is one, or forward to a named person; in the UK, also forward to the NCSC's scam email service; in the US, ReportFraud.ftc.gov or IC3.gov for fraud. Say what happens after a report and when the reporter hears back.

### 6. Plan the 30 minutes

0 to 5: why it matters, using a made-up local story. 5 to 15: examples, spot the signs together. 15 to 22: the three rules and the call-back script. 22 to 27: quiz. 27 to 30: how to report and questions.

### 7. Write the quiz

Five to eight items: "Phish or fine? What would you do?" Each answer explains the sign and the action. No trick questions.

### 8. One-page staff rules

Numbered, one action per line: two-step sign-in on, password manager, updates, email links, payment changes, codes, lost devices, reporting. End with "If in doubt, stop and ask [name, phone]."

### 9. Reporting drill (no simulation needed)

Hand out three printed or screenshot examples and time how long the team takes to report each through the real route. This tests the process without tricking anyone.

### 10. Simulation (optional, gated)

Only with the owner's written authorisation and HR's agreement, using a tool the client already licenses (for example Microsoft's Attack simulation training on plans that include it). Report group trends and reporting rates only. No names to managers, no sanctions.

## Checks

- Every example is made up and labelled; no real brand imitation that could fool outsiders.
- The reporting route is real, named and tested.
- The call-back rule names who calls and which number list is used.
- Measures count reports and time to report, not just clicks.
- No individual is named, ranked or punished.
- Any simulation has written authorisation and HR agreement on file.
- Staff rules fit on one page in plain words.

## Output format

1. **Session plan** with timings and speaker notes.
2. **Examples sheet** (made up, labelled).
3. **Quiz** with answers and explanations.
4. **One-page staff rules.**
5. **Reporting drill** instructions and a results table (time to report, route used).
6. **Measures** to check in 30 days.

## Example

*Made-up example: "Kestrel Joinery" and its supplier "Oakline Timber" are invented for illustration.*

**Example 2 (labelled "practice example"):**
"Hi, Oakline Timber here. We've moved banks; please use the new details below for this week's invoice. Our phones are down so email only. Thanks, Accounts."

**Signs:** changed bank details; pressure to avoid the phone; generic sign-off.
**Action:** Stop. Call Oakline on the number in your supplier file, not one in the email. Report the email to Sam (07700 900000) using the report button.

**Quiz item:** "A text says your parcel can't be delivered and asks for a £1.45 fee. Phish or fine?" **Answer:** Phish. Small fees are a common way to collect card details. Delete it, and if you clicked, tell Sam today.

**Staff rule 5:** Any change to how we pay someone is checked by phone on the number we already hold, by someone other than the person who received the request.
