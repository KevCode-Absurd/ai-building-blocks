# Webinar programme

A webinar is a sales conversation with a room of people at once. It works when the right people come, learn something they need to decide, and know exactly what to do next.

## When to use

- A B2B team wants a webinar, workshop or LinkedIn Live that leads to meetings.
- Past webinars drew registrations but nobody followed up, or follow-up went to everyone the same way.
- A partner offers a co-hosted session.
- An account programme needs a tier 2 or tier 3 play.

## What you need

1. **The next step** attendees should take: a demo, an assessment, a pilot, a cost review.
2. **Audience**: the account tiers and buying-group roles you want in the room.
3. **Topic and speakers**: what you can teach with real authority, and who presents.
4. **Date and platform**: Zoom Webinars, Livestorm, LinkedIn Live (needs a third-party streaming tool and LinkedIn's access criteria), or another.
5. **Past numbers** if any: registrants, attendees, replay views, meetings booked.
6. **Lists you may email**, with each list's consent basis by country.

**Access:** level 1 to read past reports and lists. Level 2 to set up the webinar unpublished, draft the registration page, emails and LinkedIn event, and build a draft event audience. Level 3 to publish the registration page, launch approved promotion within the cap, send approved invitations and reminders to approved lists, and export attendee reports. Promoting to a new list or audience needs a fresh yes.

## Steps

### 1. Fix the next step and write the follow-up first
Write four follow-ups before anything else:
- **Attendees:** thanks, the replay, one useful extra, the next step.
- **No-shows:** the replay and a short summary of what they missed, same next step.
- **Engaged target accounts:** a personal note from the account owner, referencing their question or poll answer.
- **Everyone, a week later:** a second useful piece and a softer version of the ask.
The session exists to make these emails make sense.

### 2. Outline the session
A promise (what they leave able to do), three teaching points, one real story or case, the next step, then questions. Plan for attention: Livestorm's 2025 data showed an average watch time of 26 minutes against 68 minutes scheduled, so put the core teaching early. Add a poll or two that also tells you who is ready to talk.

### 3. Write the run sheet
Minute by minute: who speaks, what is on screen, who runs chat and Q&A, when polls go out, and a fallback for each failure (speaker drops, slides fail, audio fails). Schedule a rehearsal on the platform.

### 4. Set registration questions
Ask only what you will use: name, work email, company, role. Add one question that helps follow-up (the challenge they want answered). Add accessibility needs (captions, other formats) as an optional question. Show the privacy notice and, where needed, a separate unticked box for marketing consent. Don't ask for anything sensitive.

### 5. Plan promotion
Invite target accounts first through account owners, then opted-in email lists, the LinkedIn event, partners and paid promotion if budgeted. In Livestorm's 2025 data, 49.7% of registrations arrived in the final seven days and 15.3% on the day, so keep promoting and send reminders in the final week, the day before and an hour before. Retarget LinkedIn event engagers who have not registered, if the audience reaches the 300-member minimum.

### 6. Decide replay access
Public replays drew about 3.7 times the views of gated ones in Livestorm's data. Gate only when the lead is worth more than the reach; otherwise publish and capture interest with the next step.

### 7. Run and report
After the session: send follow-ups the same day (at level 3 within approved lists), pass engaged target-account attendees to account owners by name, and report registrants, attendees, show-up rate, replay views, questions, next steps taken, meetings booked and, at the end of the sales cycle, pipeline and revenue. Compare with the client's own past webinars, not with averages.

## Checks

- The next step is decided before the invitation is written.
- Follow-up differs for attendees, no-shows and engaged accounts.
- The run sheet has a fallback for each likely failure and a rehearsal date.
- Registration asks only what will be used, with privacy notice and consent handled by country.
- No false scarcity ("only 20 seats") when there is no limit.
- Reports separate registrants from attendees and never invent counts.
- Level 2 and level 3 steps are marked.

## Output format

1. Next step and follow-up emails (four)
2. Session outline with timings
3. Run sheet with fallbacks
4. Registration page copy and questions
5. Promotion calendar by day, with owners and lists
6. Report template

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business: Ledgerline, accounting software for multi-site dental groups. Figures are invented for illustration.*

> **Next step:** a 30-minute review of the attendee's month-end close, booked from the follow-up.
>
> **Outline (45 minutes):** 0:00 Promise: "close the month in three days, not ten". 0:03 Point 1: the three reconciliations that cause most delay. 0:13 Point 2: one chart of accounts across sites. 0:23 Point 3: who signs off what. 0:30 Story: a seven-site group's first quarter, told by its finance manager. 0:37 Next step and poll ("Would a review of your close help?"). 0:40 Questions.
>
> **Run sheet, 0:13:** Slides 8 to 14, Ana presents; Jo posts the template link in chat. *If Ana's audio fails:* Jo takes over from the speaker notes and Ana rejoins by phone.
>
> **Report (two weeks after):** 212 registered, 96 attended live (45%), 61 replay views. 18 said yes to the poll; 11 reviews booked; 7 from tier 1 and 2 accounts, passed to their owners the same afternoon.
