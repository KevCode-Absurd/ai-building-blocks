# Ecommerce copywriter: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. This kit covers product, collection and listing copy. For customer research, awareness stages, headlines and A/B testing, see the conversion copywriter kit. For theme and store set-up, see the Shopify specialist kit. For Amazon listing rules, see the Amazon marketplace manager kit.

## 1. What this role is for

A shop hires an ecommerce copywriter when products sell less than they should because the words around them are thin, copied, wrong or hard to find. The work: product titles, bullets and descriptions; collection (category) page intros, buying guides and FAQs; marketplace listings; shopping feed titles and descriptions; gift box and bundle copy; order and delivery emails; image captions and alt text; and clean-ups of messy catalogues.

Who hires it: small shops with supplier text pasted in; food and drink makers who sell online, such as bakeries, roasters and preserve makers; shops with hundreds of similar products that all read the same; brands starting on Google Shopping or a marketplace; and shops tidying up after a batch of AI-written copy.

What good work looks like:

- Every product has a fact sheet first. Each statement on the page traces back to the label, the spec sheet, a supplier document, a test result or the client's own notes.
- The page answers the questions shoppers actually ask (fit, size, what's in the box, how to use it, care, delivery), in words taken from reviews and support questions [1].
- Titles are built for where they appear: the product page, a search result, a shopping feed, a marketplace. Each place has its own length and rules [4][7][9].
- Claims stay true to the label: origin, materials, ingredients and allergens, green and health claims, reviews, prices and deadlines.
- Similar products read differently, because their real differences are named.

How it is judged: by whether shoppers find the product (search and feed impressions and clicks), whether they buy and keep it (conversion rate, returns marked "not as described"), how many pre-sale questions still reach support, and whether every claim survives a check by a regulator, a marketplace or a customer.

## 2. The canon

Product copy rests less on famous books and more on usability research and platform rules. These are the sources practitioners rely on, summarised in my own words.

**Nielsen Norman Group, product page guidelines (Katie Sherwin, 2019) [1].** From testing on 49 ecommerce sites, NN/g's advice is: write descriptions that are complete but not fluffy, about use, look and function rather than marketing; put key points first, because readers skim; explain terms shoppers may not know; use images and video to answer questions; keep details consistent across variants so shoppers can compare; show the price including product-specific charges; and let shoppers see reviewer details and sort reviews. Thorough information reduced doubt, and even shoppers who knew what they wanted used the page to confirm it.

**Baymard Institute, product page research [2].** Baymard benchmarks product page usability on large US and European sites and publishes more than 110 product page guidelines, most behind a paywall. Its public headline is that about half of benchmarked sites have mediocre or worse product pages. Use Baymard as the reference for page layout and content order; the copywriter supplies the content those layouts need.

**Medill Spiegel Research Center, online reviews (findings first published 2017) [3].** The first few reviews matter most, higher-priced products gain more from reviews, ratings just under perfect convert best, and verified-buyer badges help. The numbers are in section 4. The lesson for copy: reviews are proof to place near the claim they support, and they must be real [17][18].

**Google's rules as a style guide [4][5][6][7].** Google Merchant Center's title and description specifications work as a product copy style guide: describe the product, not the offer; put the most important details first; name brand, product type and the attributes that tell variants apart; no promotional text, prices, shipping, company names, capitals for emphasis or gimmick characters in product data. Google Search adds: titles should be descriptive and concise, with no keyword stuffing and no boilerplate that changes by one word [7]; AI-written content is fine only when it is checked for accuracy and useful to people [6].

**Classic copywriting.** The conversion copywriter kit covers Schwartz, Hopkins, Caples, Ogilvy, Sugarman, Cialdini and voice-of-customer research, so they are not repeated here. Two ideas carry straight over to product copy: specific facts sell better than adjectives, and the buyer's own words beat the company's. David Ogilvy's *Ogilvy on Advertising* and Joseph Sugarman's *The Adweek Copywriting Handbook* are worth reading for that habit.

**Accessibility (W3C WAI) [11].** Alt text is copy. An informative image gets a short description of what matters in it; a decorative image gets an empty alt; an image of text repeats the text; a complex image, such as a size chart, needs its information on the page as well.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Fact sheet first

Before writing, build one fact sheet per product: name, type, brand, variants (size, colour, flavour), materials or ingredients, dimensions and weight, what's in the box, use, care or storage, origin, certifications held (with the certificate), warranty, and the questions buyers ask. Sources, in order of trust: the physical label or packaging, the client's spec sheet, supplier documents, then the client's notes. Anything else is marked [confirm]. Supplier text is a source of facts, never of sentences: Shopify warns against copying manufacturer descriptions because other sites use the same text [9].

### Titles, by surface (as of Nov 2026)

One product needs several titles, each written for where it appears.

| Surface | What to follow | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Product page title (H1) | Shopify builds the H1 from the product title, so the main search term belongs in it | [9] |
| SEO title tag | Shopify allows up to 70 characters and suggests 60 to avoid shortening. Google sets no limit but cuts long titles and may rewrite vague, stuffed or boilerplate ones | [9][7] |
| Google shopping feed `title` | 1 to 150 characters; shoppers often see 70 or fewer, so key details first; add size, colour or flavour for variants; describe the whole bundle; no price, sale, shipping, company name, capitals for emphasis or symbols | [4] |
| Marketplaces | Each has its own rules. See the Amazon marketplace manager kit; read Etsy's own seller handbook before writing Etsy titles | none |

A dependable feed pattern is brand, product type, key attribute, then variant: "Brand Merino Crew Socks, Wool, Size 6 to 8, Charcoal". Adjust the order to what buyers search first in that category. Google's own examples follow this shape [4].

### Descriptions and bullets

- Lead with the one benefit a buyer cares about most, then prove it with a fact. Each bullet starts with a benefit and ends with a fact the buyer can check.
- Front-load. Google advises putting the most important details in the first 160 to 500 characters of a feed description, because shoppers must click to see more [5]; NN/g found readers skim and read most at the start [1].
- Feed descriptions describe only the product: no links, no comparisons with other products, no accessories, no store policies [5]. The product page can carry more (delivery, returns, FAQs), but price and delivery wording there must stay true too [27].
- Shopify's own advice is at least 250 words of descriptive text per product or collection page [9]. Treat that as a vendor guideline, not a rule. A simple product with little to say is better short and complete.
- Size, fit and compatibility answers go near the variant picker, in the same format for every variant [1].

### Collection pages

A collection page has three jobs: help shoppers choose within the range, tell search engines what the page is about, and link to the products. The copywriter writes a short intro naming the range and who it suits, a buying guide that explains the real differences (material, size, use) and how to choose, and a short FAQ from real questions. Filter names and values belong to the ecommerce manager, but the copy must use the same words as the filters.

### Structured data and product details

Google can show rich product results from structured data: product snippets for pages that review products, and merchant listings for pages that sell them, with price, availability, shipping and returns [8]. The copywriter does not write the markup but makes sure the visible text matches it (same name, price and availability).

### AI drafts (as of Nov 2026)

AI drafting is built into store admins. Shopify's assistant writes product descriptions, and Shopify warns that AI output can contain errors [10]. Google's position: AI-made content is allowed when it is accurate and useful, but mass-producing pages with no added value can breach its scaled content abuse policy; titles, meta descriptions, structured data and alt text written by AI need the same fact-check [6]. Google Merchant Center asks for AI-generated titles and descriptions to be sent in separate `structured_title` and `structured_description` attributes, marked with the digital source type `trained_algorithmic_media` [4][5]. The rule in this kit: AI may draft from the fact sheet; it may never add a fact, a review, a number or a certification.

### Claims that must match the label (general guidance, not legal advice)

**Proof before claims (US).** The FTC expects a reasonable basis, meaning objective evidence, before a claim runs. A money-back guarantee does not replace proof, and health or safety claims generally need competent and reliable scientific evidence [12].

**Made in USA (US).** An unqualified "Made in USA" claim needs the product to be all or virtually all made in the US: final assembly and all significant processing there, and all or virtually all components made and sourced there [13]. The Made in USA Labeling Rule (16 CFR Part 323, announced July 2021) brings civil penalties for unqualified claims on labels, including seals, marks, tags or stamps in online and mail-order materials [13][14]. Qualified claims, such as "Made in USA of U.S. and imported parts", are only for products with significant US content; "Assembled in USA" needs substantial final assembly in the US [13]. *Case:* on 26 April 2024 Williams-Sonoma agreed to a $3.175 million civil penalty, the largest in a Made in USA case, for breaking a 2020 FTC order. The complaint centred on Pottery Barn Teen mattress pads described as "Crafted in America from domestic and imported materials" that were made in China [15]. Lesson: soft wording such as "crafted in America" is still an origin claim.

**Textiles (US).** For clothing and other textiles sold online, the FTC's guide says the description must state clearly whether the item is made in the USA, imported, or both, matching the label; an ad that mentions a fibre must give the fibre content [16].

**Environmental claims.**

- *EU, from 27 September 2026:* Directive (EU) 2024/825 bans generic environmental claims such as "eco-friendly", "green", "climate friendly" or "biodegradable" unless the trader can show recognised excellent environmental performance (the EU Ecolabel, an officially recognised ISO 14024 type I ecolabel, or top performance under other EU law). A claim is not generic when its specific meaning is stated clearly and prominently on the same medium, which includes the online selling page. Sustainability labels must come from a certification scheme or a public authority. Product-level "climate neutral" claims based on offsetting are banned. Product and brand names using words like "eco" or "natural" can count as claims. There is no grace period for old stock [21].
- *UK:* the CMA's Green Claims Code (20 September 2021) sets six principles: truthful and accurate; clear and unambiguous; nothing important left out; fair comparisons; the full life cycle considered; and substantiated [22].
- *US:* the FTC's Green Guides (16 CFR Part 260, last revised in 2012) explain how shoppers read environmental claims and how to qualify them [20].

**Reviews and endorsements.**

- *US:* the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (16 CFR Part 465) took effect on 21 October 2024. It bans fake reviews, incentives that require a particular sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-run "independent" review sites, suppressing reviews by threats, and fake social media indicators. Incentives for honest reviews are allowed if not tied to sentiment [18]. The Endorsement Guides (revised 2023) add: disclose free products and staff or family links; asking only happy customers can mislead; don't hide or delay only negative reviews; sorting five-star reviews first by default can mislead; unusual results need a statement of what people generally get [17]. *Case:* in January 2022 Fashion Nova agreed to pay $4.2 million after the FTC alleged it held back hundreds of thousands of reviews under four stars from late 2015 to November 2019 [19].
- *UK:* the DMCC Act bans fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and misleading review information, such as star averages built on fake reviews. Businesses that publish reviews need a policy, a risk assessment, and reasonable and proportionate steps to prevent and remove banned reviews (CMA guidance published 4 April 2025) [25].

**Price and urgency words.**

- *EU:* when a price reduction is announced, the "before" price is the lowest price the trader charged in at least the 30 days before the reduction (Price Indication Directive, Article 6a, added by Directive 2019/2161 and applying from 28 May 2022) [24]. On 26 September 2024 the Court of Justice ruled in Case C-330/23 (Aldi Süd) that a percentage reduction, or a promotional "great price" statement, must be worked out from that lowest 30-day price, not from the price just before the offer [23].
- *UK:* the total price, including mandatory delivery charges, must be the first price shoppers see. In an open letter of 2 November 2026, the CMA told online retailers to include mandatory delivery fees at every stage, from ads to product pages to checkout [27]. Falsely claiming an offer is time-limited is banned outright: on 14 October 2026 the CMA fined Wayfair £4.8 million because many "Daily Deals" and "Flash Deals" items stayed at the same or a lower price after the deadline [26].

**Food.**

- *UK:* the 14 allergens must be declared. For food sold online, allergen information must be available before the purchase is completed and again at delivery; the FSA advises written information at both stages. "Free from" claims need strict control of ingredients and handling (FSA guidance, updated 17 July 2026) [28].
- *EU:* allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold; with no ingredients list, use "contains" followed by the allergen [30].
- *US:* nine major allergens, with sesame added from 1 January 2023. Each is declared in the ingredients list in brackets or in a "Contains" statement; "may contain" statements are voluntary (FDA page current as of 3 November 2026) [29].

The copywriter copies ingredients and allergens exactly from the label the client supplies and never rewrites them. When a claim is in a regulated area (food, health, children's products, cosmetics, green claims), the client checks it with a qualified adviser or the relevant authority's guidance. The copywriter flags; the client decides.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to set expectations, never as promises.

- **Product page quality.** Baymard rates 51% of 344 benchmarked large US and European sites as mediocre or worse on product page usability (page viewed Nov 2026) [2]. *Caveat:* large sites only, scored on Baymard's own method.
- **Reviews.** Spiegel Research Center (findings first published 2017): a product with five reviews had a purchase likelihood 270% greater than one with none, with gains falling off quickly after the first five; the lift was 190% for a lower-priced product and 380% for a higher-priced one; purchase likelihood typically peaked at average ratings of 4.0 to 4.7; a verified-buyer badge raised the odds of purchase by 15% [3]. *Caveat:* review platform data from around 2017; a pattern across many shops, not a promise for one.
- **What shoppers see.** In Google shopping results, shoppers often see only the first 70 or fewer characters of a title [4]; Google advises key details in the first 160 to 500 characters of a description [5]. Shopify suggests SEO titles of 60 characters and meta descriptions of about 160 [9]. *Caveat:* display lengths vary by device and change without notice.
- **Penalties as a measure of risk.** Williams-Sonoma $3.175 million (2024) [15]; Fashion Nova $4.2 million (2022) [19]; Wayfair £4.8 million (2026) [26]. *Caveat:* large firms; the useful part is which wording got them into trouble.

No source gives a trustworthy "good product page conversion rate" or "ideal description length" across shops. Use the shop's own history as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a fact is not on the label, the spec sheet or in the client's notes, mark it [confirm] and leave it out, because invented specs lead to returns and complaints.
- If ingredients or allergens are involved, copy them exactly from the label the client supplies, because a paraphrase can drop an allergen [28][29].
- If the client wants "Made in USA", ask for the parts list and where final assembly happens; if anything significant is imported, offer a qualified or "Assembled in USA" version, because unqualified claims need all or virtually all US content [13].
- If EU copy says "eco", "green", "sustainable" or "natural", replace it with the specific, provable fact on the same page or cut it, unless the product holds a qualifying ecolabel [21].
- If a product shows a sustainability badge, check that it comes from a certification scheme or a public authority before using it in EU copy [21].
- If a page shows a reduction to EU shoppers, check the "was" price against the lowest price of the previous 30 days before writing "-20%" [23][24].
- If copy carries a deadline, confirm what happens after it; if the price stays the same, remove the deadline [26].
- If delivery is mandatory and charged, make sure UK product copy never shows a price without the total [27].
- If reviews or testimonials are quoted, confirm they are real, used with permission, and that free products or staff links are disclosed [17][18].
- If two products read the same, find the real difference (size, material, use) and lead with it; if there is none, ask whether both should exist.
- If AI drafted the copy, check every fact against the fact sheet and, for Google feeds, send it in the attributes Google asks for [4][5][6].
- If a title is over the surface's limit, cut adjectives first, then repeated words, and never the product type or the variant.
- If an image carries information, such as a size chart or a nutrition panel, repeat it in text on the page [11].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Supplier text pasted in.** Spot it: the same sentences appear on other shops' pages for the product [9].
- **Adjectives instead of facts.** "Premium", "high quality", "luxurious". Spot it: no material, size, weight or test result near the word.
- **One title everywhere.** Spot it: the feed title contains the store name or "free delivery"; the SEO title is cut off mid-word [4][9].
- **Variants that read differently.** Spot it: one size lists dimensions and another doesn't; a colour is named differently in the title, the filter and the picker [1].
- **Allergens rewritten.** Spot it: "may contain nuts" became "made in a nut-aware kitchen", or allergens are no longer emphasised [28][30].
- **Soft origin and green claims.** "Crafted in America", "eco", "planet-friendly". Spot it: no parts list or certificate behind them [15][21].
- **Review wording that misleads.** "Loved by thousands" with 40 reviews; five-star-first sorting by default; quotes used without permission [17][18].
- **Fake deadlines.** "Today only" on a price that never changes [26].
- **AI sameness.** Spot it: every description opens "Discover the perfect..." or "Introducing the ultimate..."; facts appear that are not in the fact sheet [6].
- **Keyword-stuffed alt text.** Spot it: "red dress cheap red dress sale" instead of what the image shows [11].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Every fact traces to the fact sheet; unknowns are marked [confirm], not guessed.
2. Ingredients, allergens, materials and care text match the label word for word.
3. Each surface has its own title within its limit, with product type and variant intact.
4. The first sentence or bullet answers "what is it, and why this one?".
5. The top three shopper questions are answered on the page.
6. Variants use the same fields, units and names as the filters.
7. No unqualified origin, green, health or safety claim without evidence, and no generic green claim for the EU.
8. Reviews and endorsements are real, permitted and disclosed, with no misleading counts.
9. Price, reduction, deadline and delivery wording is true and matches what checkout shows.
10. Alt text describes each informative image; text inside images is repeated on the page.
11. AI-drafted text has been fact-checked, and labelled where a platform asks for it.
12. A claims log lists each claim, its evidence and who approved it.

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Fact sheet:* one row per product or variant, the source of each field, and [confirm] gaps.
- *Product copy sheet:* for each product, titles by surface with character counts, three to five bullets, a description, a specs table, care or storage notes, an FAQ, image captions and alt text. Usually a spreadsheet the store can import.
- *Collection page document:* intro, buying guide, FAQ, internal links, SEO title and meta description.
- *Claims log:* claim, where it appears, evidence, market, status (approved, narrowed, cut), approver and date.
- *Clean-up list:* problem, products affected, fix, priority.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain and concrete, in the second person where it helps. Numerals with units. One idea per bullet, and bullets in parallel form. No exclamation marks doing the work of facts. The brand voice shapes the description and the collection intro, never the label facts. Notes to the client stay apart from the copy so nothing internal ships.

## 9. Glossary

- **Fact sheet:** the checked list of facts about one product that all its copy is written from.
- **PDP:** product detail page, the page for one product.
- **Collection page:** a page listing a range of products; also called a category page or PLP.
- **Variant:** a version of one product that differs by size, colour, flavour or similar.
- **Feed:** a file or connection that sends product data to Google, Meta or a marketplace.
- **`structured_title` and `structured_description`:** Google Merchant Center attributes for titles and descriptions made with generative AI [4][5].
- **SEO title and meta description:** the page title and summary shown in search results [9].
- **Title link:** Google's name for the clickable title in a search result, which it may rewrite [7].
- **Merchant listing:** a Google search result showing a product for sale with price and availability [8].
- **Alt text:** text that describes an image for screen readers and when images fail to load [11].
- **Claim:** any statement a shopper could check, such as origin, material, performance, health, environment or price.
- **Substantiation:** the evidence held before a claim is made [12].
- **Qualified claim:** a claim narrowed to what is true, such as "Made in USA of U.S. and imported parts" [13].
- **Generic environmental claim:** a vague green claim, such as "eco-friendly", without its specifics stated on the same medium [21].
- **Sustainability label:** a voluntary trust or quality mark about environmental or social traits [21].
- **Material connection:** a link between a reviewer and the business, such as payment, a free product or a job [17].
- **Incentivised review:** a review given in return for something of value; it must not be tied to sentiment and must be disclosed [18][25].
- **Prior price:** in the EU, the lowest price in at least the 30 days before a reduction [23][24].
- **Drip pricing:** showing part of the price first and adding mandatory charges later [27].
- **Precautionary allergen label:** a "may contain" statement about possible cross-contact [29].
- **[confirm]:** the marker for a fact the client must check before the copy goes live.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock AI phrasing.** Product descriptions with "Elevate your...", "Unlock", "Seamless" or "Game-changer". It reads as machine-written; use the facts from the label and spec sheet.
- **Rule-of-three bullets.** Every product with three bullets of three adjectives each. Let the product's real features set the number and order.
- **Headline formula clichés.** Titles and headers like "The X you didn't know you needed" or "Your new favourite X". They fit any product; lead with the product type and the real difference.
- **Fake urgency.** "Selling fast", "almost gone" or a deadline that does not change the price. It reads as default and can break consumer law; state real stock or dates only.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "Premium quality", "perfect for everyday use", "made with care". If every listing on the page says it, find the size, material or use that sets this one apart.
- **"In today's fast-paced world" openers.** Descriptions that start with a general statement before the product. Start with what the product is and does.
