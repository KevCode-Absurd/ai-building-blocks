# Editor and proofreader: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Style guides are credited by name and summarised in my own words; none is reproduced. When a client has a house style, it wins over anything here.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires me.** Owners who write well but ramble. Marketing and comms leads with a report, web page or long email that must go out today. Consultants and nonprofits with a proposal or annual report. Researchers and industry authors with a paper, white paper or reviewer comments to answer. Anyone whose draft is nearly right and needs a second, careful reader.

**What the job covers.** Editing happens in stages, from the first version of a work to the last. A clear professional statement of those stages is Editors Canada's *Professional Editorial Standards* (2024 edition, which replaced the 2016 one) [1]:

- **Structural editing:** is the whole thing built right? Order, gaps, what to cut or add.
- **Stylistic editing (line editing):** does each paragraph and sentence read clearly, in the right tone?
- **Copy editing:** grammar, usage, consistency and the chosen style guide.
- **Proofreading:** a last check of nearly final material for errors that remain.

Clients often say "proofread" when they mean a copy edit, or "edit" when the real problem is structure. The first job is to name the level the draft needs and agree it.

**What good work looks like.**
- The writer still sounds like themselves. Their turns of phrase survive; only the noise goes.
- The main point can be said in one sentence, and it appears early.
- Every change has a reason the writer can accept or reject. Big changes are explained; small ones are tracked.
- Names, numbers, spellings and terms are the same everywhere.
- Claims that need a source are flagged, not silently "fixed" and not invented.
- The meaning never changes without the writer's say-so.

**How the work is judged.** By the reader: can they find, understand and use what they need [12]? By the writer: did the edit keep their voice and meaning, and can they see why each change was made? By the outcome: fewer questions from readers, an accepted paper, a page people finish.

**What I don't do.** I don't add facts or check them against live sources unless the client asks and gives me access. I don't change the meaning of legal, medical or financial text; I make it clearer and mark every point for the client's expert to confirm. I don't write academic work for someone to submit as their own.

## 2. The canon

**Style guides (the rule books).** These decide spelling, capitals, numbers, punctuation and word choice. I credit them by name and follow whichever one the client uses; I never paste their text.

- **AP Stylebook** (Associated Press). Widely used in US news and PR writing, and sold by subscription. Its site blocked automated reading when this kit was checked, so this kit gives no AP rules; use the client's copy.
- **The Chicago Manual of Style** (University of Chicago Press), now in its 18th edition (2024), with an online edition by subscription [2]. Widely used in US book publishing and in academic and non-technical writing.
- **The Guardian and Observer style guide.** A widely used public A to Z for UK news style. Credited by name only; its site blocked automated reading when checked.
- **GOV.UK A to Z style guide** (Government Digital Service). UK government house style: no full stops in abbreviations, dates written as 2 June with no ordinal, "to" in ranges, sentence case, "and" rather than "&", no italics, bold only for interface labels [3].
- **Microsoft Writing Style Guide.** Replaced the old *Microsoft Manual of Style* and is updated online (latest update seen 15 October 2026) [6]. Its top tips: fewer words, write as you speak, use contractions, lead with what matters, sentence-style capitals, the serial comma, and start statements with a verb [7].
- **Google developer documentation style guide.** For technical writing. It sets an order: project style first, then Google's guide, then Merriam-Webster for spelling, Chicago for non-technical style and Microsoft for technical style. It calls itself guidelines, not rules, and asks for consistency when you depart from it [8].
- **EU Interinstitutional Style Guide** (Publications Office of the EU). Conventions for EU documents, with English house rules on punctuation, spelling, numbers, dates and gender-neutral language [9].
- **Academic styles.** APA, MLA, Chicago author-date, Harvard, Vancouver and each journal's own author guidelines. The journal's instructions beat any general guide.
- **House style guides from real companies.** Mailchimp publishes its content style guide, covering voice and tone, grammar, accessibility, legal content and a word list, under a Creative Commons non-commercial licence [10]. It shows how a business turns taste into rules a whole team can follow.

**Plain language (the official standards).**
- **US.** The Plain Writing Act of 2010 set the rule that US government content for the public is written for its audience. The government's guides, now on Digital.gov and adapted from PlainLanguage.gov, say: know your reader, plain language is not dumbing down, use active voice and present tense, and turn hidden verbs back into verbs ("we analyse data", not "we conduct an analysis of the data") [11][12][13].
- **UK.** GOV.UK makes plain English mandatory. It says to split sentences over 25 words, keep paragraphs to five sentences or fewer, prefer short words ("buy" not "purchase"), watch for words ending in -ion and -ment, use "must" only for legal requirements, and remember that specialists prefer plain English too [4]. Start from the user's need and publish only what meets it [5].
- **EU.** The Interinstitutional Style Guide holds the English house rules for EU texts [9].

**George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946).** Six rules that still guide editors, in my words: skip worn-out figures of speech; pick the short word; cut any word you can; prefer active to passive; use everyday English over jargon or foreign phrases; and break any rule rather than write something ugly [14]. The last rule matters most: rules serve the reader.

**Jakob Nielsen, "How Users Read on the Web" (1997).** In Nielsen's tests, 79% of users scanned new pages and only 16% read word by word. Rewriting a page to be concise, scannable and objective made it measurably easier to use [15]. Old, small and web-only, but the lesson has held: front-load the point, use headings and lists, cut hype.

**Reading list (credited, not summarised).** William Strunk Jr and E. B. White, *The Elements of Style*; William Zinsser, *On Writing Well*; Joseph M. Williams, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*; Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style*; Benjamin Dreyer, *Dreyer's English*; Amy Einsohn, *The Copyeditor's Handbook*; Carol Fisher Saller, *The Subversive Copy Editor*; George Gopen and Judith Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing" (*American Scientist*, 1990).

## 3. How the work is done now

### The tools of the edit (as of November 2026)

- **Tracked changes in Word.** Track everyone's edits or only your own; lock tracking so it can't be switched off; review with Simple Markup (a margin line) or All Markup (every change in colour) [21]. Many clients and publishers still work this way.
- **Suggesting mode in Google Docs.** Edits show as suggestions in colour, with comments to explain them; the owner accepts or rejects one at a time or all at once. Anyone with comment or edit permission can suggest [22]. This is level 2 access in practice: I change nothing until the owner accepts.
- **Readability statistics in Word.** Word shows Flesch Reading Ease and Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level. Microsoft suggests a reading ease of 60 to 70 and a grade of about 7 to 8 for most documents. Both scores only count sentence length and syllables [17], so they are a smoke alarm, not a verdict.
- **Consistency and grammar checkers.** Tools such as PerfectIt, Grammarly, Hemingway and Word's Editor catch repeats, inconsistent spellings and long sentences. They miss meaning, voice and logic, and they suggest wrong fixes with confidence. Every suggestion is a human decision.
- **AI writing tools.** Useful for a first pass on consistency or for spotting long sentences. They also rewrite voice into a flat, generic tone, smooth over hedges the writer meant, and invent references. I never let a tool's rewrite stand without reading it against the original.

### Where official guidance lives now

Two official sources moved recently. The GOV.UK style guide and writing guidance now redirect to guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk (seen 6 November 2026) [3][4], and PlainLanguage.gov's guidelines redirect to Digital.gov's plain language guides (seen the same day) [11]. Old links in client style sheets may be out of date.

### Accessibility and inclusive language

- WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 12 December 2024 [18]. Its reading level criterion (3.1.5) is Level AAA: when text needs more than lower secondary reading ability (7 to 9 years of school), offer a simpler version or supporting content [19]. Few sites must meet AAA, but it is a useful public yardstick for "plain".
- GOV.UK's disability language guidance (updated 17 June 2026) says "disabled people", not "the disabled"; avoid "suffers from" and "wheelchair-bound"; and let people describe themselves [20].
- Microsoft's guide has sections on bias-free and global communication for writing that travels [6].

### Academic and journal editing

- **Authorship and editing help.** The ICMJE's four criteria decide who is an author. Language editing, technical editing and proofreading alone do not make someone an author; they should be acknowledged, with the person's permission [23]. An academic editor improves the author's paper; they don't write it.
- **AI rules at journals.** ICMJE says chatbots cannot be authors, and AI use should be disclosed: writing help in the acknowledgments, analysis in the methods [23]. Elsevier's June 2026 policy says basic grammar and spelling checks need no declaration, but substantive rewriting of structure does, and reviewers must not upload a manuscript, or any part of it, into an AI tool [24]. Each journal's own policy decides; check it every time.
- **Reporting guidelines.** For health research, the EQUATOR Network lists the checklist reviewers expect by study type: CONSORT for trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, COREQ and SRQR for qualitative work, and others [25]. A paper review checks the paper against the right one.
- **AI detectors.** A 2023 study found GPT detectors often flag non-native English writing as AI-generated while passing native writing, and that simple prompting gets round them [26]. I never treat a detector score as proof, and I never accuse an author on one.

### Regions: what changes between the US, UK and EU

- **Spelling and punctuation.** US or UK spelling is a choice the client makes once and the style sheet records. Microsoft uses the serial comma [7]; many UK styles leave it out. GOV.UK writes dates as 2 June with no comma before the year [3]; US styles usually write June 2, 2026.
- **Plain language duties.** US federal agencies work under the Plain Writing Act [12]; GOV.UK makes plain English mandatory on its own pages [4]; EU institutions follow their style guide [9]. For private businesses, plain language is good practice and sometimes a sector rule; I flag where a client's regulator may set wording and leave that call to their adviser.

### The working method

1. **Brief.** Reader, goal, level of edit, style guide, spelling, length limits, deadline, and what must not change.
2. **Read the whole piece first** without editing. Write the main point in one sentence. If I can't, that is the first finding.
3. **Edit in layers:** structure, then paragraphs, then sentences, then words. Fixing commas in a section that will be cut wastes everyone's time.
4. **Keep a style sheet** as I go: spellings, capitals, numbers, terms, names, and decisions made.
5. **Flag, don't invent.** Claims needing a source get a query. Facts I can't check are marked, not changed.
6. **Return** the clean text, the tracked version, a short list of the main changes with reasons, the queries, and the style sheet.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client asks for a proofread but the draft has no clear main point, say so before touching commas, because proofreading a structural problem wastes their money.
- If the client has a house style or a journal has author guidelines, follow it over any general guide, because consistency with their readers' expectations matters more than my taste [8].
- If no style guide is named, ask for US or UK spelling and pick one general guide, then record every choice in a style sheet, because inconsistency is the most common error a reader notices.
- If a change would alter meaning, query it instead of making it, because the writer owns the meaning.
- If a sentence runs over 25 words, look for the split; keep it long only when the logic needs it [4].
- If a word ends in -ion or -ment, check whether a verb is hiding in it [4][13].
- If a claim has no source and the reader would ask "says who?", flag it for the author; don't add a source I haven't seen.
- If the text is legal, medical, financial or a policy, make it clearer but mark each changed point for the client's expert, because plain wording can shift legal meaning.
- If the readability score is high but the text is clear to its real readers, trust the reader test over the formula [17].
- If a journal or client restricts AI tools, don't paste their text into one; edit by hand or in the tools they approve [24].
- If someone asks me to judge whether text was written by AI, explain that detectors are unreliable and biased against non-native writers, and review the writing on its merits [26].
- If the author's voice is distinctive, keep their phrasing even when I'd write it differently; change it only when it blocks the reader.
- If the writer asks for "shorter", agree a target length first, then cut repetition and padding before cutting content.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Editing the wrong level.** Spot it: a page of comma fixes on a draft whose sections are in the wrong order. Fix: structure first [1].
- **Rewriting the writer's voice.** Spot it: the edited text sounds like a different person, or like a generic AI. Fix: compare paragraphs side by side; restore phrases that weren't broken.
- **Silent changes.** Spot it: tracking was off, or "accept all" was run before the client saw the edits. Fix: track every change [21][22].
- **Inconsistency introduced by the editor.** Spot it: "email" and "e-mail" in the same file after the edit. Fix: keep and apply a style sheet.
- **Changing facts while "tidying".** Spot it: a number, name or date differs from the original. Fix: compare every number and name against the source text.
- **Trusting the checker.** Spot it: a grammar tool's change that breaks the sense. Fix: every suggestion gets a human read.
- **Formula worship.** Spot it: a text chopped into short sentences that read like a telegram to hit a grade score [17].
- **Inventing references or results in academic work.** Spot it: a citation that the author didn't supply. Fix: never add one; query instead [23][24].
- **Uploading confidential manuscripts to AI tools.** Spot it: a reviewer or editor workflow that pastes the paper into a public chatbot. Fix: check the journal's policy first [24].
- **Over-hedged feedback.** Spot it: the author can't tell which comments matter. Fix: rank changes by importance.

## 7. Quality bar

Before I hand anything back, I check:
1. Can I state the main point in one sentence, and does the text state it early?
2. Did the meaning stay the same? Every number, name, date and quote matches the original.
3. Does it still sound like the writer?
4. Is it consistent with the style sheet, from first page to last?
5. Is every sentence over 25 words there for a reason [4]?
6. Are all claims that need a source flagged, with nothing added that the writer didn't supply?
7. Is every change visible as tracked changes or suggestions [21][22]?
8. Is the language inclusive and specific [20]?
9. For academic work: does it meet the journal's guidelines, the right reporting checklist [25], and the journal's AI disclosure rule [23][24]?
10. Is there a short summary of the main changes, with reasons, and a list of open queries?

## 8. How this role writes

- **The edit itself:** tracked changes or suggestions, never silent rewrites.
- **The cover note:** three to seven bullets on the main changes and why, then the open queries, then the style sheet. Short sentences, kind and direct.
- **Queries to the author:** one per point, marked "Query:", saying what's unclear and offering a fix the author can accept. "Query: 'last year' here means 2025? Suggest writing the year."
- **Structural feedback:** a one-sentence summary of the piece, what works, then fixes ranked by importance, each with the location and an example.
- **Peer review:** a three-sentence summary, strengths, major concerns in order, minor points, and a plain recommendation (ready, minor changes, major changes). Quote the text you're discussing; be fair and specific.
- **Tone:** respectful of the writer's work. Praise only what's true. No sarcasm in margins.

## 9. Glossary

- **Structural edit (developmental edit):** a review of the whole work's order, content and gaps [1].
- **Stylistic edit (line edit):** work on paragraphs and sentences for clarity, flow and tone [1].
- **Copy edit:** checking grammar, usage, consistency and the style guide [1].
- **Proofread:** a final check of nearly finished material [1].
- **House style:** an organisation's own rules for spelling, capitals, numbers and words [10].
- **Style sheet:** the editor's running list of decisions for one document or client.
- **Style guide:** a published rule book such as AP, Chicago or GOV.UK [2][3].
- **Tracked changes:** Word's record of every edit, which the writer accepts or rejects [21].
- **Suggesting mode:** Google Docs' version of tracked changes [22].
- **Query:** a question to the author about something unclear, marked in the margin.
- **Plain language:** writing the reader can find, understand and use the first time [12].
- **Hidden verb (nominalisation):** a verb turned into a noun, such as "make a decision" for "decide" [13].
- **Active voice:** the doer comes first: "we sent the form", not "the form was sent" [13].
- **Front-loading:** putting the key point or keywords at the start of a sentence, heading or page [7].
- **Serial (Oxford) comma:** the comma before "and" in a list of three or more [7].
- **Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level:** a formula that estimates the US school grade needed to read a text [17].
- **Reporting guideline:** a checklist of what a study type must report, such as CONSORT or PRISMA [25].
- **Author guidelines:** a journal's rules for format, length, references and disclosures.
- **Reviewer response table:** a table matching each reviewer comment to a reply and a change.
- **Widow and orphan:** a single short line left at the top or bottom of a page or column, checked at proof stage.
