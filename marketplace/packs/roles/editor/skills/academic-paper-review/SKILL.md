# Academic paper review

## When to use

- An author wants a hard, fair read before submitting.
- A team needs an internal review of a white paper or research report.
- Reviewer comments have come back and the author needs a response plan.
- A paper must be checked against a journal's author guidelines.

Not for: writing a paper or assignment for someone to submit as their own work, inventing references or results, or acting as an official reviewer for a journal (the journal's invited reviewer must do their own review, under the journal's rules).

## What you need

1. The manuscript (and supplementary files if the review covers them).
2. The field, the target journal or audience, and the stage (early draft, pre-submission, revise and resubmit).
3. The journal's author guidelines and its AI policy.
4. For revisions: the decision letter and every reviewer comment.
5. The reporting checklist the authors used, if any.

**Access:** level 1 to read the manuscript, guidelines and comments. Level 2 to add comments to the manuscript, prepare a tracked revised copy and draft the response table. Level 3 only to upload the agreed files to a portal, and submitting always needs the author's yes for that submission.

**Confidentiality and AI.** If the paper is under review at a journal, treat it as confidential. Elsevier, for example, tells reviewers not to upload a manuscript or any part of it into an AI tool (policy updated June 2026). Use only tools the author or journal allows.

## Steps

### 1. Summarise in three sentences

What question the paper asks, what it did, and what it claims. Give this to the author first: if your summary surprises them, the paper isn't saying what they think.

### 2. Check the argument chain

For each main claim: is there a clear question, a claim that answers it, evidence that supports the claim, and a conclusion that doesn't go further than the evidence? Mark where a link breaks: a conclusion stronger than the results, a claim with no data, results not connected to the question.

### 3. Check method and evidence, as far as the text shows

- **Design:** does it fit the question?
- **Sample:** who, how many, how chosen, who dropped out.
- **Measures:** what was measured, and are the tools named and justified?
- **Analysis:** is it described well enough to repeat? Are effect sizes and uncertainty reported, not only p-values?
- **Limits:** does the paper name its own limits honestly?
- **Reporting checklist:** for health research, check the right EQUATOR guideline: CONSORT for randomised trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, COREQ or SRQR for qualitative work, STARD for diagnostic accuracy, CARE for case reports. Note missing items.

Don't claim to have checked anything the text doesn't show. Say "not reported" or "unclear from the text".

### 4. Check references and integrity, without inventing

- Flag citations that don't seem to support the sentence they're attached to, and claims with no citation. Ask the author to check; never supply a reference yourself.
- Check authorship and acknowledgments against the journal's rules. Under ICMJE, language editing and proofreading alone don't make someone an author, and AI tools can't be authors; AI use should be disclosed where the journal requires.
- Don't use AI-detector scores to judge the text. A 2023 study found detectors often misclassify non-native English writing as AI-generated.

### 5. Write the review

Strengths first, then major concerns in order of importance, then minor points. For each concern: quote or locate the text, say what's wrong, why it matters, and what would fix it. End with a plain recommendation: ready, minor changes, or major changes. Be fair, specific and kind; criticise the work, never the author.

### 6. For revisions: the response table

One row per reviewer comment: reviewer and comment number, the comment (short), the author's reply, the change made, and where (page and line). Reply politely to every comment, including ones the author disagrees with, giving the reason and evidence. Mark any comment that needs new analysis or data the author doesn't have.

### 7. For journal fit: the rules checklist

List each rule from the author guidelines the author pasted (word limit, abstract format, structure, reference style, figures, data statement, disclosures, AI statement) and mark met, not met or unclear, with the change needed.

## Checks before handing over

- The three-sentence summary matches the paper.
- Every concern points to a place in the text.
- Nothing is claimed about methods or data that the text doesn't show.
- No reference, result or quote has been invented or "corrected" from memory.
- The right reporting checklist was used, or the review says none applies.
- The review follows the journal's AI and confidentiality rules.
- The recommendation follows from the concerns listed.

## Output format

**Review:** summary (three sentences); strengths; major concerns (ranked); minor points; reporting checklist gaps; recommendation.

**Response table:** reviewer, comment number, comment, reply, change made, location, status.

**Journal checklist:** rule, met or not, change needed.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real study: a pre-submission review of a 12-page study of staff turnover in eight small dental clinics. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Summary:** The study asks why staff leave small dental clinics. It surveyed 64 staff in eight clinics and interviewed 12 who had left. It concludes that unpredictable rotas, not pay, drive turnover.
>
> **Strengths:** The interview quotes are well chosen and clearly linked to themes. The rota data from clinic records is a strong, objective measure.
>
> **Major concern 1:** The conclusion (page 10) says rotas "drive" turnover, but the design is cross-sectional and can show an association only. Suggest "are associated with" and add this to the limits.
>
> **Major concern 2:** The survey response rate isn't reported (page 4). Please give how many staff were invited and how many replied.
>
> **Minor:** The qualitative methods don't say how themes were coded or by how many people (COREQ items). Add a short paragraph.
>
> **Recommendation:** major changes before submission.
