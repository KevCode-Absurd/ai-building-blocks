# Structural edit

## When to use

- A long draft "doesn't work" and the writer can't say why.
- Readers stop halfway, or reviewers say "unclear" without saying where.
- Several people wrote sections and the whole has no single line of argument.
- Before paying for a copy edit, to make sure the structure is worth polishing.

Not for: sentence-level polishing (use `copy-edit-and-proofread`) or a journal-style peer review of a research paper (use `academic-paper-review`).

## What you need

1. The full draft, including appendices and figures.
2. The reader and the purpose: who it's for, what they should think, decide or do after reading.
3. The constraints: length limit, required sections (for example a funder's template), deadline.
4. What the writer already knows is wrong, and what they're attached to.

**Access:** level 1 to read the draft. Level 2 to add comments in the document and save a proposed outline beside it. Level 3 is rarely needed for this skill; moving sections in the master copy happens only within the client's limits and after they agree the new outline.

## Steps

### 1. Read the whole thing once, without marking

Read at the speed of the real reader. Note where your attention dropped, where you got lost and where you wanted something that came later.

### 2. Say the main point in one sentence

Write: "This piece argues (or shows, or asks the reader to) ___, because ___." If you can't, the main finding of the edit is that the draft has no single point yet. Offer two or three candidate sentences from the draft itself and ask the writer to pick one.

### 3. Map the draft

Make a table with one row per section or major paragraph group: heading, what it does in one line (claims, explains, proves, describes, repeats), how long it is, and whether it serves the main point (yes, partly, no). This reverse outline shows the real structure, which often differs from the headings.

### 4. Test the order against the reader

Ask what the reader needs first. Common patterns:

- **Decision papers:** recommendation, reasons, options considered, risks, what happens next. Background last or in an annex.
- **Guides and help content:** what this is, who it's for, the steps in order, what to do when things go wrong.
- **Arguments and articles:** the claim, the strongest evidence, the strongest objection and the answer, what follows.
- **Reports:** summary of findings, then findings in order of importance, then method.

Mark where the draft's order fights the reader's needs.

### 5. Find gaps, repeats and dead weight

- **Gaps:** a claim with no evidence; a step that assumes knowledge the reader lacks; an objection the reader will raise that the draft ignores.
- **Repeats:** the same point made in two or more places. Pick the strongest place; cut or cross-refer the rest.
- **Dead weight:** sections that don't serve the main point. Suggest cutting or moving to an annex.
- **Proportion:** a minor point that takes a third of the length; a major point given one paragraph.

### 6. Rank the fixes

List fixes in order of how much they help the reader, not in page order. For each: what's wrong, where (section and page), why it matters to the reader, and a concrete suggestion. Give an example rewrite for the top one or two fixes so the writer can see the change, in their voice.

### 7. Propose the new outline

Give a short outline with headings, one line on what each section does, and a rough share of the length. Mark what moves, what's new and what's cut.

### 8. Say what works

List two to four things the writer should keep, with examples. Writers revise better when they know what not to break.

## Checks before handing over

- The main point is in one sentence, in the writer's terms.
- Every fix has a location, a reason tied to the reader, and a suggestion.
- Fixes are ranked; the top three would change the reader's experience most.
- No fact or claim was added; gaps are flagged for the writer to fill.
- The proposed outline fits the length limit and any required template.
- The tone is respectful and specific; praise is true and concrete.

## Output format

1. **One-sentence summary** of the piece's main point (or candidates if missing).
2. **What works** (two to four bullets).
3. **Reverse outline table:** section, what it does, length, serves the point (yes, partly, no).
4. **Ranked fixes:** number, problem, location, why it matters, suggestion.
5. **Example rewrite** for the top fix.
6. **Proposed outline.**
7. **Questions for the writer.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real organisation: a 4,000-word report for a community charity, asking its board to fund a youth mentoring pilot. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Main point (as drafted, page 9):** The board should fund a 12-month mentoring pilot for 40 young people because the trust's current drop-in sessions don't reach the young people who most need support.
>
> **What works:** the quotes from young people in section 4 are vivid and specific; the budget table is clear.
>
> **Fix 1 (most important):** The recommendation first appears on page 9 of 11. A busy board member may decide before reaching page 9. Move the recommendation and the cost to the opening paragraph, and make sections 2 and 3 (history of the trust) a one-paragraph annex.
>
> **Fix 2:** Section 5 claims the drop-in "misses" the young people most in need but gives no figures. Query: do you have attendance or referral data? If not, say what the pilot will measure instead.
>
> **Fix 3:** Risks appear only in the last paragraph. Add a short "What could go wrong and how we'd know" section after the budget.
>
> **Example rewrite (opening):** We ask the board to fund a 12-month mentoring pilot for 40 young people, costing [amount from table 2]. Our drop-in sessions reach many young people, but not the ones who most need support. This report shows why, what the pilot would do, and how we'll judge it.
