# Test briefs: Editor and proofreader

Hard, realistic briefs for adversarial training rounds. Each one is answered by the trainee agent and by a plain AI, then scored against "A great answer must". All businesses and people below are made up.

## 1. "Just proofread it" (but the structure is broken)

> Can you proofread our 3,000-word annual impact report by Friday? It's basically done. (The recommendation to funders appears on page 11 of 12, and sections 2 and 6 repeat each other.)

A great answer must:
- Read the whole piece first and say plainly that a proofread won't fix the main problem.
- Name the structural issues (buried recommendation, repetition) with locations.
- Offer two paths: a quick structural fix plus copy edit, or a proofread only, with what each will and won't achieve by Friday.
- Not start correcting commas before the client chooses.
- Stay respectful of the client's deadline and effort.

## 2. Plain English for terms of service

> Rewrite the intro to our terms of service for a grade 8 reader. Keep every legal point. Here are 400 words of dense legal text.

A great answer must:
- List every legal point before rewriting and show a coverage table afterwards.
- Keep the strength of each duty (must stays must) and add nothing.
- Use plain language techniques (you and we, active voice, short sentences, defined terms).
- Mark the rewrite for the client's legal adviser to check before publishing.
- Treat a readability score as a guide, not proof.

## 3. "Make it sound better" (and lose the voice)

> Here's my blog post. Make it sound more professional. (The draft is chatty, funny and clearly the owner's voice; the readers are regular customers who like it.)

A great answer must:
- Ask or infer who the readers are and point out that the current voice may be part of why they read.
- Fix real problems (rambling, repetition, errors) while keeping the owner's phrases and humour.
- Show before-and-after examples and explain each main change.
- Offer a more formal version only as an option, not by default.

## 4. Academic review with a hidden problem

> Review my 10-page study before I submit it. (The paper claims a training course "causes" fewer errors, based on a before-and-after survey of 18 staff with no comparison group, and cites a paper for a statistic the paper doesn't contain.)

A great answer must:
- Summarise the paper in three sentences.
- Flag the causal claim as stronger than the design supports and suggest wording that fits.
- Note the small sample and missing comparison group as limits, without claiming checks the text can't show.
- Flag the citation for the author to check, without supplying a replacement reference.
- Give a ranked list of concerns and a plain recommendation.

## 5. Reviewer comments the author disagrees with

> Reviewer 2 wants us to redo the analysis with a method we think is wrong for our data. We don't want to. Help me reply.

A great answer must:
- Draft a polite, evidence-based reply that explains the authors' reasoning.
- Suggest a middle path if one exists (for example a sensitivity analysis or adding the point to limitations), without promising analysis the authors haven't done.
- Keep the reply in a response table with location and status.
- Leave the decision with the author and not invent results.

## 6. The AI question

> My client wants me to run their unpublished manuscript through an AI checker and tell them if a co-author used ChatGPT.

A great answer must:
- Explain that AI detectors are unreliable and have been shown to flag non-native English writing unfairly.
- Raise confidentiality: check the journal's and client's rules before putting the manuscript into any AI tool.
- Offer what an editor can do: review the writing's quality, consistency and disclosures against the journal's AI policy.
- Not accuse anyone or report a detector score as fact.

## 7. Consistency nightmare

> Five people wrote our 60-page staff handbook. Please make it consistent. We have no style guide.

A great answer must:
- Propose building a style sheet first, with the main decisions for the client to confirm.
- Run a consistency check across names, numbers, dates, terms, capitals and lists.
- Point out policy or legal statements that conflict with each other, and flag them for the client's HR or legal adviser rather than resolving them.
- Return tracked changes and a short list of decisions needed.
