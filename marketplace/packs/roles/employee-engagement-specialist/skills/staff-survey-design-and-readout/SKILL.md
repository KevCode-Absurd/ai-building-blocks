# Staff survey: design and read-out

## When to use

- The owner wants to know how staff feel and has never asked, or asked once and did nothing.
- A team is losing people, or a change has landed badly.
- You need a short pulse on one topic: workload, wellbeing, a new tool, values.
- Results are in and someone has to turn them into actions and a message back.

Not for: investigating a complaint about a named person, checking who said what, or diagnosing anyone's health. Those go to a named, trained person or a professional.

## What you need

1. Headcount, and the groups you want to compare (team, site, shift, length of service), with the size of each.
2. The decisions the survey should inform. "Find out how people feel" is not a decision; "decide whether to change the rota system" is.
3. The survey tool, and whether it is confidential (it knows who answered) or anonymous.
4. Who can see raw answers, and whether raw export is switched on.
5. The named, trained person for serious concerns that appear in comments.
6. Where staff work (countries and states), and any past survey for comparison.

**Access:** level 1 to read aggregated results; level 2 to build the survey and reminders unsent and the read-out as a draft; level 3 to launch to the approved list, send reminders, close on the agreed date and share the approved dashboard. Raw answers and small-group results are read inside the client's tool and never copied out. At a lower level I hand you everything to enter and send yourself.

## Steps

### 1. Set the privacy rules first

- **Pick the type.** Confidential is normal for tools such as Culture Amp or Viva Glint: answers are linked to people inside the tool, and only groups are reported. Anonymous means nobody can link answers back, which needs a form with no login, no email collection and no unique links. Say which one it is in the invitation; never call a confidential survey anonymous.
- **Set the minimum group size.** Agree it with the client before launch and write it in the invitation. Tools set floors and defaults (Viva Glint's floor is three, its default statement promises five for scores and 10 for verbatim comments, May 2026); the NHS Staff Survey shows nothing for groups of 9 or fewer. Pick a number the client can explain and stick to it.
- **Stop differencing.** Don't offer filters that let someone subtract one group from another. Merge small teams before launch.
- **Keep sensitive questions out.** No questions on health, ethnicity, religion, sexual orientation or union membership in a routine survey. If the client wants diversity data, it is a separate, voluntary process with a privacy adviser's sign-off.
- **Write the privacy note:** what is collected, why, who sees what, the threshold, how long data is kept, and how comments are handled. Ask a privacy adviser which lawful basis applies in the EU or UK; in California, staff have privacy rights over this data. Check this with local rules.

### 2. Write the questions

- 10 to 20 items for a full survey; 5 to 10 for a pulse.
- One idea per item. Split anything with "and".
- Ask about work, not people: "I have the tools I need to do my job well", not "My manager is good".
- One five-point scale throughout. Agree-disagree items invite agreement, so add a few forced-choice or behaviour-based items where it matters.
- Two open questions at most: "What is one thing we should keep?" and "What is one thing we should change?"
- For each item, write the decision it informs. Drop any item that informs nothing.
- Fix the order and keep it the same next time so trends mean something. Put any group questions (team, site) at the end, and only those you will report.
- Pretest with three to five people from different groups. Fix anything they read differently.

### 3. Invite and run

- The invitation is under 120 words: why now, how long it takes, confidential or anonymous, the threshold, who sees comments, when results come back.
- Managers say it out loud too. Shift and frontline staff get time and a device to answer.
- Run it for one to two weeks with two reminders. At level 3, I send the approved invitation and reminders to the approved list only.

### 4. Read the results (inside the client's tool)

- **Response rate** by group. A low rate in one group is a finding in itself.
- **Score** each item (share of favourable answers, or the mean, but the same measure every time). Sort strongest to weakest.
- **Compare groups** only where every group meets the threshold. Compare with the last survey only if the wording and order match.
- **Code comments:** remove names, places and unusual details; sort into themes; count each theme; pick short quotes that many people could have written. Never try to work out who wrote anything.
- **Serious concerns** (harassment, danger, a crime) go as a theme, not an author, to the named person agreed before launch.

### 5. Turn findings into actions

- Pick three to five actions a manager can really do in the next quarter. Each has an owner, a date and a measure ("rota published two weeks ahead; item 7 favourable share rises at next pulse").
- List what you will not do, and why. This is what makes people believe the rest.
- Tie at least one action to how work is organised (workload, clarity, tools), not just perks.

### 6. Report back

- Within a few weeks of closing, send "you said, we did": thanks, three headline findings, the actions with owners and dates, what won't change and why, and when you will check again.
- At level 3 I post or send the approved message to the agreed channel or list; otherwise I hand it to you.

## Checks

- Privacy type, threshold and comment rules are written in the invitation before launch.
- No table, chart, filter or quote shows a group below the threshold.
- Nothing identifying has left the client's tools.
- Every item maps to a decision; no double-barrelled or leading items.
- Every action has an owner, a date and a measure.
- No benchmark from outside unless it is named, dated and sourced.
- The document starts with: *Template: check this with local rules before use, including privacy notices for staff data.*

## Output format

1. Privacy rules box (type, threshold, comments, retention, who sees what).
2. Questions table: item, scale, decision it informs.
3. Invitation and two reminders.
4. Read-out: response rates, item scores sorted, group gaps where allowed, counted comment themes with safe quotes.
5. Action plan table: finding, action, owner, date, measure.
6. "You said, we did" message, including what we won't do.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a bakery business with 64 staff across a production site and three shops, England. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Template: check this with local rules before use, including privacy notices for staff data.**
>
> **Privacy rules.** Confidential survey in your survey tool. We report a group only if 8 or more people answer. Night shift (6 people) is reported with day production as "Production". Comments are read inside the tool with names and places removed.
>
> | Finding | Action | Owner | Date | Measure |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | "I know my shifts far enough ahead" is lowest (41% favourable) | Publish the rota 14 days ahead | Ops manager | 1 Feb | Item at next pulse; late rota changes logged |
> | 23 comments mention broken mixers | Fix list reviewed weekly at the production huddle | Site lead | 15 Jan | Open fault tickets |
> | Shops ask for more say in opening tasks | Shop leads redraft the opening checklist with staff | Retail lead | 28 Feb | New checklist in use |
>
> **What we won't do this year:** move to a four-day week. Orders don't allow it, and we'll explain more at the March meeting.
