# Turnover analysis and stay interviews

## When to use

- Several people left in a short time, or new starters leave in their first months.
- The owner says "people just leave" but nobody has counted.
- Exit interviews happen but nobody reads them.
- Before a change that might push people out (a move, a new system, a pay freeze).

Not for: judging or scoring named people, predicting who will resign, or legal advice on dismissals, contracts or discrimination. Those go to an HR adviser or employment lawyer.

## What you need

1. An HR export with one row per person who worked in the period: ID (not name), team, role, site, start date, leave date, leaving reason category, and whether leaving was voluntary. No birth dates, health details or pay.
2. Monthly headcount, or enough rows to work it out.
3. Exit survey answers or interview notes, with names and identifying details removed.
4. Team sizes, so you know which groups are big enough to report.
5. The countries and states where staff work.

**Access:** level 1 to read the export and exit answers; level 2 to draft the interview guides, the playbook and a staff message; level 3 to book stay interviews or check-ins in managers' calendars and send the approved staff message, within your limits. The export is read where it lives; I don't copy person-level rows out of your tools.

## Steps

### 1. Check the data

- If names, birth dates, health or pay appear, set those columns aside and ask for an anonymised version.
- Profile it: rows, date range, teams, roles, leave reasons. Report the counts and the gaps ("leave reason blank for 14 of 52 leavers").

### 2. Work out the rates and show the sums

- **Turnover rate** = leavers in the period ÷ average headcount in the period × 100. Average headcount = (headcount at start + headcount at end) ÷ 2, or the mean of monthly counts if you have them.
- Split by voluntary and involuntary, team, role, site, length of service band and month.
- **Early leaving:** leavers with less than 90 days' service, and less than a year, as a share of starters in the same period.
- Show every sum in a table so the owner can check it: "Warehouse: 9 leavers ÷ average headcount 30 = 30% over 12 months."
- Don't compare small teams by rate alone: two leavers from a team of five is 40%, and may mean little.

### 3. Read exit answers for themes

- Code each answer into themes (manager, pay, hours, workload, growth, commute, onboarding, personal) and count them.
- Split by team only where the group is large enough to protect people.
- Use short quotes that many people could have written.

### 4. Map warning signs by team and role

| Team or role | Signs in the data | Possible cause to test | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| One row per team or role, never per person | e.g. early leaving, one theme dominating, a spike in one month | A hypothesis, not a verdict | One or two practical actions with an owner |

### 5. Write the stay interview guide

For current staff, run by their manager or a trusted lead, 20 to 30 minutes, once a year or after big changes.
1. What do you look forward to at work?
2. What do you dread?
3. What would make you think about leaving?
4. What would make your job better tomorrow?
5. What do you want to learn or do next?
6. How do you like to be thanked?

Record answers the same way each time: themes, not verbatim speeches. Promise only what the manager can do, and follow up within two weeks.

### 6. Write the exit interview guide

Run by someone other than the leaver's manager where possible. Open questions on why they're leaving, what would have changed their mind, onboarding, manager support, workload and pay fairness. Record themes. If the leaver raises harassment, discrimination or a safety problem, pass it to the named person under the grievance or whistleblowing policy; this isn't an exit survey matter. Check this with local rules.

### 7. Write the manager playbook

- **First 90 days:** check-ins at set points (30, 60 and 90 days), a buddy, a first real task, and the same five questions each time.
- **Regular one-to-ones:** see the leadership coach kit for the one-to-one system.
- **When someone seems unhappy:** ask early, listen, agree one change, follow up. Don't promise pay or promotion you can't give.

### 8. Draft the staff message

What leavers and stayers told you (themes and counts), the changes you will make with dates, and what you won't change and why. At level 3 I send it once you approve it.

## Checks

- Every rate shows its sum and its period.
- No named or identifiable person appears anywhere, including in the risk map.
- Small groups are merged or not reported.
- Exit and stay themes are counted, not just listed.
- Legal points are marked for an HR adviser or employment lawyer.
- The document starts with: *Template: check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

1. Data check: rows, period, gaps.
2. Rates table with sums: overall, voluntary, by team, role, site and service band.
3. Early leaving table.
4. Exit themes with counts and safe quotes.
5. Warning-sign map by team and role with actions.
6. Stay interview guide.
7. Exit interview guide.
8. Manager playbook.
9. Staff message draft.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business: Brightwater Care, 85 staff, home care visits in two towns, US.*

> **Template: check this with local rules before use.**
>
> | Group | Leavers (12 months) | Average headcount | Rate | Sum |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Care workers | 26 | 60 | 43% | 26 ÷ 60 |
> | Office and coordinators | 2 | 15 | 13% | 2 ÷ 15 |
> | Care workers, under 90 days' service | 11 of 31 starters | — | 35% | 11 ÷ 31 |
>
> **Top exit themes (care workers, 22 answers):** rota changes at short notice (9), mileage and travel time between visits (7), no shadow shifts before working alone (5).
>
> **Action:** two paid shadow shifts for every new care worker before solo visits. Owner: care manager. Start: 1 March. Measure: under-90-day leavers next two quarters.
