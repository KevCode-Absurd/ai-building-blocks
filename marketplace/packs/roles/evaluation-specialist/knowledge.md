# Monitoring and evaluation specialist: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general evaluation practice for charities, schools, councils and small public programmes. It is not legal advice on data protection, research ethics or children's privacy. Where a step turns on those rules, I point to the official guidance and say who to ask. Check this with local rules before you rely on any legal point here.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a monitoring and evaluation (M&E) specialist.** Charities reporting to several funders, schools and youth programmes, community groups, social enterprises and council teams. The person asking is often a programme manager or a chief executive with a grant report due, a funder asking for "outcomes, not numbers", or a board that wants to know whether a programme is worth keeping. Many already collect attendance, sign-up forms and some feedback, but have never turned it into evidence.

The agents built on this kit do five things:
- a one-page logic model and theory of change, with the assumptions behind each link;
- outcome measures and a data collection plan the team can really run;
- matched before-and-after surveys, with consent and a way to pair answers without names;
- an evaluation report from the client's own data;
- a short funder summary under the funder's own outcome headings.

**What good work looks like.**
- It starts from the decisions the evaluation must inform and the questions people need answered, not from a list of things that are easy to count.
- It says clearly what changed, for whom, and how sure we can be. Results carry the number of people behind them and who is missing.
- It matches the claim to the design. With no comparison group, a report says outcomes "changed alongside" the programme, not that the programme caused them [2].
- It is proportionate: a small, well-evidenced programme gets light monitoring; a costly, new or risky one gets more [2].
- It is useful to the people running the programme, not only to the funder. The CDC framework ends with "act on findings" for this reason [5].

**How the work is judged.**
- Would an independent reader agree the conclusions follow from the data?
- Are the limits stated as clearly as the wins [2]?
- Could the team collect the measures again next year without the evaluator?
- Did the programme change anything because of what the evaluation found?
- Did participants give informed consent, and was their data kept safe [2][9]?

**What this role never does.** Inflate results to please a funder; claim cause without a design that supports it; evaluate clinical or health outcomes; run research that needs formal ethics approval; or send surveys and collect data without the client's say-so.

**Related kits.** Training evaluation (Kirkpatrick's four levels, ROI) is covered in the `learning-development-specialist` kit; consent forms and interview methods for customers in `customer-insights-researcher`; staff surveys and small-group privacy in `employee-engagement-specialist`. This kit points to them rather than repeating them.

## 2. The canon

**HM Treasury and the Evaluation Task Force, the Magenta Book (first published 2011, updated 15 May 2026).** The UK government's guide to evaluation, and a sound reference for anyone [1]. It names three types of evaluation, each with its own question [2]:
- **Process:** what can be learned from how the intervention was delivered?
- **Impact:** what difference did it make, and how much can be put down to it?
- **Value for money:** is it a good use of resources?

It asks for a theory of change that maps inputs to outcomes with the assumptions and evidence behind each step, built with stakeholders, stress-tested and updated, and suggests a "negative programme theory" to find how things could go wrong. It ties effort to proportionality: profile, risk, cost, learning potential, scale, novelty, existing evidence and decision timing. Its impact approaches are experimental (random allocation), quasi-experimental (a comparison group or period when randomising is not possible) and theory-based (testing whether the causal chain holds and ruling out other explanations; contribution analysis gives an evidenced line of reasoning, not proof). Realist evaluation asks what works, for whom and in what circumstances [2]. The May 2026 update adds value-for-money guidance and two annexes, on transparency and on "Test and Learn" [1][2].

**The OECD DAC evaluation criteria (revised 2019).** Six criteria first set out in the OECD's 1991 principles, with coherence added in 2019 [3]. The questions:
- **Relevance:** is the intervention doing the right things?
- **Coherence:** how well does it fit with other interventions in the same place, sector or organisation?
- **Effectiveness:** is it achieving its objectives, including different results across groups?
- **Efficiency:** how well are resources being used: results delivered in an economic and timely way?
- **Impact:** what difference does it make: significant higher-level effects, positive or negative, intended or not?
- **Sustainability:** will the benefits last?

Two principles go with them: apply the criteria thoughtfully and in context, and use them according to the evaluation's purpose rather than mechanically, giving more or less time to each [3]. The OECD's own pages blocked automated reading on 6 November 2026, so these come from ALNAP's 2023 review, which restates them. ALNAP's 2025 humanitarian guide adapts them to seven criteria, adding coverage and inclusion, and inter-connection [4].

**Theory of change and logic models.** The Center for Theory of Change describes a theory of change as an explanation of how and why a change is expected to happen, filling the "missing middle" between activities and long-term goals, built by working backwards from the goal to the preconditions it needs [6]. NPC's ten steps run from situation analysis and target groups to impact, outcomes, activities, change mechanisms, sequencing, the diagram, enabling factors and assumptions. NPC warns against too much detail and against setting targets or asking measurement questions too early [7]. A logic model is the one-page summary. Global Affairs Canada's version separates outputs (products or services delivered) from immediate outcomes (capacity: knowledge, skills, awareness), intermediate outcomes (behaviour and practice) and the ultimate outcome (lasting change in people's well-being), and treats the theory of change as the assumptions, risks and outside factors behind the boxes [8].

**The CDC Program Evaluation Framework (updated 2024).** Six steps: assess the context, describe the programme, focus the questions and design, gather credible evidence, generate and support conclusions, act on findings. Five standards: relevance and utility, rigour, independence and objectivity, transparency, and ethics [5].

**Ethics codes.** The American Evaluation Association's five Guiding Principles (updated 2025) are systematic inquiry, competence, integrity, respect for people and common good and equity; respect covers informed consent and reducing harm [9]. The UN Evaluation Group's Norms and Standards (2005, revised 2016, a new norm added in 2025) apply across UN bodies [10]. The UK Social Research Association's 2021 guidance covers protecting participants and complying with the law [19].

**Survey method.** Pew Research Center's guidance is the practical core: clear, specific questions; no double-barrelled items; beware agree-or-disagree formats, which invite people to agree; question order changes answers; pretest; and, to measure change, keep the wording and context identical [11].

**Reading list (credited, not quoted).** Donald Kirkpatrick, *Evaluating Training Programs: The Four Levels* (for training, see the learning-development-specialist kit, which covers it in depth); Carol Weiss, *Evaluation*; Michael Quinn Patton, *Utilization-Focused Evaluation*; Ray Pawson and Nick Tilley, *Realistic Evaluation*; William Shadish, Thomas Cook and Donald Campbell, *Experimental and Quasi-Experimental Designs for Generalized Causal Inference*; Rick Davies and Jess Dart, *The "Most Significant Change" Technique*; Don Dillman, Jolene Smyth and Leah Christian, *Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed-Mode Surveys*. Together they push one habit: be clear about the question, the design and the use before collecting anything.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Scoping
- Ask what decision the evaluation will feed (renew, change, scale, stop), who will use it, and by when. Then write a short list of evaluation questions. Map them to process, impact and value for money [2], and, if the funder uses them, to the DAC criteria [3].
- Decide the scale using the Magenta Book's proportionality factors [2]. For most small charities that means good monitoring, a before-and-after design and honest limits, not a trial.
- Write down the funder's own outcome headings at the start, so the data plan collects what the report will need.

### Theory of change and logic model
- Work backwards from the long-term change to the outcomes that must come first [6]. Then add activities and outputs.
- For each arrow, write the assumption ("young people who attend six sessions will trust their mentor") and the evidence for it, if any [2][8].
- Keep it to one page. NPC's warning about too much detail applies [7].
- Add the negative path: how the programme could fail or cause harm [2].

### Measures and the data plan
- A small number of measures per outcome, each with a source, a collection point, an owner and a baseline [8]. Validity first: does it measure what it claims to [8]?
- Reuse established questions where they fit. The ONS personal well-being questions (four items on a 0 to 10 scale) are widely used in the UK, with set reporting bands (released January 2025) [13]. Keep the exact wording if you want to compare.
- Monitoring data (attendance, sessions, referrals) shows delivery; it cannot by itself answer causal questions [2].
- Collect the baseline before the programme starts. The Magenta Book is blunt that late planning can make an evaluation impossible or far more expensive [2].

### Surveys and consent
- Same questions, same order and same scale at the start and the end [11]. Match answers with a participant code, not a name.
- Pretest with a few people like the participants, ideally talking aloud as they answer, before anything goes out [11].
- Consent: tell people what the survey is for, who sees answers, how long data is kept, that taking part is voluntary and that they can stop at any time [2][14]. Under the EU and UK GDPR, consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, and as easy to withdraw as to give [14]. Consent is not always the right legal basis for processing data; it is still the right ethical step for taking part. Check this with local rules.
- Staff surveys: employees generally cannot consent freely to their employer [14], so use another lawful basis and stress that taking part is voluntary.
- Health, ethnicity, religion and similar data get stronger protection [15]. Ask only if a funder truly needs it, and report it only in large groups.
- **UK:** the Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 clarifies that research can include commercial research and allows consent to broad areas of related research [16]. Most of its data protection changes took effect on 5 February 2026 [17].
- **US schools:** the Protection of Pupil Rights Amendment covers surveys and evaluations given to students in programmes funded by the Department of Education that touch eight protected areas, including mental or psychological problems, illegal behaviour, religion and family income. Its guidance was under review as of 26 August 2026 [18]. Ask the school or district before any such question goes in.
- **Children and vulnerable people:** the Magenta Book calls for extra care, and sometimes a formal risk assessment [2]. If the work needs formal ethics approval, it is beyond this role.

### Analysis and reporting
- Report each outcome with the number of people behind every figure, how many started and finished, and who is missing.
- Show both the change and the uncertainty. With small groups, say so in words.
- Match the claim to the design: a matched comparison group supports "the programme reduced X"; a single before-and-after group supports "X changed alongside the programme" [2][21].
- Present findings no more definitively than the evidence allows, and publish the limits with the results [2].
- Use one or two real participant stories, with consent, to show how change happened. Never use a story as proof of size.

### Real example: an honest national evaluation
The UK's Troubled Families Programme (2015 to 2020) published its evaluation in March 2019 [20]. Using a matched comparison group, it reported 1.7% of programme children looked after by councils at 19 to 24 months, against 2.5% in the comparison group, a 32% difference; adults receiving custodial sentences 1.2% against 1.6%; and Jobseeker's Allowance claims 9.3% against 10.5%, with no significant difference for Employment and Support Allowance [21]. The report states its own limits: the comparison group may also have received services, and its family survey had no comparison group, so it says those changes cannot be attributed to the programme [21]. Two lessons: a "32% difference" was 0.8 percentage points, so give both forms; and naming limits does not weaken a report.

### Funder and public reporting
- Write under the funder's headings, in their order, with the evidence for each.
- UK central government evaluations must be registered on the Government Evaluation Registry from 1 April 2024, and pre-registering the design is encouraged [2]. Small charities are not bound by this, but writing the plan down before collecting outcome data is the same good habit.
- EU-funded and EU-law evaluations follow the Commission's Better Regulation guidelines (2021) and toolbox (December 2025) [22].

### Other countries: where to check
I give no detail on these countries' rules. Start with the official source and a qualified adviser locally.
- **Australia:** the Australian Centre for Evaluation's Commonwealth Evaluation Toolkit [23]; for privacy, the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner.
- **Canada:** the Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat's evaluation pages and the Policy on Results [24]; for privacy, the Office of the Privacy Commissioner of Canada.
- **India:** NITI Aayog's Development Monitoring and Evaluation Office [25]; for data rules, the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology.

## 5. Decision rules

- If there is no decision or user for the evaluation, ask for one before designing anything, because an evaluation nobody uses is wasted effort [5].
- If the programme is small, low-risk and well-evidenced, recommend light monitoring and an honest before-and-after, because the Magenta Book ties effort to risk, cost and uncertainty [2].
- If there is no comparison group, write "changed alongside the programme", not "caused", because a before-and-after alone cannot rule out other causes [2][21].
- If a funder asks for proof of long-term change from a short course, say what the data can show and propose a follow-up point, because outcomes after the programme need data after the programme.
- If you want to measure change, keep the wording, order and scale identical at both points, because changing them changes answers [11].
- If an established question set fits the outcome, use it with its exact wording, because comparisons need the same question [13].
- If a question asks two things, split it [11].
- If a survey touches health, ethnicity, religion or similar data, ask whether it is truly needed and how it will be protected [15]. Check this with local rules.
- If participants are children or students in US Department of Education-funded programmes, check PPRA with the school before asking about the eight protected areas [18].
- If the work needs formal ethics approval or involves clinical outcomes, stop and refer it on.
- If a group is too small to report without identifying people, merge it or leave it out, and say so.
- If a relative difference sounds large, also give the absolute difference [21].
- If the client asks to drop an unhelpful result, keep it and explain why openness protects them [2][9].
- If the OECD DAC criteria are used, pick the ones that fit the purpose rather than covering all six evenly [3].

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Counting outputs as outcomes.** Spot it: "We ran 40 sessions for 80 young people" under an outcome heading. Fix: outputs show delivery; outcomes show change in people [8].
- **The everything logic model.** Spot it: three pages of boxes. Fix: one page, the main pathway and its assumptions [7].
- **Measures nobody can collect.** Spot it: a plan that needs staff time no one has. Fix: fewer measures, built into existing forms.
- **No baseline.** Spot it: an end-of-programme survey only. Fix: plan collection before the start [2].
- **Changed wording at follow-up.** Spot it: "improved" questions in the second survey. Fix: freeze the wording [11].
- **Cause claimed without a design.** Spot it: "The course got 14 people into work." Fix: "14 of 40 found work in the three months after", plus a line saying we cannot tell how many would have found work anyway [2].
- **Percentages without people.** Spot it: "90% improved" with no count. Fix: "18 of 20 who completed both surveys".
- **Hiding drop-outs.** Spot it: results only for completers. Fix: report starters, completers and what is known about leavers.
- **Leading or double-barrelled questions.** Spot it: "How much did the helpful staff and good sessions improve your confidence?" [11].
- **Consent as an afterthought.** Spot it: no information sheet, or a pre-ticked box [14].
- **Stories as proof.** Spot it: one moving case used to show the size of change. Fix: stories explain how; numbers show how much.
- **Relative numbers only.** Spot it: "a 32% fall" with no base rate [21].

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Does every evaluation question tie to a decision and a user?
2. Does the logic model fit on one page, with assumptions written for each main link?
3. Does each outcome have a measure, source, timing, owner and baseline?
4. Are the before and after surveys identical where they need to be, plain enough for the youngest participant, pretested, and matched by code, not name?
5. Is consent informed and voluntary, with a privacy note that says who sees data and how long it is kept?
6. Does every figure show the number of people behind it, and are starters, completers and missing groups stated?
7. Does every claim about cause match the design?
8. Is there a "what this data can't show" box?
9. Are small groups merged or hidden so no one can be identified?
10. Is every figure from the client's data, with nothing invented?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Formats.** A one-page logic model as a left-to-right table; a measures table (outcome, measure, source, when, who); matched survey pairs side by side; reports outcome by outcome, each with a result line, a "how sure" line and a "what to improve" line.
- **Numbers.** "18 of 20 (90%)", never a bare percentage. Absolute and relative differences together.
- **Claims.** "Changed alongside the programme" unless a comparison design supports more.
- **Tone.** Kind, steady and honest. Encouraging about what the programme does, careful about what the data proves.
- **Words to avoid.** "Proves impact", "life-changing", "transformational", "100% of participants", and "beneficiaries" (say participants or the people you work with).
- **Hand-offs.** Each deliverable ends with open questions: missing data, consent gaps, decisions for the client, and anything for a privacy or ethics adviser.

## 9. Glossary

- **Monitoring:** routine tracking of delivery (attendance, sessions, referrals) to spot problems early [2].
- **Evaluation:** a planned judgement of how a programme was delivered, what it changed and whether it was worth it [2].
- **Theory of change:** how and why a programme expects change to happen, with its assumptions [6].
- **Logic model:** a one-page chain from inputs and activities to outputs and outcomes [8].
- **Output:** a product or service delivered, such as sessions run [8].
- **Outcome:** a change in people or conditions, short, medium or long term [8].
- **Assumption:** a condition that must hold for one step to lead to the next [2].
- **Indicator (measure):** the specific thing counted or asked to track an outcome [8].
- **Baseline:** the value of a measure before the programme starts [8].
- **Counterfactual:** what would have happened without the programme [2].
- **Comparison group:** similar people who did not take part, used to estimate the counterfactual [2].
- **Contribution analysis:** a theory-based way to build an evidenced case that a programme contributed to change [2].
- **Realist evaluation:** asks what works, for whom and in what circumstances [2].
- **Process evaluation:** looks at how a programme was delivered [2].
- **Value for money:** whether a programme is a good use of resources [2].
- **OECD DAC criteria:** relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact and sustainability [3].
- **Proportionality:** matching the size of an evaluation to the programme's risk, cost and uncertainty [2].
- **Matched surveys:** the same questions at two points, paired by a participant code.
- **Attrition:** people who drop out between the first and last data collection.
- **Informed consent:** agreement to take part, given freely, after clear information, with the right to withdraw [2][14].
- **Pre-registration:** writing the design and analysis down before outcome data comes in [2].
- **ONS4:** the four UK personal well-being questions on a 0 to 10 scale [13].
