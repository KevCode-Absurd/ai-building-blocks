# Logic model and theory of change

## When to use

- A funder asks for a theory of change or "outcomes, not outputs".
- Before choosing measures or writing surveys, so you measure the right things.
- A team runs a programme but cannot say how it leads to the change it promises.
- A programme is being redesigned, merged or scaled and the logic needs testing.

Do not use it to make a weak programme look strong. If a link has no evidence and looks unlikely, say so.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the client's programme description, funding bids, past reports and any existing model.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draw the model on a new whiteboard or in a draft document.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** update the shared board or page the client named, within their limits. Log each change.

## What you need

1. What the programme does in a typical week, who comes and how many.
2. The long-term change it hopes to contribute to, in the client's words.
3. Funders' outcome headings and any required format.
4. What the team already believes makes it work (the "secret sauce").
5. Any evidence: past data, research the team relies on, similar programmes.
6. Who should be in the room: staff, a participant voice if possible, a funder contact.

## Steps

### 1. Name the long-term change and the people
Write one sentence for the long-term change and one for the people the programme serves. Keep it to what the programme could plausibly contribute to, not a whole social problem. The Center for Theory of Change calls the space between activities and long-term goals the "missing middle"; this is where the model earns its keep.

### 2. Work backwards to the outcomes
Ask "what must be true just before this?" until you reach changes the programme can influence directly. Sort them:
- **Short-term:** knowledge, skills, confidence, awareness.
- **Medium-term:** behaviour and practice.
- **Long-term:** lasting change in people's lives or conditions.

Global Affairs Canada's model uses the same split (immediate, intermediate, ultimate). Most small programmes can only measure short and medium-term outcomes; mark the long-term one as "contributes to".

### 3. Add activities and outputs
List what the programme does (activities) and what it delivers (outputs: sessions run, people reached, hours of mentoring). Outputs are delivery, not change. Keep them out of the outcome columns.

### 4. Write the mechanism and the assumption for each main link
For each arrow, one line on why it should work (the mechanism) and one on what must hold true (the assumption), with evidence if any: "Mentors meet young people weekly → young people trust a mentor. Assumes: mentors stay at least six months; evidence: the team's 2025 records." NPC warns against hiding mechanisms inside assumptions; state both.

### 5. Add the negative path
The Magenta Book suggests a negative programme theory: how could this fail or cause harm? Examples: people drop out before the change happens, the programme replaces something that worked, stigma from being labelled. Two to four lines are enough.

### 6. Check the model with the team
Read it aloud with staff. Ask: does this match what really happens? What is missing? Which link do you trust least? Change the model, not the reality.

### 7. Write the evaluation questions it raises
From the weakest links and the funder's asks, write a short list of questions. Tag each:
- **Process:** is it delivered as planned, and to whom?
- **Impact:** what changed, for whom, and how sure are we?
- **Value for money:** is it a good use of resources?

If the funder uses the OECD DAC criteria (relevance, coherence, effectiveness, efficiency, impact, sustainability), map each question to one. Use the criteria that fit the purpose; there's no need to cover all six evenly.

### 8. Fit it on one page
Left-to-right table: inputs, activities, outputs, short-term, medium-term, long-term outcomes, with assumptions underneath. The full theory of change narrative goes on one more page.

## Checks

- One page for the model; one more page at most for the narrative.
- Outputs and outcomes are in different columns.
- Every main link has a mechanism, an assumption and the evidence, or "[no evidence yet]".
- The long-term outcome is marked as "contributes to" unless the programme can measure it.
- A negative path is included.
- Every evaluation question is tagged process, impact or value for money.
- No claim, figure or study is invented; gaps are marked [NEEDED].
- Participants' names and personal stories stay out unless they agreed to be included.
- If the programme serves children or vulnerable adults, safeguarding and consent are flagged for the client's lead. Check this with local rules.

## Output format

1. Long-term change and people served (two sentences).
2. Logic model table (one page).
3. Assumptions and evidence table: link, mechanism, assumption, evidence, confidence (high, medium, low).
4. Negative path (two to four lines).
5. Evaluation questions, tagged.
6. Open questions for the client.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up programme for illustration: Brightwell Youth Trust, weekly mentoring for 80 young people aged 13 to 16.*

> | Inputs | Activities | Outputs | Short-term | Medium-term | Long-term |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 30 trained volunteer mentors; 1 coordinator | Weekly one-to-one sessions; termly group trips | Sessions held; young people matched; months matched | Young person trusts their mentor; confidence to ask for help | Attends school more; asks teachers for help | Contributes to: stays in education or training at 17 |
>
> | Link | Mechanism | Assumption | Evidence | Confidence |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Weekly sessions → trust | A steady adult who keeps turning up | Matches last at least six months | [NEEDED: how long matches lasted in 2025] | Medium |
> | Confidence → attendance | Young people raise problems early | School responds when they do | None yet | Low |
>
> **Negative path:** if matches end early, young people may feel let down again; track early endings.
>
> **Questions:** How many matches last six months, and why do others end? (process) Does confidence to ask for help rise between the start and month six? (impact)
