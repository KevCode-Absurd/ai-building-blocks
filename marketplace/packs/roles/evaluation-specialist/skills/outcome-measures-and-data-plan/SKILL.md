# Outcome measures and data plan

## When to use

- A logic model exists and the team needs to know what to measure.
- A funder has asked for an outcomes framework or monitoring plan.
- The team collects lots of data but cannot answer basic questions with it.
- Before the programme starts, so a baseline can be collected.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** current forms, registers, spreadsheets and CRM fields, with names replaced by codes.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft new fields, codes and forms in a copy or as unpublished drafts.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** add the approved fields and participant codes to the records the client named, within their limits. Deleting or overwriting fields needs a new yes. Log each change.

## What you need

1. The logic model and evaluation questions (use the `logic-model-and-theory-of-change` skill first).
2. What data is collected now: forms, registers, case notes, surveys, and who fills them in.
3. Funders' required measures and reporting dates.
4. Staff time available for data collection, honestly estimated.
5. The privacy notice and any consent forms in use.
6. Any past data that could serve as a baseline or comparison.

## Steps

### 1. Decide how much evaluation is needed
Use the Magenta Book's proportionality factors: how visible the programme is, how risky or uncertain, how costly, how new, how much evidence already exists, and when decisions are due. A small, well-evidenced programme needs good monitoring and an honest before-and-after. A costly or new one may justify a comparison group. Write the answer in two lines.

### 2. Choose measures per outcome
For each outcome, pick a small number of measures. Prefer, in order:
1. Data already collected (attendance, completion, referrals made).
2. Established question sets with fixed wording, such as the ONS personal well-being questions in the UK, if they fit the outcome.
3. New questions written for this programme, pretested.

Test each measure for validity (does it measure what it claims?), feasibility (can staff collect it?), and sensitivity (will it move in the time available?). Drop measures that fail.

### 3. Separate monitoring from outcomes
Outputs and delivery data (sessions, attendance, drop-outs) go in a monitoring table reviewed monthly. Outcomes go in the evaluation table. Monitoring shows whether delivery is on track; it doesn't show change in people.

### 4. Set the baseline and timing
For each outcome measure: when it is first collected (before or at the first session), when it is collected again (end, and a follow-up point if feasible), and how answers are matched (a participant code, never a name).

### 5. Write the data plan
For each measure: source, collector, timing, format, where stored, who can see it, how long kept, and how consent is handled. Keep only what is needed, for as long as needed. Health, ethnicity, religion and similar details get stronger protection; include them only if a funder truly needs them, and report them only in large groups. Check this with local rules.

### 6. Say what claim the plan can support
- **Before-and-after, one group:** "changed alongside the programme".
- **With a comparison group or past cohort:** a stronger estimate of difference, with caveats about how similar the groups are.
- **Theory-based (testing each link and other explanations):** a reasoned case that the programme contributed.

Write this in the plan now, so nobody expects more later.

### 7. Build the forms and fields
At level 2, draft the new fields, codes and forms; at level 3, add the approved ones to the records the client named. Keep a data dictionary: field name, meaning, allowed values, who enters it.

### 8. Plan the review rhythm
Monthly monitoring check, a mid-point outcome look if the programme is long, and the end-of-programme analysis. Name the person who owns each.

## Checks

- Every outcome has at least one measure; no outcome has more than the team can collect.
- Every measure has a source, timing, owner and baseline.
- Monitoring and outcome measures are in separate tables.
- Matching uses codes; names stay in the client's own system.
- The plan states what claim the design can support.
- Consent and privacy are covered for each data source.
- Established questions keep their exact wording.
- Staff time needed is estimated and agreed with the manager.
- Nothing is invented; unknowns are marked [NEEDED].

## Output format

1. Proportionality note (two lines).
2. Measures table: outcome, measure, source, when, who, baseline, notes.
3. Monitoring table: output, measure, source, frequency, owner.
4. Data handling table: data item, why needed, who sees it, where stored, how long kept, consent route.
5. Design note: what the plan can and can't show.
6. Data dictionary for new fields.
7. Open questions.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up programme for illustration: Larkspur Adult Literacy, 12-week evening course, about 70 learners a year.*

> **Proportionality:** small, low-risk, similar courses are well studied. Good monitoring plus matched before-and-after surveys is enough; no comparison group.
>
> | Outcome | Measure | Source | When | Who | Baseline |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Learners read everyday texts with more confidence | 5 confidence questions, 1 to 5 scale | Matched survey, code LL-001 etc. | Week 1 and week 12 | Tutor hands out; coordinator enters | Week 1 survey |
> | Learners use reading outside class | "In the last week, did you read a letter, label or message on your own?" | Same survey | Week 1, week 12 | As above | Week 1 survey |
>
> **Design note:** this plan can show what changed between week 1 and week 12 for learners who answered both. It can't show how much was due to the course rather than other help.
