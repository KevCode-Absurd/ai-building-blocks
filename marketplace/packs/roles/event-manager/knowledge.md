# Event manager: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Every rule and platform fact carries a source number [n] from `sources.json` and the date it was true. Facts in section 10 are re-checked every 60 days. This is event practice, not legal, safety or insurance advice: rules are summarised from official guidance and flagged for the client, the venue and qualified advisers to confirm, never ruled on.

Related material covers some of this ground, and this file points to it rather than repeating it: the Traction channel files on offline events, trade shows and speaking (cheap first tests and what to measure), the B2B demand generation manager kit (webinars, target accounts and lead scoring), and the Email marketing specialist kit (sending invitations and follow-ups that reach the inbox).

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires me.** Small teams and organisers running conferences, launches, offsites, meetups, community days, workshops and parties, and B2B firms that exhibit or sponsor at trade shows a few times a year. The person asking is often doing events on top of another job: an office manager, a founder, a marketing lead, a volunteer chair.

**The jobs.**
1. **Brief and budget.** One page on why the event exists, who it is for, what success looks like, and a budget with every line, estimates marked.
2. **Plan.** A countdown timeline worked back from the date, a supplier list, a risk list and an access plan.
3. **Suppliers and venues.** Clear briefs, comparable quotes, and the contract terms that matter, checked before anyone signs.
4. **Guests and data.** Invitations, registration, reminders, practical information, and attendee data handled with care.
5. **The day.** A run sheet by the minute, with an owner for each line and a plan for what goes wrong.
6. **Trade shows.** Which shows and sponsorships to take, the booth plan, staff kit and lead capture.
7. **After.** Thank-you messages, follow-up for every lead, and a short results report.

**What good work looks like.** A plan the client can print and follow. Every budget line sourced or marked "estimate". Every run-sheet line with an owner and a backup. Access information sent before anyone has to ask. Leads sorted and followed up within the time the team agreed. A results report that counts meetings, sales or outcomes, not just headcount.

**How the work is judged.** Did the event happen safely and on budget? Did the right people come and get what they came for? Was every lead or guest followed up? Did the event meet the goal set in the brief?

**What I never do.** Sign a contract, pay a deposit or cancel a booking without a fresh yes from the client each time. Hold or move the client's money. Give safety, licensing, insurance or legal sign-off, which belongs to the venue, the authorities and qualified advisers. Quote prices I cannot check without marking them as estimates. Find or buy attendee contact details.

## 2. The canon

Ideas summarised in my own words and credited. None of these authors or bodies has reviewed or endorsed AI Blocks.

**Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares, *Traction* (2015).** Treat events and trade shows as channels to test cheaply before committing. The Traction channel files in this library update those chapters with 2026 facts.

**The ADA National Network, *A Planning Guide for Making Temporary Events Accessible to People with Disabilities* (2015) [1].** Site choice decides most of an event's accessibility, so think about access at the start; plan one accessible route linking parking, drop-off, entrances and facilities; use temporary ramps, matting and reserved spaces where nothing permanent exists; advertise the access you provide; and train staff to know where it is. Built on the 2010 ADA Standards, with measurements for routes, ramps, seating and parking.

**HSE, event safety roles (UK) [9].** The event organiser has prime responsibility for the health and safety of workers, the audience and contractors working for them; the venue owner keeps the premises safe; where control is shared, responsibilities must be clearly assigned and safety information shared.

**Events Industry Council, Sustainable Event Standards [26].** Eight sector standards (from venues and accommodation to event organisers, exhibitions and food) covering climate, waste, supply chain, accessibility and social impact, with third-party verification and levels from Bronze to Platinum by the share of criteria met.

**Reading list (credited, no claims quoted from them).** Priya Parker, *The Art of Gathering* (2018), on starting from a specific purpose. Atul Gawande, *The Checklist Manifesto* (2009), on checklists in complex work. *The Purple Guide*, the UK industry's health, safety and welfare guide for outdoor and music events.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 The brief

Start with the purpose and the one result that would make the event worth it: new stockists, booked meetings, a team that leaves aligned, a community that comes back. Then audience, date, guest count, budget and non-negotiables. Choose the format from the purpose: in person when trust, tasting or touch matters; online when reach and cost matter; hybrid only when both audiences are worth the extra work.

### 3.2 Budget

- **Lines:** venue, food and drink, AV and production, staff and crew, speakers, travel and accommodation, signage and print, registration platform and payment fees, insurance, access provision (interpreters, captioning, ramps, assistive listening), security, cleaning, waste, and a contingency line.
- **Estimates marked.** Every figure says "quote", "invoice" or "estimate". Only quotes count as firm.
- **Full cost.** Include staff time and travel, not just the venue fee; for trade shows, the stand fee is often the smaller part.
- **Payment schedule.** List each deposit, balance and cancellation charge by date. Money stays with the client: I prepare the payment list; the client pays.

### 3.3 Timeline worked back from the date

Set milestones backwards: venue confirmed, speakers, invitations, registration open, catering numbers, final numbers, set-up. Some lead times are set by others:
- **UK licensing.** A Temporary Event Notice in England and Wales covers licensable activity for fewer than 500 people at any one time, including staff, for up to 168 hours; it must reach the council at least 10 clear working days before the event, or 5 to 9 days for a limited number of late notices; there are caps per person and per premises each year [7].
- **Road closures.** For voluntary events, most councils need four to six weeks' notice to close a quiet street, and busy roads need several months (Cabinet Office guide, February 2019) [8].
- **Access support.** Sign language interpreters and other language support should be booked early because demand is high, and meeting papers sent about two weeks ahead where possible (GOV.UK, June 2026) [5].
- **Registration.** Webinar platforms see most registrations late; in Livestorm's 2025 data, 49.7% arrived in the final seven days [20]. Plan catering and seating numbers for late sign-ups and no-shows.

### 3.4 Venue and supplier contracts

Read every contract for these terms and put them in a one-page summary for the client:
- **Dates, spaces and access times:** load-in, set-up, event, load-out.
- **Deposit and payment schedule.**
- **Cancellation scale:** what the client owes if it cancels at each date.
- **Attrition and minimums:** what the client owes if fewer guests come or fewer rooms are used than promised; food and drink minimum spend.
- **Force majeure:** which events release both sides, and what happens to money paid.
- **Exclusive suppliers:** whether the venue's own caterer, AV or security must be used.
- **Insurance and liability:** what cover the venue requires and who is liable for what.
- **Access and safety:** step-free routes, accessible toilets, hearing loops, evacuation, and who holds which duties.

Under the Terrorism (Protection of Premises) Act 2025 (UK), whoever controls the venue for the event is the responsible person; duties can't be handed off, though contracts can require the hirer to share them [6]. Add that question to UK venue checklists.

A contract summary is not legal advice. Before signing anything binding, the client checks the terms with a qualified lawyer or adviser. Signing, paying a deposit and cancelling a booking each need the client's fresh yes, every time.

### 3.5 Safety, licensing and security

- **UK.** The organiser carries prime responsibility for health and safety [9]. Voluntary events have lighter duties, but organisers should still think through what could go wrong, who could be hurt and how to prevent it [8]. Martyn's Law: a qualifying event is open to the public, has entry checks (ticket, pass, payment or membership) and expects 800 or more people at once, including staff; such events face the enhanced duty, while premises expecting 200 to 799 fall in the standard tier [6]. The Security Industry Authority regulates; the implementation period is at least 24 months from Royal Assent on 3 April 2025, so duties will not start before about April 2027, and the government will announce the date [6].
- **US.** Permits, fire capacity, alcohol and food rules are set by states, counties and cities. Ask the venue and the local authority early.
- **EU.** Rules are national and often local. Ask the venue and the municipality.

The agent writes the plan and the questions. Sign-off belongs to the venue, the authorities and the client's qualified advisers.

### 3.6 Accessibility

- **US (ADA).** Businesses serving the public must communicate effectively with people who have vision, hearing or speech disabilities, using aids such as interpreters, real-time captioning, large print, Braille and assistive listening devices; they cannot require a person to bring their own interpreter; the undue burden exception still requires another effective aid if one exists (ADA.gov, updated February 2020) [2]. Service animals are dogs trained to do work for a person's disability; staff may ask only whether the dog is required because of a disability and what task it performs, and emotional support animals are not service animals under the ADA [3]. Accessible seats must be sold in the same way, at the same times and at no higher price than other seats in the section, with up to three companion seats alongside, and venues cannot demand proof of disability [4]. The ADA National Network guide gives measurements for temporary events: accessible routes at least 36 inches wide, ramps no steeper than 1:12, at least 5% of tables accessible, and free assistive listening receivers [1].
- **UK.** GOV.UK's inclusive communication guidance (Disability Unit, 17 June 2026) advises mentioning access facilities in all publicity, checking the venue is accessible, offering contact options beyond a phone number, asking about adjustments on booking forms, booking interpreters early, sending papers in advance and planning regular breaks [5]. The Equality Act 2010 duties to disabled customers are explained in the Equality and Human Rights Commission's codes, which blocked our fetcher; check them directly.
- **EU.** The European Accessibility Act covers e-commerce among its services, and ticketing machines among its products [12]. Check with the ticketing provider whether the online booking flow is in scope in each country you sell into.

### 3.7 Food and dietary needs

- **UK.** Fourteen allergens must be declared for food sold loose, in writing or verbally with a clear sign; at buffets, allergen information is given for each dish, not the buffet as a whole (FSA guidance, updated July 2026) [10].
- **US.** Nine major food allergens, with sesame added from 1 January 2023 (FDA, March 2026) [11].
- Ask for dietary needs once, pass them to the caterer by name or by count as agreed, and label every dish.

### 3.8 Registration and attendee data

- **Ask only what you need.** EU data protection requires that data be adequate, relevant and necessary for the purpose, kept no longer than needed, and used only for the purpose given [14].
- **Health data.** Dietary and access needs can reveal health information, which is special category data in the EU with stricter conditions [13]. Make those questions optional, explain why you ask, limit who sees the answers and delete them after the event.
- **Marketing consent.** Registration for an event is not consent to marketing. Use a separate, unticked choice where the law needs one.
- **Lists from organisers.** An attendee list or badge-scan data from an organiser can be used for marketing in the EU only if the organiser can show lawful collection and that any consent covered passing it on; people must be told at first contact and can object [15].
- **Follow-up email rules.** US: CAN-SPAM applies to business email with no B2B exception; opt-outs within 10 business days [16]. UK: marketing emails to individuals need permission and must offer an easy opt-out [17]. Canada: consent is implied when a person gave you their address (a business card at a booth, say) without saying no and your message relates to their role; unsubscribes within 10 business days [18]. California: since 1 January 2023, the CCPA covers business contact data for businesses over its thresholds [29].

### 3.9 Trade shows and sponsorships

Pick shows where your buyers are a large share of visitors, compare full cost per qualified lead, and book meetings before the doors open. The Traction trade-shows file has cheap tests (walk the floor, a shared stand, an online wholesale market) and the numbers to watch.

- **Market context.** IAEE's 2026 CEIR Index Report (4 May 2026) forecasts the US B2B exhibition index to grow 2.1% in 2026, while noting risks from trade policy, costs and travel [21]. CES 2026 recorded 148,392 audited attendees and more than 4,100 exhibitors (CTA figures reported April 2026) [22]. Large shows are crowded; a well-matched small show often beats them.
- **Sponsorship packages.** Compare what each package adds over a standard stand (meetings, speaking slots, attendee lists and the consent behind them) against the full price difference.
- **Lead capture.** Agree the fields before the show: name, company, role, need, timing, next step, consent choice, who follows up. Count qualified conversations, not scans. If the client advertises on LinkedIn, its Conversions API can send in-person event leads back to measure campaigns, with emails hashed and data deleted after 180 days [27].
- **Follow-up planned first.** Write the follow-up emails and the call list before designing the stand; the stand exists to feed them.

### 3.10 Online, hybrid and the backup plan

- **Show-up.** Livestorm's average webinar show-up rate in 2025 was 47.7% [20]. PheedLoop's check-in data from over 1,070 in-person events found a median no-show rate of about 20%: about 28% for free events and 17% for paid ones, and about 32% for events of 10 to 49 expected attendees (vendor data, April 2026) [19].
- **Pivot when you must.** Adobe cancelled its in-person Summit about a month before the 2020 date, when around 23,000 attendees were expected, and relaunched it online; LinkedIn's case study reports registrations three times the original goal and more than 700,000 online views [23]. A written online fallback for key moments makes such a switch possible.

### 3.11 Tools, access and AI

Agents in this role work in the client's tools at the level granted per tool (read; read and draft; read, write and publish within limits), as listed in `access.md`. Eventbrite charges no fees on free events and adds per-ticket and payment fees on paid tickets [24]. Zoom publishes MCP servers that use OAuth and scoped permissions [25]; HubSpot publishes a remote MCP server for CRM records, useful for logging leads and follow-up [28]. AI drafts plans, briefs and messages from the client's facts; it never invents a quote, a supplier's terms or an attendee.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the brief has no purpose or success measure, write one with the client before booking anything, because every later choice depends on it.
- If a budget line has no quote, mark it "estimate" and get a quote before the client commits.
- If a contract has a cancellation scale, attrition clause or food and drink minimum, put the money at risk by date in the summary before the client signs.
- If the event is in the UK and expects 800 or more people at once with entry checks, flag Martyn's Law and ask the venue who holds the duties [6].
- If the event in England or Wales sells alcohol or offers licensable entertainment on unlicensed premises, check whether a Temporary Event Notice fits and diary the deadline [7].
- If the venue cannot confirm step-free access, accessible toilets and assistive listening, ask in writing before booking, because site choice decides most access [1].
- If the event is free, plan reminders and expect more no-shows than for a paid event [19].
- If registration asks about dietary or access needs, make it optional, limit who sees it and delete it after the event [13][14].
- If an organiser offers an attendee list as part of a sponsorship, ask how it was collected and what people agreed to before counting it as value [15].
- If a lead's country is Canada and they handed you a card, record the date and that your message relates to their role [18].
- If key moments depend on one person, one connection or good weather, write the backup into the run sheet.
- If a step would sign, pay or cancel anything, stop and get the client's fresh yes.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Budget with only the venue fee.** Spot it: no lines for staff time, travel, AV or access.
- **Booking before reading the cancellation terms.** Spot it: the client can't say what they owe if they cancel next month.
- **Access as an afterthought.** Spot it: access details appear only when someone asks [5].
- **One allergen sign for a whole buffet.** Spot it: dishes without labels [10].
- **Catering for registrations, not attendance.** Spot it: no adjustment for no-shows [19].
- **Run sheet without owners.** Spot it: lines that say "AV" or "team" instead of a name.
- **No backup for the risky moment.** Spot it: an outdoor event with no wet-weather plan.
- **Badge scans treated as leads.** Spot it: a results report with scans and no meetings.
- **Emailing a sponsor list without checking its source.** Spot it: no record of what attendees agreed to [15].
- **Health data kept forever.** Spot it: dietary and access answers still in the spreadsheet months later [14].

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Does the brief state the purpose, audience and success measure?
2. Is every budget line a quote, invoice or marked estimate, with a payment schedule by date?
3. Does the timeline include outside deadlines (licences, road closures, interpreters, catering numbers)?
4. Are contract terms summarised with the money at risk by date, and flagged for the client's adviser?
5. Does every run-sheet line have a time, an owner and, for key moments, a backup?
6. Is access information in the invitation, with a way to ask for adjustments?
7. Are allergens labelled per dish and dietary needs passed on as agreed?
8. Does registration ask only what is needed, with health questions optional and a deletion date?
9. Is follow-up written before the event, with owners and lawful lists?
10. Is the access level per tool written down, and does every sign, pay or cancel step say "fresh yes"?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Event brief:** one page: purpose, success measure, audience, date, guest count, budget, non-negotiables, owner.
- **Budget:** a table of line, supplier, amount, status (quote, invoice, estimate), payment dates, notes; totals and contingency shown apart.
- **Countdown timeline:** weeks to the date down the side, milestones and owners across.
- **Run sheet:** time, what happens, where, owner, cue, backup. Set-up and pack-down included.
- **Risk list:** "If this goes wrong, then this, and this person does it."
- **Supplier brief:** what you need, numbers, times, access, budget range, decision date, contact.
- **Guest messages:** why come, when, where, how to get there, access, what to bring, who to ask.
- **Booth kit:** goal, opener, qualifying questions, lead fields, rota with breaks, do's and don'ts.
- **Results report:** goal against outcome, attendance and show-up, leads by grade, meetings, cost per outcome, what to change.

**Tone.** Calm, specific, printable. Times in 24-hour or with am/pm consistently, with the time zone for online events. Owners named. Estimates marked.

## 9. Glossary

- **Run sheet:** the minute-by-minute plan for the day, with owners and cues.
- **Load-in and load-out:** the times suppliers may enter to set up and must leave after.
- **Banquet event order (BEO):** a venue's written order for food, drink and room set-up for one function.
- **Attrition:** a charge if fewer guests or rooms are used than the contract promised.
- **Food and drink minimum:** the least the client must spend on catering, whatever the numbers.
- **Room block:** hotel rooms held for guests at an agreed rate until a cut-off date.
- **Force majeure:** a clause that releases both sides when events outside their control stop the event.
- **Contingency:** money and plans held back for the unexpected.
- **Temporary Event Notice (TEN):** permission in England and Wales for licensable activity at a small, short event [7].
- **Qualifying event (Martyn's Law):** a public UK event with entry checks expecting 800 or more people at once [6].
- **Effective communication:** the ADA duty to communicate with disabled people as well as with others [2].
- **Auxiliary aids:** interpreters, captioning, large print, Braille and listening devices [2].
- **Assistive listening system:** equipment that sends sound straight to a listener's receiver or hearing aid [1].
- **Service animal:** under the ADA, a dog trained to do work for a person's disability [3].
- **Companion seat:** a seat next to an accessible seat, sold to the same buyer [4].
- **Special category data:** sensitive data, including health, with stricter rules in the EU [13].
- **Lead retrieval:** a show's system for scanning visitor badges at a stand.
- **Qualified lead:** a visitor who matches the buyer profile and has a need, timing and next step.
- **No-show rate:** registrations that did not attend, divided by registrations [19].
- **Hybrid event:** one event with in-person and online audiences.
