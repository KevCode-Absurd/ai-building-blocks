# Event brief, budget and timeline

Most event trouble starts with a vague purpose, a budget that only counts the venue, or a deadline nobody knew about. This skill fixes all three before anything is booked.

## When to use

- Someone says "we're doing an event on this date with this budget" and nothing else exists.
- A plan exists, but it mixes quotes with guesses and has no payment dates.
- A new organiser needs a plan they can print and follow.
- The client wants to compare formats (in person, online, hybrid) before committing.

## What you need

1. **The ask**: what the event is, the date or date range, the city, rough guest numbers and the budget.
2. **The purpose**: why it exists and what result would make it worth it. If missing, ask.
3. **Non-negotiables**: dates, people who must attend, brand rules, access needs already known.
4. **Quotes or past invoices**, if any.
5. **Who decides and who pays** on the client's side.

**Access:** level 1 to read the brief, quotes and past budgets. Level 2 to build the budget and timeline in a shared document and draft quote requests. Level 3 to edit the agreed live sheets and send approved, non-binding quote requests to agreed suppliers. Signing, accepting a binding quote or paying anything needs the client's fresh yes every time; payments stay with the client.

## Steps

### 1. Write the brief
One page: purpose, the one success measure, audience (who and why they come), format, date, guest count, budget ceiling, non-negotiables, decision maker. If the purpose is unclear, offer two or three options and ask the client to pick.

### 2. Choose the format
Compare in person, online and hybrid against the purpose. In-person suits trust, tasting, touch and relationships; online suits reach and cost; hybrid doubles the production work and suits only events where both audiences matter. Say which you recommend and why.

### 3. Build the budget
Lines: venue; food and drink; AV and production; staff, crew and volunteers; speakers and fees; travel and accommodation; signage and print; registration platform and payment fees; insurance; access provision (interpreters, captioning, hearing loops or assistive listening, ramps); security; cleaning and waste; contingency. For each line: supplier, amount, status (quote, invoice or estimate), what it includes, payment dates, cancellation charges. Show the contingency apart from the lines, sized by the client after seeing how many lines are still estimates. Mark every estimate as [ESTIMATE].

### 4. Map payments
A schedule of every deposit and balance by date and payee, with cancellation charges at each stage. This is for the client to approve and pay; the agent prepares it.

### 5. Work the timeline back from the date
List milestones with owners and dates: venue confirmed, speakers confirmed, invitations out, registration open, catering numbers, final numbers, supplier briefs, run sheet, rehearsal, set-up, the day, pack-down, follow-up. Add outside deadlines that apply, for example:
- England and Wales: a Temporary Event Notice at least 10 clear working days ahead if licensable activity happens on unlicensed premises.
- Road closures: often weeks of notice for quiet streets and months for busy roads; ask the council.
- Interpreters and captioners: book early.
- Catering final numbers: from the caterer's contract.
Mark any deadline you could not confirm as [TO CONFIRM].

### 6. Write the risk list
For each risk: what could happen, how likely, what it would cost, the prevention, the backup and the owner. Always consider weather, a key person missing, low sign-ups, a supplier failing, tech failure, access gaps, medical incidents and crowding.

### 7. Check access and data
Add access to the plan from day one: venue route, toilets, seating, hearing support, quiet space, dietary labelling, and how guests can ask for adjustments. Registration should ask only what is needed, with dietary and access questions optional and deleted after the event.

## Checks

- The brief has one success measure.
- Every budget line has a status; estimates are marked.
- Payments are listed by date and payee, for the client to pay.
- The timeline includes outside deadlines and owners.
- The risk list has a backup and owner per risk.
- Access is in the plan, not added at the end.
- Signing, paying and cancelling are marked "fresh yes".

## Output format

1. Brief (one page)
2. Format recommendation
3. Budget table with statuses and contingency
4. Payment schedule
5. Countdown timeline with owners
6. Risk list
7. Open questions and [TO CONFIRM] items

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a design agency planning a client evening for 80 guests. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

> **Purpose:** thank our 30 biggest clients and show the new service; success is 10 booked follow-up meetings within a month.
>
> | Line | Supplier | Amount | Status | Pay by |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Venue (6pm to 11pm) | Venue A | £2,400 | Quote, valid to 31 Jan | 30% deposit on signing; balance 14 days before |
> | Food and drink, 80 | Venue A in-house | £3,600 | Quote; minimum spend £3,000 | With venue balance |
> | AV: screen and two mics | AV supplier | £450 | [ESTIMATE] | On the day |
> | Contingency | | £600 | Set by client | |
>
> **Timeline:** Week -8: venue signed (client's yes). Week -6: invitations out. Week -2: final numbers to venue. Week -1: run sheet and rehearsal. Day: doors 6:30pm.
>
> **Risk:** low attendance from busy clients. Prevention: personal invitations from account leads and a reminder a week before. Backup: invite 20 prospects from the waiting list after week -3. Owner: Sam.
