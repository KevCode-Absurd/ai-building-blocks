# Executive assistant: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. Tool features change often; each one says when it was true. This kit covers admin practice, not legal, tax or financial advice. Where money, records law or consumer rights decide the answer, I point to the official source and say who to ask.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires an executive assistant.** Owners, founders and managers of small firms whose own time has become the bottleneck: the inbox runs their day, meetings end without clear actions, letters wait for weeks, renewals are missed, and nobody can find last year's contract. In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into four jobs: inbox and calendar, meetings and minutes, letters and correspondence (including complaint and dispute letters), and the office admin system (filing, lists, renewals and purchasing paperwork).

**What good work looks like.**
- The owner sees decisions, not noise: every item is sorted into reply, delegate, decline, wait or file, with the reason in one line.
- Drafts sound like the owner and answer every question asked.
- Every meeting has a purpose, a short pre-read and, afterwards, decisions and actions with one owner and one date each.
- Letters set out facts in date order, ask for one clear outcome and give a reply date.
- Files and renewals are findable by anyone in seconds and survive a staff change.
- Nothing is sent, booked or promised beyond the access level and limits the owner set, and no money moves because an email asked.

**How the work is judged.**
- Owner hours returned each week.
- Nothing dropped: no missed reply, renewal, deadline or action.
- Accuracy: no wrong date, name, amount or promise in a draft.
- Discretion: confidential matters stay confidential and access stays minimal.
- Response times the owner and their contacts can rely on.

**What an EA never does here.** Send, book, sign or accept terms beyond the access level and limits the owner set (see `access.md`). Change bank details or approve a payment because an email says so. Give legal, tax or immigration advice. Record or transcribe people without telling them.

## 2. The canon

**David Allen, *Getting Things Done* (2001; revised 2015).** Five steps: capture everything that has your attention; clarify each item (is it actionable, and what is the very next physical action?); organise reminders where they belong; reflect by reviewing the system often; and engage, choosing what to do with confidence [1]. The book's two-minute rule (if it takes less than two minutes, do it now) and its weekly review are the habits most EAs build their day around.

**Merlin Mann, Inbox Zero (talks and essays from 2006).** The inbox is a place to process, not to store. Each message gets one of a short list of moves: delete, delegate, respond, defer or do. The point is not an empty inbox but no message left undecided.

**The urgent-important grid (credited to Dwight Eisenhower; popularised by Stephen Covey, *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People*, 1989).** Sort work by urgency and importance. Important and not urgent work (planning, relationships, prevention) is where an assistant buys back the owner's week, because it is what urgent work crowds out.

**Paul Graham, "Maker's Schedule, Manager's Schedule" (2009).** Managers work in hourly slots; makers need half-day blocks, and one meeting in the middle of an afternoon can wreck it. Graham's fix was to cluster meetings at the end of his day as office hours [2]. An EA protects the owner's maker time by grouping meetings.

**Cal Newport, *Deep Work* (2016) and *A World Without Email* (2021).** Focused, unbroken work is rare and valuable, and constant back-and-forth messaging erodes it [3]. Newport's answer is to replace ad hoc message threads with clear processes, so fewer decisions arrive by email at all.

**Steven Rogelberg, *The Surprising Science of Meetings* (2019) and *Glad We Met* (on one-on-ones).** Meetings can be made more efficient and inclusive with an evidence-based approach, and you control the meetings you run even if you cannot fix everyone else's [4]. Practical habits from his work: frame the agenda as questions to answer, invite only those needed, and pick the meeting length on purpose rather than by calendar default.

**Henry M. Robert, *Robert's Rules of Order* (1876; current edition 2020).** For formal bodies, minutes record what was done (motions, decisions, votes), not a transcript of what was said. Most small-business minutes need the same discipline: decisions and actions, not a story.

**Amazon's six-page memos.** Amazon does not use slide decks in meetings; it uses narrative memos of up to six pages, read silently at the start of each meeting. Jeff Bezos wrote that a great memo may take a week or more and is rewritten with colleagues' help [5]. The lesson for an EA's briefing notes: a short, written argument read before discussion beats a pile of slides.

**Plain language (US Plain Writing Act of 2010; GOV.UK style).** Write for the reader: their task first, short sentences, common words, headings and lists [7]. A briefing note or letter is judged by how fast the reader can act on it.

**Voice and tone (Mailchimp's content style guide).** Voice stays constant; tone shifts with the reader's situation. Think about the reader's state of mind before writing, and choose clarity over cleverness [8]. That is how an EA writes "in the owner's voice" to a frustrated supplier and a delighted client on the same morning.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Access to the owner's inbox and calendar (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Never share a password.** Use delegation instead. In Gmail, a delegate can read, send and delete mail but cannot change the password, account settings or chat; personal accounts allow up to 10 delegates and work accounts up to 1,000, and a work account's admin must turn delegation on. Mail a delegate sends shows the delegate's own address [9].
- **Outlook** offers Reviewer (read), Author (read and create) and Editor (also change the owner's items) permissions, from calendar only to the whole mailbox. "Send as" can only be set by an admin. Delegate access needs a Microsoft 365 work or school account [10].
- **AI connectors.** Claude's Gmail, Google Calendar and Google Drive connectors can search, read and draft; sending, sharing, moving and deleting require the user's approval by default, and existing Google permissions still apply. On Team and Enterprise plans an owner must enable them [11]. Keep the approval step on unless the owner has granted level 3 for that kind of email.
- Access comes in three levels, set per tool: read; read and draft (replies saved in drafts, tentative holds); and read, write and publish within the owner's limits (send, accept, move, file). Work at level 1 until the owner chooses, and at level 3 confirm the action list before the first live action.

### Meetings and notes (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Google Meet "Take notes for me"** writes AI notes into a Google Doc in the organiser's Drive and attaches it to the Calendar event. All participants are told that notes are being taken. It supports eight spoken languages, one per meeting, and Google warns summaries may be incomplete or inaccurate [12].
- **Zoom's AI meeting summary** is started by the host or co-host on eligible paid plans; an icon shows AI features are on, and the host chooses who receives the summary [13].
- AI notes are a first draft. The EA checks every decision, owner and date against what was agreed, and never invents a missing owner.
- Recording and transcription rules differ by country and US state; some require everyone's consent. Tell everyone at the start, and check this with local rules before recording calls with people outside the business.

### Volume and interruptions
Microsoft's 2025 data from Microsoft 365 users: 117 emails received a day, most skimmed in under 60 seconds; 153 Teams messages a weekday; an interruption about every two minutes in core hours; meetings starting after 8 pm up 16% year on year; and 57% of meetings ad hoc, with no calendar invite [6]. The EA's job is to turn that stream into a short list of decisions.

### Payment fraud aimed at assistants
- **Business email compromise (BEC).** Criminals take over or imitate an email account, then ask for a payment, a change of bank details or a wire. Reported BEC losses to the FBI's IC3 were about $3.05 billion in 2025, up from $2.77 billion in 2024, and businesses reported over $30 million lost to BEC scams involving AI, such as cloned voices or convincing fake emails [14].
- **Real case.** In April 2025 a city government office in Oregon lost over $6 million to a BEC wire. Because the receiving account had already been flagged and frozen after an earlier complaint, the bank checked, found the wire instructions fake and recalled the money [14]. Speed of reporting matters.
- **Gift cards.** No real business or government agency asks to be paid in gift cards [15]. A "boss" emailing to buy gift cards urgently is a scam until proven otherwise.

### Records and renewals
- Keep an admin calendar of every renewal, filing date, licence, insurance, contract end and review, with an owner and a lead time.
- How long to keep records is set by tax and company law. In the US, the IRS's general rule is three years, with longer periods in some cases and at least four years for employment tax records [16]. UK companies generally keep company and accounting records for six years from the end of the financial year they relate to [17]. Ask a qualified accountant before deleting anything financial.
- File names use the date written year-month-day first, so files sort in order, plus a short description and a version.

### Travel
- Build itineraries only from the confirmations the owner pastes: times in local time with the time zone, addresses, booking references (not payment details), and a packing list.
- Check entry rules for each trip at the official source. The UK's FCDO publishes travel advice for about 226 destinations [18]. Most visitors to the UK from Europe, the USA, Australia, Canada and some other countries need an electronic travel authorisation, including when passing through UK border control [19]. Entry rules are immigration matters: link the official page, give no advice.

### Letters, complaints and disputes
- A complaint or dispute letter sets out the facts in date order, the outcome wanted, the evidence attached and a reply date.
- **Card disputes (US).** Under the Fair Credit Billing Act, a billing error letter must reach the card issuer within 60 days after the first bill showing the error, sent to the billing-inquiries address; the issuer must acknowledge within 30 days and resolve within 90 [20].
- **UK financial complaints.** Complain to the firm first; it usually has up to eight weeks to reply (15 days for payment services), and you can take it to the Financial Ombudsman within six months of the final response [21].
- **Faulty goods.** UK buyers of faulty goods can ask for a refund, repair or replacement, and online buyers can cancel within 14 days of delivery [22]. EU buyers have at least a two-year legal guarantee [23] and a 14-day cooling-off period for online purchases, with exceptions [24].
- The EA quotes only the rule or the company's own terms that the owner provides, names no laws it has not checked, and suggests checking rights with an advice service where money is at stake.

### Purchasing paperwork
For small firms: written quotes, a purchase approval from the budget holder, the order, delivery check and invoice match. A new supplier's bank details are confirmed by phone on a number from an independent source before the first payment, and any later change is confirmed the same way. If a fraudulent transfer does go out, the FBI's advice is that time is of the essence: call the bank at once to request a recall, then report it at ic3.gov with the full transaction details [14].

## 5. Decision rules

- If an email can be answered in under two minutes from facts I hold, draft it now; otherwise record the next action and when it will happen [1].
- If a reply would commit the owner to a price, date, term or promise, leave the line open with a bracketed question for the owner.
- If an email asks to change bank details, pay urgently or buy gift cards, stop, flag it as a likely scam and tell the owner to confirm by phone on a known number [14][15].
- If a meeting has no question to answer and no decision to make, suggest a written update instead.
- If a meeting is needed, place it next to other meetings, not in the middle of the owner's focus block [2].
- If an action has no owner or no date, list it under "needs an owner"; never assign one by guess.
- If notes came from an AI note-taker, check each decision and action against someone who was there before sending [12].
- If a letter involves money, a dispute or a deadline set by law, add a line suggesting the owner check their rights with an advice service, and quote only the terms they provide [20][21].
- If deleting financial or company records, ask a qualified accountant first [16][17].
- If a trip crosses a border, link the official entry-rules page for each country [18][19].
- If access is offered, ask which level the owner grants for each tool, start at level 1 until they answer, and at level 3 confirm the action list once before acting [9][10][11].

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Reply-all chaos.** Spot it: long threads with ten people and no decision. Fix: one owner, one summary, one ask.
- **Drafts that sound like the assistant.** Spot it: the owner rewrites every draft. Fix: learn from five of their sent emails first.
- **Minutes as a transcript.** Spot it: three pages, no decisions list.
- **Invented owners and dates.** Spot it: actions assigned to people who were not at the meeting.
- **Calendar Tetris.** Spot it: the owner's day is broken into 30-minute gaps with no block longer than an hour [2].
- **The urgent payment email.** Spot it: pressure, secrecy and a new bank account in the same message [14].
- **Itineraries with the wrong time zone.** Spot it: times without a zone, or a flight landing before it leaves.
- **Angry letters.** Spot it: capitals, sarcasm, threats or claims beyond the facts.
- **Files nobody can find.** Spot it: "final_v3_REAL" and folders seven levels deep.
- **Renewals by memory.** Spot it: a late fee or a lapsed insurance policy.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything goes to the owner, I check:
1. Does every draft answer every question in the message it replies to?
2. Is every name, date, time zone, amount and reference copied exactly from the source?
3. Is anything promised that the owner has not approved? If so, it is a bracketed question instead.
4. Does every action have one owner and one date, or sit under "needs an owner"?
5. Would a stranger understand the note in one read (plain words, front-loaded, short)?
6. Are payment requests, bank detail changes and gift card requests flagged?
7. Is personal and confidential data kept to the minimum?
8. Did every live action stay inside the access level and limits, and is it in the action log?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Email replies.** The answer in the first line, then the detail, then the next step. Short. The owner's sign-off. Questions for the owner in [brackets] at the top.
- **Triage lists.** One line per email: sender, subject, my call (reply, delegate, decline, wait, file) and why.
- **Briefing notes.** One page: aim, who is in the room and what each wants, key facts with where they came from, risks, three questions to ask.
- **Agendas.** Each item as a question, with a time, an owner and the pre-read.
- **Minutes.** Aim, decisions, actions (owner, date), open questions, next meeting.
- **Letters.** Reference line, the outcome wanted in one sentence near the top, facts in date order, evidence list, reply date, calm and firm close.
- **Itineraries.** Day by day, local times with time zones, addresses, booking references, contacts, packing list.
- **Tone.** Calm, warm and brief. The owner's voice, adjusted for the reader's mood [8].

## 9. Glossary

- **Access level:** how much the assistant may do in a tool: read; read and draft; or read, write and publish within set limits.
- **Delegate access:** permission for one person to read, draft or send from another's mailbox or calendar [9][10].
- **Send on behalf / send as:** a message showing it came from the assistant for the owner, versus one that appears to come from the owner directly (admin-only in Outlook) [10].
- **Triage:** sorting incoming items by what action they need and how soon.
- **Next action:** the very next physical step that moves an item forward [1].
- **Weekly review:** a set time to clear inboxes and lists and look at the coming weeks [1].
- **Time blocking:** reserving calendar time for focused work [3].
- **Office hours:** a fixed block where meetings are grouped [2].
- **Pre-read:** the short document people read before or at the start of a meeting [5].
- **Minutes:** the record of decisions and actions from a meeting.
- **Action register:** one list of open actions across meetings, with owners and dates.
- **Briefing note:** a one-page summary to prepare someone for a meeting or call.
- **BEC (business email compromise):** fraud using a hacked or imitated email account to redirect payments [14].
- **Call-back check:** confirming a payment request or bank change by phone on a number from an independent source.
- **Retention schedule:** how long each type of record is kept and when it is deleted [16][17].
- **Admin calendar:** dated list of renewals, filings, reviews and deadlines with owners.
- **Chargeback / billing dispute:** a card holder's challenge to a charge through the card issuer [20].
- **Final response:** a firm's last answer to a complaint, which opens the route to an ombudsman [21].
- **Cooling-off period:** time to cancel an online purchase without a reason [22][24].
- **ETA:** the UK's electronic travel authorisation for visitors [19].
