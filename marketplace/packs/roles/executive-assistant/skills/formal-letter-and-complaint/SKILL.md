# Formal letter and complaint

## When to use

- A supplier, retailer, landlord, bank, utility or council has not fixed a problem.
- A bill or card charge looks wrong.
- A complaint has gone nowhere and needs escalating to a complaints team or an ombudsman.
- The owner needs a formal letter (a request, a notice of a problem, a polite final reminder) that is calm and clear.

Do not use it for court papers, legal claims or threats. Do not tell the owner what the law says about their case; quote only the terms they paste or an official page, and suggest an advice service or a qualified adviser where money or rights are at stake.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the email thread, receipts and records in the owner's inbox and files.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the letter and email version in drafts or the owner's documents, and file the timeline.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send the letter or submit the web form from the owner's account. Disputes involve money, so each letter needs the owner's yes on its facts and outcome before it goes, even at level 3. Log what was sent, to whom and when.

## What you need

1. Every fact: dates, amounts, order or account references, names of people spoken to, what was said and promised.
2. Copies of emails, receipts, photos and the company's own terms or policy, if the owner has them.
3. The outcome the owner wants: refund, repair, replacement, a corrected bill, an apology, a reply by a date.
4. Who it goes to and how: a named person, a complaints address, a web form with a character limit.
5. What has been tried already.

Never include full card numbers, bank details, passwords or ID numbers. A reference number and the last four digits of a card are enough.

## Steps

### 1. Build the timeline

Put every fact in date order: date, what happened, who, evidence. This becomes the middle of the letter and a record to keep. Mark any fact the owner is unsure of with [CONFIRM].

### 2. State the outcome

Write the outcome in one sentence near the top: "Please refund £240 to my original payment method by 28 November." If the owner gives no reply date, use [date, about 14 days from sending] and ask them to confirm.

### 3. Draft the letter

- **Reference line:** order or account number, date.
- **Opening:** who is writing and the one-sentence outcome.
- **What happened:** the timeline in short paragraphs, facts only.
- **Why this outcome:** quote the company's own terms or policy if the owner pasted them. Do not name laws you have not checked.
- **Evidence:** a numbered list of what is attached.
- **Reply date and contact:** how and by when to respond.
- **Close:** calm and firm. No sarcasm, capitals, threats or exaggeration.

### 4. Shorten for email or web forms

A version under 200 words with the reference, outcome, three key facts, evidence list and reply date. Check any character limit the owner gives.

### 5. Draft the follow-up

For when there is no reply by the date: shorter, refers to the first letter by date, restates the outcome and gives a new date. Mention the next step the owner can take, such as the company's complaints process or an ombudsman, only where the owner has confirmed it applies.

### 6. Note routes and deadlines to check

Some routes have time limits. For example, a US card billing error dispute must reach the card issuer within 60 days of the first bill showing the error, and UK financial firms usually have up to eight weeks to give a final response before the Financial Ombudsman can look at it. List the routes that may apply, with the official page, and suggest the owner checks with an advice service. Do not decide which applies.

### 7. Final check

Read the letter as the person receiving it: can they see what happened, what is wanted and by when, in one minute?

## Checks before handing over

- Every fact comes from the owner; nothing is added or exaggerated.
- The outcome is one clear sentence with a date.
- Evidence is listed and matches what the owner holds.
- No card numbers, bank details, passwords or ID numbers appear.
- No legal claims or law names the owner has not provided or that were not checked on an official page.
- Tone is calm and firm, with short paragraphs.
- A line suggests checking rights with an advice service where money or a dispute is involved.
- The owner said yes to this letter's facts and outcome before it went out (level 3), or has it as a draft to send; a copy is filed either way.

## Output format

1. Timeline (date, what happened, who, evidence)
2. Letter
3. Email or web form version
4. Follow-up letter
5. Routes and deadlines to check, with official links
6. Items to confirm

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Tilly's Bakehouse, a café in Bath, UK, writing to an equipment supplier about a faulty oven.*

> **Ref: Order 55821, combination oven, delivered 2 October 2026**
>
> Dear Customer Service Team,
>
> I am writing about the combination oven delivered on 2 October. Please repair or replace it by 20 November, or refund the price if you cannot.
>
> **What happened.** The oven stopped heating on 14 October, twelve days after delivery. I reported it by phone the same day (call reference 7712) and was told an engineer would visit within five working days. No one came. I emailed on 23 October and 30 October and have had no reply.
>
> **Your terms.** Your warranty page, which I have attached, says faults in the first 12 months are repaired free of charge.
>
> **Attached:** 1. Order confirmation. 2. Delivery note. 3. Photos of the error screen. 4. My two emails.
>
> Please reply in writing by 20 November.
>
> Yours faithfully,
> Tilly Moran, Tilly's Bakehouse
>
> *Before sending: check your rights with an advice service, as this involves money.*
