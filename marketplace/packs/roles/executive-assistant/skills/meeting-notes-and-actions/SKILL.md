# Meeting notes and actions

## When to use

- After a team, client or supplier meeting, from a transcript, an AI summary or rough notes.
- Before a meeting, to write an agenda that leads to decisions.
- For a recurring meeting, to keep one action register and spot actions that never move.
- To decide whether a recurring meeting should stay, shrink or become a written update.

Do not use it for formal board minutes that have legal requirements; those follow the company's own rules and a company secretary or adviser's guidance. The host starts any recording or AI note-taker, with everyone told.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the transcript, AI notes, agenda and calendar invite.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the minutes and follow-up email as drafts, and update a draft action register.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send the minutes and follow-up to attendees, update the live action register and send the next agenda, within the confirmed limits (which meetings, which attendees). Confidential meetings always need a fresh yes. Log every send.

## What you need

1. The transcript, AI summary or notes, with the date and the list of attendees.
2. The agenda, if there was one.
3. The previous action register, for recurring meetings.
4. Who the follow-up email goes to, and anything that must not be in it (confidential items).

## Steps

### 1. Read for structure

Note the meeting's aim, who spoke, and where each agenda item started and ended. If the notes came from an AI note-taker, treat them as a first draft: they can be incomplete or wrong, especially on names and numbers.

### 2. Pull out decisions first

A decision is something the group agreed, with the reason if one was given. Write each as one sentence in plain words: "We will move the launch to 3 March because the packaging arrives late." If people discussed something but did not agree, it is not a decision; it goes under open questions.

### 3. Pull out actions

Each action needs a verb, one owner and one date: "Tom: send the revised quote to Oakfield by Thu 12 Nov." If the owner or the date is missing, put the action under "needs an owner or date" and do not guess. Two owners means no owner: ask who leads.

### 4. List open questions and parked topics

Questions raised but not answered, and topics pushed to a later meeting, each with who will bring it back.

### 5. Check against the source

Check every name, number, date and decision against the transcript or notes. Where the notes are unclear, mark [CHECK WITH ATTENDEE] rather than smoothing it over.

### 6. Write the minutes

One page: meeting name, date, attendees, aim, decisions, actions table, needs an owner or date, open questions, next meeting. No narrative of who said what unless a decision depends on it.

### 7. Draft the follow-up email

Three short parts: what we decided, who is doing what by when (each person's actions as a bullet with their name first), and what is still open. Leave out anything marked confidential. At level 3, send it to the attendees within the confirmed limits; otherwise it goes to the owner as a draft.

### 8. Update the action register

For recurring meetings, merge new actions into one register: action, owner, date set, due date, status, meetings it has appeared in. Highlight overdue items and any action that has appeared in three meetings without progress.

### 9. Write the next agenda

Each item as a question to answer, with a time, an owner and any pre-read. Put decisions first, updates last, and suggest moving pure updates into a written note. If an item has no question and no decision, suggest dropping it.

## Checks before handing over

- Every decision is something actually agreed, in one sentence.
- Every action has one owner and one date, or sits under "needs an owner or date".
- No owner, date or decision has been invented.
- Names and numbers match the source; unclear items are marked.
- Confidential items are out of the follow-up email.
- The agenda items are questions with times and owners.

## Output format

1. Minutes (one page)
2. Follow-up email draft
3. Action register (updated)
4. Next agenda
5. Items to check with attendees

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Brightwater Physio, a three-clinic physiotherapy practice in Leeds, UK; weekly managers' meeting.*

> **Managers' meeting, Tue 3 Nov. Attendees: Asha, Ben, Carys.**
> **Aim:** decide winter opening hours and fix the booking backlog.
>
> **Decisions**
> - Headingley clinic opens at 7:30 on weekdays from 1 Dec, because early slots fill first.
> - New patient forms move online from 17 Nov.
>
> | Action | Owner | Due |
> |---|---|---|
> | Update opening hours on the website and booking system | Ben | Fri 13 Nov |
> | Draft the online form and send to Asha | Carys | Wed 11 Nov |
>
> **Needs an owner or date:** tell existing patients about the new hours (discussed, nobody named).
> **Open question:** do we need a second receptionist at Headingley from December? Asha to bring numbers on 10 Nov.
