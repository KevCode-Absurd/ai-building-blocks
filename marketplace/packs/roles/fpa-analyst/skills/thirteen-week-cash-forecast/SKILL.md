# 13-week cash forecast

## When to use

- The owner asks "will we have enough cash?" or "when does it get tight?"
- Before a hire, a stock order, a lease or a loan repayment.
- A lender, landlord or investor asks for a short-term cash view (the owner sends it).
- Income is lumpy or seasonal, or customers pay late.
- Each week, as a rolling update.

It is a plan on stated assumptions, not a promise that cash will arrive. Tax payments are entered as the owner or accountant gives them; I do not work out tax. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant about any tax amounts and dates.

## What you need

1. Today's bank balance for each business account, from the statement or banking app, with the date.
2. Bank exports for the last 3 to 12 months (more if the business is seasonal).
3. Unpaid customer invoices: customer, amount, due date, and how late that customer usually pays.
4. Unpaid supplier bills and known bills coming (rent, software, insurance, utilities).
5. Payroll dates and amounts (totals, not names), contractor payments.
6. Loan repayments, lease payments and card repayment dates.
7. Tax payment dates and amounts from the owner or accountant.
8. Planned one-offs: equipment, stock orders, marketing, owner drawings or dividends.
9. The cash floor the owner never wants to go below.

**Access:** level 1 to read bank exports, invoices and bills; level 2 to build the forecast in a copy or as a scenario in your cash tool; level 3 to update the live forecast each week within your limits. Moving money and chasing customers or suppliers stay with the owner.

## Steps

### 1. Set the frame

13 weekly columns starting Monday of this week. Rows: opening cash, money in (by type), money out (by type), net movement, closing cash, floor, headroom (closing minus floor). Closing cash of each week is the opening cash of the next.

### 2. Learn the patterns from the past

Sort past bank lines into groups: customer receipts, card or platform payouts, payroll, rent, suppliers, loans, tax, owner, other. Find what repeats weekly, monthly, quarterly and yearly, and what was one-off. Note the weekday each repeat lands on.

### 3. Forecast money in

- Known: unpaid invoices placed in the week each customer usually pays, not the due date. Use each customer's own history; if there is none, use the due date plus the average lateness from the ledger and mark it as an assumption.
- Expected: new sales that will be invoiced and paid inside 13 weeks, from the sales pattern or pipeline the owner gives. Card and platform sales land on the payout schedule, net of fees.
- Other: grants, refunds, loan drawdowns, owner money in. Only if confirmed.

### 4. Forecast money out

Payroll and contractor runs on their real dates, rent and fixed bills on their days, supplier bills on the payment terms the business really uses, loan and lease repayments, tax payments as given, card repayments, planned one-offs and owner drawings.

### 5. Tie the start

Opening cash in week 1 must equal the balance the owner gave, with the date. If the balance is from a few days ago, add the known movements since.

### 6. Build three cases

- **Expected:** as above.
- **Slow pay:** the largest customers pay later by their worst recent lateness.
- **Bad:** slow pay plus the main sales driver down, using a size the owner agrees is plausible.
Change only a few named drivers between cases.

### 7. Find the tight weeks

For each case, list weeks where closing cash falls below the floor, the size of the gap and the cause (a payroll week, a tax date, a slow payer).

### 8. Cost three fixes per gap

Examples: chase two named invoices, move a supplier payment by agreement, delay a planned purchase, draw on an agreed facility, trim a cost. For each: the week it helps, the amount, the risk and who must agree. I list options; the owner decides and acts.

### 9. Write the weekly update routine

Each Monday: replace last week's forecast with actuals, roll one week on, update opening cash from the bank, update invoices and bills, recheck the floor. Keep last week's version so the forecast can be backtested. At level 3, I run this update in the live forecast within your limits and log it; at a lower level, I hand you the updated file.

## Checks

- Opening cash equals the owner's bank balance and date.
- Each week: opening plus in minus out equals closing.
- No sale counted as cash before the customer usually pays.
- Tax, payroll and loan dates are in the right week.
- Cases differ only by named drivers.
- Every assumption is listed with its source and confidence.
- The forecast carries: *A plan, not a promise. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant about tax amounts and dates.*

## Output format

1. Headline: lowest cash week and amount in each case, and whether the floor is breached.
2. The 13-week table (expected case), with cases on separate tabs.
3. Tight weeks table: week, case, gap, cause.
4. Fix options: action, week, amount, risk, who decides.
5. Assumptions list.
6. Weekly update routine.
