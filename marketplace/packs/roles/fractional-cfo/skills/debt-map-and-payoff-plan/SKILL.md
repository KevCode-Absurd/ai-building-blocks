# Debt map and payoff plan

## When to use

- The owner has several loans, cards or supplier plans and isn't sure what each costs.
- A new loan or refinancing offer has arrived.
- Repayments are starting to squeeze cash.
- Before a sale or purchase, when a buyer or lender will ask what is owed.

I prepare the map, the maths and the questions. Paying, moving money and negotiating stay with the owner. If the business may not be able to pay its debts as they fall due, stop and tell the owner to get advice from a licensed insolvency professional now. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer about tax, security and legal terms.

## What you need

1. The latest statement for every loan, card, overdraft, lease, asset finance, supplier plan and tax payment arrangement.
2. The agreements or offer letters: rate (fixed or variable), fees, term, security, covenants, early repayment terms.
3. Any personal guarantees the owner has signed.
4. Monthly profit and loss and bank statements for 12 months, or a cash forecast.
5. The owner's cash floor: the lowest balance they are comfortable with.
6. For a new offer: the offer letter or term sheet and what the money is for.

**Access:** level 1 to read statements and agreements; level 2 to build the map in a copy or draft a letter to a lender for the owner to send. Anything sent to a lender needs the owner's fresh yes every time, even at level 3, and payments are always the owner's.

## Steps

### 1. List every debt

One row each: lender, type, balance, rate and whether it is fixed or variable, monthly payment, fees still to pay, end date, security, covenants, early repayment cost, personal guarantee (yes or no). Ask directly about cards, supplier plans, tax arrangements and director loans; they are often missed.

### 2. Work out the true cost of each

Interest to the end of the term at today's rate, plus fees, plus any early repayment cost if it would be paid early. For a variable rate, show the cost at today's rate and at a higher rate the owner agrees is plausible, and say it is an assumption.

### 3. Test cash cover

From the cash forecast or the last 12 months, set operating cash for each month against total debt payments. Show the months where cover is thin or the balance would drop below the floor. If a loan agreement has a covenant, use its own definition and test dates, quoted from the agreement, and show the headroom.

### 4. Compare payoff orders

Run at least two orders with the same extra monthly amount the owner can afford:
- **Most expensive first:** spare cash goes to the debt with the highest true cost.
- **Smallest balance first:** clears whole debts quickly and frees their payments.
Add a third if it fits: **guarantee first** (debts the owner has guaranteed personally) or **covenant first** (the debt whose covenant is tightest).
For each order: the date each debt clears, total interest, debt-free date and the lowest monthly cash. Do not pick for the owner; say what each order is good for.

### 5. Break down a new offer (if there is one)

Amount received after fees, total repaid to the end, the cost per year in plain words, security, covenants, guarantees, events of default, early repayment terms, and what changes for existing lenders. Write the questions to ask the lender. Compare with the debt it would replace using total cost, not the headline rate.

### 6. Plan the lender conversation (if payments are tight)

A short, factual plan: the numbers, the cause, the ask (for example a payment holiday or a longer term), a fallback and the date. Draft it for the owner to send or say. Point to free help: in the UK, Business Debtline; for tax, the tax authority's payment plans.

## Checks

- Every debt the owner mentioned and every one on the balance sheet is on the map.
- Fees, early repayment costs and guarantees are included.
- Covenant tests use the agreement's own wording and dates.
- Payoff orders use the same extra payment so they compare fairly.
- Variable rate assumptions are labelled.
- No legal reading of security or default clauses; those are questions for a lawyer.
- The output says: *I prepare the numbers; you decide, pay and speak to lenders. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*

## Output format

1. Headline: total owed, total cost to the end, payments due in the next 12 months, and the tightest month.
2. Debt map table.
3. Cash cover table by month, with covenant headroom if any.
4. Payoff order comparison table.
5. New offer breakdown and questions (if any).
6. Lender conversation plan (if needed).
7. What to confirm with an accountant, lawyer or lender.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up business for illustration: Hollin Bakery, two shops.

> **Headline:** You owe 96,400 across five debts. Payments are 3,950 a month. Cash covers them every month, but only just in January and February.
>
> | Debt | Balance | True cost to end | Payment | Ends | Guarantee |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | Bank loan (oven) | 41,000 | 7,900 | 1,450 | Mar 2029 | Yes |
> | Card 1 | 8,200 | 3,100 if minimum only | 250 | none | Yes |
> | Card 2 | 2,900 | 900 if minimum only | 90 | none | No |
> | Supplier plan | 6,300 | 0 | 700 | Aug 2027 | No |
> | Asset finance (van) | 38,000 | 6,800 | 1,460 | Jan 2030 | Yes |
>
> | Extra 500 a month | Debt-free | Total interest | Lowest month |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Most expensive first | Jan 2030 | 15,200 | 2,100 |
> | Smallest first | Jan 2030 | 16,000 | 2,600 |
>
> Most expensive first saves about 800; smallest first frees two card payments seven months sooner. Your choice.
