# Finance health check and 90-day plan

## When to use

- The owner asks "are we healthy?", "what should I worry about?" or "where is the money going?"
- Before a loan application, a big hire, a second site, or the start of sale preparation.
- When profit looks fine but cash keeps running short.
- Quarterly, as a repeat check against the last 90-day plan.

This is a finance leader's view from the owner's files. It does not audit the books, value the business, or decide tax or accounting treatment. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer about anything that turns on tax, law or how a figure must be reported.

## What you need

1. Profit and loss by month for 24 months (12 at minimum), from the accounting software.
2. Balance sheets at each year end and the latest month end.
3. Bank statements or exports for the same period, and today's balance for each account.
4. Aged receivables and payables at the latest month end.
5. A list of every loan, card, lease and supplier plan, with balance, payment and end date.
6. Sales by customer for the last 12 months (names can be coded) and by product or service line.
7. What the owner most wants to know or decide.

**Access:** level 1 is enough for the whole check (reports, exports, statements). Level 2 lets me save the report as an unshared draft in your reporting tool; level 3 lets me share the approved report with the people you name. Nothing here moves money or changes the books.

## Steps

### 1. Ask what the owner wants to decide

Write the question at the top: "Can we afford a second site next spring?" reads differently from "Why is cash always tight?" The question sets which area gets the most depth.

### 2. Tie the base

- Net profit for the year flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet.
- Cash on the balance sheet matches the bank statements at each date.
- Receivables and payables totals match the aged reports.
If anything fails, stop and list it as finding one, with the gap and the date. Ask the bookkeeper to fix it before rating.

### 3. Rate the five areas

For each area: the measure, this year against last year (and the monthly trend), a rating and one line of evidence. Ratings compare the business with its own history, its plan and its debts, not with industry averages I can't source.
- **Growth:** sales by month and by line; new against lost customers if known. Is growth broad or one customer?
- **Gross margin:** gross profit as a share of sales, by line where possible. Separate price, cost and mix moves.
- **Overheads:** fixed costs as a share of sales and in money; which lines grew faster than sales?
- **Cash cycle:** days to collect, days of stock, days to pay suppliers, now and a year ago. Did growth tie up cash?
- **Debt:** what is owed, what it costs, the payments due in the next 12 months, and whether cash from operations covers them. Note personal guarantees and covenant tests.
Green means it supports the owner's plans; amber means it needs watching or a fix within the year; red means it threatens cash, a covenant or the plan within months.

### 4. Check concentration and owner reliance

Top ten customers as a share of sales; top five suppliers as a share of costs; which relationships, skills or logins only the owner holds. These rarely fit in one area but often matter most.

### 5. Rank the three biggest issues

Rank by money at stake over the next 12 months (lost margin, cash tied up, interest, a covenant breach). For each, name the cause behind the symptom: "Margin fell because two price rises were not passed on to the three largest customers", not "margin fell". If the data can't prove the cause, write "linked with" and the check that would prove it.

### 6. Write the 90-day plan

Five actions at most, ranked by impact. Each has an owner, a date, the expected money effect and the number that shows it worked. Include at least one action that protects cash in the next 30 days if any area is red.

### 7. List what I could not check

Gaps in the data, questions for the bookkeeper, and anything for the accountant, lawyer or lender.

## Checks

- The base ties, or the tie-out gap is finding one.
- Every rating has a measure, a comparison and a line of evidence.
- No rating rests on an unsourced industry benchmark.
- Issues are ranked by money at stake, with causes or "linked with" plus a check.
- Each action has an owner, date, money effect and measure.
- Nothing values the business or gives tax or legal treatment.
- The report ends with: *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*

## Output format

1. The question and the one-line answer.
2. Tie-out result.
3. Ratings table: area, measure, this year, last year, rating, evidence.
4. Concentration and owner reliance notes.
5. Top three issues: money at stake, cause, first action.
6. 90-day plan table: action, owner, date, money effect, measure.
7. What I could not check, and questions for advisers.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up business for illustration: Larkfield Signs, a 14-person sign maker.

> **Question:** Can we afford a second workshop next spring? **Answer:** Not yet. Sales grew, but the cash cycle and one customer hold the answer; fix those first.
>
> | Area | This year | Last year | Rating | Evidence |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Growth | Sales +11% | +6% | Green | Broad: 9 of top 10 customers grew |
> | Gross margin | 38% | 43% | Red | Vinyl and aluminium costs up; prices unchanged since last spring |
> | Overheads | 24% of sales | 25% | Green | Grew slower than sales |
> | Cash cycle | 71 days | 52 days | Amber | Customers pay 17 days slower |
> | Debt | 48,000 owed | 61,000 | Green | Payments covered by operating cash every month |
>
> **Issue 1 (about 60,000 a year):** margin. Materials rose; prices didn't. **Action:** reprice the three standard ranges by 30 June; owner; measure: margin back above 41% by September.
>
> **Issue 2 (about 45,000 of cash tied up):** one customer, 22% of sales, now pays at 75 days. **Action:** agree stage payments on new orders; owner; by 15 May.
>
> *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*
