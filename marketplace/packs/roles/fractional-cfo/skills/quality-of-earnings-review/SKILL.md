# Quality of earnings review

## When to use

- **Buy side:** someone is buying a small business and wants to know whether the profit is real before making or confirming an offer.
- **Sell side:** an owner is preparing to sell and wants their adjusted profit and add-backs ready, with proof, before a buyer's adviser looks.
- A lender asks for adjusted profit to support a loan for an acquisition.

I rebuild and test the numbers from the documents given. I cannot confirm that the seller's figures are true, and I do not value the business or advise on price. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer about tax, legal terms and how the deal should be structured.

## What you need

1. Three years of annual accounts and the current year's monthly profit and loss.
2. Bank statements for at least the last 12 months (all accounts).
3. Sales by customer and by month; purchases by supplier.
4. Payroll summary by role, including the owner and family members.
5. Aged receivables and payables, stock records, deposits taken from customers.
6. Loan, lease and finance agreements; any tax arrangements.
7. The broker's summary or the seller's adjusted profit figure, if there is one.
8. On the buy side: the asking price and what the buyer worries about.

**Access:** level 1 to read the accounts, statements and data room. Level 2 to build the schedule and request list in a private folder. Sending requests to a seller or broker needs the owner's fresh yes each time.

## Steps

### 1. Tie the numbers

For each year: profit and loss to balance sheet, and reported sales to deposits in the bank (allowing for VAT or sales tax, card fees and timing). Put three years side by side. Any gap is a red flag until explained.

### 2. Start from reported profit

Use EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) as the starting line, because that is what most small business buyers and brokers quote. Write the definition at the top.

### 3. List adjustments, each with proof and a strength

For each line: description, amount by year, proof (invoice, payslip, contract, bank line), and strength:
- **Strong:** documented and clearly not continuing (a one-off legal bill with the invoice and settlement).
- **Medium:** likely but partly documented (a family member's salary for light duties; the replacement cost is an estimate).
- **Weak:** asserted without proof, or a cost that happens most years.
Typical lines: owner pay above or below a market replacement cost (both directions), personal costs run through the business, family wages, one-off income or costs, rent paid to the owner at off-market rates, unusual year-end accruals, and income that won't continue after the sale. Headline adjusted EBITDA includes strong and medium lines only; weak lines are shown separately.

### 4. Check upkeep spending

Compare capital spending with depreciation for three years. If the business needs regular spending to stay as it is (vehicles, equipment, fit-out), note the yearly amount, because the profit a new owner can take out is lower by that much.

### 5. Test concentration and dependence

Top ten customers and top five suppliers as a share of the total, by year. Contracts, renewal dates and change-of-ownership clauses for the biggest. Which customers and skills depend on the owner personally.

### 6. Read working capital and debt-like items

Monthly receivables, stock and payables for 12 months, so the normal level and the seasonal swing are visible. Customer deposits, unpaid tax, deferred income, holiday pay owed, old supplier balances and anything due soon that a buyer would inherit. Flag them; how they are treated in the price is for the advisers.

### 7. Rank the red flags

By likely effect on what the buyer would pay or the owner would receive: each with the evidence, the question to ask and who answers it (seller, accountant, lawyer).

### 8. Write the request list

Numbered, grouped by area, ranked by what could change the price. On the sell side, turn the same list into a "fix before a buyer asks" list.

## Checks

- Three years tie to the bank, or each gap is a red flag.
- Every adjustment has proof and a strength; weak lines are outside the headline.
- Owner pay is adjusted in both directions to a stated replacement cost.
- Upkeep spending is shown, not ignored.
- Concentration and debt-like items are in the report.
- No valuation, price advice or tax treatment.
- The report says: *Questions, not answers. I cannot confirm the figures are true. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*

## Output format

1. Headline: reported EBITDA, adjusted EBITDA (strong and medium only), weak lines total, and the three biggest red flags.
2. Tie-out table by year.
3. Adjustment schedule: line, amounts by year, proof, strength.
4. Upkeep spending note.
5. Concentration tables.
6. Working capital by month and debt-like items.
7. Red flags ranked, with questions and who answers.
8. Request list.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a pet supplies business with one shop and a web store, being bought. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.

> **Headline:** Reported EBITDA last year 112,000. Adjusted EBITDA 114,300 on strong and medium lines (the owner takes no salary, so a market manager cost comes off). The broker's figure of 135,300 also counts a weak 21,000 line, shown separately.
>
> | Adjustment | Last year | Proof | Strength |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Owner's car and phone | +6,800 | Lease and bills in company name | Strong |
> | One-off shop refit repairs | +9,500 | Invoices, insurance letter | Strong |
> | Owner works 60 hours; market manager cost | -28,000 | Job adverts for similar roles | Medium |
> | Spouse's salary, "light duties" | +14,000 | Payslips; duties not documented | Medium |
> | "Marketing test that failed" | +21,000 | None; similar spend in prior two years | Weak |
>
> **Red flag 1:** web sales fell 18% this year while shop sales rose. Ask for web sales by month and ad spend by month (seller).
> **Red flag 2:** three customer deposits for bulk orders (8,400) are in sales but not yet delivered. Ask how they are treated at completion (accountant).
>
