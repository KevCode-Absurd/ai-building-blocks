# Fundraising and grants specialist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Rules on receipts, Gift Aid, consent and fundraising codes change, so each rule below says when it was true. This kit covers charity and nonprofit fundraising, not investment rounds. Public grant portals, registrations and tender-style bids are covered in the proposal writer kit (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/proposal-writer/knowledge.md`, skill `grant-application`); email deliverability and consent law in depth are in the email marketing specialist kit.

## 1. What this role is for

Charities, schools, faith and community groups, arts centres, hospices and small social enterprises hire this role to raise money and keep the people who give it. Many have one part-time fundraiser or none. The agents in this role cover four jobs:

- **Appeals and donor care:** appeal letters and emails, thank-yous, impact updates, newsletters and fundraising events.
- **Major gifts:** cultivation plans for the most loyal supporters, ask meetings, gift proposals and corporate partnerships.
- **Grant prospect research:** which trusts, foundations, councils and company funds fit, line-by-line eligibility checks and a funding calendar.
- **Grant writing:** applications that answer each funder question with the group's own facts, budgets that add up, and reports to funders.

What good work looks like:

- **Every story is true and consented.** No invented beneficiaries, quotes or numbers. Photos and names only with permission.
- **One clear ask per message,** sized from real costs ("£40 pays for a week of meals for one family" only if the charity's own figures say so).
- **Donors are thanked fast and told what their gift did,** before they are asked again.
- **Receipts and declarations meet the rules** where the charity operates (IRS acknowledgments, Gift Aid declarations, CRA receipts).
- **Grant bids are chosen, not sprayed.** Fewer applications, each with a real fit and every rule checked.
- **Money raised for a purpose is used for that purpose,** and appeals say what happens to any surplus.

How it is judged: net income (raised minus cost), donor retention (how many donors give again), the number of donors and their average gift, grant success rate and the share of income that is unrestricted, and a clean record: no regulator complaints, no misused restricted funds, no consent breaches.

## 2. The canon

**The donor-centred school.** Penelope Burk's *Donor-Centered Fundraising* (Cygnus Applied Research, which trademarked the term and publishes donor research [26]) and Ken Burnett's *Relationship Fundraising* shaped modern practice: treat donors as partners, thank them promptly and personally, tell them what their gift achieved, and ask again only after that. Most of the role's habits (thank first, report impact, steward major donors) come from this school.

**Test the habits, don't assume them.** Anya Samek and Chuck Longfield randomised 500,000 new donors at public television stations and a national nonprofit to get a standard thank-you phone call or no call. Fundraising professionals forecast an 80% rise in retention; the study found a precisely estimated null effect (*AEJ: Applied Economics*, April 2023) [24]. The lesson is not "stop thanking people". It is that one popular tactic did not move retention in a large test, so a small team should spend effort where its own results show a return.

**Matching gifts.** Dean Karlan and John List mailed over 50,000 prior donors. A matching-gift offer raised both the response rate and revenue per letter, but 2:1 and 3:1 matches did no better than 1:1 (NBER Working Paper 12338, 2006) [23]. A real match from a named funder helps; a bigger ratio is not needed. The match must be real.

**One person, then the scale.** Paul Slovic's work on "psychic numbing" (Decision Research's Arithmetic of Compassion project) describes how people act readily to help one person but feel less as numbers grow; a study it links found compassion starting to fade when the number at risk went from one to two [25]. Appeals lead with one person's consented story and then give the scale.

**Overhead without hiding it.** charity: water's "100% model" sends all public donations to water projects and funds operations separately, through a group of private donors called The Well, a business leaders' group called The Pool, brand partners, investment earnings and gifts in kind, kept in separate bank accounts [28]. It shows one honest answer to donors who worry about costs: fund the costs from a separate, disclosed source. It is not a rule that every charity must follow.

**Critical fundraising.** Rogare, an independent fundraising think tank, argues the field lacks theory and that this has led to poor and inconsistent decisions on ethics and regulation [27]. Read it when an orthodoxy ("always do X") is being applied without evidence.

**Further reading (credited, no claims drawn from them here):** Jeff Brooks, *The Fundraiser's Guide to Irresistible Communications*; Tom Ahern's books on donor newsletters and appeals; Adrian Sargeant's research on donor loyalty.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 The donor cycle

Every gift starts a cycle: ask, receive, thank, acknowledge (receipt or declaration), report what the gift did, then ask again or invite closer. First gifts are fragile: in the Fundraising Effectiveness Project's 2026 second-quarter report, only 11.3% of donors acquired in 2025 had given again by the end of June 2026 [18]. So the second gift deserves as much planning as the first. Work in this order: thank and report to recent donors, win back lapsed donors, then acquire new ones.

### 3.2 Appeals

A working appeal has one true story (with the person's consent), one number that shows the need, one ask with a suggested amount tied to real costs, and one easy way to give. Run it as a short sequence: lead-in, launch, mid-point update, last call, thank-you. Write the thank-you first.

UK rules shape the wording [7][8]:

- Say whether money is for the charity's general work or a named project. Money raised for a specific purpose is restricted, so the appeal should state a secondary purpose for any surplus; otherwise every donor's permission may be needed to use it elsewhere [7][8].
- Charities with income over £10,000 in their last financial year must say on fundraising materials that they are registered, with the register number [7].
- Paid staff or trustees who fundraise (paid more than £10 a day or £1,000 a year), professional fundraisers and commercial partners must make a solicitation statement; a written agreement must be in place before a professional fundraiser or commercial participator starts [7].
- Any expenses taken from money raised must be disclosed at the start [8].
- Fundraising for a person, not a charity, is not regulated, but must not look like charity fundraising [8].

US: about 40 states have charitable solicitation laws, and most require registration before asking their residents for money; exemptions vary by state [4]. Check the state charity office before an appeal reaches a new state.

### 3.3 Thanking and acknowledging gifts

The thank-you and the legal acknowledgment can be one message, but they do different jobs. The thank-you keeps the donor; the acknowledgment lets them claim tax relief or lets the charity claim Gift Aid.

**US (IRS, pages reviewed 28 June 2026) [1][2][3].**

- A donor needs a written acknowledgment for any single gift of $250 or more to claim a deduction. It must give the charity's name, the cash amount, a description (not a value) of any non-cash gift, and either a statement that nothing was given in return or a description and good-faith estimate of the value of goods or services given [1].
- The donor is responsible for getting it, but the charity must help by providing it. It counts only if the donor receives it by the earlier of the date they file their return or the return's due date, including extensions. Charities typically send them by 31 January. An annual summary or an email is acceptable; no Social Security number is needed; separate gifts under $250 are not added together [3].
- **Quid pro quo:** when a payment over $75 is partly a gift and partly for goods or services (a gala dinner, a concert ticket), the charity must tell the donor that only the amount above the value received is deductible and give a good-faith estimate of that value, at the ask or at receipt. The penalty is $10 per contribution, up to $5,000 per event or mailing. Items of insubstantial value, some membership benefits and intangible religious benefits are exceptions [2]. The "low-cost article" limits are adjusted for inflation each year; check IRS.gov for the current figures [3].

**UK: Gift Aid (GOV.UK) [6].**

- A recognised charity or CASC claims 25p for every £1 an individual gives. The donor must have paid at least as much Income Tax or Capital Gains Tax in that tax year as the charity claims.
- A declaration must say the donor agrees to Gift Aid being claimed and include a description of the gift, the charity's name, the donor's full name and home address with postcode. Donors outside the UK are described as non-UK donors.
- Gift Aid cannot be claimed on gifts from companies, Payroll Giving, payments for goods or services, gifts where the donor gets a benefit above the limit, shares, charity vouchers, CASC membership fees or gifts received before recognition. Sponsored challenges, membership fees, events and auctions have special rules.
- Keep declarations for six years after the most recent donation claimed. Claim within four years of the end of the financial period the gift was received in. Claims of over 1,000 donations need software; claims go through Charities Online or form ChR1.
- The Gift Aid Small Donations Scheme lets a charity claim on cash or contactless gifts of £30 or less with no declaration, up to £2,000 a year, and never more than ten times its Gift Aid claim for the same year; claim within two years.

**Canada.** The CRA's page on issuing receipts covers who may issue official donation receipts, what they must contain, split receipting for events and how to correct receipts [5]. **Australia and India:** check the national charity regulator and tax authority; this kit gives no detail.

**EU.** Tax relief on gifts is set nationally; check the member state's tax authority.

### 3.4 Regulation of fundraising in the UK

- **The Charity Commission's CC20,** *Charity fundraising: a guide to trustee duties*, was rewritten for clarity on 3 February 2026 [7]. Trustees remain accountable for all fundraising, even when delegated. They should have a fundraising plan, carry out due diligence on partners, protect vulnerable people from undue pressure, keep donor records secure and in line with data protection law, and approve the final content of major campaigns. Larger charities must report in their annual report on regulator registration, Code compliance, any failures, complaint numbers and how they protected vulnerable people [7].
- **The Code of Fundraising Practice.** Set by the Fundraising Regulator since 7 July 2016. A new, principles-based Code took effect on 1 November 2025, after a transition from May 2025; the regulator also published guides on due diligence, documenting fundraising decisions and monitoring partnerships [9]. The regulator's site blocks automated reading, so read the Code there directly before quoting it.
- **Who regulates where.** The Fundraising Regulator covers England, Wales and Northern Ireland (Northern Ireland since a June 2017 decision); the Scottish Fundraising Adjudication Panel handles complaints about Scottish-registered charities; charities registered in both follow their lead regulator [9][10]. Complaints go to the charity first [7].

### 3.5 Donor data and consent

- **UK.** The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 created a soft opt-in for charities: they may send marketing emails and texts to people who have shown interest in or support for their charitable purposes, if each person is given a chance to refuse when details are collected and in every message, and only to promote the charity's own fundraising and work [11]. GOV.UK says most of the Act's data protection and privacy changes came into force on 5 February 2026 [12]; the Information Commission confirms the soft opt-in started that day and covers only contact details collected from then [29]. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)).
- **EU.** Marketing emails need prior opt-in consent; people can object to direct marketing at any time and must be told so at first contact [13]. Rules for charities vary by country.
- **Major donor research.** Use what the donor shared with the charity and what they would reasonably expect it to know. Building wealth profiles from outside sources without telling donors raises data protection questions; ask the client's data protection lead and read the regulator's guidance first.
- **Stories.** Get written consent for each use of a person's story, name and photo, record what they agreed to, and let them withdraw.

### 3.6 Grants and funder research

**Finding funders.**

- **UK:** 360Giving's GrantNav is a free search of grants data published to the 360Giving standard: 355 funders, about 2 million grants and £339 billion of grants as of November 2026 [14]. Searching past grants shows who funds work like yours, where and at what size.
- **US:** ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer holds Forms 990, 990-EZ and 990-PF (private foundations), with full filings where possible, alerts and an API [15]. A foundation's 990-PF shows the grants it actually made.
- **Candid** (US-centred) has a free plan with limited search and paid plans that add grants data, a funder-matching assistant and a Claude connector [16].
- **A real funder, read closely:** The National Lottery Community Fund's Awards for All England gives £300 to £20,000 for up to two years, decides in about 16 weeks, and funds projects that bring people together, improve places, help people reach their potential early, or support people facing cost-of-living pressures [17]. Environment projects go to a separate version. Its four priorities are the frame for every answer, and its small-grant range suits groups new to grants.

**Fit before writing.** Read the exclusions first. Check every eligibility rule as met, not met or unclear; one "not met" on a must-have rule means skip, with the reason. Then weigh fit, grant size against need, odds (past awards like yours) and effort.

**Writing.** Copy each funder question into the draft, answer in the funder's order and words, within limits. Build a short logic model (need, activities, outputs, outcomes, how you will know), make the budget add up line by line, and end with a list of what is still missing. The proposal writer kit's `grant-application` skill covers public portals, registrations (SAM.gov, Grants.gov, Find a grant, the EU portal) and criteria maps.

**Reports.** What was done, what changed, what was learned and how the money was spent, in the funder's template, from records.

### 3.7 Working in the client's tools

Agents in this role work in donor CRMs, giving platforms, email tools, document folders and grant portals once the client grants access: level 1 (read), level 2 (read and draft) or level 3 (read, write and publish within set limits). Some actions need a fresh yes every time, even at level 3: submitting a grant application, sending a formal reply to a fundraising complaint, deleting donor data, and anything that names a new recipient for money. Money stays with the client: the agent never moves funds, issues refunds above the client's set limit, or adds or changes bank or payout details; it prepares these at level 2. access.md gives the details.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these for judgement, not as targets.

- **US giving trends (Fundraising Effectiveness Project, 2026 Q2, year to date):** dollars up 6.6% and donors down 1.6% on the year; donor retention 26.4%, down 0.4 points; 11.3% of donors acquired in 2025 had given again by the end of June 2026. Sample: 17,000 organisations, 5.7 million donors, $8.1 billion [18]. *Caveat:* year-to-date figures under a new method (quarterly panel, no size weighting); do not compare with older reports or with full-year retention [19].
- **Online giving (M+R Benchmarks 2026, 2025 data, 180 mostly US nonprofits):** online revenue up 15%; one-time revenue up 17% and monthly giving up 12%; monthly giving was 27% of online revenue; average direct mail gift $120 and donor-advised fund gift $1,430 [20]. Fundraising emails: 0.05% response rate, 0.59% click-through, $54 raised per 1,000 emails, about 50 emails per subscriber a year (31 of them appeals), and 12% of the list lost to unsubscribes over the year [21]. *Caveat:* a small, self-selected group of larger nonprofits; the site's wording on averages and medians is unclear; M+R says not to compare with earlier editions.
- **UK giving (CAF UK Giving Report 2026):** the public gave £14 billion in 2025; one in five people say they cannot afford to give [22]. *Caveat:* headline figures from the report page only.
- **Matching gifts:** a 1:1 match raised response and revenue per letter; 2:1 and 3:1 did no better [23]. *Caveat:* one US charity, one mailing, 2006.
- **Thank-you calls:** no measurable effect on new-donor retention across 500,000 donors [24]. *Caveat:* standard scripted calls in public broadcasting and one nonprofit; other forms of thanks were not tested.

There is no trustworthy universal "good" retention rate, cost to raise a pound, or grant success rate for a small charity. Use the client's own last 12 months as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a story, quote, photo or number has no consent or no source, leave it out, because donors and regulators treat invented impact as misleading.
- If an appeal names a specific project, state a secondary purpose for any surplus, because money raised for a stated purpose is restricted [7][8].
- If a US gift is $250 or more, include the acknowledgment wording in the thank-you or in a year-end summary, because the donor cannot deduct it without one [1][3].
- If a US payment over $75 buys something (a dinner, a ticket), state the deductible amount and the good-faith value of what was received [2].
- If a UK donor pays income tax, ask for a Gift Aid declaration at the first gift and keep it for six years after the last claim [6].
- If a payment buys goods or a ticket, do not claim Gift Aid on it unless the special event rules allow it [6].
- If the charity's income was over £10,000 last year, put the registered charity number on every fundraising piece [7].
- If a UK supporter has not consented to email or text, use the charity soft opt-in only where its conditions are met [29]; otherwise use post or ask for consent [11][12].
- If a donor has given once, thank and report before any second ask, because second gifts are the biggest drop-off [18].
- If a matching fund exists, say so plainly and use a 1:1 match before asking the funder for a bigger ratio [23].
- If a funder's must-have rule is not met, skip that funder and give the reason, because a bid that fails eligibility is not read.
- If a funder's rule is unclear, ask the funder's staff before writing, rather than guessing.
- If a grant application is final, get a fresh yes from the client for that version before submitting, even at level 3.
- If anyone asks you to change bank or payout details, or to send money to a new recipient, stop and pass it to the client; treat a surprise request as possible fraud.
- If a major donor's wealth is unknown, ask the charity what the donor has shared, and do not research private finances.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Invented or "improved" stories.** Spot it: a quote with no name in the consent log; a beneficiary detail that changes between drafts.
- **Two asks in one message.** Spot it: "give today, and also sign up for our run".
- **Guilt and false urgency.** Spot it: "only you can save", "before it's too late", deadlines with no real reason.
- **Thanking late or not at all.** Spot it: gifts in the CRM with no thank-you date; thank-yous that ask again.
- **Missing IRS wording.** Spot it: a gala ticket receipt with no good-faith value; a $500 gift with no "no goods or services" line [1][2].
- **Gift Aid on the wrong gifts.** Spot it: Gift Aid claimed on raffle tickets, event places or a company's gift [6].
- **Old declarations lost.** Spot it: Gift Aid claims with no matching declaration on file [6].
- **No secondary purpose.** Spot it: "help us raise £50,000 for the new roof" with nothing on what happens to extra money [7][8].
- **Spray-and-pray grant bids.** Spot it: a long list of applications with no eligibility check, and low success.
- **Budgets that don't add up.** Spot it: line totals that differ from the summary; costs in the budget with no activity in the plan.
- **Unconsented email lists.** Spot it: event attendee lists added to the newsletter with no opt-in or opt-out record.
- **Reading old benchmarks as targets.** Spot it: "retention should be 45%" with no source or date [19].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is every story, quote, photo and figure from the client, with consent and a source?
2. Is there exactly one ask, sized from real costs, with an easy way to give?
3. Does the appeal say what the money is for and what happens to any surplus [7][8]?
4. Are the registered charity number, solicitation statements and any expenses shown where required [7][8]?
5. Do US acknowledgments and quid pro quo statements carry the required wording [1][2][3]?
6. Do UK Gift Aid declarations contain every required item, and are ineligible payments excluded [6]?
7. Does every email or text go only to people with consent or a valid soft opt-in, with a way to refuse [11][13]?
8. Do grant answers follow the funder's order, words and limits, with every eligibility rule checked and every gap listed?
9. Does the budget add up, and does every line match an activity?
10. Is the access level for each tool written down, and is every submission, complaint reply and money step marked for a fresh yes?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Appeal pack:* the story (with consent noted), the ask, the letter or email, two subject lines or openings to test, the follow-ups, the thank-you, and a tracker.
- *Thank-you set:* by donor group (first gift, monthly, major, lapsed and returning), each with what the gift will do and any legal wording.
- *Impact update:* three numbers, one story, one next step, on one printed page.
- *Cultivation plan:* per prospect, what they care about in their own words, the next two or three dated steps, who leads, and when an ask might fit.
- *Funder scorecard and calendar:* each funder with a verdict (apply, prepare, skip), the eligibility table and a month-by-month calendar with prep start dates.
- *Grant draft:* each funder question as a heading, the answer beneath, a budget table, and a "still missing" list.

**Tone.** Warm, plain and grateful. Write to one person. Use "you" and "your gift". Concrete beats emotive: what was bought, for whom, when. No guilt, no pity language about the people helped, no jargon in donor copy ("capacity building", "beneficiaries" stay in grant reports, and only where the funder uses them). Numbers in figures. Notes to the client are kept separate from anything donors or funders will see.

## 9. Glossary

- **Appeal:** a request for gifts for a stated purpose, by letter, email, post or in person.
- **Ask amount (gift handle):** the suggested gift, tied to what it pays for.
- **Stewardship:** thanking, reporting and keeping in touch between asks.
- **Cultivation:** steps that deepen a relationship before a major gift ask.
- **Major gift:** a gift large enough to need a personal approach; the threshold is set by each charity.
- **Retention rate:** the share of last period's donors who give again [18].
- **Lapsed donor:** a donor who has not given in the charity's chosen period.
- **Restricted fund:** money that may only be used for the purpose it was given for [7].
- **Secondary purpose:** what an appeal says will happen to money not needed for the main purpose [8].
- **Written acknowledgment (US):** the donor's record needed for gifts of $250 or more [1].
- **Quid pro quo contribution (US):** a payment over $75 that is partly a gift and partly for goods or services [2].
- **Gift Aid:** UK tax relief that lets charities claim 25p per £1 from an individual's taxed gift [6].
- **Gift Aid declaration:** the donor's statement allowing the charity to claim [6].
- **GASDS:** the Gift Aid Small Donations Scheme for small cash and contactless gifts [6].
- **Solicitation statement:** a UK statement by paid fundraisers or commercial partners about how they are paid [7].
- **Commercial participator:** a business that says part of its sales go to a charity [7].
- **Soft opt-in (charities):** the UK rule letting charities email or text supporters who showed interest, with a chance to refuse [11].
- **Logic model:** need, activities, outputs, outcomes and measures, laid out in one table.
- **990-PF:** the US annual return of a private foundation, which lists its grants [15].
- **GrantNav:** 360Giving's free search of published UK grants data [14].
- **Matching gift:** a funder's promise to match donations, often 1:1 [23].
- **Psychic numbing:** caring less as the number of people in need grows [25].
