# Major gift cultivation and ask

A major gift is a decision a supporter makes after they understand what their money will do and trust the charity to do it. The work is mostly before the ask: thanking, reporting, inviting, listening. This skill plans that work and prepares a clear, kind ask.

## When to use

- A charity has loyal regular donors but has never asked anyone for a larger gift.
- A capital or project appeal needs lead gifts before going public.
- A local business has sponsored an event and might become a longer partner.
- A major donor has gone quiet and the charity wants to reconnect.

Not for: researching private wealth, property or family finances; tax advice on large gifts, legacies or Gift Aid (ask a qualified accountant, and check this with local rules); pressure, guilt or deadlines that are not real.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read giving history and contact notes in the donor database. Level 2 to draft notes, tasks, letters and proposals. Level 3 to log meetings and next steps in the database within the limits. Sending a gift agreement or a proposal the charity treats as binding needs a fresh yes.

- **The charity's own records:** giving history, events attended, volunteering, interests the supporter has shared, conversation notes. Names can be removed.
- **The client's criteria** for "top prospect" (for example loyalty, closeness to the cause, recent contact, past gift size).
- **Real projects with real costs** that a major gift could fund, and how the donor would hear about the result.
- **Who leads each relationship** (chief executive, chair, trustee, fundraiser) and their time.
- **Recognition options** the charity can actually deliver (naming, reports, visits), and any gift acceptance policy.

## Steps

1. **Agree the criteria.** Ask the client to rank what matters. Show the criteria in the summary so they can change them.
2. **Pick the top prospects** from the records only. For each, note: years giving, pattern, last contact, what they have said they care about (in their words), and gaps in what the charity knows. Do not search outside sources for wealth. If the client wants research beyond what donors shared, point them to their data protection lead and the regulator's guidance first.
3. **Check the basics first.** Has this person been thanked for every gift? Told what their gifts did? If not, the first step is a thank-you and a report, not an ask.
4. **Write a cultivation plan per prospect.** Two or three dated next steps (a thank-you visit, a project update, a site visit, an invitation to see the work, a question about what they would like to change), who leads each, and the signal that an ask may fit (they ask how they could help more; they ask about costs).
5. **Choose the project and the amount** with the client. The amount comes from the project cost and what the client knows of the donor's past giving and interests. The client decides the figure.
6. **Prepare the ask meeting.** Aim of the meeting; three key facts about the project; how to open (thank them, recall their own words); the ask in one plain sentence with the amount; then silence. List likely questions with honest answers (costs, timing, what happens if the target is missed, how they will hear about results). Plan the follow-up note whatever the answer.
7. **Draft the proposal or letter.** The need, the plan, what the gift makes possible (in numbers the charity can stand behind), the timeline, how the donor will hear about results, and recognition if wanted. For a business partner: their goals, what the partnership involves, what they receive, the gift or sponsorship, and next steps. Commercial partners who say part of their sales go to a charity may need a written agreement and a solicitation statement in the UK; check this with local rules.
8. **Plan stewardship.** Thank within days, a personal note from the person who asked, a report at agreed points, an invitation to see results, and the anniversary of the gift. The next conversation should feel natural, not sudden.
9. **Log everything** in the donor database at the agreed level, with dates.

## Checks

- Every fact about a donor comes from the charity's records or the donor's own words.
- Every prospect has been thanked and told what past gifts did before an ask is planned.
- Every prospect has a dated next step and a named lead.
- The ask is one sentence with an amount the client chose.
- Impact figures in the proposal come from the project budget or results, not estimates.
- No tax claims about the gift; tax questions go to a qualified accountant.
- Proposals sent as binding, gift agreements and recognition commitments have the client's fresh yes.

## Output format

1. **Prospect summary table:** prospect (initials or code), years giving, last contact, interests in their words, readiness, next step, lead, date.
2. **Cultivation plan** per top prospect.
3. **Ask meeting prep sheet:** aim, facts, opening, the ask, questions and answers, follow-up note.
4. **Proposal or letter draft.**
5. **Stewardship calendar** for the next 12 months.

## Example of good output (short)

*Elmfield Hospice is a made-up charity, used only to show the format. The prospect and figures are invented.*

| Prospect | Giving | Last contact | Cares about (own words) | Next step | Lead | Date |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| D.R. | 9 years, monthly, plus December gifts | Card, Dec last year | "The garden made Mum's last weeks bearable" | Thank-you visit and garden update; no ask | Chief executive | 14 Jan |

**Note:** D.R. has never been told what the gifts paid for. Hold the ask. The visit and an update on the garden redesign come first.

**Ask (planned for spring, if the visit goes well):** "Would you consider a gift of £5,000 to rebuild the garden path so every bed can reach it?" (Path cost from the contractor's quote: £14,800. Amount chosen by the chief executive.)

**Honest answer ready:** "If we don't reach the full cost by June, we'll still build the first section, and we'll tell you exactly which part your gift paid for."
