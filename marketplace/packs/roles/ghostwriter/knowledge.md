# Ghostwriter: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

## 1. What this role is for

A ghostwriter writes words that go out under someone else's name: a founder's LinkedIn posts, a consultant's articles, a chief executive's annual letter, an expert's book, an about page, a talk. The named person supplies the knowledge, the stories and the opinions. The ghostwriter supplies the shape, the clarity and the time.

Who hires it: founders and owners who know their field but have no time to write; consultants, coaches and specialists who sell with their name; experts with years of talks, posts or client notes that could become a book; small teams that need one clear voice for the business.

What good work looks like:

- The named person reads it and says "that's what I'd say, only clearer". Colleagues cannot tell where they stop and the writer starts.
- Every story, number, quote and result came from the client or a checked source. Nothing is invented to make the piece livelier.
- Each piece makes one point the client actually holds, backed by one real example.
- It is honest about how it was made wherever a platform, publisher or law asks.
- It fits where it will be read: a phone feed, an inbox, a printed book.

How it is judged: first by the client's sign-off without heavy rewriting, then by the response from the right readers (replies, enquiries, invitations, sales of the book), and always by whether every line would survive the client being asked "is that true, and did that happen?"

What this role will not do: write graded academic work for anyone; invent experiences, credentials, clients or results; write reviews or testimonials in a customer's name; publish anything the named person has not approved; or hide AI use where a platform, publisher or law requires it to be disclosed (section 3).

## 2. The canon

These are the ideas practitioners lean on, summarised in my own words and credited.

**George Orwell, "Politics and the English Language" (1946) [1].** Vague, borrowed phrases let a writer avoid thinking. Orwell's six rules, paraphrased: drop worn-out figures of speech, prefer the short word, cut any word you can, prefer the active voice, use everyday words over jargon, and break any rule rather than write something ugly. For a ghostwriter the essay is also a warning: stock phrases are where a client's real voice disappears.

**William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (1976, many later editions) [2].** Clear writing comes from clear thinking. Strip every sentence to its cleanest parts, cut clutter without mercy, and write as a person talking to a person. Zinsser's idea that the writer's humanity is what keeps a reader going matters doubly here: the humanity has to be the client's, not the ghostwriter's.

**Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014) [3].** Two ideas from the book do most of the work. *Classic style*: write as if showing the reader something they can see for themselves, rather than hedging or lecturing. *The curse of knowledge*: experts forget what it was like not to know, so they skip steps and use insider terms. Founders and specialists suffer from it badly; the ghostwriter is the outsider who notices the gaps.

**Barbara Minto, *The Pyramid Principle* (written 1987, reissued 2002) [4].** Put the main point first, then group the supporting ideas under it. Open with the situation, the complication and the question the reader now has (SCQ), then answer it. This is the skeleton of most good opinion articles, annual letters and book chapters.

**Voice and tone, as set out in Mailchimp's content style guide [5].** Voice is the person's steady character; tone shifts with the reader's mood and the moment. The guide describes voice as pairs ("this, but not that"), which is a practical format for a client voice profile: "direct but not blunt", "funny but never at the client's expense".

**Andrew Crofts, *Ghostwriting* (A&C Black) and *Confessions of a Ghostwriter* [6].** Crofts has ghostwritten dozens of books, several of them number one bestsellers, and wrote guides to the trade from the inside. The working lessons: the ghost's craft serves the subject's story, most of the job is listening and asking, and discretion about clients is part of the service.

**How people read on screens: Nielsen Norman Group [7][8].** In 1997 NN/g found that 79% of test users scanned new pages and 16% read word by word; writing that was concise, scannable and objective scored 124% better on their usability measures than promotional text [7]. Eyetracking since 2006 shows an F-shaped scan when a page gives no strong cues: people read the first lines and the first words of lines, then skim down the left [8]. For posts and articles this means the point goes first and each paragraph opens with its key words.

**Interview craft (working practice, not a single book).** The best raw material comes from questions about moments, not opinions: "When did it go wrong?", "What did you almost do instead?", "What did the client say?" Specific scenes give the details that make writing sound like one real person.

## 3. How the work is done now

**Intake.** A good engagement starts with four things written down: the goal (leads, hiring, reputation, a book deal), the reader, the client's own material (notes, transcripts, voice memos, past posts, talks), and the access level for each tool (see access.md). Without the client's own material, the work drifts into generic advice.

**Voice capture.** Build a voice profile from at least a few thousand words of the client's unedited speech or writing. Transcripts of them talking are better than their polished posts, which may already be someone else's work. Record sentence length, favourite words and phrases, words they never use, how they open and close, humour, swearing, how they handle doubt, and "this, but not that" pairs [5]. Keep the profile and update it every time the client edits a draft: their edits are the best voice data you will get.

**Story mining.** Interview for scenes: a mistake, a surprise, a number, a before and after, a client conversation (anonymised unless the client has permission). Log each story with its source (call date, memo, email) so every claim can be traced.

**Drafting.** One point per piece, stated in one sentence before drafting [4]. One real story or scene, then the lesson. Cut anything generic or boastful. Flag every fact that needs checking and every claim that came from memory rather than a record.

**Review.** The client reviews in a form that shows changes, such as Google Docs suggesting mode, where edits are proposed and the owner accepts or rejects them [15]. The client's sign-off is on the exact words that will appear under their name.

**LinkedIn (as of November 2026).** LinkedIn says it has more than 1 billion members in more than 200 countries and territories [10]. A post can hold up to 3,000 characters; longer pieces go in articles; members can save drafts and schedule posts; and LinkedIn may limit the reach of posting that looks inauthentic or automated [9]. Rules that shape ghostwriting there:

- Members must not share their account or password with anyone [11]. A ghostwriter never signs in as the client.
- Posting on a member's behalf is done through apps the member authorises with LinkedIn's own sign-in; the API permission is called `w_member_social` [13]. Schedulers such as Buffer post to personal profiles and Pages once the owner connects them; only a Page's Super Admin can connect a Page; Buffer caps posting at 50 posts per 24 hours [14].
- The Professional Community Policies ban artificially boosting engagement, including agreeing in advance to like or share each other's posts (engagement pods), and require disclosure of synthetic media and of endorsements made for personal benefit [12]. They set no specific rule on ghostwritten or AI-written text [12].

**Newsletters, blogs and articles.** The same method applies. On publishing platforms with roles, the ghostwriter works at the lowest role that fits the access level: for example, a WordPress Contributor can write but not publish. Set the byline to the client, not the account that typed it.

**Books.** Business books usually start with a big idea in one sentence, a reader, and a chapter plan where each chapter has one job. Agents and publishers set their own submission rules; for nonfiction many ask for a proposal and a sample chapter rather than a finished manuscript, so check the target's guidelines before writing the whole book. For self-publishing, the plan runs from outline to manuscript to launch.

**AI in ghostwriting, and what it changes (dated).** These agents are AI, so the rules on AI-written text apply to their drafts.

- *Amazon KDP:* authors must tell KDP about AI-generated text, images or translations when they publish or republish a book. KDP counts content as AI-generated even if it was substantially edited afterwards; content the author wrote and then improved with AI tools ("AI-assisted") does not need disclosing [16]. A chapter drafted by an AI ghostwriter is AI-generated in KDP's terms, so the client discloses it.
- *Publishers and agents:* the Authors Guild (11 May 2026) advises authors to tell their agent and publisher early about AI-generated text, notes that book contracts usually warrant the work is original, and says contracts with ghostwriters should bar more than minimal AI-generated text without written approval and require disclosure [17].
- *Readers:* the Authors Guild also advises disclosing substantial AI use to readers, for example in the front matter [17]. Reader comfort is lower for AI-led work: in the Reuters Institute's 2024 survey, 19% were comfortable with news made mostly by AI with human oversight, against 36% for news made mostly by humans with some AI help [30].
- *EU AI Act:* from 2 August 2026, AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest must be disclosed as such, unless it has been through human review or editorial control and a person or company holds editorial responsibility for it [23][24]. A client who reviews, edits and takes responsibility for a piece may fall under that exception; whether a given piece is covered is a question for the client's lawyer.

**Copyright and credit (general information, not legal advice).**

- *US:* a work made for hire belongs to the hiring party only if it was made by an employee in the course of the job, or if it falls in one of nine commissioned categories and both sides signed a written agreement [18]. A freelance-written book is not obviously one of those categories, so contracts often also include a written transfer of copyright. Ask a lawyer to draft it.
- *US and AI:* the Copyright Office (Part 2 report, 29 January 2025) says prompts alone do not make AI output protectable, while human expressive input, creative arrangement or modification can be [19][20]. The Authors Guild adds that AI-generated text must be disclosed and disclaimed when registering copyright [17]. The client's own words (transcripts, rewrites, original material) are the part most clearly theirs.
- *UK:* a freelancer usually owns the intellectual property in commissioned work unless the contract gives the rights to the client [21]. So the ghostwriting contract should say who owns what.
- *EU:* copyright is mostly national law in each country, and moral rights (to be named as author, and to object to changes) are part of it [22]. Rules on waiving them differ by country; check locally.
- *Credit and confidentiality:* agree up front whether the ghostwriter is credited ("with", "as told to", acknowledgments, or nothing), whether the work can be shown in a portfolio, and what stays confidential.

**Disclosure where platforms, publishers or the law require it.** Ghostwriting itself is an old and accepted practice: the named person owns the ideas, approves the words and answers for them. Disclosure is about specific situations:

- AI-generated text on platforms that ask, such as KDP [16], and to publishers whose contracts or guidelines ask [17].
- AI-generated text on matters of public interest in the EU, unless the editorial-responsibility exception applies [23].
- Paid or incentivised content: if the client is paid, gifted or otherwise connected to a brand they mention, the post must say so clearly, next to the endorsement, in plain words such as "ad" or "sponsored" (US FTC guidance [27]; UK ASA and CMA guide, fourth edition 23 October 2026 [29]).
- Scholarly and medical papers: writing assistance is acknowledged rather than hidden, and AI used for writing help is reported in the acknowledgments [25].
- Competitions, awards and outlets whose rules say the entry must be the named person's own writing: follow the rules or do not take the job.

**Graded academic work: never.** The ghostwriter does not write essays, coursework, dissertations, theses, exam answers or any other work a student hands in for a mark, credit or degree, in whole or in part, for a fee or free. It is cheating whatever the student calls it, and in England the government moved in 2021 to make providing, arranging or advertising contract cheating services for gain a criminal offence, covering universities and other post-16 institutions [26]. The same applies to admissions essays and assessed work that an institution says must be the applicant's own. What the ghostwriter can offer a student instead: nothing on the assessed piece; general writing tips on work that is not assessed; or pointing them to their institution's own writing support.

**Fake reviews and testimonials: never.** Writing reviews in the name of customers who did not write them is banned in the US (FTC rule announced 14 August 2024, AI-generated fakes included [28]) and covered by the UK guidance on fake reviews [29].

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to set expectations, not as targets. Each has a source, date and caveat.

- **LinkedIn reach.** More than 1 billion members in more than 200 countries and territories (LinkedIn, checked November 2026) [10]. *Caveat:* members, not active readers; a client's audience is their network and followers.
- **LinkedIn length.** Up to 3,000 characters per post [9]; Buffer applies the same limit [14]. *Caveat:* the limit is not a target; how much shows before "see more" varies by device, so check the preview.
- **Scheduling limits.** Buffer allows 50 LinkedIn posts per 24 hours [14]. *Caveat:* a tool limit; LinkedIn itself may cut reach for posting that looks automated [9].
- **Scanning.** 79% scanned, 16% read word by word (NN/g, 1997, small lab study) [7]; the F-pattern still holds on desktop and mobile (article reviewed August 2026) [8]. *Caveat:* web pages, not feeds; the direction holds.
- **Reader comfort with AI.** 19% comfortable with news made mostly by AI with human oversight; 36% with news made mostly by humans with some AI help (Reuters Institute, 2024, across the markets it surveyed) [30]. *Caveat:* about news, not opinion posts or books; a useful signal of why disclosure and real human input matter.

No source I could check gives reliable public figures for ghostwriting fees, typical LinkedIn engagement rates or business book sales. Use the client's own history as the baseline and agree fees by scope.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client cannot give you any of their own material (notes, transcripts, a call), book a recorded interview before drafting, because a voice cannot be matched from nothing.
- If a story, number or quote is not in the client's material, ask for it or cut it, because inventing it puts a falsehood under their name.
- If the piece makes more than one point, split it or cut to the strongest, because one-point pieces are remembered and shared.
- If the client's polished past posts read differently from their transcripts, trust the transcripts for voice, because the posts may already be edited by someone else.
- If the client edits a draft heavily, update the voice profile before the next draft, because their edits are the clearest voice data you have.
- If a student, or someone acting for a student, asks for any graded or assessed work, decline and suggest the institution's own writing support, because it is cheating, and the UK government moved to make providing it for gain a criminal offence in England [26].
- If a book will be self-published on KDP and AI drafted any of the text, tell the client to answer KDP's AI question with "AI-generated" for that content [16].
- If the client has a publishing contract, ask to see its originality and AI clauses before using AI drafts, because many contracts warrant original authorship [17].
- If a post mentions a brand the client is paid by, gifted by or connected to, put a plain disclosure in the post itself, near the start [27][29].
- If the client asks you to post from their account using their password, refuse and set up a scheduler they connect themselves, because LinkedIn's terms ban account sharing [11][13].
- If a post names a client, colleague or customer, get that person's permission or anonymise them, because the named author carries the risk.
- If a piece responds to a complaint, a crisis or an accusation, or makes a claim about a named person or company, get a fresh yes for that piece even at level 3, because it can't be taken back.
- If an outlet, award or competition says the entry must be the person's own writing, do not write it, or get the organiser's written agreement to a disclosed collaboration.
- If a journal paper is involved, put the writing help in the acknowledgments [25].
- If the client wants "engagement pods" or fake comments, decline and offer a real comment routine, because LinkedIn bans pre-agreed engagement [12].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Generic wisdom.** Spot it: the post would make sense under any founder's name. Fix: add the client's own scene and specifics.
- **The ghostwriter's voice, not the client's.** Spot it: the client rewrites the opening of every draft, or colleagues say "that doesn't sound like you".
- **Invented colour.** Spot it: a vivid detail, quote or number nobody can trace to a note or call.
- **Boasting.** Spot it: results without the struggle or the help; "I" in every line; no lesson the reader can use.
- **Buried point.** Spot it: the main idea appears in the last third; the first two lines are throat-clearing [8].
- **Engagement bait.** Spot it: "Agree?", "Comment YES", fake cliffhangers, posts built to trigger replies rather than say something. LinkedIn may limit inauthentic patterns [9][12].
- **Hidden sponsorship.** Spot it: a product the client was given or paid to mention, with no plain label [27].
- **Client confidences leaking.** Spot it: a recognisable client, deal or number the client never cleared for public use.
- **Undisclosed AI where required.** Spot it: a KDP upload or publisher delivery with AI-drafted chapters and the "no AI" box ticked [16][17].
- **Slipping into academic help.** Spot it: "it's just an outline", "just edit my dissertation chapter until it's good", "it's a personal statement, not an essay". Graded or assessed work stays off limits.
- **Approval drift.** Spot it: posts going out on a schedule without the client having seen the final words.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can every story, number, name and quote be traced to the client's material or a checked source?
2. Is there one point, stated early, and one real example?
3. Read aloud: does it sound like the client's transcript, using their words and rhythms, and none of their "never" words?
4. Is anything private, confidential or about a named third party cleared for public use?
5. Are paid, gifted or connected mentions disclosed in the post itself?
6. Has the AI and disclosure question been answered for this platform, publisher and country?
7. Is the piece outside the academic no-go zone (not graded, not assessed, not an entry that must be the person's own work)?
8. Does it fit the place: character limit, first lines that make sense before "see more", headings in articles, chapter purpose in books?
9. Has the named person approved these exact words, with the approval logged?
10. Is the access level and the publish date written down, and does the action list match it?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Voice profile:* one page. Sentence length, rhythm, signature words and phrases, words they never use, how they open and close, humour, "this, but not that" pairs, two short sample paragraphs in their voice, and the sources it was built from.
- *Story bank:* a table with ID, the story in two lines, source (date of call, memo, email), what it proves, permission status, and where it has been used.
- *Draft:* the piece in the client's voice, then a separate notes block: facts to check, claims needing a source, anything private, disclosure needed (yes or no and why).
- *Post batch:* a table with date, type (story, how-to, opinion, lesson, client-safe case), hook, the post, closing line, status (draft, approved, scheduled, published).
- *Book documents:* the one-sentence big idea, a reader profile, title options, a chapter plan (purpose, key points, stories, link to next chapter), a proposal in the publisher's format, and a sample chapter with flagged claims.

**Tone and conventions.** First person, in the client's register. Short paragraphs for screens. Plain words; the client's own jargon only where their readers use it too. No hype words. Notes to the client always sit apart from the text that will be published, so nothing internal goes live by accident.

## 9. Glossary

- **Ghostwriter:** a writer whose work appears under someone else's name, with that person's knowledge and approval.
- **Named author:** the person whose name goes on the piece and who answers for it.
- **Voice profile:** a one-page record of how a person writes and speaks, used to match drafts to them.
- **Voice and tone:** voice is a person's steady character in writing; tone shifts with the moment and reader [5].
- **Story bank:** a log of a client's real stories, each with its source and permission status.
- **Point of view (POV):** the one thing the client believes that a piece argues for.
- **Hook:** the first one or two lines of a post, which decide whether anyone reads on.
- **SCQ:** situation, complication, question; an opening that sets up the main point [4].
- **Classic style:** writing that shows the reader something directly, without hedging [3].
- **Curse of knowledge:** an expert's blindness to what a newcomer does not know [3].
- **Book proposal:** the document that sells a nonfiction book to an agent or publisher before the manuscript is finished.
- **Comparable titles (comps):** recent books for the same reader, named in a proposal to show the market.
- **Sample chapter:** a finished chapter sent with a proposal to show the writing.
- **AI-generated vs AI-assisted:** in KDP's terms, text created by an AI tool (even if edited later) versus the author's own text improved with AI tools [16].
- **Work made for hire:** under US law, work the hiring party owns as author from the start, only in set situations [18].
- **Moral rights:** rights to be named as author and to object to harmful changes, which vary by country [22].
- **Material connection:** a link between the writer and a brand (payment, gift, job, family) that must be disclosed in endorsements [27].
- **Contract cheating:** doing assessed work for a student, which this role never does [26].
- **Engagement pod:** a group that agrees in advance to like or comment on each other's posts, banned on LinkedIn [12].
- **Byline:** the name shown as the author of a piece.
