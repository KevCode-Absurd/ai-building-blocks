# Book proposal and chapter plan

A business book is usually built from material the expert already has: years of posts, talks and client work. The job is to find the one idea that holds it together, prove it with their own stories, and package it the way agents and publishers ask. Minto's pyramid applies at book scale: one main idea, grouped supporting ideas, and an order that answers the reader's questions in turn.

## When to use

- An expert says "I should write a book" and has material but no plan.
- A self-published author needs a chapter plan before drafting.
- A traditional route needs a proposal and sample chapter for agents or publishers.
- A first chapter is needed from transcripts or posts.

## What you need

- The author's material: posts, talks, transcripts, client notes (anonymised), frameworks, slides.
- The reader: who they are, what they are stuck on, what they will pay for.
- The author's reason: clients, talks, credibility, legacy, income.
- The route: self-published (and on which platforms) or traditional (and which agents or publishers, with links to their submission guidelines).
- Comparable titles the author knows and respects. You check they exist; you never invent them.
- Any publishing contract already signed, especially originality and AI clauses.
- Access: level 1 to read material; level 2 to draft in their manuscript document. Sending anything to an agent or publisher needs a fresh yes each time.

## Steps

1. **Check the job.** Confirm the author, not someone else, holds the know-how and the stories, and that the book is not graded or assessed academic work (for example a dissertation turned into a book for a degree). If it is, stop.
2. **Inventory the material.** List every usable story, framework, case and data point with its source. Note which stories need client permission.
3. **Shape the big idea.** One sentence: who it is for, what changes for them, and how. Test it: Is it new to this reader? Is it useful on Monday morning? Can the author back it with their own cases? Write two or three versions and let the author pick.
4. **Test against the reader.** Write the reader's top five questions in their words. Every chapter must answer one.
5. **Title options.** Five to eight title and subtitle pairs: the promise in the subtitle, the hook in the title.
6. **Chapter plan.** Usually 10 to 14 chapters for a business book, but the material decides. For each: a one-line purpose, three to five key points, the stories and cases it uses (by ID), and how it leads to the next. Cut or merge any chapter without a clear job.
7. **Proposal (traditional route).** Follow the target's own guidelines. Common sections: overview with the big idea; the reader and market; comparable titles (named by the author, checked by you); the author's platform and credentials (facts only); chapter summaries; a marketing plan built on the author's real audience; and the sample chapter. Mark any figure the author must supply as [author to supply].
8. **Sample chapter.** Draft one chapter of 3,000 to 5,000 words from the author's own material, in their voice. Flag every claim needing a source and every story needing permission. Never fill gaps with invented anecdotes.
9. **AI and disclosure note.** Explain plainly:
   - If this draft goes into a book on Amazon KDP, KDP counts text an AI tool created as AI-generated even after heavy editing, and asks the author to disclose it when publishing.
   - If there is a publisher, the contract's originality and AI clauses decide what is allowed; the Authors Guild advises telling the agent and publisher early.
   - For copyright, the US Copyright Office says prompts alone do not make AI output protectable; the author's own words and real creative changes matter. Suggest the author rewrites key passages in their own words and keeps their transcripts.
10. **Ownership and credit.** List the questions for the author's lawyer: who owns the copyright (in the US, a freelance book is not obviously a work made for hire, so a written transfer is common; in the UK a freelancer usually keeps rights unless the contract says otherwise; in the EU it depends on the country), whether the ghostwriter is credited, and what stays confidential.
11. **Launch plan outline (optional).** A 90-day outline built on the author's existing audience: early readers, review copies (with honest review requests, never paid for positive reviews), talks, podcasts, email and content drawn from the book.

## Checks

- The big idea is one sentence the author would say out loud.
- Every chapter has one job and answers one reader question.
- Every story is the author's own, with a source ID and permission status.
- Comparable titles are real books the author named, checked to exist; no invented sales figures.
- The proposal follows the target's guidelines, not a generic template.
- The sample chapter flags claims and contains no invented anecdotes or quotes.
- The AI disclosure and ownership notes are included and say "ask a lawyer" for contracts.
- No graded or assessed academic work.

## Output format

1. **Material inventory:** table of ID, type, summary, source, permission.
2. **Big idea:** two or three versions, with the test results.
3. **Reader questions:** five, in the reader's words.
4. **Title options.**
5. **Chapter plan:** table of number, working title, purpose, key points, stories used, link to next.
6. **Proposal draft** (traditional route) or **manuscript plan** (self-published).
7. **Sample chapter** with a flags block.
8. **AI, disclosure and ownership notes.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Elm Street Hiring is a made-up consultancy used only to show the format. Its founder "Marcus", his 40 posts (P1 to P40) and the stories are invented for this example.*

**Big idea (chosen):** "Small firms hire badly because they write the job ad before they define the job; this book shows owners how to define it in an afternoon."

**Reader questions:** "Why do my good hires leave in six months?" / "Do I need a recruiter?" / "What do I put in the ad?" / "How do I interview without HR?" / "How do I know in the first month if it's working?"

**Chapter plan (extract)**

| # | Working title | Purpose | Stories used | Leads to |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The ad is the last step | Show why starting with the ad fails | P4 (the bakery that hired three managers in a year) | Defining outcomes first |
| 2 | Write the first 90 days | Define the job as results, not tasks | P11, P27 | Turning outcomes into a scorecard |
| 3 | The one-page scorecard | Give a tool the reader uses today | P19 (template from client work, anonymised) | Where to find people |

**Flags for chapter 1:** P4 names the bakery; Marcus to confirm it stays anonymous. The line "most small firms" needs either a source or "in my experience".
