# LinkedIn post batch

LinkedIn posts can run to 3,000 characters, members can save drafts and schedule posts, and LinkedIn may limit reach for posting that looks inauthentic or automated (LinkedIn Help, checked November 2026). Its policies ban pre-agreed engagement such as pods, and its User Agreement bans sharing your account. So a batch is built on the client's real material, approved by them, and posted through tools they connect themselves.

## When to use

- A monthly batch for a founder, consultant or specialist who sells with their name.
- A launch, hiring push or event where posts need to tell one story over several weeks.
- A company Page that needs posts in the founder's or team's voice.

## What you need

- The goal (leads, hiring, authority in a niche) and the buyer or reader.
- The voice profile (skill: voice-profile-from-samples).
- This month's raw material: a 20 to 30 minute voice memo or call answering "What happened this month? What surprised you? What did a client ask? What did you get wrong? What do you disagree with?" Plus any numbers they are happy to share.
- A "never" list: clients, deals, numbers and topics that stay private.
- Any brand relationships (paid, gifted, partner, investor).
- Access level for the scheduler and the account (see access.md). Level 1 if not stated.

## Steps

1. **Mine the material.** List every usable story, number, question and opinion with a source ID. Mark anything private or naming a third party.
2. **Plan the mix.** For twelve posts, a workable spread is: story, how-to, opinion, lesson, client-safe case, with no two of the same type in a row. Tie each to the goal and the reader.
3. **Write one point per post.** One sentence each, in the client's words.
4. **Write the hook.** One or two lines that make sense on their own before "see more". Lead with the scene, the surprising number, or the blunt claim. How many lines show varies by device, so check in the scheduler preview.
5. **Write the body.** Short lines and short paragraphs. One concrete example. A take the client would defend in a room of peers. Cut anything generic.
6. **Write the close.** A real question you want answered, or a clear point to end on. No "Agree?", no "Comment YES for the guide", no fake cliffhanger.
7. **Disclose.** If a post mentions a brand the client is paid by, gifted by or connected to, add a plain label such as "Ad" or "Sponsored" near the start; a platform toggle alone is not enough under FTC guidance, and the UK ASA and CMA guide asks for clear labels too.
8. **Clear private details.** Anonymise clients unless they gave permission. No screenshots of private messages.
9. **Build the approval table** (below). The client approves each post's exact text.
10. **Schedule (level 3 only).** For approved posts, schedule through the scheduler the client connected with LinkedIn's own sign-in (for example Buffer, which also caps LinkedIn at 50 posts per 24 hours). Before the first scheduled post, show the action list and limits and get a yes. Comments and direct messages go out from the client.
11. **Log and report.** Record what was scheduled, where, when and the result. If a post fails or a thread turns hostile, stop and ask.
12. **Review next month.** Ask the client for screenshots of post analytics and the conversations each post started. Keep what brought the right people; drop what did not.

## Checks

- Every story and number traces to a source ID; none invented.
- Each post makes one point and its hook stands alone.
- No engagement bait, no pods, no fake comments.
- Paid, gifted or connected mentions are labelled in the post.
- No private client details; named people have agreed.
- Within 3,000 characters.
- The approval column says "approved" with a date before anything is scheduled.
- The client's account was never signed in to by anyone else.

## Output format

1. **Batch plan:** table of number, date, type, point, source ID.
2. **Posts:** each with hook, body and close, plus character count.
3. **Approval table:** number, status (draft, approved, scheduled, published), approved by and date, notes.
4. **Notes:** private details removed, disclosures added, questions for the client.
5. **Log** (level 3 only): what, where, when, result.

## Example of good output (short)

*Northdale Food Safety is a made-up consultancy used only to show the format. Its founder "Rhona", the source IDs and the numbers are invented for this example.*

**Batch plan (extract)**

| # | Date | Type | Point | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 Nov | Story | The audit that failed on a label, not on hygiene | M2 (memo, 30 Oct) |
| 2 | 6 Nov | How-to | Three checks before a new supplier's first delivery | M5 |
| 3 | 11 Nov | Opinion | Small makers over-invest in certificates and under-invest in training | M7 |

**Post 1 (draft, 912 characters)**

> The cleanest kitchen I saw this year failed its audit.
>
> Not on hygiene. On a label.
>
> A jar of chutney had a supplier's old allergen sheet stuck to the shelf. The recipe had changed in March. Nobody had told the floor.
>
> We fixed it in an afternoon: one sheet per product, dated, signed by whoever changes the recipe.
>
> Most of the failures I see aren't dirt. They're paperwork that stopped matching the food.
>
> What's the one document in your kitchen nobody has checked since spring?

**Approval table (extract)**

| # | Status | Approved by and date | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Approved | Rhona, 1 Nov | Client not named (M2 says no) |
| 2 | Draft | | Check supplier example is generic |
| 3 | Draft | | Rhona to confirm she's happy to say this publicly |
