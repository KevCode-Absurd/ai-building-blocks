# Story interview to article

Most founders' first drafts fail the same way: a list of beliefs with no scene, written in borrowed phrases. Orwell's advice still applies: stock phrases are where thinking stops. This skill starts from the client's own moments and builds one clear point on one true story.

## When to use

- An opinion or thought leadership article (800 to 1,200 words).
- A founder story for an about page or pitch (400 to 700 words, plus a 100-word version).
- An about page that tells buyers who the client is and why to trust them.
- The opening of a talk, an annual letter, or a guest article for a trade outlet.

## What you need

- The client's notes, voice memos or transcripts on the topic. If thin, run the interview in step 2.
- The voice profile (skill: voice-profile-from-samples). If there is none, build a quick one from the same material.
- The goal and the reader, and where it will run.
- Any proof the client holds: numbers, dates, documents, client permissions.
- Access: level 1 to read material; level 2 to place the draft as suggestions in their document; level 3 only to publish the approved final, where you have it.

## Steps

1. **Check the job is in bounds.** Not graded or assessed academic work; not an award or competition entry that must be the person's own writing; not a review or testimonial in someone else's name. If it is, stop and say why.
2. **Interview for scenes, not opinions.** Ask one question at a time and follow up on anything vague with "Can you give me the exact moment?"
   - When did this go wrong, or nearly go wrong?
   - What did you almost do instead?
   - What did someone say that changed your mind? (Paraphrase unless they agree to be quoted.)
   - What do most people in your field get wrong about this, and how do you know?
   - What number or detail would a sceptic ask for?
3. **Log the material.** Every story gets an ID, two lines of summary, the source and date, and a permission status for anyone named.
4. **Find the one point.** Write it as one sentence the client would say out loud. If you cannot, ask which of two candidate points they would defend in a room of peers. Use Minto's frame to test it: situation, complication, the reader's question, the answer.
5. **Pick the scene.** The single story that proves the point best, with a moment, a detail and a turn. One scene beats three summaries.
6. **Plan the shape.** For an article: hook with the scene or a blunt claim, the point, two or three reasons each tied to a real example, the strongest objection answered, a close the reader can act on. For a founder story: the problem, the turn, what changed, the lesson, what it means for the reader now. For an about page: who you serve, why you do this, proof, how to start.
7. **Draft in their voice.** Use their words and rhythms from the profile. Put the point in the first two short paragraphs, because readers scan. Start paragraphs with their key words.
8. **Cut.** Remove anything generic, boastful or that any rival could sign. Remove hedges they would not use. Aim to cut about a tenth of the first draft.
9. **Flag.** In a notes block, list facts to check, claims needing a source, anything private, people named and their permission status, and disclosure needs (a paid or gifted brand link; AI-drafted text going to a platform or publisher that asks about AI).
10. **Make the short version.** A 100-word version or a social post that points to the piece.
11. **Hand over for approval.** Place the draft where the client reviews it, as suggestions if you have level 2. Their approval is on the final words.

## Checks

- One point, stated in the first two paragraphs.
- One real scene from the log, with its source ID.
- No invented events, quotes, titles, clients or numbers. If a detail is missing, it is marked [ask], not filled in.
- People named have agreed, or are anonymised.
- Voice: read it aloud against the voice profile; no word from the "never" list.
- Disclosure decided: paid or gifted links labelled in the piece; AI questions answered for the platform or publisher.
- Notes are separate from the text, so nothing internal is published.

## Output format

1. **Point:** one sentence.
2. **Scene used:** story ID and one-line summary.
3. **The piece:** title options (two or three), then the full draft.
4. **Short version:** about 100 words.
5. **Notes for the client:** facts to check, sources needed, private details, named people and permissions, disclosure.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a lettings agency, with its founder "Ama" and her story S3. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

**Point:** Small landlords lose more money to slow repairs than to empty months.

**Scene used:** S3, the boiler that failed on a Friday in January (voice memo, 14 Oct).

**Title options:** "The repair that cost more than the rent" / "Why I answer repair calls before sales calls"

**Opening (draft):**
"The call came at 4:40 on a Friday in January. A boiler had failed in a flat we managed, and the tenant had a newborn. The owner's plumber couldn't come until Monday.

We found someone that night. It cost more. The tenant renewed for two years.

I've managed rentals for eleven years [check: 11 or 12?], and I'd put it simply: the repairs you're slow on cost you more than the months a flat sits empty."

**Notes for the client:**
- Check years in business (memo says 11, website says 12).
- Tenant mentioned only as "a tenant with a newborn"; no name or address. Keep it that way unless they agree.
- No brand links, so no ad label needed.
- This draft was written with AI from your memo. If it goes into a book on KDP, that content is AI-generated in KDP's terms.
