# Voice profile from samples

A ghostwriter's first deliverable is not a post. It is a record of how the client actually sounds, built from evidence, that any later draft can be checked against. Mailchimp's style guide separates voice (a steady character) from tone (how it shifts with the moment); this profile captures the voice and notes the tones.

## When to use

- At the start of an engagement, before the first draft.
- When the client keeps rewriting the first lines of every draft.
- When the client's past posts read differently from how they talk, which often means someone else wrote them.
- When a second writer joins and needs to match the same voice.

## What you need

- At least a few thousand words of the client's unedited words. Best to worst:
  1. Transcripts of them talking about their work: a recorded interview, a podcast, a talk, a voice memo.
  2. Emails or chat messages they wrote quickly, to colleagues or clients (private details removed).
  3. Past posts and articles, but only ones they say they wrote themselves.
- Their goal and their reader (from intake).
- Any "never" list: words, topics or tones they dislike.
- Access: level 1 is enough. Transcripts and documents are read, not changed.

If there is nothing, book a 30 to 45 minute recorded conversation. Ask about a recent hard decision, a client win and a client loss, an opinion they hold that peers disagree with, and how they explain their job to a stranger. Get everyone's consent before recording.

## Steps

1. **Log the samples.** Give each an ID (T for transcript, E for email, P for post), date, length, and who wrote it. Set aside anything someone else drafted.
2. **Read everything once without notes.** Write down your first impressions: what is striking, what repeats, what they never do.
3. **Measure rhythm.** From three or four samples, note typical sentence length (short and punchy, long and winding, or mixed), paragraph length, and how often they ask questions. Use real counts from the samples, labelled as counts from these samples only.
4. **List signature language.** Words and phrases that recur, field terms they use naturally, the way they address the reader ("you", "we", "folks"), favourite verbs. Quote them with the sample ID.
5. **List the absences.** Words they never use, especially common business filler. These become the "never" list for drafts.
6. **Note structure habits.** How they open (a story, a blunt claim, a question), how they build an argument, how they close (a question, a single line, a call to act).
7. **Note stance and humour.** How confident they sound, how they handle doubt ("I might be wrong, but..."), whether they joke, about what, and what is off limits.
8. **Write "this, but not that" pairs.** Four to six pairs, each backed by a sample: "direct, but not harsh", "plain-spoken, but not folksy".
9. **Note tone by context.** How the voice shifts in a celebration, an apology, a teaching post, a disagreement.
10. **Write two sample paragraphs.** On a topic from their material, in their voice. Mark them as samples, not publishable pieces.
11. **Test it.** Show the client the profile and the samples. Ask: "Which lines sound like you? Which don't?" Fix the profile from their answers.
12. **Keep it alive.** After each round of client edits, add what you learned. Their edits are the best voice data you will ever get.

## Checks

- Every trait is backed by at least one sample ID. Nothing is guessed from the client's job title or industry.
- Samples written by someone else are excluded or labelled.
- Counts are real counts from named samples, not general claims about the client.
- The "never" list is specific (actual words), not "no jargon".
- The sample paragraphs use only facts from the client's material.
- Private details (names of clients, deals, numbers) are not copied into the profile.

## Output format

1. **Sources:** table of sample ID, type, date, length, written by.
2. **Summary:** three sentences on how this person sounds.
3. **Rhythm:** sentence and paragraph habits, with sample counts.
4. **Signature words and phrases:** with sample IDs.
5. **Never list.**
6. **Openings, arguments, closings.**
7. **Stance and humour.**
8. **This, but not that:** four to six pairs.
9. **Tone by context.**
10. **Two sample paragraphs.**
11. **Open questions** for the client.

## Example of good output (short)

*Harbour Lane Joinery is a made-up business used only to show the format. Its owner, "Dev", and the samples T1 to T3 and E1 to E6 are invented for this example.*

**Summary:** Dev talks like a tradesman explaining a job to a customer he respects: short sentences, concrete nouns, the odd dry joke, and no sales talk. He builds every point from a job he did. He ends with a practical next step, never a slogan.

**Rhythm (T1 to T3, E1 to E6):** most sentences in the transcripts run under 15 words; he often follows a long explanation with a two-word line ("That's it." "Measure twice."). Paragraphs in his emails are one to three sentences.

**Signature words:** "proper job" (T1, T3, E2), "on site" (T1, T2, E4), "honestly" as a softener before bad news (T2, E5). Calls customers "people", not "clients".

**Never list:** "solutions", "bespoke", "passion", "game", exclamation marks.

**This, but not that:**
- Blunt about mistakes, but never about the customer's choices (T2).
- Proud of the craft, but not boastful: credits the team by name (T3, E1).
- Funny about himself, never about the trade or rivals (T1).

**Sample paragraph (not for publishing):** "Last spring we fitted a staircase that looked perfect and creaked by June. Honestly, that one was on us. The timber came in damp and we didn't wait. Now we leave it on site for a week first. Costs us a week. Saves a callback."

**Open question:** the transcripts mention a "rule of three quotes" twice. Is that a rule he wants public, or internal only?
