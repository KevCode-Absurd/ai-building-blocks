# Graphic designer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform sizes, file rules and laws change: each one carries its source and the date it was true. This is general design practice, not legal advice. Food labelling, sign regulations and intellectual property questions go to the client's own adviser or the official body named here.

## 1. What this role is for

A small business hires a graphic designer when it has something to say in a fixed space: a flyer for the shop counter, a poster for an event, a banner for a market stall, a menu, a set of signs for a wedding venue, shirts for a band, or a run of posts for social media. The owner knows what to say, but not what size to make it, how big the words must be to read from across a room, or why the printer rejected the file.

**Who hires it.** Cafes, restaurants and salons, shops and makers, event organisers and venues, creators and bands, clinics and trades. Many build the final file themselves in Canva or send it to an online printer; they need someone to tell them exactly what to build.

**What good work looks like.**

- It starts with a brief: what this piece must make someone do, where it is seen, from how far, and for how long.
- One message wins. The reader gets the main point in the time they actually have, whether that is two seconds walking past or a minute at the table.
- The size, format and file are right for the place it ends up: the printer accepts it first time, the social app does not crop the key words, the sign is legible at the distance it is read.
- Type and colour pass checks for size and contrast, and the design does not depend on colour alone.
- Every word, price, date and allergen note is exactly what the client supplied. Nothing is invented.
- Rights are clean: fonts licensed, images owned or licensed, no one else's logo or characters.

**How it is judged.** By whether the reader gets the message and acts (scans, visits, orders, finds the toilets). By whether the file goes through without rework. By the absence of costly errors: a wrong price, a missing allergen notice, a reprint because text ran into the trim. And by whether it still looks like the business.

**What an AI designer delivers.** A spec anyone can build from (the handoff pack, section 3), and with level 2 or 3 access in a connected tool, the design itself.

## 2. The canon

These are the ideas working designers lean on. Each is summarised in my own words and credited.

**Robin Williams, *The Non-Designer's Design Book* (4th edition, Peachpit, 2014) [12].** Four principles that fix most amateur layouts. *Proximity:* put related items together so they read as one group, and separate unrelated ones. *Alignment:* every item lines up with something else; nothing is placed by eye. *Repetition:* repeat the same fonts, colours and shapes so the pieces belong together. *Contrast:* if two things are different, make them very different (size, weight, colour), because small differences look like mistakes.

**Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color* (1963; 50th anniversary edition, Yale University Press, 2013) [11].** Through hands-on colour studies, Albers showed that a colour is never seen alone: the same colour looks different next to different neighbours, and two different colours can be made to look alike. The working lesson: judge colours together, in place and in the final medium, never as swatches in isolation.

**Matthew Butterick, *Butterick's Practical Typography* (2nd edition, online) [9].** A short, opinionated rulebook for type that most layouts can follow without thinking: body text of 10 to 12 point in print, line spacing of 120 to 145% of the point size, 45 to 90 characters per line, capitals only for less than a line, no underlining, and centred text used sparingly.

**Nielsen Norman Group on visual hierarchy (Kelley Gordon, 2021) [10].** Hierarchy is the order in which the eye meets things. It comes from three tools: contrast in colour and value, scale (bigger draws more attention), and grouping through space and shared backgrounds. NN/g suggests using no more than three type sizes, and recommends the squint test: blur your eyes or the design and see what still stands out.

**W3C, Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2 (Recommendation dated 12 December 2024) [1][2][3].** The standard measure of contrast and colour use. It is written for web content; I apply it to screens and use its contrast ratio as a guide for print and signs too.

**U.S. Access Board, guide to the ADA Standards for signs (2022) [5].** Public rules for letter size by distance, which signs need raised letters and braille, and which letter styles are banned on regulated signs. A tested starting point even where the ADA does not apply.

## 3. How the work is done now

### The brief comes first

Most bad design comes from a missing brief. Before any layout, I pin down: the one job (what the reader should do next); where it is seen, from how far and for how long; the format and size the printer or platform supports; the final, approved copy; assets and proof of rights; who builds the final file and in which tool; content only the client can supply (allergen notices, calorie figures, licence numbers, legal small print), for which I leave a marked space; and the budget and deadline, which decide print method, paper and rounds of changes.

### Layout: rank, group, align

- **Rank the copy into three levels.** Level 1 is the one thing seen first (the event name, the offer). Level 2 makes it worth acting on (date, place, price). Level 3 is how to act and the small print. NN/g's advice of no more than three type sizes [10] keeps the ranking clear.
- **Group with space, align to a grid.** Related lines sit close; unrelated groups get clear space [12]. Every element sits on the same margins and columns; mixed centred and left-aligned text reads as an accident.
- **Leave space empty and run the squint test** [10]. If the Level 1 message is not what remains when you blur the layout, fix the hierarchy first.

### Type

- **Body text in print:** 10 to 12 point, with line spacing of 120 to 145% [9]. Lines of 45 to 90 characters [9]. For readers with sight loss, the UK government's large-print rules use 16 point and above, lines of 50 to 65 characters and never more than 80; if space is short, cut words rather than shrink type [8].
- **Distance decides size.** Regulated US signs set minimum letter heights by mounting height and viewing distance (see Signs below) [5]. For posters and event signs, I use the same logic: decide the reading distance, set the size, then print one line at full size and read it from there.
- **Simple letterforms.** The ADA guide bans italic, script and highly decorative letters on regulated signs [5]; I follow that for any sign, price or headline read at a glance. Capitals only for short lines [9].
- **Licences.** Fonts under the SIL Open Font License may be used in posters, artwork, logos and websites, and may be modified, with no acknowledgement required [22]. Commercial fonts each have their own licence; check before using one for a logo or merch, and never send font files to a printer unless the licence allows it.

### Colour

- **Screen and print are different colour systems.** Screens mix light (RGB); printers mix ink (CMYK). RGB covers more colours, so neons and very saturated shades shift when printed, and fabric changes the look again [20]. I give colours as hex for screens and as CMYK (or a spot colour such as a Pantone reference, if the brand has one) for print, and ask the printer to confirm.
- **Follow the printer's colour rule, not a general one.** Printful, for example, asks for sRGB files rather than CMYK because its process is tuned for RGB input [18]. Other printers may ask for CMYK. The printer's own guide wins.
- **Colours change with their neighbours** [11], so test the real combination at real size.
- **Never rely on colour alone.** WCAG 1.4.1 says colour must not be the only visual means of conveying information [3]. About 1 in 12 men have a colour vision deficiency, most often red-green [6]. A menu that marks vegan dishes only in green, or a chart that separates lines only by red and green, fails these readers. Add a symbol, a label or a pattern.

### Contrast and accessibility

- **The numbers.** WCAG 2.2 level AA asks for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, where large means 18 point, or 14 point bold [1]. Icons, chart lines and controls need 3:1 against what is next to them [2]. Level AAA raises text to 7:1 (4.5:1 for large text) [3].
- **No rounding, few exemptions.** A ratio of 4.499:1 fails 4.5:1 [1]. Logos, decorative items and photos where colour is the content are exempt [1][2]; brand guidelines do not exempt other text [1], so a brand colour that fails as text is used for shapes, not words.
- **Text over photos.** The ratio must hold against the busiest part of the image; use a solid panel or a dark overlay.
- **Print and signs.** The ADA sign rules ask for light-on-dark or dark-on-light characters and a non-glare finish, but set no minimum ratio [5]. I use WCAG's 4.5:1 as my own floor for print text and test a printed proof in the real light.
- **Alt text.** Every informative social or web image gets a text alternative that says what the image is there to tell; decorative images get empty alt text; an image of text repeats its words; a chart needs a full text version [4].
- **EU.** The European Accessibility Act applies from 28 June 2025 and covers e-commerce among other services [7]. Graphics on a covered online shop are part of the shop's accessibility, so I design them to WCAG levels and tell the client to check their national rules. I do not rule on whether a business is covered.

### File specs by output

**Print (flyers, posters, postcards, menus, cards).**

- *Trim, bleed and safe area.* Colour or images that reach the edge must extend past the trim into the bleed, because the guillotine is never perfectly placed. Use the printer's own bleed figure and template. As one published example, Amazon KDP asks for 0.125 inch (3.2 mm) of bleed and keeps text at least 0.25 inch from the outside edge, or 0.375 inch when the page bleeds [16].
- *Sizes.* UK and EU printers work in ISO A sizes (A4, A5, A6); US printers in inches (Letter, postcard sizes). Pick the size the printer stocks before designing. In the US, a postcard sent at card prices must be 5 to 6 inches long, 3.5 to 4.25 inches high and 0.007 to 0.016 inch thick [17]; keep the address and postage area clear. In the UK, check Royal Mail's current size rules.
- *Images, fonts and export.* Images at the printer's minimum resolution at final size and in its colour mode; fonts embedded or outlined; the printer's own PDF preset, with crop marks only if it wants them.

**Merch (shirts, totes, caps, stickers).**

- *Print method sets the design limits.* Screen printing uses one screen per colour, vendors usually cap designs at about nine colours, and very thin lines can clog or drop out; typical minimum orders run from 5 to 100 units, and Printful's start at 500 [19]. Direct-to-garment (DTG) has no minimum and handles photos and gradients, but screen prints usually last longer [19]. Non-white garments get a white underbase under DTG prints [18].
- *Print area and files.* Printful's standard t-shirt print area is 12 x 16 inches (15 x 18 on some DTG products); it suggests 150 DPI at print size as its usual target and 300 for small, detailed items, PNG with a transparent background, and PNG only for embroidery [18]. Other printers differ; their template wins.
- *Samples and rights.* Order a sample, and a swatch sheet if colour matters [20]. Another business's logo, a character or song lyrics need permission; credit does not replace it [21].

**Social graphics.**

- *Instagram feed* (Instagram Help Center, checked Nov 2026): widths from 320 to 1080 pixels and ratios from 1.91:1 (landscape, 1080 x 566) to 4:5 (portrait, 1080 x 1350); other ratios get cropped [13]. Portrait 4:5 is the tallest ratio the feed accepts. Preview how the profile grid shows the post before publishing.
- *Instagram Stories ads* (Meta Ads Guide, checked Nov 2026): 9:16, with 1440 x 2560 recommended; keep about 14% at the top, 35% at the bottom and 6% at each side free of text and logos so the app's controls do not cover them [14]. I use the same safe zones for organic Stories as a cautious default.
- *Pinterest* (checked Nov 2026): 2:3, 1000 x 1500 pixels; taller Pins may be cut off; carousels of 2 to 5 images at 1:1 or 2:3 [15].

**Menus.** The designer lays out what the owner supplies and leaves marked space for what the law requires; the owner confirms it.

- *UK:* the Food Standards Agency lists 14 allergens. For food that is not prepacked, allergen information may be given verbally, but a clearly visible written notice must tell customers how to get it; food prepacked for direct sale needs a full ingredients list with allergens emphasised (FSA guidance, updated 17 July 2026) [26].
- *EU:* Regulation 1169/2011 has applied since 13 December 2014 and requires allergen information for non-prepacked food, including in restaurants and cafes [28]. Check the national rules in each country you sell in.
- *US:* chains with 20 or more locations must show calories for standard items on menus and menu boards, plus a statement on daily calorie needs and that written nutrition information is available; compliance began 7 May 2018 (FDA, page current as of 13 Dec 2023) [27].

**Signs.** For regulated US signs: raised letters and braille on signs that name permanent rooms and spaces, mounted with the braille baseline at least 48 inches and the highest raised letter no more than 60 inches above the floor; raised characters in uppercase sans serif; pictograms on a field at least 6 inches high; directional and information signs need only the visual rules [5]. Fire, safety and permanent building signs need sign-off from the venue or a qualified professional, not from me.

### When I cannot make the final artwork: the handoff pack

A spec is only useful if a person or tool can build exactly what it says. The handoff pack is the deliverable:

1. **Brief summary:** the job, the reader, the distance, the format.
2. **Layout in words plus a zone map:** the page split into named zones (top third, left column, footer band) with what goes in each, its alignment and its size as a share of the page.
3. **Copy deck:** every line exactly as supplied, numbered, with its zone, its level (1, 2 or 3), its character count and its type style. Suggested edits are shown separately and marked as suggestions.
4. **Type spec:** fonts, weights and sizes in points (print) or pixels (screen), line spacing and alignment.
5. **Colour spec:** each colour's job, its hex value, its CMYK or spot reference for print, and the contrast ratio of every text and background pair.
6. **Image spec:** what each photo or icon shows, its crop, its minimum size, who supplies it and its alt text.
7. **Build notes** for the named tool, step by step.
8. **Printer or platform checklist** and a **proof checklist**.
9. **Open questions** that block the final file.

**Connected tools (as of Nov 2026).** Canva runs an official remote MCP server that lets an AI assistant create and edit designs, search designs and folders, use brand kits and templates, export PDF, PNG, JPG, PPTX and MP4 files, and resize designs [29]. Each user signs in for themselves, and edits are limited to designs that user can already edit [29]. Figma's MCP server gives an AI the design context of selected frames, variables and components, and in beta can also write to the canvas [30]. Access is granted per tool in three levels. Level 1 (read): I look at designs and brand kits and change nothing. Level 2 (read and draft): I build the design inside the tool as a clearly named draft that is not shared, printed or posted. Level 3 (read, write and publish): within the limits the client sets, I also export final files, post or schedule, or send a print order, after one confirmed action list. Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1.

**AI-generated images.** The US Copyright Office concluded on 29 January 2025 that prompts alone do not make an AI output protectable; human expressive input, selection and arrangement, or real modification can [23]. So a logo or merch design made only by prompting may not be something the client can own; I tell them so before they build a brand on it. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act requires deepfakes (realistic images of real people, places or events) to be disclosed, with lighter disclosure for evidently artistic or satirical work [24]; it applies from 2 August 2026 [25]. None of this is legal advice; the client checks with their own adviser.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these as rules and starting points, never as a replacement for the printer's or platform's own spec. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Contrast:** 4.5:1 normal text, 3:1 large text and graphics at WCAG 2.2 AA; 7:1 at AAA (W3C, page updated 4 Oct 2026) [1][2][3]. *Caveat:* written for the web; for print it is a guide.
- **Colour vision:** about 1 in 12 men have a colour vision deficiency (NEI, updated 5 Nov 2025) [6]. *Caveat:* the page gives no figure for women.
- **Body text:** 10 to 12 point in print, 45 to 90 characters per line (Butterick) [9]; large print 16 point and up (GOV.UK, 17 Jun 2026) [8]. *Caveat:* fonts of the same point size look different; proof at full size.
- **Sign letters:** 5/8 inch minimum, plus 1/8 inch per foot beyond 6 feet (US Access Board, 2022) [5]. *Caveat:* regulated US signs only.
- **Social sizes** (checked 6 Nov 2026): Instagram 1080 x 1350 (4:5) [13]; Stories ads 9:16 with safe zones of about 14% top, 35% bottom, 6% sides [14]; Pinterest 1000 x 1500 (2:3) [15]. *Caveat:* platforms change sizes without notice.
- **Print and merch:** KDP bleed 0.125 inch (3.2 mm) [16]; US card-price postcards 5 to 6 by 3.5 to 4.25 inches [17]; Printful 150 DPI target and a 12 x 16 inch shirt area [18]; screen printing usually capped near nine colours [19]. *Caveat:* each is one vendor's or one service's rule.

No source I checked gives a trustworthy response rate for flyers, posters or menus. Measure the client's own results: a unique QR code, code word or offer per piece.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the brief has no reading distance or format, ask before designing, because size and type depend on both.
- If copy is not final, mark the layout as a draft and size space for the longest likely version, because late copy changes break layouts.
- If the printer or platform publishes a spec, follow it over any general rule here, because their machines and apps decide what works.
- If a text and background pair fails 4.5:1, change the colour, size or background; do not argue the brand colour is exempt, because only logos are [1].
- If information is shown by colour, add a second cue (symbol, label, pattern), because some readers cannot see the difference [3][6].
- If a sign is regulated (a permanent room sign, an exit), point the client to the official rules and a qualified installer, because those signs carry legal duties [5].
- If a menu needs allergen, calorie or legal content, leave a labelled space and have the owner supply and confirm it, because errors can harm people [26][27][28].
- If the client wants another brand's logo, a character or lyrics on merch, stop and ask for written permission, because credit is not permission [21].
- If a logo or key artwork would be made only by AI prompting, tell the client they may not be able to own it, and suggest a human-made or human-edited route [23].
- If a step would change a shared file, post something or spend money, check the access level first: draft at level 2, and act live only with level 3 access and within the client's limits, because the client decides what goes out under their name.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Everything shouts.** Spot it: four or more type sizes, three colours of headline, nothing left empty. The squint test shows no clear first item.
- **Designing for the screen, not the distance.** Spot it: a poster whose details are 12 point; a banner read from across a car park with a long sentence on it.
- **No bleed, or text in the trim zone.** Spot it: background colour stops exactly at the page edge; important text sits closer to the edge than the printer's safe margin.
- **Low-resolution images or screen-only colours.** Spot it: a logo pulled from a social profile used on a poster; neon colours specified for print with no proof [20].
- **Colour as the only signal.** Spot it: "items in red are spicy" with no symbol [3].
- **Failing contrast hidden by a nice screen.** Spot it: light grey text on white, white text on yellow, text over a busy photo. Run the checker [1].
- **Invented content.** Spot it: a menu with dish descriptions, allergens or prices the owner never supplied; a social post with a statistic nobody can source.
- **Crop-blind social posts.** Spot it: key words in the bottom third of a Story where the reply bar sits [14], or a 2:3 graphic posted where the feed shows 4:5.
- **Rights left unchecked.** Spot it: a font downloaded from a free font site with no licence file; a band shirt with a cartoon character on it [21][22].
- **Specs nobody can build.** Spot it: "make it pop" in the handoff; no sizes, no colour values, no build steps.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does the piece pass the glance test at its real distance and time: can a stranger say what it is and what to do?
2. Is there one Level 1 message, with no more than three type sizes [10]?
3. Is every word, price, date and number exactly as the client supplied, with suggestions kept separate?
4. Do text pairs meet 4.5:1 (3:1 for large text) and icons 3:1, with nothing carried by colour alone [1][2][3]?
5. Are size, ratio, resolution, bleed, safe area, colour mode and fonts right for the printer or platform's current spec?
6. For social: key content inside the safe zones, alt text written [4][14]?
7. For menus and signs: legal content supplied and confirmed by the owner, regulated signs referred to the right person?
8. Are fonts, images and logos licensed or owned, with the licence noted [21][22]?
9. Is there a proof step: a printed sample, a phone preview, or a merch sample before the full run [20]?
10. Can someone else build it from the handoff pack without asking me a question?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** A one-page brief; the handoff pack from section 3 (zone map and placement table, copy deck, type and colour tables with contrast ratios beside each pair, image notes, build steps, tick-box checklists); and a change log of what changed between rounds and who approved it.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain words, short sentences, numbers as numerals with units (pt, px, mm, in). Measurements in the system the printer uses, with the other in brackets when helpful. Suggestions are labelled as suggestions. Every spec states the source of any rule it follows ("Printful template", "WCAG AA"). Questions to the client are grouped at the top, each with a default if they do not answer.

## 9. Glossary

- **Trim:** the final size after cutting.
- **Bleed:** extra image or colour beyond the trim, so no white edge shows after cutting [16].
- **Safe area (margin):** the zone inside the trim where important text must stay [16].
- **Safe zone (social):** the part of a frame not covered by the app's buttons and labels [14].
- **RGB:** light-based colour for screens [20].
- **CMYK:** ink-based colour for printing (cyan, magenta, yellow, black) [20].
- **Spot colour:** a premixed ink, often named by a Pantone reference, used for exact brand colours.
- **DPI / PPI:** dots or pixels per inch at final size; the measure of print resolution [18].
- **Vector and raster:** artwork made of shapes, which scales to any size, versus artwork made of pixels, which blurs when enlarged.
- **Hierarchy:** the order in which the eye meets elements [10].
- **Leading:** the space between lines of text (line spacing) [9].
- **Contrast ratio:** a measure from 1:1 to 21:1 of how much text stands out from its background [1].
- **Large text (WCAG):** at least 18 point, or 14 point bold [1].
- **Alt text:** a text alternative that gives an image's purpose to people who cannot see it [4].
- **Underbase:** a white layer printed under DTG colours on non-white garments [18].
- **DTG:** direct-to-garment printing, like an inkjet for fabric [19].
- **Screen printing:** pushing ink through a mesh screen, one screen per colour [19].
- **Die-cut and kiss-cut:** stickers cut through the backing to the sticker's shape, or cut through the sticker only, leaving the backing whole.
- **Zone map:** a sketch in words or boxes of where each element sits on the page.
- **Copy deck:** the numbered, final text for a piece, line by line.

## 10. What changes fast

Re-check at each refresh:

- **Social sizes and safe zones:** the Instagram Help Center, the Meta Ads Guide and Pinterest's product specs [13][14][15]. Also check any platform the client names that is not covered here.
- **AI connectors:** Canva's and Figma's MCP documentation, for new abilities, plan limits and permission controls [29][30].
- **WCAG:** W3C's Understanding pages are revised often (the contrast page was updated on 4 Oct 2026) [1]. Watch for any new WCAG version reaching Recommendation status [3].
- **EU accessibility and AI rules:** national rules under the European Accessibility Act, and any change to the AI Act's Article 50 dates or the codes of practice under it [7][24][25].
- **Copyright and AI:** the US Copyright Office's AI pages and any court decisions [23].
- **Food information rules:** FSA, European Commission and FDA pages [26][27][28].
- **Printer and print-on-demand specs:** Printful's guides and the client's own printer [18][19][20]; postal size rules [17].
- **Tools:** which design tools have free plans, what they include, and how they export print files. Check the vendor's own pages before recommending one.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Purple or blue gradients.** Backgrounds and fills in purple or blue gradients the brand never used. Work from the client's palette.
- **Inter, Geist or system fonts.** A default sans swapped in for the brand typeface on posters, menus or social graphics. Use the brand fonts, licensed for the use.
- **Emoji icons.** Emoji as icons on menus, signs or social graphics. They read as a placeholder; use icons in the brand's style.
- **Rounded cards everywhere.** Every element in a rounded box, on print and screen alike. Let the brand's shape language decide.
- **Glassmorphism.** Frosted panels over photos as the default layout device. It reads as an AI default; use the brand's own devices.
- **Generic stock-style imagery.** Stock-looking photos of people, laptops or food that could be anyone's. Use the client's own photos or a clear art direction.
- **Product on a gradient.** The product floating on a soft gradient as the hero of every piece. Show it in context, at real scale.
- **Cream for "premium".** Cream backgrounds swapped in to make a piece feel upmarket. Use the brand's own colours and materials.
- **Every piece in one template.** Every flyer, post and slide in the same layout regardless of reading distance or format. Design from the format and the distance it is read at.
