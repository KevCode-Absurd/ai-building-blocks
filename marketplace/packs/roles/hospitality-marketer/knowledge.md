# Hospitality marketing manager: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Google, booking sites and regulators change features and rules often, so every platform fact below says when it was true. Basics shared with other kits are not repeated here: Business Profile rules, categories, verification and the honest review-asking system are in the local marketing specialist kit; platform posting and short video in the social media manager kit; list building, consent and deliverability in the email marketing specialist kit; privacy-safe replies in the front desk coordinator kit.

## 1. What this role is for

Independent restaurants, cafés, bakeries and bars, breweries, wineries and distilleries with a taproom or cellar door, small hotels, inns and B&Bs, and event venues hire this role to fill seats, rooms and events on thin margins. The owner usually works a shift as well as running the place.

What good work looks like:

- **The words match the place.** Menu lines, tasting notes and posts describe what is actually served, in the owner's voice.
- **Listings are right everywhere.** Hours, menu, prices, booking links and photos agree on Google, booking sites, delivery apps and the website.
- **Prices are honest from the first look.** Mandatory fees and service charges are in the price people first see, where the law says so.
- **Quiet times get offers that make money.** Every offer has a food or drink cost check, a day and time, and an end date.
- **Reviews get specific replies,** and the hard ones are handled with the owner.
- **Allergen and alcohol rules are respected.** No allergen or health claim the kitchen or maker cannot stand behind.

How it is judged: covers or bookings on target days, rooms booked direct against through booking sites, event and taproom attendance, club or list sign-ups, review rating and reply rate, and margin after offers and commissions. Likes are not the measure.

## 2. The canon

**Reviews move revenue for independents.** Michael Luca matched Yelp ratings to restaurant revenue and found a one-star rise led to a 5 to 9% revenue increase, driven by independent restaurants; chain restaurants were unaffected (Harvard Business School working paper, 2011, revised 2016) [28]. Independents depend most on what reviews say, so reply quality and honest review habits matter more for them than for chains.

**Reputation and room rates.** Chris Anderson at Cornell's Center for Hospitality Research found that a hotel could raise its price by about 11.2% for a one-point higher review score on a five-point scale and keep the same occupancy, and that a 1% higher online reputation score went with up to 0.89% higher average daily rate, 0.54% higher occupancy and 1.42% higher revenue per available room (2012) [29]. Old data: treat it as direction, not a current figure. Reputation is part of the price.

**The platforms' own rulebooks.** Google's help pages for restaurants and hotels [1][2][3][4], its hotel price accuracy policy [6] and its fake engagement policy [8], and Booking.com's description of how it ranks and moderates [7], are the rules of play. A marketer who knows them better than the owner avoids lost listings and suspended links.

**The regulators.** The FTC's fake reviews rule and fees rule [10][13], the CMA's fake reviews and price transparency guidance [11][14][15], EU consumer rules [12][17], food allergen law (FSA, FDA, EU) [19][20][21], and alcohol advertising codes (CAP, TTB, Portman Group) [24][25][26] turn "be honest" into duties.

**Further reading (credited, no claims drawn from them here):** Danny Meyer, *Setting the Table*; Will Guidara, *Unreasonable Hospitality*. Both are by restaurateurs and are widely read in the trade for how hospitality itself becomes the marketing.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 Restaurants on Google (as of November 2026)

Google's restaurant guide lists what a Business Profile can carry [1]:

- **Menus.** The menu editor builds a structured menu (sections, items, descriptions, prices) shown under "Menu" on Search and Maps. A menu link to the website, and menu photos or a PDF, are alternatives. Google can turn a menu photo or PDF into a structured menu with AI (marked experimental, needing Google's generative AI terms), and may copy menu data from the website; owners can edit, delete or opt out. Changes can take 24 to 48 hours to show. Several profiles need the Business Profile API to update menus together [2].
- **Ordering.** "Order with Google" and a choice of preferred online order provider [1].
- **Bookings and waitlists.** Reserve with Google connects a booking provider; providers appear on the profile within a week, and the provider may charge. Waitlists are for restaurants only and need a Reserve with Google provider. A custom booking link is an alternative, but performance data comes only through a Reserve with Google provider [3].
- **Posts, photos, review replies and insights,** including menu clicks and bookings [1].

The practical rule: one source of truth for the menu. Decide whether Google shows the editor menu or the website menu, keep that one current, and remove stale menu photos (customers' outdated photos can be flagged) [2].

### 3.2 Hotels on Google

- **Prices and booking links** on a hotel's profile come from Google Hotel Ads and free booking links, not from the owner's dashboard; the hotel class is set by Google; hotels cannot edit opening hours (open 24 hours); verified owners can edit amenities [4].
- **Free booking links** show a hotel's or booking site's name and rate for the guest's dates. They are free, with no charge per click, and bids do not affect their ranking. Hotels join through Hotel Center or ask their booking system (connectivity partner) to switch them on. Ranking signals include the history of price accuracy [5].
- **Price accuracy policy.** The total on the booking page must match the total shown on Google, for the dates, room and guests chosen, and must include all mandatory charges, including resort fees. Optional extras must not be included by default. Repeated mismatches move the score to At Risk or Failed, which turns off ads and free booking links; persistent problems can suspend a hotel or account [6].

### 3.3 Booking sites and direct bookings

Booking.com's own description (version of 31 May 2025; a new version applies from 10 December 2026) [7]:

- It earns commission from properties, charged after the stay. Properties in its Preferred Partner Programme pay a higher commission; listings marked "Ad" paid for placement.
- The default sort weighs click-through, bookings and cancellations, and also review scores, price, policies, photo quality, commission paid and Genius or Preferred status.
- Only guests who booked and stayed or arrived can review; reviews are accepted within three months of checkout and may stop showing after 36 months; scores weight recent reviews; properties may reply.

Other booking sites (Expedia, Airbnb and others) set their own commission, ranking and review rules in their partner help pages; read them before advising. For direct bookings, a booking engine on the hotel's own site, connected to a channel manager, keeps rates and availability in step across sites. The marketing job is to give guests a reason to book direct (a perk that costs less than the commission, flexible terms, a better room choice) without breaking any rate agreement the hotel has signed; ask the owner what their contracts say.

### 3.4 Menus, allergens and calories

**Writing menu lines.** Name the main ingredient, the cooking method and one taste or texture word. Keep lines short enough for a board and a phone. Use the kitchen's words. No "best in town", no health claims.

**Allergens, UK (FSA guidance, updated 17 July 2026) [19].** Fourteen allergens: celery, cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, lupin, milk, molluscs, mustard, peanuts, sesame, soybeans, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above ten parts per million) and tree nuts. For food that is not prepacked (restaurants, cafés, takeaways), allergen information must be available for every item that contains one; it can be written (menu, chalkboard, information pack) or given verbally if a clearly visible written notice explains how to get it. The FSA says written information backed by a conversation works best. Food prepacked for direct sale needs a full ingredients label with allergens emphasised. Online and phone orders need allergen information before purchase and at delivery, ideally in writing both times. "Free-from" claims need strict controls, and gluten-free claims are not made where cross-contamination cannot be ruled out.

**Allergens, US (FDA, current 11 March 2026) [20].** Nine major allergens: milk, eggs, fish, crustacean shellfish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans and sesame (added by the FASTER Act, effective 1 January 2023). Federal allergen labelling covers packaged foods; it does not apply to food placed in a wrapper or container after the customer orders, and FDA enforcement excludes most unpackaged restaurant food. State and local food codes set restaurant duties; check the local health department.

**Allergens, EU [21].** EU food information law has required allergen information for non-prepacked food, including in restaurants and cafés, since 13 December 2014. Check the member state's rules on how it must be given.

**Calories.** US restaurant chains with 20 or more locations must show calories on menus and boards, with written nutrition information on request (compliance from 7 May 2018) [22]. In England, calorie labelling rules for the out-of-home sector came into force on 6 April 2022 [23]; check the guidance to see whether the business is covered.

The marketer's rule: allergen and calorie information comes from the kitchen's records, never from the marketer's guess. Where none is supplied, the copy says "please ask our team about allergens".

### 3.5 Prices, fees and service charges

**US.** The FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees applies from 12 May 2025 to short-term lodging (hotels, motels, inns, vacation rentals, and the platforms and travel agents that sell them) and live-event tickets. The total price, including all mandatory fees such as resort fees, must be shown upfront and more prominently than other prices; only government charges, shipping and truly optional add-ons may be left out; any ad that shows a price must show the total [13]. The FTC's guidance does not mention restaurants. California's honest pricing law (from 1 July 2024) lets restaurants and bars add a mandatory fee only if it is clearly shown wherever prices appear; voluntary tips are unaffected [16]. Other states differ; check the state attorney general.

**UK.** Under the CMA's price transparency guidance (first published 18 November 2025) the price in any invitation to buy, including adverts, emails, social posts and web pages, must include mandatory charges. The CMA lists mandatory cover or service charges at a restaurant, routine cleaning fees, booking fees, and resort fees and local taxes payable at hotels among them; refundable card holds at hotel check-in are not mandatory charges [15]. Optional charges must be disclosed but may be shown separately; "from" prices must include mandatory charges; amounts paid on arrival are shown separately from amounts paid now [14]. Breaches can bring fines of up to 10% of turnover or £300,000, whichever is greater [14]. Since 1 October 2024 all tips, gratuities and service charges must reach workers without deductions, under a statutory code [18]; marketing copy must not suggest otherwise.

**EU.** Consumers must be told the total price including all taxes and additional charges, and paid extras need an explicit opt-in, not a pre-ticked box [17].

This is general practice, not legal advice. Pricing questions go to the owner's adviser.

### 3.6 Reviews

**Asking.** Google allows asking for genuine reviews by link or QR code but bans incentives (discounts, free items) for reviews or for changing or removing them, asking only happy guests, discouraging negative reviews, staff review quotas, asking for particular content such as a staff name, pressing guests to review on the premises, and reviews from staff or family [8][9]. Booking.com reviews come only from guests who stayed [7].

**The law.** US: the FTC's rule (announced 14 August 2024) bans fake or AI-written reviews, rewards tied to positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites and suppressing reviews with threats; knowing violators face civil penalties [10]. UK: fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews and misleading review information are banned, and businesses that publish reviews need a policy, a risk assessment and checks (CMA, 4 April 2025) [11]. EU: traders publishing reviews must say how they check reviews are genuine and must not alter them [12].

**Replying.** Google advises short, specific, polite replies with no promotions, no private details and no personal attacks; acknowledge real mistakes, apologise where right, and move complex issues to phone or email [9]. Replies to negative reviews need the owner's fresh yes every time, even at level 3, because they are public, permanent in effect, and may touch refunds, safety or legal claims. Positive-review replies can run within agreed limits.

### 3.7 Drinks makers, bars and alcohol promotions

- **UK advertising (CAP Code section 18) [24].** Alcohol ads must not appeal particularly to under-18s or run in media where more than 25% of the audience is under 18. Anyone shown drinking or playing a significant role must be, and look, 25 or over. No links to social or sexual success, daring or irresponsible behaviour, or driving; drinking at work only in exceptional cases; no health, therapeutic, fitness or weight claims (only "low-alcohol", "reduced alcohol" and "reduced energy" are allowed); strength may be stated as fact but not emphasised; promotions must not encourage excessive drinking.
- **UK packaging, naming and promotions (Portman Group) [26].** The Code of Practice on the Naming, Packaging and Promotion of Alcoholic Drinks (sixth edition) covers naming, packaging, point of sale and merchandising, plus a separate sponsorship code, for alcohol marketing aimed at the UK not already regulated by the ASA or Ofcom.
- **UK licensed premises [27].** Mandatory licensing conditions in England and Wales include a ban on irresponsible promotions, free drinking water, smaller measures available, an age verification policy and no sales below duty plus VAT. Happy-hour and bottomless deals are checked against these with the licence holder.
- **US (TTB) [25].** For alcohol producers and wholesalers, menus, wine lists, shelf talkers, websites, social posts and paid influencer posts all count as advertising. Ads must carry the responsible advertiser's name and city and state or other contact details, and the class or type; spirits ads also give alcohol content. "Clean" paired with health language can be a misleading health claim. Naming retailers in a producer's posts can raise tied-house issues. State rules apply on top.
- **EU.** Alcohol advertising rules are national; check the member state.

### 3.8 Quiet nights, offers and events

Every venue has a weekly and yearly rhythm. Map covers or bookings by day and hour, then give each quiet slot a reason to come: a set menu, a quiz, a tasting, a supper club, a local partner night, a taproom release. Check the food or drink cost of every offer before writing it, prefer adding value (a paired glass, a dessert) to cutting price, and avoid offers that fill seats already full or teach regulars to wait for deals. State the deal, day, time and end date exactly as the owner gave them. Events work back from the date: announce, remind, last call, day-of, thank-you.

### 3.9 Working in the client's tools

Agents in this role work in Business Profile, booking systems, booking-site extranets, email tools and social accounts once the owner grants access: level 1 (read), level 2 (read and draft) or level 3 (read, write and publish within set limits). Even at level 3, a fresh yes is needed every time for replies to negative reviews, formal complaint replies, event or group contracts sent as binding, deleting data, and anything naming a new recipient for money. Payouts, refunds above the owner's limit and bank details on booking and delivery platforms stay with the owner. access.md gives the details.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these for judgement, not as targets.

- **Reviews and restaurant revenue:** one Yelp star associated with 5 to 9% more revenue for independents, none for chains [28]. *Caveat:* restaurants only, one review platform, a 2011 paper revised 2016.
- **Reviews and hotel pricing:** about 11.2% price headroom per review-score point; up to 0.89% ADR, 0.54% occupancy and 1.42% RevPAR per 1% reputation score [29]. *Caveat:* 2012 data from Travelocity, ReviewPro and STR; association, not proof of cause.
- **Platform timings:** menu edits 24 to 48 hours to appear [2]; Reserve with Google providers within a week [3]; Booking.com reviews accepted within three months of checkout and may stop showing after 36 months [7].
- **Rule thresholds:** US calorie labelling at 20 or more locations [22]; UK alcohol ads limited where over 25% of the audience is under 18, and people shown drinking must be 25 or over [24]; UK price breaches can bring fines of up to 10% of turnover or £300,000, whichever is greater [14].

No trustworthy cross-industry figures exist that I could check for "good" cover counts, direct-booking share or review reply rates. Use the venue's own last 12 months and the same week last year as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a menu item's allergens are not supplied by the kitchen, write "please ask our team about allergens" and do not guess [19][20].
- If a dish might be called "gluten-free" or "nut-free", check the kitchen can rule out cross-contamination; if not, drop the claim [19].
- If a restaurant adds a mandatory service or cover charge, put it in the price people first see in the UK, and in California show it clearly wherever prices appear [15][16].
- If a hotel has a resort, cleaning or other mandatory fee, show the total from the first price in US and UK marketing and on Google, because the FTC rule, the CMA guidance and Google's price policy all require it [6][13][15].
- If the Google price and the booking page price can differ, fix the feed before running hotel ads or free booking links, because Failed accuracy turns them off [6].
- If an offer's food or drink cost leaves no margin, change it (add value, move it to a quieter day) or drop it.
- If a review is negative, draft a calm, specific reply and wait for the owner's fresh yes before posting.
- If a review may break platform policy (fake, conflict of interest, off-topic), report it; never ask for removal just because it is negative [8].
- If the owner wants to reward reviews or ask only happy guests, say no and offer the honest version [8][10][11].
- If a drinks post shows people drinking, check they are and look 25 or over for UK audiences, and keep strength and health out of the message [24].
- If a happy hour or bottomless deal is planned at UK licensed premises, check it against the mandatory conditions with the licence holder [27].
- If a US producer's post names bars or shops that stock the drink, check the TTB guidance on listing retailers first [25].
- If the menu changes, update every listing the same day: website, Google menu, booking sites, delivery apps and printed menus.
- If a booking site's terms limit rates or perks shown elsewhere, follow the contract; ask the owner before promoting a direct-booking perk.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Two menus on Google.** Spot it: an old photographed menu and a current menu link showing different prices [2].
- **Guessed allergen lines.** Spot it: "may contain nuts" added by marketing, not the kitchen's matrix [19].
- **Service charge in small print.** Spot it: "12.5% service charge" only at the bottom of the menu while the headline prices omit a mandatory charge [15].
- **Resort fee shown at checkout.** Spot it: a room advertised at one price, with a daily fee added on the payment page [6][13].
- **Discounts that fill full nights.** Spot it: a two-for-one on Friday, the busiest night.
- **Generic review replies.** Spot it: the same "Thanks for your feedback!" under every review [9].
- **Arguing in public.** Spot it: a reply that disputes the guest's account or reveals booking details [9].
- **Rewarded reviews.** Spot it: "free dessert for a five-star review" on a table card [8].
- **Health words on drinks.** Spot it: "clean", "guilt-free" or "good for you" in a cider post [24][25].
- **Under-25s drinking in photos.** Spot it: a young-looking staff member holding a pint in an ad [24].
- **Hours out of step.** Spot it: holiday closures missing on Google and on booking sites.
- **Unmeasured offers.** Spot it: no count of covers, redemptions or bookings for the offer night.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does every menu line, tasting note and claim match what the kitchen or maker supplied?
2. Is allergen information from the kitchen's records, with the right fallback line where it is missing [19][20][21]?
3. Do prices include mandatory fees and charges where the law or platform requires [6][13][15][16][17]?
4. Does every offer have a cost check, day, time, end date and one measure?
5. Are review asks open to every guest, with no reward and no filter [8]?
6. Are review replies specific, private details removed, and negative ones approved by the owner?
7. Does alcohol copy meet CAP, Portman Group, licensing or TTB rules for where it runs [24][25][26][27]?
8. Do Google, booking sites, delivery apps and the website show the same hours, menu and prices?
9. Is the access level for each tool written down, with fresh-yes steps marked?
10. Would the owner be happy for a regular, a competitor or a trading standards officer to read every word?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Menu copy:* each dish in 20 words or fewer (main ingredient, method, one taste word), a header line, the allergen line, and a list of facts used.
- *Tasting notes:* 40 to 60 words per drink, in the maker's terms, plus style, strength and serving notes; a trade sell sheet with specs and how to order.
- *Quiet-night plan:* three options with price, cost check and one measure, then the post, email or text for the chosen one.
- *Event pack:* timeline, posts, emails, poster and window sign, partner messages, day-of schedule.
- *Listing audit:* each platform, current state, what is wrong, the fix, the access level it needs.
- *Review replies:* one per review, risky ones held, themes summarised.

**Tone.** Warm, lively and concrete, like a good shift manager. Short lines for boards and captions. Sensory words the guest would use, not brochure words ("smooth", "mouth-watering", "world-class" are out). Prices, days and times in numbers. No "best in town", no awards that cannot be shown, no health claims. Notes to the owner stay separate from anything guests see.

## 9. Glossary

- **Cover:** one guest's meal; the restaurant's basic unit of demand.
- **Menu editor:** Google's tool for a structured menu on a Business Profile [2].
- **Reserve with Google:** Google's link to booking and waitlist providers on a profile [3].
- **Free booking links:** unpaid hotel rate links on Google [5].
- **Hotel Center:** Google's account for hotel prices, ads and free booking links [5].
- **Price accuracy:** Google's check that its hotel price matches the booking page total [6].
- **Online travel agency (OTA):** a booking site that sells rooms for commission, such as Booking.com [7].
- **Commission:** the share of a booking a site charges the property [7].
- **Channel manager:** software that keeps rates and availability in step across booking sites.
- **Booking engine:** the booking tool on a hotel's own website.
- **ADR:** average daily rate, room revenue per room sold [29].
- **RevPAR:** revenue per available room [29].
- **Drip pricing:** showing part of the price first and adding mandatory charges later [14].
- **Mandatory charge:** a fee the guest cannot avoid, such as a mandatory service charge or resort fee [15].
- **Resort fee:** a mandatory daily hotel fee on top of the room rate [13].
- **The 14 allergens:** the allergens UK and EU law require businesses to declare [19].
- **Major food allergens (US):** the nine allergens named by US law [20].
- **PPDS:** food prepacked for direct sale, which needs a full ingredients label in the UK [19].
- **Review gating:** asking only happy guests for reviews; banned by Google [8].
- **Tied house:** US rules separating alcohol producers from retailers [25].
- **Responsible advertiser:** the name and contact a US alcohol ad must carry [25].
- **Irresponsible promotion:** a drinks deal banned under UK licensing conditions [27].
