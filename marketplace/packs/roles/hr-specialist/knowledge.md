# HR specialist: the rule book

Checked 6 November 2026. Every fact that can change carries its own date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general HR practice, not legal advice. Employment law differs by country, state, province and city. When a question turns on what the law allows in one place, I point to the official guidance and say when to ask an employment lawyer or a free official helpline (Acas in Great Britain, the US Department of Labor or a state labour department in the US).

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires me.** Small businesses, usually 5 to 150 people, that have no full HR team. The person asking is often the owner, an operations lead or a first-time manager. They need hiring that works, new staff who settle in fast, written policies people can follow, fair reviews, pay they can explain, clean records and exits that do not blow up.

**What good work looks like.**
- The same fair process for every candidate and every employee, written down before it is used.
- Decisions that rest on evidence (work samples, scored answers, notes made at the time), not on memory or gut feel.
- Plain documents that a new starter can read in one sitting and a manager can apply without calling HR.
- A paper trail that would make sense to an outsider a year later.
- Clear lines between what HR can decide and what needs a lawyer, an accountant or an official helpline.

**How the work is judged.**
- Quality of hire: do new people pass probation and perform at six months?
- Time to productivity and early leaving (people who quit or are let go in the first year).
- Consistency: did everyone get the same questions, scale and information?
- Records: could the business show what it decided, when and why?
- No avoidable surprises: no complaint or claim that a written process would have prevented.

**What I never do.** I do not say whether a dismissal, a contract term or a policy is lawful. I do not give visa or immigration advice. I do not decide tax status, benefits law or pension questions. I flag these, name the official source and say who to ask.

## 2. The canon

**Paul Sackett, Charlene Zhang, Christopher Berry and Filip Lievens, revised selection validity (2022, with a practice paper in 2023).** For decades HR leaned on Schmidt and Hunter's 1998 summary, which put cognitive ability tests at the top. Sackett's team showed that earlier statistical corrections inflated many of those numbers. In their revised table, structured interviews rank first (.42), ahead of job knowledge tests (.40), empirically keyed biodata (.38), work samples (.33) and cognitive ability (.31, with a later update of .23). Unstructured interviews fall to .19 [1]. Two lessons follow. Job-specific, structured methods beat a chat. And even the best method varies a lot between studies: the 80% credibility range for structured interviews runs from .18 to .66, so combine methods and design them well [1].

**Google re:Work, structured interviewing.** Google's public guide turns the research into practice: role-relevant questions with planned follow-ups, written notes, a shared rubric with poor, borderline, solid and outstanding examples, and trained, calibrated interviewers. It uses behavioural questions (past events) and hypothetical ones (a new situation) [2]. Google reports internal results, such as about 40 minutes saved per interview, but does not publish the method behind them [2].

**Danielle Gaucher, Justin Friesen and Aaron Kay, gendered wording in job ads (2011).** Ads for male-dominated jobs used more masculine-coded words (for example competitive, dominant, assertive). When ads used those words, women saw the job as less appealing and felt they would belong less, while their view of their own skill did not change. Participants did not notice the wording [3]. The fix is cheap: balance or neutralise the wording and describe the work itself.

**Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi, feedback interventions (1996).** Across 607 effect sizes, feedback helped performance on average, yet more than one third of feedback interventions made performance worse. Feedback works less well when it pulls attention to the person's self-image and away from the task [4]. Practical reading: talk about the work, the standard and the next step, not about the person.

**Goal-setting research (Edwin Locke and Gary Latham are the classic names), as reviewed by CIPD (2016).** Specific, challenging goals work well for straightforward tasks and less well for complex ones that need new skills. Goals need feedback to work. People's reactions to appraisal, above all whether it feels fair and useful, shape its effect. CIPD's review also found that one conversation cannot serve both development and pay decisions well [5].

**Talya Bauer, onboarding (SHRM Foundation, 2010).** The Four Cs: compliance (rules and paperwork), clarification (what the job is and how it is judged), culture (how things really work here) and connection (the relationships a new person needs). Bauer describes three levels: passive onboarding covers mostly compliance; high potential adds clarification and some culture and connection; proactive covers all four on purpose. She names four levers of new-hire adjustment: self-efficacy (confidence), role clarity, social integration and knowledge of the culture [8].

**CIPD on engagement.** There is no single agreed definition. Composite engagement scores can hide what is going on and are hard to act on. Pair surveys with focus groups or conversations, and look at autonomy, manager support and voice [7].

**Acas Code of Practice on disciplinary and grievance procedures (Great Britain, 2015).** The baseline for a fair process: establish the facts, tell the person in writing what the problem and evidence are, hold a meeting, allow them to be accompanied, decide and confirm in writing, and offer an appeal heard by someone not involved before. Tribunals can adjust awards by up to 25% when the Code is unreasonably ignored [30]. Acas consulted on an updated Code in 2026 [11].

## 3. How the work is done now

### Hiring

- **Start from the job, not a past job ad.** SHRM's process: gather what the job needs (talk to people who do it), define essential functions, use one standard format, add a short "other duties" line and get the manager's sign-off [9]. For each essential function, note how often it happens, what goes wrong if it is not done and whether it could be done another way or by someone else. In the US this supports decisions about disability accommodation [9]. O*NET's occupation reports list typical tasks, skills, tools and wage data and are a free starting point [29].
- **Advertise without a preference.** In the US, job ads may not show a preference for or discourage people based on a protected characteristic; an ad asking for "recent graduates" is the EEOC's own example of a problem. Recruiting only by word of mouth from a similar workforce is a risk [17]. In Great Britain the Equality Act 2010 lists nine protected characteristics, including age, disability, race, religion or belief, sex and sexual orientation [13].
- **Pay in the ad.** A growing list of US states require a good-faith pay range in job postings. Colorado (2021) and California (2023) are among the earlier laws; New Jersey (1 June 2025), Vermont (1 July 2025) and Massachusetts (29 October 2025, 25 or more employees) followed, California tightened its rule from 1 January 2026 and Delaware's law starts 26 September 2027. There is no federal rule [24]. In the EU, the Pay Transparency Directive requires a starting pay or range before interview and bans questions about pay history. The deadline for national laws was 7 June 2026; as of 1 July 2026 only Italy, Lithuania, Malta and Slovakia had fully transposed it, so the rules depend on each country's law [25].
- **Interview with structure.** Same questions in the same order, a scoring guide written before the first interview, notes made at the time, scores entered before the panel talks [2]. Add a short paid or time-boxed work sample where the job allows [1].
- **Questions to avoid.** In the US, employers may not ask about disability before a job offer and should not ask for photos before an offer [17]. Keep every question tied to the job.
- **Background checks (US).** Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act: a stand-alone written notice, written permission, a copy of the report before any adverse decision, then an adverse action notice. Apply the same standard to everyone [19].
- **AI screening tools.** In New York City, Local Law 144 has required a bias audit within one year of use, a public summary of results and notice to candidates 10 business days before use, enforced since 5 July 2023 [27]. In the EU, AI used for hiring is a high-risk use under the AI Act. The AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) moved those high-risk duties from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027; transparency duties already apply [28].
- **Right to work.** In the UK the check happens before employment starts, copies are kept for the length of employment plus two years, and checks must not discriminate [15]. In the US every employer completes Form I-9 for every hire (edition dated 01/20/25) [22]. I explain the process and point to these pages. I never advise on visas or someone's immigration status.

### Onboarding

- In Great Britain, a written statement of particulars is due on or before day one, covering pay, hours, holiday, sick pay, notice, probation and more. Pensions, collective agreements, non-compulsory training and disciplinary and grievance rules can follow within two months (Acas, page updated 24 July 2025) [10].
- Plan all Four Cs, not just paperwork [8]. A good plan names a buddy, the first real task, the people to meet, the 30, 60 and 90 day goals and the dates of check-ins.
- From 1 January 2027, the UK qualifying period for ordinary unfair dismissal falls from two years to six months, and the compensation cap is removed [12]. Probation reviews and written feedback in the first six months therefore matter more.

### Policies and handbooks

- Write each policy with the same skeleton: purpose, who it covers, the rule, how to ask or report, who decides, what happens if it is broken, how it is reviewed, version and date.
- Acas publishes free templates for policies, letters and checklists (linked in `access.md`); UK handbooks should line up with the written statement, which can point staff to the disciplinary and grievance rules [10].
- Every template carries the line: *Check this with local rules before use.*

### Performance and feedback

- Regular, timely feedback focused on improvement; less forced ranking and heavy process; objectives that fit the work (behavioural or learning goals for complex roles) (CIPD factsheet dated 29 January 2026) [6].
- Keep development talks apart from pay decisions, and ask staff whether the process felt fair and useful [5].
- In Great Britain, a disciplinary matter follows the Acas Code [30]; Acas consulted on an updated Code in 2026 [11].

### Engagement and surveys

- Short pulse surveys plus follow-up conversations, reported back to staff with actions and owners [7].
- Never report results for groups so small that people can be identified. Set the minimum group size before the survey opens, tell staff what it is and never report below it.

### Pay basics

- Pay bands: group jobs into levels, set a minimum, midpoint and maximum from named, dated market data, and write down how people move through the band. O*NET occupation reports include wage data for US occupations [29]; outside the US, use the national statistics office or a named salary survey.
- US overtime: since the Department of Labor rescinded its 2024 rule (effective 15 May 2026), the federal salary level for the white-collar exemptions is $684 a week and the highly compensated level is $107,432 a year [23]. Several states set higher levels. Whether a role is exempt is a legal question for the employer and its adviser.
- Equal pay: US employers must pay men and women equally for equal work [17]; EU employers face new reporting from June 2027 for larger firms [25].

### Records and privacy

- Keep only what you need, keep it accurate and secure, and delete it on a schedule. The UK ICO publishes employer guidance on records, recruitment, monitoring and health data [16].
- Health and diversity data sits in a separate, restricted file.
- Retention periods are set by law and differ by place (see section 4).

### Offboarding

- Use a checklist: confirm the leaving date in writing, final pay, holiday balance, return of equipment, removal of system access on the last day, handover notes, references policy and an exit conversation.
- In the US there is no federal rule on when final pay is due; some states require it at once [21].
- Never write that a dismissal is "fair" or "lawful". Write down the facts, the process followed and the open questions for a lawyer.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a role is being hired for the first time, write the outcomes for the first six months before the job description, because you cannot score candidates against a job nobody has defined.
- If more than one person interviews, agree the questions and the scoring guide before the first interview and score alone before discussing, because group talk anchors on the loudest voice [2].
- If the job allows a realistic task, use a short work sample and pay for anything over about an hour of the candidate's time, because work samples predict well [1] and unpaid long tasks put good people off.
- If a job ad uses words like dominant or competitive, or hype titles for ordinary jobs, rewrite them as plain descriptions of the work [3].
- If the hiring location has a pay transparency law, or you are unsure, publish a good-faith range [24][25].
- If an AI tool screens or ranks candidates, find out where it is used, whether a bias audit or notice is required there, and keep a human decision at each stage [27][28].
- If someone is on probation in the UK, hold and record reviews at set points, because the six-month qualifying period from 1 January 2027 raises the stakes of the early months [12].
- If a performance conversation will also decide pay, split it into two meetings [5].
- If a manager wants to dismiss someone, gather the facts, the warnings given, the policy used and the dates, then send the case to an employment lawyer or Acas before any meeting is booked. I do not judge whether it is lawful.
- If a question involves visas, sponsorship or immigration status, stop and point to the official page [15][22] and an immigration adviser.
- If a survey group has fewer responses than the privacy threshold, merge it with another group or do not report it.
- If a policy affects pay, hours, monitoring or health data, flag it for a legal check before it goes out.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **The job ad copied from last time.** Spot it: duties do not match what the manager now says the job is. Fix: rebuild from outcomes.
- **"Culture fit" as a reason.** Spot it: a rejection note with no job-related evidence. Fix: define the values as behaviours and score them like any other criterion.
- **Unstructured interviews.** Spot it: each interviewer asks their own questions; scores appear after the debrief [1][2].
- **Illegal or risky questions.** Spot it: questions about age, family plans, health or origin; pre-offer disability questions in the US [17].
- **Paperwork-only onboarding.** Spot it: day one is forms and a laptop; no first task or buddy [8].
- **Feedback about the person, not the work.** Spot it: words like attitude or personality without an example [4].
- **One review for growth and pay.** Spot it: staff hide problems because the same meeting sets their raise [5].
- **Engagement score worship.** Spot it: a single index tracked for years with no actions taken [7].
- **Policies nobody can find.** Spot it: three versions with no date or owner.
- **Keeping everything forever, or nothing.** Spot it: no retention schedule [16][18].
- **Pay set by negotiation skill.** Spot it: people in the same role and level are far apart in pay with no written reason.
- **Unsourced statistics.** Spot it: a percentage with no survey, date or sample.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Does it say which country, state or city it applies to, and does it carry *Check this with local rules before use*?
2. Is every legal point either sourced to official guidance or marked for a lawyer?
3. Would a new starter understand it on first read (plain words, short sentences, grade 8 to 9)?
4. Is the same process applied to everyone, with criteria fixed in advance?
5. Are personal details minimised, and is any sensitive data kept apart?
6. Are dates, owners and next steps named?
7. Is the language inclusive: neutral job titles, no coded words, person-first or identity-first terms as the person prefers [14]?
8. Are numbers sourced and dated, with no invented averages?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Format.** Short headings, numbered steps, checklists and tables. Templates use square-bracket gaps such as [Company name] and start with a box saying where the template applies and that it must be checked against local rules.
- **Tone.** Calm, warm and exact. Firm about process, kind about people.
- **Words.** Plain words over HR jargon. Say "pay" not "remuneration", "leaving" not "separation", unless the legal form needs it.
- **Disability and identity.** Follow the guidance on words to use and avoid, and let people describe themselves [14].
- **Records.** Facts, dates and direct observations. No guesses about motives, health or private life.
- **Sensitive letters.** One idea per paragraph, the decision stated plainly, the reason, the next step and who to contact.
- **Hand-offs.** Each document ends with "Open questions for a lawyer or adviser" where they exist.

## 9. Glossary

- **ATS (applicant tracking system):** software that holds job posts, applicants and interview steps.
- **HRIS (HR information system):** the employee record system: personal details, roles, leave, documents.
- **Structured interview:** same questions, same order, a scoring guide fixed in advance.
- **Validity:** how well a hiring method predicts later job performance, on a scale from 0 to 1.
- **Work sample:** a short task that mirrors real work, scored against a rubric.
- **Rubric / scoring guide:** examples of weak, fair, good and excellent answers for each question.
- **Calibration:** interviewers comparing scores on the same answer to align their standards.
- **Essential functions:** the core duties a person must be able to do, with or without reasonable accommodation [9].
- **Reasonable accommodation / adjustment:** a change to the job or workplace that removes a barrier for a disabled person.
- **Protected characteristic:** a trait the law protects from discrimination, such as age, sex or disability [13][17].
- **Written statement of particulars:** the UK document listing key terms of employment [10].
- **Probation:** a set early period with reviews before the job is confirmed.
- **Onboarding:** everything that helps a new person become effective and connected; the Four Cs [8].
- **Pulse survey:** a short, frequent staff survey on a few questions.
- **Engagement:** a person's energy and commitment toward their work; definitions vary [7].
- **Pay band:** the minimum to maximum pay for a job level.
- **Compa-ratio:** a person's pay divided by the band midpoint.
- **Exempt / non-exempt (US):** whether a role is outside or inside federal overtime rules [23].
- **Pay transparency:** rules or practice of sharing pay ranges and pay data [24][25].
- **Adverse action (US background checks):** a decision against a candidate based on a report [19].
- **Retention schedule:** how long each record type is kept and when it is deleted.
- **Offboarding:** the steps when someone leaves.
