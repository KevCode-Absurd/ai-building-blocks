# Performance review

## When to use

- Quarterly or yearly review time.
- A manager needs to give feedback that is hard to hear.
- Reviews are written from memory the night before.
- Staff say reviews feel unfair or pointless.

If the situation is moving toward a warning, a performance improvement plan with consequences, or dismissal, stop. Follow the disciplinary or capability procedure and get advice from an employment lawyer or official helpline. This skill does not judge whether action is fair or lawful.

## What you need

1. The person's role, the goals set at the start of the period and any changes since.
2. Evidence: work results, numbers, examples, feedback from others, notes from one-on-ones.
3. The review form in use, if any.
4. Whether pay or promotion decisions are linked to this review, and when.

**Access:** level 1 to read goals, evidence and past reviews in your review tool or HR system; level 2 to set up the review form and save draft reviews that the person can't see yet; level 3 to file the agreed summary in the HR record and book the next check-in, within your limits. Ratings, pay and promotion decisions stay with the manager.

## What the research says (short)

- Feedback helps on average, but in a large 1996 review (Kluger and DeNisi) more than a third of feedback attempts made performance worse. Feedback about the task works; feedback that turns attention to the person's self-image does not.
- Specific, challenging goals help on straightforward tasks; complex roles need learning or behaviour goals too (CIPD evidence review, 2016).
- People's sense that the review was fair and useful shapes whether it helps. One meeting that does both development and pay works badly; split them.

## Steps

### 1. Set up the cycle (first time only)

- Pick a rhythm: short monthly check-ins plus a written review each quarter or half year.
- Hold the pay conversation at a different meeting, ideally weeks apart.
- Give every person three to five goals per period: some results goals ("cut quote time to one day"), some behaviour or learning goals ("learn the new stock system and train one colleague").
- Start an evidence log per person: date, what happened, source. Managers add one line a week.

### 2. Gather evidence

For each goal, collect what happened, with dates and sources. Ask two or three colleagues for short, specific input: one thing to keep doing, one thing to change, an example of each. Ask the person for their own view first, using the same form.

### 3. Draft the review from evidence

At level 2, save it as a draft in the review tool, hidden from the person until the manager shares it. For each goal:
- **What was expected.**
- **What happened**, with examples and numbers.
- **Rating**, if your form uses one, with a one-line reason tied to the evidence.

Then:
- **Strengths to build on** (two, with examples).
- **One or two things to change**, each with an example and what good would look like.
- **Support the business will give** (training, time, tools).

Write about behaviour and results, never personality. "Missed three of five deadlines in March; each time the client chased" works. "Lacks commitment" does not.

### 4. Check for bias

- Compare ratings across the team: are people with similar evidence rated the same way?
- Look for vague praise for some and vague criticism for others.
- Check that leave, part-time hours, adjustments or caring duties have not been treated as poor performance.
- Remove anything about health, family, age or other protected characteristics.

### 5. Hold the growth conversation

- Send the draft a day before.
- Start with the person's own view.
- Agree the facts before talking about meaning.
- End with two or three goals for the next period and the support agreed.
- Afterwards, ask: did that feel fair and useful? Note the answer.

### 6. Follow up

- Write a short summary of what was agreed and share it within two working days.
- Put the next check-in in the calendar (level 3).
- Keep the review in the HR record, with access limited to the person, their manager and HR (level 3 files the agreed version; otherwise the manager does).

## Checks before handing over

- Every rating or judgement has at least one dated example.
- No sentence describes personality, health or private life.
- The pay decision is not in the review document.
- Goals for next period are specific and observable.
- If the review mentions a formal process, it names the procedure and says advice was sought.
- The form carries: *Template: check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

1. Review summary (person, role, period, reviewer, date)
2. Goals table (goal, expected, what happened with evidence, rating and reason)
3. Strengths with examples
4. Things to change, with examples and what good looks like
5. Support agreed
6. Next period goals
7. Bias check notes (for the manager only, not shared)
8. Follow-up date

## Example of good output (short)

> **Goal 2:** Answer all quote requests within one working day.
> **What happened:** 212 of 240 requests (88%) answered within one working day, up from 61% last quarter (source: inbox report, 1 Oct). The misses clustered in the week of 14 Aug, when stock data was down.
> **Rating:** Meets, because the target was nearly hit and the misses had a clear outside cause.
>
> **One thing to change:** In two client calls (3 and 17 Sep) the price was given before checking stock. Next quarter: check stock first, and say "I'll confirm by 3pm" if the system is slow.
