# Structured hiring kit

## When to use

- A business is about to open a role, new or replacement.
- The last hire did not work out and nobody can say why.
- Interviewers ask their own questions and decide in the debrief.
- A manager wants a fair way to compare a shortlist.

Do not use it to decide whether a rejection, a contract term or a visa route is lawful. Flag those for an employment lawyer or official helpline.

## What you need

Ask for these. Work with what you get and mark the gaps.

1. The role title, team, manager and where the person will work (country, state or city, and remote or on site). Location decides which rules apply.
2. Why the role exists now: the problem it solves, or the work that is piling up.
3. What success looks like at 30 days, 90 days and six months.
4. Pay range and benefits, or the budget. If none exists, note it and use the pay band prompt first.
5. Who will interview, and how many hours each can give.
6. Any past job ad or description (only as raw material).

**Access:** level 1 to read past posts and pipelines in your applicant tracker; level 2 to save the job, scorecards and candidate replies there as drafts; level 3 to publish the approved ad and send the agreed replies once people have decided, within your limits. The offer always needs a new yes.

## Steps

### 1. Write the outcomes first

Turn the "why now" into three to five outcomes for the first six months, each one observable. "Reduce the support backlog from 300 to under 50 tickets" beats "excellent customer focus". Confirm them with the manager before writing anything else.

### 2. Derive the criteria

From the outcomes, list four to six criteria the person must show. Mix:
- Role knowledge or skills (what they must already know).
- Problem solving (how they approach something new).
- Ways of working (collaboration, ownership, communication), each written as a behaviour, never as "culture fit".

Mark each criterion "must have at start" or "can learn in 90 days". Only must-haves go in the screening stage.

### 3. Draft the job description

Use one standard format: title, purpose, outcomes, essential functions, criteria, working pattern, pay range, benefits summary, location, how to apply, closing date. For each essential function note how often it happens and whether it could be done another way, which helps with later requests for adjustments. Add one line: other reasonable duties may be added.

### 4. Write the job ad

- Lead with the work and the outcomes, not a list of adjectives.
- Replace masculine-coded words (dominant, competitive, aggressive, assertive) with plain descriptions of the task. Avoid hype titles.
- Cut "nice to have" lists to the minimum; long lists put off qualified people.
- State the pay range. Many US states and EU countries now require it; where unsure, include it.
- Do not ask for age, photos, years since graduation or health details.
- Add how to ask for an adjustment in the process.
- At level 2, save the ad as a draft job in the tracker. At level 3, publish it only after the owner approves the final text.

### 5. Build the interview

Plan one structured interview of 45 to 60 minutes plus one work sample. For each criterion write:
- One main question: behavioural ("Tell me about a time you...") for skills shown in the past, or hypothetical ("Imagine that...") for new situations.
- Two planned follow-ups that ask what they did, why, and what happened.
- A four-level scoring guide with a short example answer at each level: 1 poor, 2 borderline, 3 solid, 4 outstanding.

Every candidate gets the same questions in the same order. Interviewers take notes on what was said, not impressions.

### 6. Design the work sample

A task that mirrors real work, done in under an hour, or paid if longer. Give the same brief, data and time to everyone. Write the scoring guide before anyone sees the task. Remove anything that tests access to expensive tools or free evenings rather than skill.

### 7. Scorecard and decision meeting

- Each interviewer scores alone, with evidence notes, before any discussion.
- The decision meeting starts with the scores on screen, then discusses only the gaps between interviewers.
- Decide against the criteria, not against the other candidates' charm.
- Record the decision and the reasons in one paragraph per candidate.

### 8. Close the loop

Draft replies for every outcome: invite, reject after screen, reject after interview, offer. At level 3, send the invites and rejections the hiring manager decided on; the offer always needs a new yes. Keep notes and scorecards for the period your local record rules require, then delete them.

## Checks before handing over

- Every criterion traces back to an outcome.
- Every question maps to one criterion and has a four-level guide.
- No question touches a protected characteristic (age, disability, pregnancy or family plans, religion, origin, sexual orientation and so on).
- The ad shows a pay range or explains why not, and names the location rules checked.
- The kit says who scores, when, and how ties are broken.
- The template box is present: *Check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

One markdown document with these headings:

1. Role summary and six-month outcomes
2. Criteria table (criterion, must have or learnable, how assessed)
3. Job description
4. Job ad
5. Interview guide (question, follow-ups, scoring guide per criterion)
6. Work sample brief and scoring guide
7. Scorecard (table)
8. Decision meeting agenda
9. Candidate reply drafts
10. Open questions for a lawyer or adviser

## Example of good output (short)

> **Template: check this with local rules before use. Applies to: Leeds, UK, on site.**
>
> **Outcome 2:** By month three, every customer order over £500 gets a written quote within one working day (now three days).
>
> **Criterion:** Turns a messy request into a clear quote. Must have at start. Assessed by: interview question 2 and the work sample.
>
> **Question 2 (behavioural):** Tell me about a time a customer asked for something that did not fit your price list. What did you do?
> Follow-ups: What did you ask them first? How did you check the price was right?
>
> | Score | What it sounds like |
> |---|---|
> | 1 Poor | Sent the request to someone else; cannot describe the steps. |
> | 2 Borderline | Produced a quote but skipped checking cost or scope. |
> | 3 Solid | Clarified scope, checked costs, sent the quote, followed up. |
> | 4 Outstanding | Did all of that and changed the process so the next quote was faster. |
