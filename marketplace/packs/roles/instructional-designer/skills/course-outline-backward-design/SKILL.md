# Course outline by backward design

## When to use

- An expert, team or company has material (notes, slides, SOPs, help docs, talks) and wants a course people finish.
- A course exists but learners drop out, fail the checks or still raise the same support tickets.
- A customer academy needs learning paths by user role.
- Before any storyboard, video script or lesson is written, so everyone agrees on goals, timing and checks.

Do not use it to promise income, jobs or health results, to claim accreditation, or to design regulated or safety-critical training without the client's expert signing it off.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the client's material, existing course and its results, ticket export (with customer details removed), LMS or course platform reports.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** create the course shell, modules and lesson titles as an unpublished draft in the LMS or course platform; save the outline in the client's documents.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved outline or course page and open enrolment within the client's limits (which course, which audience, which dates). Changing prices, starting a sale or emailing a new audience needs a new yes. Log every action.

## What you need

1. Who the learners are, where they start, and why they take the course (required, chosen, bought).
2. What they must be able to do at the end, in the client's words.
3. The material the course must use. Anything beyond it will be marked for the expert.
4. The time learners have: total hours, weeks, minutes per sitting, device (phone or desk).
5. The delivery: LMS module, course platform, email or chat, live sessions, or a mix.
6. For a customer academy: user roles and the top support ticket topics with counts.

## Steps

### 1. Write the result sentence

"By the end, [learner] can [do what] [to what standard] [in what situation]." If the client gives a topic, ask what learners will do with it. If they cannot say, propose one and mark it for approval.

### 2. Break it into goals

2 to 5 goals per module, each an action you could watch ("build a pivot table that totals sales by month"), never "understand" or "know". Choose verbs that match the level of thinking needed: recall and explain for facts, apply and decide for procedures and judgement, create for projects.

### 3. Decide the evidence first

For each goal, how will you know the learner can do it? A task in a test account, a scenario decision, a work sample, a project step. Write the check before any content.

### 4. Plan practice that builds to the evidence

Easy to hard: a worked example, guided practice, then practice alone. Practice uses situations from the learner's real work. A judgement goal gets a scenario; a procedure gets a hands-on task; facts get spaced recall questions.

### 5. Add only the content needed

For each practice, the minimum explanation, example or demonstration learners need. Long policies and reference detail stay as linked resources, not lessons. Mark anything not in the client's material as [confirm with your team].

### 6. Size modules and lessons

4 to 8 modules for a full course. Each lesson holds one idea, an example, one practice task and a check, sized to the sitting length and device. Count the total against the hours available; if it does not fit, say what to cut and why.

### 7. Sequence and add a final project

Order lessons so each builds on the last. Add a final project that uses everything, with a short rubric.

### 8. Note accessibility and delivery from the start

For each lesson, note video (needs captions and a transcript), images (need alt text), interactions (need keyboard access and no drag-only steps) and timed elements (need a way to extend). For an LMS, note which package type the client's LMS supports (SCORM 1.2, SCORM 2004, xAPI or cmi5) so the build team can plan.

### 9. For a customer academy

Group tickets into topics and count them. The biggest topics become the first lessons. Each lesson names the ticket topic it should reduce. Paths are built per user role.

## Checks

- One result sentence the client approved.
- Every goal is an action with a check; every check has practice before it.
- Every lesson has one idea and fits the sitting length.
- Total time fits the hours available.
- Content traces to the client's material; additions are marked.
- Accessibility needs are noted per lesson.
- Sales copy, if any, promises only what the course teaches.
- At level 2 or 3, only the named course was created or changed, and the log is updated.

## Output format

1. Result sentence and learner profile.
2. Outline table: module, lesson, goal, practice, check, minutes, media.
3. Final project with rubric.
4. Accessibility and delivery notes.
5. What was cut or moved to references, and why.
6. Confirm with your team.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a training provider's four-week course "Excel for bookkeepers", 3 hours a week. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Result:** By the end, a junior bookkeeper can turn a bank export into a checked monthly summary in Excel, using tables, SUMIFS and a pivot table, in under 30 minutes.
>
> | Module | Lesson | Goal | Practice | Check | Min |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | 2 Clean the data | 2.1 Turn a range into a table | Convert a bank export into a named table with correct data types | Clean a sample export from a made-up bank | Upload the table; 3 auto-checks | 25 |
> | 2 | 2.2 Fix dates that are text | Convert text dates to real dates | Fix 40 dates in two formats | 5 dates in a new file | 20 |
> | 3 Summarise | 3.1 SUMIFS by category | Total spend by category and month | Build the summary for March | Match the answer file to the penny | 30 |
>
> **Cut:** "History of spreadsheets" and "macros" (not needed for the result); macros move to a bonus reference.
>
> **Accessibility:** every screen recording gets captions and a transcript; the practice files use real cell text, not images of tables.
