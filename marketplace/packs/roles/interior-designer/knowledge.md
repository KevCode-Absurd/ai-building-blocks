# Interior designer: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. Tool features change often; each one says when it was true. This kit covers refresh plans for small business spaces (cafes, salons, studios, small offices, shops and holiday lets) and window and display plans. It gives no building regulation, fire safety or accessibility rulings. Where the answer depends on the law or on the building, I say who to ask: building control, the fire and rescue authority or local fire marshal, a fire risk assessor, a registered electrician, a structural engineer, an architect or an access consultant.

Related kits: shop trading, promotions and stock are in the `retail-manager` kit; sign and print files are in the `graphic-designer` kit; running a holiday let is in the `short-term-rental-manager` kit.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires an interior designer here.** Owners of small cafes, salons, studios, offices and independent shops, and holiday let hosts, who want a space that works better and looks better without a building project. The signs: a cafe that can't fit more seats without feeling cramped, a salon that feels dark, a shop window people walk past, a holiday let whose photos look flat, a back office where nobody can find anything.

In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into two families:
- **Rooms:** a refresh plan for a cafe, salon or studio; a small office or workroom layout; holiday let styling; a photo or content corner; AI concept image prompts so the owner can see the look before spending.
- **Windows and displays:** a shop window plan, in-store table, shelf and wall displays, a year of window themes, a market stall display, and sign layouts.

**What good work looks like.**
- Every placement fits the measurements, with clearances shown.
- Walkways stay clear, nobody has to squeeze past a chair to reach an exit, and wheelchair and pram users can get in and round.
- Light comes in layers, and colour has a job: main, secondary and accent.
- The shopping list reuses what the owner has first and stays inside the budget.
- The order of work closes the business for as few days as possible.
- Anything that needs a professional is marked, with who to ask.

**How the work is judged.**
- The target is met (seats, stations, storage, sales space) and the space still feels comfortable.
- Customers step in (windows), stay (cafes) and book (holiday lets).
- The budget and the closure days hold.
- Nothing in the plan fails a fire, access or safety check later.

**What this role does not do here.** Building, electrical, plumbing, gas or structural work; planning permission, building regulations approval or fire safety sign-off; installing items; ordering or paying beyond what the client approves. Live changes in the client's tools stay within the access level and limits the client set (see `access.md`).

## 2. The canon

**Julius Panero and Martin Zelnik, *Human Dimension and Interior Space* (1979).** Spaces must fit the range of real bodies, not an average person: seated and standing, reaching, passing each other, using wheelchairs. Their method of designing from body sizes and clearances is the root of every placement table in this kit.

**Francis D.K. Ching and Corky Binggeli, *Interior Design Illustrated*.** A visual grammar of interiors: space, scale, form, light and materials, and how to read and draw plans and elevations.

**Ernst Neufert, *Architects' Data*.** The standard reference for furniture and room dimensions. Use it for first ideas, then check against the local code and the client's tape measure.

**Christopher Alexander and colleagues, *A Pattern Language* (1977).** Good places repeat patterns that suit how people live, such as daylight from more than one side and seats near light. A useful way to explain why a layout feels right.

**Edward T. Hall, *The Hidden Dimension* (1966).** People keep different distances for intimate, personal, social and public contact. Seating that ignores those distances feels cramped even when it fits.

**Josef Albers, *Interaction of Color* (1963).** A colour changes with its neighbours and the light. Always test paint samples on the real wall, in daylight and at night.

**Richard Kelly's three kinds of light.** The lighting designer described focal glow (light that draws the eye), ambient luminescence (soft general light) and play of brilliants (sparkle). Today's "ambient, task and accent" layering comes from the same idea.

**Universal design.** The Principles of Universal Design (Center for Universal Design, North Carolina State University, 1997) ask for spaces usable by everyone without special adaptation. Designing for wheelchair users, people with prams, older customers and people with low vision usually makes the space better for all.

**Visual merchandising.** Tony Morgan, *Visual Merchandising* (Laurence King), and Martin Pegler, *Visual Merchandising and Display* (Fairchild): one idea per window, a focal point, a triangle or step composition, odd-numbered groups, colour blocking and repetition. Paco Underhill, *Why We Buy* (1999), adds that shoppers need a few steps inside the door to slow down.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Measuring and drawing (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Measure first.** Wall lengths, ceiling height, doors (width and swing), windows (sill height), sockets and switches, radiators, columns, steps and floor level changes, fixed items. If a size is missing, say exactly what to measure.
- **SketchUp.** The Tape Measure tool measures lengths and creates guide lines [22]. You can import a floor plan image or photo and trace over it in Top view with Parallel Projection to build a plan, then pull it into 3D [23]. SketchUp Free runs in the browser with 10 GB of Trimble Connect storage and imports SKP, JPG and PNG; its page does not state commercial-use terms [24]. Go, Pro (with LayOut for 2D drawings) and Studio are paid [25].
- **Floor plan apps.** Floorplanner's free account allows up to 5 projects with watermarked standard-definition exports [26]. RoomSketcher's free plan draws 2D and 3D plans but leaves out measurements and area totals, which come with Pro [27].
- **Text plans.** When the client has no software, the deliverable is a top-down zone map in text and a placement table with sizes and distances, which any builder or owner can follow.
- **AI concept images** show a look, not a measured plan. Label them "concept only".
- I have not found an official AI connector for SketchUp or these floor plan apps; exported images, plan files and view links work.
- Access is granted per tool at one of three levels: read; read and draft; read, write and publish within the client's limits. At level 3 that means things like updating holiday let listing photos and room descriptions, or editing the shared plan and mood board the client named. Orders, supplier contracts and payments are covered in `access.md`. Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1.

### Accessible layouts: US
The 2010 ADA Standards for Accessible Design set dimensions for places open to the public [1]:
- Accessible route at least 36 in (915 mm) clear, narrowing to 32 in (815 mm) for no more than 24 in (610 mm) of length.
- Turning space: a 60 in (1525 mm) circle or a T-shape.
- Clear floor space for a wheelchair: 30 x 48 in (760 x 1220 mm).
- Doors: 32 in (815 mm) clear width.
- Reach: 48 in (1220 mm) high and 15 in (380 mm) low, forward or side.
- Wall-mounted objects between 27 and 80 in (685 and 2030 mm) high may stick out no more than 4 in (100 mm), so a cane user can detect them.
- Dining and work surfaces 28 to 34 in (710 to 865 mm) high, and at least 5% of dining surfaces must comply.
- Sales and service counters: an accessible part at most 36 in (915 mm) high; check-out counters at most 38 in (965 mm).
- Self-service shelves must be on an accessible route but need not all be within reach ranges.

Existing businesses must remove barriers where it is "readily achievable", judged by their size and resources, and alterations must be accessible to the maximum extent feasible [2]. Small businesses may qualify for the Disabled Access Credit (30 or fewer full-time employees, or $1 million or less in revenue), and any business can deduct up to $15,000 a year for removing barriers [2]. Tax treatment is for the client's accountant.

### Accessible layouts: UK (England)
Approved Document M Volume 2 is the statutory guidance for buildings other than dwellings in England; its 2024 amendments were incorporated on 1 October 2024 [3]. Its numbers [4]:
- Corridors and passageways at least 1200 mm unobstructed, with 1800 mm passing places where narrower than 1800 mm.
- Door clear widths (new / existing buildings): 800 / 750 mm straight on; 825 / 775 mm turning off a 1200 mm route; 1000 / 775 mm for external doors used by the public.
- Reception or sales counters: a section at least 1500 mm wide, no higher than 760 mm, with a knee recess at least 700 mm high; refreshment facilities need part of the bar or serving counter no higher than 850 mm.
- Sockets 400 to 1000 mm above the floor; switches 400 to 1200 mm.
- Visual contrast between surfaces: a light reflectance value (LRV) difference of more than 30 points (20 where light levels are over 200 lux). Avoid glare and large repeating patterns where people need to see clearly.

Meeting Approved Document M does not on its own meet the Equality Act 2010 duty to make reasonable adjustments for disabled customers and staff, and a material alteration must not leave a building less accessible than before [4]. Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland have their own guidance: check the local building control body.

### EU
Each member state sets its own building and accessibility rules; check the national building authority. EU law sets workplace minimums, including emergency routes and exits, traffic routes and lighting, through Directive 89/654/EEC, with national laws adding detail [12].

### Exits and fire safety basics
- **UK.** Whoever controls non-domestic premises (employer, owner, landlord or occupier) is the "responsible person" and must carry out and keep up a fire risk assessment, put fire safety measures in place and plan for emergencies; this also covers B&Bs and self-catering lets [7]. The Home Office publishes risk assessment guides for offices and shops, small and medium places of assembly, and sleeping accommodation [8]. New layouts change the assessment: tell the responsible person.
- **UK holiday lets.** The small paying guest accommodation guide (updated 20 January 2025) says the fire risk assessment must be recorded, recommends interlinked smoke alarms and heat alarms in kitchens, says escape routes must be kept clear and not used for storage, says furniture should meet the Furniture and Furnishings (Fire) (Safety) Regulations 1988 (usually shown by a label), and says not to provide candles, tea lights or ethanol burners [9].
- **UK building work.** Approved Document B covers fire safety in building design and was last updated on 16 September 2026 [5]. Many alterations need building regulations approval, including new or changed electrics such as fuse boxes, work near a bath or shower, plumbed bathrooms, and replacement windows and doors; installers in a competent person scheme can self-certify [6].
- **US.** In workplaces, OSHA requires exit access at least 28 in (71.1 cm) wide, exit doors that open from inside without keys or special knowledge, routes kept free of materials, equipment and highly flammable decorations, and exit doors not hidden by decorations or signs [10][11]. Building and fire codes are adopted and enforced locally: ask the local building department and fire marshal before moving walls, counters or exits.
- **EU.** Emergency routes and exits are covered by the workplace directive [12] and national fire rules.

### Older buildings and finishes
- **UK asbestos.** In non-domestic premises such as shops, the dutyholder must manage asbestos, including a survey and a plan [14]. Before drilling, sanding or removing walls, ceilings or floor tiles, ask the landlord or owner for the asbestos records.
- **US lead paint.** Anyone paid to disturb paint in homes or child-occupied facilities (such as child care) built before 1978 must be EPA lead-safe certified [15].
- **Paint and new furniture fumes.** Ventilate while painting and after, follow the label and buy only what you need [16].

### Furniture and product safety
- **UK.** Check furniture labels and the product recall database before buying, especially second-hand or marketplace furniture for holiday lets [17].
- **Case: Argos Habitat Eton sofas (8 March 2023).** Sofas and footstools sold by Argos between February 2022 and February 2023 were recalled because the fabric could catch fire and keep burning; it failed BS 5852 and did not meet the 1988 furniture fire regulations [18]. Lesson: even well-known retailers' furniture gets recalled; check the database for every model on a shopping list.
- **US.** Free-standing dressers and chests at least 27 in tall made after 1 September 2023 must pass CPSC stability tests and come with an anti-tip device [19]. Fit it, and check the CPSC recall list [20].
- **EU.** Check Safety Gate, the EU alert system for dangerous non-food products [21].

### Windows and displays
- One idea per window, tied to the shop's goal and season, with a focal point and a triangle or step layout.
- Set the focal point at the eye height of the people passing; measure on site. Anything customers must reach (a tester, a price, a leaflet) goes within the reach ranges above [1].
- Wall-mounted shelves and props on walkways must not stick out into the path at head height [1], and nothing may block an exit, fire door or exit sign [11].
- Check the heat of window lights on products and props, and keep cables off walkways.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a measurement is missing, ask for it exactly (what, from where to where) before placing anything.
- If a layout adds seats or stations, check the accessible route, the turning space and the exits before counting seats [1][4][10].
- If the business is in the US and open to the public, plan at least the ADA route widths, counter heights and dining surface share [1].
- If the business is in England, use Approved Document M Volume 2 as the guide for counters, corridors, doors and contrast, and tell the client that building control decides on building work [3][4].
- If a change moves walls, doors, exits, electrics, plumbing or ventilation, mark it for building control and the right trade before costing it [6].
- If seating, displays or storage change, tell the responsible person to review the fire risk assessment [7].
- If a holiday let is being styled, check every upholstered item for its fire label and every model against the recall database, and remove candles from the shopping list [9][17].
- If a building is older and work will disturb walls, ceilings, floors or old paint, ask for the asbestos records (UK) or check the lead-safe rules (US) first [14][15].
- If a room feels dark, add light layers (general, task, accent) and lighter finishes before painting everything white.
- If two surfaces must be told apart (wall and floor, door and wall, step edge), choose finishes with a clear LRV difference [4].
- If a display sits near a door or exit, move it; doors, exits and their signs stay visible and clear [11].
- If a budget is tight, reuse, move and repaint before buying, and spend on the items customers touch and see first.
- If the client grants access, ask which level for each tool, work at level 1 until they answer, and confirm the action list once before the first live change at level 3.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot each

- **Plans without measurements.** Spot it: "a sofa here" with no size or clearance. Fix: a placement table with sizes and distances.
- **Seats added, route lost.** Spot it: chairs pushed back block the path to the toilet or exit.
- **Counters only at standing height.** Spot it: no lowered section for wheelchair users [1][4].
- **One flat ceiling light.** Spot it: shadows on faces at the mirror or till; no accent on products.
- **Low-contrast finishes.** Spot it: pale door on pale wall, step edges that disappear [4].
- **Glossy finishes and busy patterns** where people read or lip-read, such as a reception desk [4].
- **Displays in the doorway.** Spot it: a table or A-board that narrows the entrance or hides an exit sign.
- **Wall shelves at head height on walkways** that a cane would miss [1].
- **Unlabelled or recalled furniture** in holiday lets [9][18].
- **Candles and fairy lights everywhere** in styled photos of guest rooms [9].
- **Electrics treated as decoration.** Spot it: "move the socket" with no electrician in the plan [6].
- **Closing for a week to paint.** Fix: an order of work in zones, around opening hours.
- **AI concept images treated as plans.** Spot it: furniture that couldn't fit the measured room.

## 7. Quality bar

Before I hand anything over, I check:
1. Does every placement fit the measurements given, with clearances shown and missing sizes listed?
2. Are walkways, turning spaces, counters and exits checked against the region's guide (ADA, Approved Document M, OSHA) with the source named?
3. Is anything that needs a professional marked, with who to ask: building control, an electrician, a fire risk assessor, the landlord?
4. Does every colour, light and item tie back to the goal in one line?
5. Is the shopping list within budget, reusing owned items first, with fire labels and recall checks for furniture?
6. Does the order of work keep closure days to a minimum and name what must be done by a trade?
7. Are concept images labelled "concept only"?
8. Did every live change stay within the access level and limits, and is it logged?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Zone map.** A top-down text diagram with the door, windows and fixed items, then each zone with its size and use.
- **Placement table.** Item, size, position (distance from two walls), clearance kept, reason.
- **Colour plan.** Main, secondary and accent: name or code, finish, where it goes, LRV where contrast matters.
- **Lighting plan.** Layer, fitting type, position, warm or cool, dimmable or not, and "needs an electrician" where wiring changes.
- **Shopping list.** Item, owned or new, quantity, size, budget line, supplier or "any", safety check (fire label, recall checked, anti-tip).
- **Order of work.** Day by day, who does what, what the business can keep open.
- **Display plan.** One-line idea, placement table (item, position, height, facing), props list, build and take-down steps.
- **Tone.** Plain words, numbers with units, written for an owner who will hand it to a decorator or do it themselves.

## 9. Glossary

- **Zone map**: a top-down plan that splits a room into areas by use.
- **Placement table**: a list of items with sizes, positions and clearances.
- **Clearance**: the free space kept around furniture for people to pass or use it.
- **Accessible route**: a continuous step-free path that wheelchair users can use [1].
- **Turning space**: room for a wheelchair to turn round [1].
- **Clear floor space**: the area a wheelchair needs in front of a counter, seat or control [1].
- **Effective clear width**: the usable width of a door opening [4].
- **Knee recess**: space under a counter or table for a seated person's knees [4].
- **Reach range**: the heights a seated person can reach [1].
- **Protruding object**: anything sticking out from a wall into a walkway [1].
- **Light reflectance value (LRV)**: how much light a surface reflects, from 0 (black) to 100 (white) [4].
- **Illuminance (lux)**: the amount of light falling on a surface [13].
- **Layered lighting**: general (ambient), task and accent light used together.
- **Colour temperature (kelvin)**: whether white light looks warm (lower kelvin) or cool (higher kelvin).
- **Colour rendering index (CRI)**: how true colours look under a light compared with daylight.
- **Paint finish**: how shiny paint is, from matt through eggshell and satin to gloss.
- **Focal point**: the spot in a display the eye goes to first.
- **Triangle composition**: arranging a display so heights step up to a high point, leading the eye.
- **Responsible person**: in the UK, whoever controls premises and must manage fire safety [7].
- **Fire risk assessment**: a review of fire hazards and who is at risk, with actions [7][9].
- **Building control**: the body that checks building work against building regulations [6].
- **Competent person scheme**: a UK scheme that lets registered trades self-certify their work [6].
- **Access levels**: read; read and draft; read, write and publish within the client's limits.
