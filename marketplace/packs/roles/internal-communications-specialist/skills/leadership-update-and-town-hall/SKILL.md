# Leadership update and town hall

Staff trust leaders who explain why, say what they don't know, and answer the hard question first. This skill turns a leader's notes into an update that sounds like them, and plans a town hall where questions are asked, answered and followed up.

Template: check this with local rules or HR before the update or the answers touch pay, contracts, jobs, restructuring, consultation or health.

## When to use

- A monthly or quarterly leadership update.
- A quarterly town hall or all-hands, in person, online or hybrid.
- A new leader's first message to staff.
- A leader whose written updates read like a press release.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the leader's notes, past updates, and past town hall questions or Q&A exports. Level 2 to save the update as a draft and draft the event description, run of show and answers in the event tool. Level 3 to post the approved update and answers in the channels set, and to update event details. An update to all staff needs the leader's fresh yes every time.

- The leader's notes, in their words, and two past messages they wrote themselves.
- The topics: results, decisions, changes, people news.
- What is decided, what is not, and when the rest will be known.
- Anything sensitive, and whether HR or a legal adviser has seen it.
- The event format, platform, time and length; who presents; whether it is recorded.
- Questions already raised by staff, managers or past town halls.

## Steps

1. **Capture the voice.** From the leader's own past writing, note sentence length, words they use and avoid, how they open and close. Write as they would talk on a good day.
2. **Build the update.** For each topic: what happened, why it matters, what changes for staff, what we don't know yet, where to ask. Lead with what affects most staff. Put the reason in plain words; it is the part staff most often say is missing.
3. **Flag people topics.** Pay, jobs, contracts, restructuring and consultation go to HR or a legal adviser before the update is final. Check this with local rules or HR. If affected people should hear first, say so and hold the update until they have.
4. **Plan the town hall.** A run of show with timings: welcome (2 minutes), results and decisions (10 to 15), the hard topic, addressed directly (5 to 10), questions (the largest block you can protect), close and what happens next (2 to 3). Adjust to the slot.
5. **Write the script.** In the leader's voice, for the ear: short sentences, signposts, numbers rounded with what they mean. Mark slide cues and pauses. The speechwriter kit's timing and rehearsal skill covers delivery.
6. **Prepare the questions.** List the 10 questions staff are most likely to ask, starting with the hardest. Write a short, true answer for each, using only facts the leader confirmed. Where the honest answer is "we don't know yet", write that, why, and when they will know.
7. **Set up question collection.** Before: an anonymous form or a thread in the announcement channel, plus questions gathered by managers for staff without a desk. During: the event's Q&A tool (Teams events offer Q&A, and organisers can export it to a spreadsheet) or a moderated thread. Decide who picks questions and commit to answering the most-asked ones, not the easiest.
8. **Plan access.** Captions on (any Teams participant can turn on live captions), slides sent in advance, and a recording or summary for shift workers who cannot attend. Large Teams events offer AI captions only; for important events consider a human captioner.
9. **Rehearse.** A dry run on the platform, with the presenters, the slides and the Q&A tool. Microsoft advises always scheduling one for large events.
10. **Follow up within a week.** Publish answers to questions not reached, the recording or summary (large Teams event recordings expire after 30 days by default unless extended), and any decisions promised. Tell staff what happens next.

## Checks

- Sounds like the leader; they have read it aloud.
- Each topic says why, what changes for staff, and what is not yet known.
- People topics checked with HR or a legal adviser; check this with local rules or HR.
- Affected people heard first, where relevant.
- The hardest question is answered, not avoided.
- No promise the leader has not decided and cannot keep.
- Question routes for staff without a desk.
- Captions, slides in advance, and a recording or summary.
- Follow-up answers published.

## Output format

1. Leadership update (final draft) with an approval line.
2. Town hall run of show with timings and presenters.
3. Script with slide cues.
4. Questions and answers, hardest first, with [CONFIRM] where needed.
5. Question-collection and access plan.
6. Follow-up message template.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a logistics firm with 900 staff in six depots. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Update (extract), in the chief executive's voice:**

> Three things this quarter.
> First, we hit our delivery target in every depot except Leeds, which was hit hardest by the January storms.
> Second, we're opening two new sites next year, in Doncaster and Stoke. That means new roles, and we'll post them inside first.
> Third, the pay review. I said it would be done by March. It won't be. The new payroll system went live late, and I'd rather get your pay right than get it fast. You'll have your outcome by the end of May. I'm sorry for the delay.

**Hardest question:** "Is the pay review delayed because there's no money?"
**Answer:** "No. The budget for the review is set and hasn't changed. The delay is the payroll system. If that changes, I'll tell you first." [CONFIRM budget wording with the finance director and HR]
