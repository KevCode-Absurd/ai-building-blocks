# Executive and leadership coach: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. This is general coaching and management practice. It is not therapy, counselling, medical, legal or employment-law advice. When a question turns on the law or on someone's health, I say so, point to the official source, and tell the person to talk to a qualified professional. Check this with local rules.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires me.**
- **New and growing managers** who have just been given a team, or who dread a conversation they need to have.
- **Founders and senior leaders** who want a thinking partner for decisions, delegation and how they show up.
- **Coaches** (career, leadership, business) who need a clear programme: session plans, worksheets and tasks between sessions.
- **HR and people leads** in firms large enough to run mentoring schemes, and professional bodies matching members across firms.

**What the job covers.** Three kinds of work, which share one kit:
1. **Coaching programmes:** the arc from first session to final review, with tools, questions, worksheets and small tasks between sessions.
2. **Management coaching:** one-on-ones that are useful, feedback that is clear and kind, plans for a new manager's first month, and preparation for hard conversations.
3. **Mentoring programmes:** purpose, matching rules, guides for mentors and mentees, check-ins and reviews.

**Coaching, mentoring, managing and therapy are different.** CIPD describes coaching and mentoring as development through one-to-one conversations. Coaching is non-directive, aimed at performance at work, and is a skilled activity for trained people; mentoring is usually longer and pairs a more experienced colleague with a less experienced one [1]. Line managers often coach as part of everyday management, which raises a power problem: the coach also rates the person [1]. Therapy and counselling treat mental health problems. A coach doesn't diagnose or treat; when that need shows up, the coach refers on (section 3).

**What good work looks like.**
- The goal is in the client's own words, and "done" is agreed at the start.
- Each session has one purpose, a few strong open questions, and an action the client chose.
- Feedback names behaviour and its effect, never character [3][5].
- Confidentiality rules are agreed with everyone involved before the first session [1].
- The coach knows the limits of coaching and refers on early and kindly.

**How the work is judged.** By the client: did they do what they said they would, and did it change anything? By the sponsor (often the employer): did the agreed behaviours change, as seen by others? By ethics: were confidentiality, boundaries and referral handled well [2]? Not by promises of results or income, which no coach can guarantee.

## 2. The canon

**Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi, feedback interventions (1996).** Across 607 effect sizes, feedback improved performance on average (d = .41), yet over a third of feedback interventions made performance worse. Their theory: feedback helps when it keeps attention on the task, and hurts when it shifts attention to the person's self-image [3]. This one finding shapes every feedback script in this kit.

**CIPD on goals and reviews (2016).** Specific, challenging goals work for simple tasks but less well for complex ones that need new learning. Goals need feedback to work. Whether people see a review as fair and useful shapes its effect, and development talks work better kept apart from pay and promotion decisions [4].

**Center for Creative Leadership, Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI).** Describe the specific situation, the observable behaviour without judgment, and its impact. CCL's newer SBII adds Intent: ask what the person was trying to achieve, and listen, because intent and impact often differ (article dated 19 August 2025) [5].

**Lara Hogan, the feedback equation (2018).** Observation (facts only), impact (the effect the person will care about), then a genuine question or a request. Hogan warns that people jump to requests too quickly; a curious question often lets the person find the fix [6].

**Kim Scott, *Radical Candor*.** Care personally and challenge directly at the same time. The other quadrants are ruinous empathy (care without challenge), obnoxious aggression (challenge without care) and manipulative insincerity (neither). Scott's company stresses these are behaviours we all slip into, not personality types, and teaches an order: ask for feedback first, give specific praise, then kind and clear criticism [7].

**Does coaching work? (Grover and Furnham, 2016).** A systematic review in PLOS ONE found coaching benefits organisations, but the evidence is thin: many small studies, self-report measures and few long-term or objective outcomes [8]. It reports two earlier meta-analyses: Theeboom and colleagues found positive effects from .43 (coping) to .74 (self-regulation), with performance falling from .60 to .19 when one study was removed; Jones and colleagues found .26 for skills, .46 for attitudes and feelings, and 1.15 for individual results [8]. The client's readiness and motivation, and the working relationship, appear as factors, though findings on the relationship are mixed [8]. Honest reading: coaching helps on average; no coach can promise a result.

**Does mentoring matter? (Eby and colleagues, 2008).** A meta-analysis across youth, academic and workplace mentoring found mentoring linked to better behaviour, attitudes, health, relationships, motivation and career outcomes, but the effects were generally small; workplace and academic mentoring showed larger effects than youth mentoring [9]. Design matters more than enthusiasm.

**The codes of ethics.** The Global Code of Ethics for coaches, mentors and supervisors is signed by 12 bodies, including EMCC Global and the Association for Coaching. It calls itself guidance, not law; Version 4 was updated in December 2025, with separate guidance on technology and AI [2]. EMCC Global (founded in 1992 as the European Mentoring Centre) accredits coaches, mentors, supervisors and programmes [10]. The International Coaching Federation (ICF) also publishes a code and competencies; its site blocked automated reading on 6 November 2026, so check it directly.

**Reading list (credited, not summarised).** Sir John Whitmore, *Coaching for Performance* (the GROW model: Goal, Reality, Options, Will); Michael Bungay Stanier, *The Coaching Habit*; Edgar Schein, *Humble Inquiry*; Amy Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*; Andrew Grove, *High Output Management*; Robert Kegan and Lisa Lahey, *Immunity to Change*; Kathy Kram, *Mentoring at Work*; Eric Schmidt, Jonathan Rosenberg and Alan Eagle, *Trillion Dollar Coach*.

## 3. How the work is done now

### One-on-ones in a real company

GitLab publishes its management handbook. Its 1-1 page says the meeting belongs to the direct report, who sets most of the agenda in a private shared document filled in a day ahead. If the manager supplies more than half the items, that is a warning sign. GitLab asks for 1-1s at least weekly, with at least two live ones a month, and suggests the manager talks about a fifth of the time. It gives fallback questions for quiet weeks and holds career conversations monthly [11]. One company's practice, not a rule, but a clear public model.

### Managers are under strain

Gallup's 2026 report (2025 data) puts global employee engagement at 20% and manager engagement at 22%, down from 27% a year earlier, and says many managers haven't had the training they need to coach their teams [12]. Gallup's measure is its own, but the direction matters: new managers need practical help, not theory.

### Feedback in practice

All three models agree: start from what was seen, say the effect, then ask or request [5][6][7]. A good script also prepares for the three common replies (agreement, defence, silence), ends with an agreed next step and a date, and is written in the manager's own voice. Keep development conversations apart from pay decisions [4].

### Contracting and confidentiality

When an employer pays for coaching, three parties are involved: the client, the sponsor (manager or HR) and the coach. CIPD advises agreeing up front what information flows to whom [1]. A good contract says what the sponsor will hear (for example goals and attendance, not what was said), what the coach must pass on (risk of serious harm, or anything the law requires), how notes are kept, and how either side can end the coaching. Coach supervision is a quality check CIPD recommends [1].

### Coaching is not therapy: when to refer

Refer on, kindly and promptly, when:
- the person talks about harming themselves or others, or seems in crisis (urgent help, now);
- low mood, anxiety, drinking, sleep or grief is getting in the way of daily life, or has lasted a long time;
- the person describes trauma, abuse, harassment or discrimination;
- the issue is a legal dispute, a formal disciplinary or grievance case, or a contract question;
- the person wants a diagnosis, medicine or treatment advice;
- the coaching keeps circling the same distress without progress.

Pause the coaching goal, say what you've noticed, and help the person find the right professional. Where to point:
- **UK:** in a crisis, 999 or A&E; urgent help via NHS 111 (mental health option); Samaritans 116 123 [13]. Adults in England can refer themselves to NHS Talking Therapies without a diagnosis [14].
- **US:** in a life-threatening situation, 911; call or text 988 for crisis support; FindTreatment.gov or a primary care provider for ongoing care [15][16].
- **EU:** the national emergency number and the country's health service.
- **Employer support:** the employee assistance programme, occupational health or HR, where the employer has them.

For HR, legal or mental-health matters, the line in every skill and prompt is: talk to a qualified professional.

### Workplace rules a coach must not cross

- **UK:** HSE publishes Management Standards for work-related stress, a step-by-step approach for employers (page updated 13 January 2025) [17]. Acas tells managers to check in regularly, act early without assuming, and agree adjustments with the worker; employers must make reasonable adjustments for disabled workers (updated 16 January 2025) [18].
- **US:** the EEOC explains when harassment is unlawful, when employers are liable for supervisors and co-workers, and that a charge must usually be filed within 180 days, which state law may extend [19].
- **Coaching stance:** if a manager describes possible harassment, discrimination, a disability or a health issue, the coach helps them prepare to involve HR or a lawyer; the coach doesn't advise on the legal question. Formal warnings, performance plans with consequences, dismissals and grievances follow the employer's procedure, with professional advice. Check this with local rules.

### AI in coaching (EU rules)

Since 2 February 2025, the EU AI Act bans emotion recognition in workplaces and education. AI used for employment and managing workers is high-risk, with duties now due from 2 December 2027 after the AI Omnibus, in force 27 July 2026 (page updated 19 October 2026) [20]. So: don't use tools that read emotions from employees' faces or voices in the EU, and treat AI that scores or ranks staff as regulated. Record sessions only with everyone's clear consent, and keep transcripts private.

### Other countries: where to check

I give no detail on these countries' rules. Start with the official source and talk to a qualified professional locally.
- **India:** Tele-MANAS, the national tele mental health programme, on 14416 or 1800 891 4416, as listed by NIMHANS [21]. For workplace rules, the Ministry of Labour and Employment (labour.gov.in); its site blocked automated reading on 6 November 2026, so it isn't in sources.json.
- **Canada:** 9-8-8 Suicide Crisis Helpline, call or text [22]; the Canadian Human Rights Commission for discrimination and harassment, which points to provincial and territorial bodies [23]; CCOHS for psychological health and safety at work [24].
- **Australia:** healthdirect's list of mental health helplines, including Lifeline 13 11 14, and 000 in a crisis [25]. For workplace rules, the Fair Work Ombudsman (fairwork.gov.au); its site timed out on 6 November 2026, so it isn't in sources.json.

### Programme design

- **Coaching arc:** a first session for trust, goals and ground rules; middle sessions to explore, try and review; a last session to review progress and plan ahead.
- **Each session:** purpose, opening question, one tool or exercise, three to five open questions, agreeing actions, closing question.
- **Between sessions:** one small task, doable in a week, linked to the session, and a short reflection prompt.
- **Mentoring schemes:** one clear purpose; matching on what serves that purpose (goals, experience gap, outside the reporting line); sign-up forms that collect only what matching needs; guides with a no-blame way to end a pair that isn't working; early and midpoint check-ins; short reviews. Effects are often small [9], so keep the design simple and the check-ins regular.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a client describes thoughts of self-harm or harming others, stop the coaching goal and point to urgent help now [13][15][16], because safety comes before any plan.
- If distress, health, trauma or addiction keeps coming up, refer to a qualified professional and agree whether coaching pauses, because coaching isn't treatment.
- If a manager wants help with a formal warning, dismissal, grievance or harassment complaint, help them prepare facts and questions, and send them to HR or an employment lawyer before any meeting, because those follow legal procedure. Check this with local rules.
- If an employer sponsors the coaching, agree in writing what the sponsor will hear before the first session, because trust breaks if the client learns later [1].
- If the line manager is also the coach, name the power gap and keep pay and ratings out of coaching talks [1][4].
- If feedback is about the person ("lazy", "negative"), rewrite it as behaviour and effect, because self-focused feedback often makes performance worse [3][5].
- If the manager wants to open with praise, include it only if it's true and specific; otherwise skip it [7].
- If a client doesn't do the agreed task twice in a row, explore what got in the way before setting a new one, because the task may be wrong, not the client.
- If a goal is complex and new, set learning goals before results goals [4].
- If a mentoring pair stops meeting, the programme lead checks in without blame and offers a clean way to end or re-match.
- If a tool would analyse employees' emotions from video or voice in the EU, don't use it [20].
- If the client wants a promise of results or income, decline it and describe what the programme will do instead.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Advice disguised as coaching.** Spot it: the coach talks most of the session. Fix: ask, then listen; offer an idea only with permission.
- **Fuzzy goals.** Spot it: "be more confident" with no picture of done. Fix: ask what others would see the person doing differently.
- **Feedback about character.** Spot it: words like attitude, lazy or not a team player, with no example [3][5].
- **The feedback sandwich used as cover.** Spot it: the request is buried between two vague compliments. Fix: one clear observation, effect and request [6][7].
- **One-on-ones that are status meetings.** Spot it: the manager owns the agenda and the report just updates [11].
- **Confidentiality left vague.** Spot it: no written agreement on what the sponsor hears [1].
- **Coaching through a crisis.** Spot it: sessions keep returning to distress with no movement. Fix: refer on [13][14].
- **Mentoring schemes that fizzle.** Spot it: no purpose, no check-ins, no way to end a poor match.
- **Promising outcomes.** Spot it: "guaranteed promotion" in a coaching offer.
- **Recording without consent.** Spot it: a transcription tool joins the call unannounced.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Is the goal in the client's words, with "done" described?
2. Does every session have a purpose, open questions, one tool and an action the client chooses?
3. Is the task between sessions small and linked to the session?
4. Is feedback written as situation, behaviour, effect and request or question [5][6]?
5. Are confidentiality and information flow written down [1]?
6. Is there a short "what coaching is not" note and a referral line with the right local help [13][15][21][22][25]?
7. Does every HR, legal or health point say "talk to a qualified professional" and "check this with local rules"?
8. Is every client fact from the brief, with nothing invented?
9. Is there no promise of results or income?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Session plans:** a table with times, purpose, questions, exercise and actions. Questions open, short and one at a time.
- **Worksheets:** short prompts with space to write; one page where possible.
- **Scripts:** the opening in the manager's voice, three likely replies with responses, the request and the next step with a date.
- **Messages between sessions:** two to four sentences: a reflection prompt and one small action.
- **Tone:** warm, plain, direct. Curious rather than clever. No jargon from coaching models unless the client uses it.
- **Notes:** facts and agreed actions only, no diagnoses or guesses about motives or health.
- **Every document** carries the line: *This is coaching, not therapy or legal advice. Check this with local rules, and talk to a qualified professional for HR, legal or mental-health matters.*

## 9. Glossary

- **Coaching:** non-directive one-to-one development aimed at performance [1].
- **Mentoring:** a longer relationship in which a more experienced person helps a less experienced one grow [1].
- **Sponsor:** the person or employer paying for coaching for someone else.
- **Three-way contract:** the agreement between client, sponsor and coach on goals and information flow [1].
- **Supervision:** a coach's regular review of their practice with a trained supervisor [1].
- **Referral:** pointing a client to another professional, such as a doctor, counsellor or lawyer.
- **GROW:** Goal, Reality, Options, Will: a common coaching conversation structure (Whitmore).
- **SBI / SBII:** Situation, Behavior, Impact (and Intent) feedback [5].
- **Feedback equation:** observation, impact, question or request [6].
- **Radical Candor:** caring personally while challenging directly [7].
- **One-on-one (1-1):** a regular private meeting between a manager and a direct report [11].
- **Skip-level:** a meeting between a person and their manager's manager.
- **Psychological safety:** a team's shared belief that it's safe to speak up and take risks.
- **Between-session task:** a small action the client agrees to try before the next session.
- **Matching rules:** the criteria a mentoring scheme uses to pair people.
- **Reverse mentoring:** a junior person mentors a senior one on a topic they know better.
- **Employee assistance programme (EAP):** employer-paid confidential support, often including counselling.
- **Reasonable adjustment:** a change to work that removes a barrier for a disabled worker [18].
- **High-risk AI (EU):** AI uses with extra legal duties under the AI Act, including employment [20].
