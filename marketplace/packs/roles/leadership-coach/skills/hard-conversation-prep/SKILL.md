# Hard conversation prep

This is coaching, not therapy, legal or HR advice. Check this with local rules, and talk to a qualified professional (HR, an employment lawyer or a free official helpline) for any formal or legal matter, and a health professional for mental-health matters.

## When to use

- A team member keeps missing deadlines, interrupting, or doing good work in a way that causes problems.
- A manager needs to say something they've been avoiding.
- A peer or a manager's own boss needs to hear something difficult.
- A written feedback note is needed (see the `feedback-note-drafter` prompt for notes only).

**Stop and redirect if:** the matter is or may become a formal warning, a performance plan with consequences, a dismissal, a grievance, or involves possible harassment, discrimination, disability or health. Help the manager list the facts and questions, then send them to HR or an employment lawyer before any meeting.

## What you need

1. What the manager wants to change, in one sentence.
2. What they've seen: specific events, dates and effects. Facts only.
3. What they don't know (the person's side, outside pressures).
4. The relationship: how long they've worked together, how past feedback went.
5. What the person may say.
6. The manager's natural way of speaking (a few lines of how they'd normally talk).

**Access:** level 1 to read the manager's notes. Level 2 to save the script and a draft calendar invite or message. Level 3, within the manager's limits, to book the meeting and send the neutral invite to the named person. Anything that could be part of a formal process always needs a new yes and HR's involvement.

## Steps

### 1. Separate facts from labels

List what the manager has written. Turn each label into an observation:
- "He's lazy" becomes "The weekly report was late on 3, 10 and 17 March."
- "She's negative in meetings" becomes "In the last two team meetings, she said 'that won't work' to three proposals before they were explained."

If there's no fact behind a label, drop it. Research on feedback found that feedback which draws attention to the person's self-image, rather than the task, often makes performance worse.

### 2. Write the core message

Use the situation, behaviour, impact structure, then either a question or a request:
- **Situation:** when and where.
- **Behaviour:** what was seen or heard.
- **Impact:** the effect, choosing the one the person will care about (the team, the client, their own goals).
- **Question or request:** start curious ("What was going on that week?"), then a clear request if needed ("I need the report by 10am Fridays").
- **Intent:** ask what they were trying to achieve, and listen. Intent and impact often differ.

### 3. Draft the opening

Short and calm, in the manager's voice, saying why you're meeting. Mention something you value only if it's true and specific. Don't wrap the message in vague praise.

### 4. Prepare for three replies

- **Agreement:** "Thanks. What would help you hit Friday mornings?"
- **Defence or disagreement:** acknowledge, return to the facts, ask for their view. "You might see it differently. Here's what I saw: [facts]. What am I missing?"
- **Silence or upset:** slow down, give time, offer a break or a second conversation. If distress seems serious, pause the topic and point to support (employee assistance programme, a doctor, or urgent help if anyone is at risk).

### 5. Agree the next step

End with one agreed action, who does what, and a date to check in. Write a two-line follow-up message that confirms it.

### 6. Rehearse

Offer a role-play: the coach plays the person, using each of the three replies. Then adjust the script.

### 7. Check the risks

- Does anything in the script touch health, disability, pregnancy, family, religion, age or another protected characteristic? Remove it, and if it's relevant, send the manager to HR.
- Could this become formal? If so, HR or a lawyer before the meeting. Check this with local rules.

## Checks before handing over

- Every sentence about the person describes behaviour with a date or example.
- No labels, guesses about motives, or diagnoses.
- The opening is two or three short sentences and sounds like the manager.
- Each of the three replies has a prepared response.
- There is one clear request or question, and an agreed next step with a date.
- The script carries the line: check this with local rules; talk to a qualified professional for formal, legal or mental-health matters.

## Output format

1. One-sentence aim.
2. Facts table: label, the fact behind it, keep or drop.
3. Core message (situation, behaviour, impact, question or request, intent question).
4. Opening, in the manager's voice.
5. Three likely replies and responses.
6. Next step, check-in date and follow-up message.
7. Risk notes (what to take to HR or a professional).

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: Sam manages Leo at a design studio. Leo does excellent work but has missed four client deadlines in six weeks. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Aim:** Leo meets client deadlines or tells me two days ahead if he can't.
>
> **Opening (Sam's voice):** "Leo, thanks for making time. Your work on the [Client A] rebrand was the best we've done this year, and I want to talk about deadlines, because four have slipped since September and it's starting to cost us with clients."
>
> **Core message:** On the [Client A], [Client B] and two [Client C] deliveries (dates listed), files arrived one to three days late. Two clients called me to chase. What was going on for you those weeks?
>
> **If Leo defends ("The briefs kept changing"):** "That may be right, and I want to fix that too. On these four, here's what I saw. What would have helped you flag it earlier?"
>
> **Request:** If a deadline is at risk, tell me two working days before, so I can talk to the client.
>
> **Next step:** Check in on Friday 13 November; Sam to tighten how briefs change.
>
> Check this with local rules. If this becomes a formal matter, talk to HR or an employment adviser first.
