# Mentoring programme design

Mentors are not counsellors, and this is not HR or legal advice. Check this with local rules, including privacy rules for sign-up data, and talk to a qualified professional for HR, legal or mental-health matters.

## When to use

- An employer wants mentoring for a group (new managers, early-career staff, under-represented groups, returners).
- A professional body wants to pair new and experienced members across firms.
- A past scheme fizzled and notes from it are available.
- A reverse or peer mentoring scheme is planned.

## What you need

1. Why the organisation wants mentoring, and who it's for.
2. How many people, where they work, and how much time they can give.
3. The programme lead: who runs it and how much time they have.
4. Notes or survey results from any past scheme.
5. Tools for sign-up, messages and surveys.
6. Any limits: budget, time of year, languages, accessibility.

**Access:** level 1 to read past notes and survey results (groups large enough that no one can be identified). Level 2 to build the sign-up form, guides and messages as drafts. Level 3, within the programme lead's limits, to publish the approved sign-up form, send the agreed launch and check-in messages, and send the surveys. Matching decisions stay with the programme lead.

## What the evidence says (short)

A 2008 meta-analysis across youth, academic and workplace mentoring found mentoring linked to better outcomes, with generally small effects; workplace and academic mentoring did better than youth mentoring. CIPD describes mentoring as a longer relationship in which a more experienced colleague shares knowledge, working best when both sides learn. Takeaway: design for steady, regular meetings and clear purpose, not for big promises.

## Steps

### 1. Write one purpose

One sentence that every later rule serves, for example: "Help first-year managers handle their first team." If the sponsor lists five purposes, ask them to choose one main one.

### 2. Decide the shape

- **Type:** one to one, group, reverse (junior mentors senior on a topic they know better) or peer.
- **Length:** agree a fixed length with a clear end, so pairs know when they're done.
- **Rhythm:** how often pairs meet and for how long.
- **Ending:** a closing meeting and a way to continue informally if both want.

### 3. Set matching rules

Match on what serves the purpose: goals, the experience gap, skills wanted and offered, and practical fit (time zone, format). Common rules: not in the same reporting line; the mentee has some say. Write the sign-up questions that collect only what matching needs. Leave out health, family and protected characteristics unless the scheme's purpose needs them and people volunteer them, with a clear privacy notice.

### 4. Write the mentor guide and mentee guide

Each one or two pages:
- Roles: what a mentor does (listens, shares experience, asks questions, opens doors) and doesn't do (manage, assess, counsel, fix).
- First meeting agenda: introductions, hopes, how and when to meet, what's confidential.
- Boundaries and confidentiality: what stays in the pair, and what must be passed on (serious risk of harm, or anything the law or company policy requires).
- When to refer: if a mentee raises health, distress, harassment, discrimination or a legal issue, the mentor listens, doesn't advise on it, and points to the employee support service, HR or a professional. If someone is at risk, urgent help comes first.
- If the pair isn't working: a no-blame way out through the programme lead, with an offer to re-match.

### 5. Write session templates

First meeting, a middle meeting and a final meeting, each with an agenda and four or five conversation prompts (goals, progress, a challenge, reflection, what's next).

### 6. Plan the programme lead's check-ins

An early check-in after the first few meetings, and one at the midpoint: short messages to each person ("Have you met? Is it useful? Anything I can help with?"). Quiet pairs get a friendly nudge and the offer of a no-blame exit.

### 7. Write the surveys

Midpoint and end, five to eight questions each: meeting frequency, usefulness, progress on goals, what would improve the scheme, and one open question. Report only for groups large enough that no one can be identified, and say how answers will be used.

### 8. Write the launch messages

Announcement, sign-up form text with a privacy notice, matching confirmation, and a closing thank-you.

## Checks before handing over

- The purpose drives every rule (matching, length, guides, survey).
- Sign-up collects no more personal data than matching needs, with a privacy notice.
- The guides cover confidentiality, referral, and what happens when a pair stalls.
- Mentors are told plainly they are not counsellors.
- Check-ins and surveys have owners and dates.
- No promises of promotion or outcomes.
- Every template carries: check this with local rules; talk to a qualified professional for HR, legal or mental-health matters.

## Output format

1. Programme design on a page: purpose, who, shape, length, rhythm, ending.
2. Matching rules and sign-up questions (with privacy notice).
3. Mentor guide and mentee guide.
4. Session templates (first, middle, final).
5. Check-in plan and messages.
6. Midpoint and end surveys, and how to act on results.
7. Launch, confirmation and closing messages.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up example: Calder Engineering, a fictional 600-person firm, wants mentoring for early-career engineers.*

> **Purpose:** Help engineers in their first two years build the judgment to run a small project on their own.
>
> **Matching rules:** mentor has led at least one project; not in the mentee's reporting line; match on the project type the mentee wants next; mentee picks from two suggested mentors.
>
> **Sign-up questions (mentee):** What kind of project do you want to run next? What do you want to get better at? How do you prefer to meet (in person, video)? Any times you can't meet?
>
> **If the pair isn't working:** "Either of you can tell the programme lead, privately, at any time. No reasons needed, no blame, and we'll offer a new match."
>
> Mentors are not counsellors. Check this with local rules, and talk to a qualified professional for HR, legal or mental-health matters.
