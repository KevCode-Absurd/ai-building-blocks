# One-on-one system

This is coaching and planning, not therapy, HR or legal advice. Check this with local rules, and talk to a qualified professional for HR, legal or mental-health matters.

## When to use

- A first-time manager has a team of two to ten people and no meeting rhythm.
- A manager takes over an existing team (see also the `new-manager-30-day-plan` prompt).
- One-on-ones have become status meetings, or keep being cancelled.
- A manager wants a better way to track what they promised.

## What you need

1. The team: first names or initials, roles, how long each person has been in the team, and whether anyone is new, remote or part-time.
2. The manager's calendar limits and preferred rhythm (weekly or every two weeks).
3. Tools the team already uses for notes and chat.
4. Anything the manager already knows needs attention (without health or private details).

**Access:** level 1 to read the team list and calendar free and busy times. Level 2 to create the shared notes documents and tentative calendar holds. Level 3, within the manager's limits, to book the recurring meetings and send a short invite message to the named team members.

## Steps

### 1. Set the rules of the meeting

Write a short note for the team:
- **It's your meeting.** The direct report owns most of the agenda. GitLab's public handbook makes the same point and treats a manager-filled agenda as a warning sign.
- **Rhythm:** weekly or every two weeks, at a fixed time. Cancel only as a last resort; move rather than skip.
- **Length:** agree it with each person; shorter and regular beats long and rare.
- **Private shared notes:** one document per person, shared only between the two of you, filled in before the meeting.
- **Not a status meeting:** project updates go in the team channel; this time is for blockers, growth and feedback both ways.
- **Pay is separate.** Pay and ratings are discussed in a different meeting, because development talks work better apart from pay decisions.

### 2. Build the agenda template

Five standing headings, in the report's order of choice:
1. How are you? (work and workload)
2. Progress and wins since last time
3. Blockers: what's in the way, and what the manager can remove
4. Growth: a skill, a stretch task, a career question
5. Feedback both ways: one thing to keep, one thing to change

Then: actions agreed, with owner and date.

### 3. Write the question bank

Three to five questions for each heading, and fallback questions for quiet weeks, such as:
- "What's taking more of your time than it should?"
- "What did you learn this week that surprised you?"
- "What's one thing I could do differently that would help you?"
- "If you ran the team for a week, what would you change first?"
- At the end, not on the agenda: "What would make work better for you right now?"

### 4. Plan eight weeks

A rolling plan with a light theme per week, so conversations don't repeat: getting to know you and how you like to work; current priorities; blockers and tools; feedback on how I manage; a skill to build; career hopes; a mid-point check on how these meetings are going; review and reset. The report can always override the theme.

### 5. Make promises visible

A follow-up tracker: date raised, what was promised, who, by when, done. The manager reviews it at the start of each meeting. Broken small promises cost trust fastest.

### 6. Add the growth conversation

Once a month, use one meeting for growth: what the person wants next, the skills that need work, one stretch task, and support agreed. Keep notes in the shared document.

### 7. Write the "when to get help" box

Short guidance for the manager:
- If someone raises health, family, harassment, discrimination or a complaint, listen, thank them, don't promise secrecy you can't keep, and involve HR. Talk to a qualified professional before acting.
- If someone seems in crisis or at risk, help them get urgent support: 999 or NHS 111 in the UK, 911 or 988 in the US, 9-8-8 in Canada, 000 or Lifeline 13 11 14 in Australia, Tele-MANAS 14416 in India, or the local emergency number. Point to the employee assistance programme if there is one.
- If performance may become formal, follow your company's procedure and get HR advice first. Check this with local rules.

## Checks before handing over

- The direct report owns the agenda, and the template says so.
- Pay and ratings are kept out of these meetings.
- The notes template is private to the two people.
- The eight-week plan has a theme each week and a review point.
- The tracker has owners and dates.
- Notes guidance says: facts and actions only, no health details or guesses about motives.
- The "when to get help" box is there, with local help lines.

## Output format

1. Team note: how our one-on-ones work (half a page).
2. Agenda template.
3. Question bank by heading, plus fallback questions.
4. Eight-week plan table: week, theme, two suggested questions.
5. Notes template and follow-up tracker.
6. Monthly growth conversation guide.
7. "When to get help" box.
8. Calendar plan (level 3: booked; otherwise a list for the manager to book).

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up example: Dana has just become lead of a five-person support team at a fictional software firm, Kestrel Apps.*

> **Rhythm:** weekly, 30 minutes, Tuesdays and Wednesdays, booked for eight weeks. Moves allowed; skips only for leave.
>
> **Week 1 theme, getting to know you:**
> - "What's the best and worst part of your week usually?"
> - "How do you like to get feedback: in the moment, in writing, or here?"
>
> **Tracker row:** 4 Nov | Ask product team for a fix date on the refund bug | Dana | 7 Nov | open.
>
> **Notes rule:** what we agreed and who does what. No health details. Both of us can see this document; no one else can.
>
> Check this with local rules. For HR, legal or mental-health matters, talk to a qualified professional.
