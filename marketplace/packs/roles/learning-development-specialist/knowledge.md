# Learning and development (L&D) specialist: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general workplace learning practice. It is not legal advice on apprenticeship funding, health and safety duties, pay or employment law, which differ by country, state and sector. Where a step turns on those rules, I point to the official guidance and say who to ask. Check this with local rules before you rely on any legal point here.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires an L&D specialist.** Owners, operations leads, HR leads and training coordinators in organisations that have no full L&D team, from a 6-person garage taking its first apprentice to a 400-person logistics firm with a training budget and a board asking what it bought. The agents built on this kit split the work eight ways:

- training needs analysis, an annual learning plan and a budget;
- skills matrices, career ladders and development plans;
- on-the-job training built from the client's own procedures (SOPs), with sign-off forms;
- week-by-week onboarding paths for new hires;
- workshop plans with timed agendas and facilitator notes;
- apprentice and trainee programmes with a skills log and mentor guide;
- software rollout training with reference cards and practice tasks;
- short training sessions with a job aid and a follow-up check.

**What good work looks like.**
- It starts from a business result and the behaviour that drives it, not from a topic or a course catalogue.
- It checks whether training is the fix at all. Many gaps come from unclear roles, missing tools, bad processes or hiring, and no course fixes those.
- Most of the time is spent practising the real task, with feedback, not listening.
- Managers are involved before and after, because transfer to the job happens at work, not in the room [6].
- The evaluation is planned before the training is built, with the measures agreed with the people who paid for it [1].
- Every step, name and warning comes from the client's own material. Gaps are listed, not filled with guesses.

**How the work is judged.**
- Do people do the job differently afterwards (behaviour), and does the business measure move (results) [1]?
- Time to competence for new hires and trainees.
- Sign-off rates, error rates, rework and safety incidents on the trained tasks.
- Whether the plan matched the budget and the busy seasons.
- Whether managers and staff would use the materials again without the L&D person in the room.

**What this role never does.** Set pay or pay bands; judge named staff for discipline or dismissal; decide whether someone passes probation; sign off licensed, regulated or safety-critical training without the client's qualified expert; certify qualifications; or give funding, levy, tax or legal advice.

## 2. The canon

**Donald Kirkpatrick, the four levels of training evaluation.** Kirkpatrick set out the four levels in the 1950s, around his doctoral work on a training programme for supervisors [2]. The levels as his firm describes them today [1]:
- **Level 1, Reaction:** did people find it favourable, engaging and, above all, relevant to their job?
- **Level 2, Learning:** did they gain the knowledge, skill, attitude, confidence and commitment intended? Confidence and commitment are early signs of whether they will use it.
- **Level 3, Behaviour:** do they apply the critical behaviours at work, with support and accountability?
- **Level 4, Results:** did the targeted business outcomes happen?

Kirkpatrick Partners says to plan from Level 4 backwards, using leading and lagging indicators the organisation already tracks, and not to wait 90 days before checking Level 3. Jim and Wendy Kayser Kirkpatrick put the weight on Level 3 and business partnership in the 2010s, and a 2026 update adds the performance environment (what helps or blocks people at work). The firm's current model also uses return on expectations, agreed in advance with stakeholders, and "contributive ROI", which estimates the share of improvement reasonably linked to learning rather than claiming training alone caused it [1].

**Jack and Patti Phillips, the ROI Methodology.** ROI Institute adds a fifth level, return on investment. Its steps: isolate the effect of the programme (control groups, trend lines, estimates by participants and managers), convert the business gain to money, count fully loaded costs (needs analysis, design, delivery, participants' time, evaluation), then report the benefit-cost ratio (benefits divided by costs) and ROI (net benefits divided by costs, as a percentage). It reports intangible benefits that are not turned into money and asks for conservative choices at every step [4].

**Will Thalheimer, LTEM.** The Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model has eight tiers. The lower tiers cover measures that cannot show learning worked; the upper half measures transfer to the job. Thalheimer argues the four-level model puts all learning in one bucket, and LTEM splits it into knowledge, decision-making and task performance. Version 13 was released on 31 October 2024 [5]. Practical reading: a quiz on facts is not evidence that someone can make the decision or do the task.

**Eduardo Salas, Scott Tannenbaum, Kurt Kraiger and Kimberly Smith-Jentsch, "The Science of Training and Development in Organizations" (2012).** What matters before, during and after training. Before: a learning climate that supports it, and a training needs analysis that sets what to train, who needs it and how it fits the system. During: help people into the right mindset and use sound instructional principles. After: make sure people transfer it to the job, and evaluate so the next round improves [6].

**Talya Bauer, "Onboarding New Employees: Maximizing Success" (SHRM Foundation, 2010).** The Four Cs: compliance (rules and paperwork), clarification (the job and how it is judged), culture (how things work here) and connection (the relationships a new person needs). Bauer reported that about 30% of organisations stopped at passive onboarding (mostly compliance) and about 50% at a middle level, where culture and connection were covered only in part [11]. Her four levers for a new hire's adjustment are confidence in doing the job, role clarity, social integration and knowledge of the culture.

**Training Within Industry, Job Instruction.** A wartime method for training people who were new to their tasks: break the job into the one best way (what to do, how, and why), present and demonstrate it, let the worker learn by doing, then check progress with coaching and questions [27]. Most good on-the-job training today is a version of this.

**Henri Lipmanowicz and Keith McCandless, Liberating Structures.** Simple, timed formats that include every voice. The best known, 1-2-4-All, runs about 15 minutes: a minute alone, two minutes in pairs, five in fours, then a few points shared with the whole group; 1-3-All suits online or large groups. The content is free under CC BY-SA 4.0 [12].

**The 70:20:10 idea.** The claim that most learning happens on the job, some through others and a little in courses. The institute that promotes it calls it a reference model and a metaphor, not a measured ratio, and says the split varies with the work [25]. Use it to remind managers that practice and coaching at work matter; never use it to cut all formal training or as a budget rule.

**Reading list (credited, not quoted).** Robert Mager and Peter Pipe, *Analyzing Performance Problems*; Thomas Gilbert, *Human Competence*; Cathy Moore, *Map It*; Robert Brinkerhoff, *The Success Case Method*; Ruth Colvin Clark, *Evidence-Based Training Methods*. All push the same habit: find the performance problem first, then design the smallest thing that fixes it, and check it worked.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Training needs analysis

- Start with next year's business goals, then gather evidence of gaps: survey results, review themes (never named individuals), manager requests, error and incident logs, customer complaints, ticket topics, audit findings. Count each gap and show where the number came from.
- Sort every gap into "training can fix this" (people do not know how) and "something else" (no time, wrong tools, unclear rules, mixed messages from managers, wrong hire). CIPD's 2023 survey found 57% of L&D professionals now try to understand a performance issue before recommending a solution, up from 32% in 2021 [7]. That habit is the core of the job.
- Rank fixes by business impact, number of people and urgency. Link evaluation to the gap you found, so the result can be checked against it later [8].
- When the plan needs money, build a short business case. CIPD's 2026 pathway is built for small organisations too, and notes that cases are often won in conversations as much as in documents [28].

### Skills frameworks, matrices and career ladders

- A skills matrix lists 10 to 15 skills per role, a rating scale with plain meanings for each step, and ratings by the person and their manager. The useful outputs are thin spots, skills held by only one person, and a ranked list of fixes.
- Borrow words rather than invent them. Free frameworks as of November 2026: **SFIA 9** for digital and IT roles (published October 2024, seven levels of responsibility: Follow, Assist, Apply, Enable, Ensure and advise, Initiate and influence, Set strategy, inspire and mobilise; free to use after registration) [13][14]; **ESCO** v1.2.1 from the European Commission (3,039 occupations, 13,939 skills, 28 languages, free API) [15]; **O*NET** from the US Department of Labor (900+ occupations, over 19,000 task statements) [16].
- Career ladders describe each level by scope, impact and examples of evidence. Pay stays out of the ladder; promotion rules go to HR, and anything that changes contracts goes to an employment adviser.

### Designing programmes

- Pick the format from the gap. Knowledge gaps suit short e-learning or a job aid. Skill gaps need practice with feedback: on the job, in role-play or in a test account. Judgement gaps need realistic cases and discussion.
- In the US, training time per employee fell to 40 hours in 2025 from 47 in 2024, and online or computer-based delivery made up the largest share of training hours (34%), ahead of classroom (28%) and virtual classroom (24%) [10]. Short, practical, mixed formats are the norm.
- Sessions run in short blocks: a quick explanation, a worked example, then practice on a case from the client's own work, including one tricky case.
- Workshops have a clear output (a decision, a plan, a skill), a timed agenda that adds up, breaks in anything over 90 minutes, and a write-first or pairs option so quiet people are heard [12].
- Every programme leaves something behind for the job: a one-page job aid, a checklist or a reference card.

### On-the-job training from SOPs

- Read the SOP line by line and mark each step a new person must do, see or decide. Keep every safety warning word for word, before the step it covers.
- Show, try, check, sign off: the trainer demonstrates with one line of why per step, the learner does it while watched, the trainer coaches, and sign-off happens only after watched attempts against a pass line [27].
- If the SOP has gaps or steps out of order, list them for the owner to fix. Do not fill them in.

### Onboarding paths

- Work back from "ready": the tasks a fully ready new hire can be watched doing. Split the time into weeks with two or three goals each, mostly hands-on, with a readiness check and manager check-in questions at the end of each week.
- Cover all Four Cs [11]. **Real example:** IBM's Assimilation Process, as Bauer described it in 2010, had three stages: affirming before day one (welcome, workstation ready, a coach assigned), beginning in the first 30 days (met in person, introduced to the team, roles clarified, check-in times set) and connecting through the first year, with a coach check-in at two months and a focus on how work gets done at four to six months [11].

### Apprentices and trainees

- **England.** Apprenticeships last 8 months to 6 years. The employer makes an apprenticeship agreement and a training plan, pays at least the minimum wage, gives time off in the working week for training and has the apprentice work with experienced staff [17]. Skills England lists 2,087 apprenticeships as of 6 November 2026, now in three forms: apprenticeship standards, foundation apprenticeships and apprenticeship units. For new starts from 1 August 2025 the minimum duration is 8 months [18]. Funding rules for starts from 1 August 2026 to 31 July 2027 are in version 3, updated 3 August 2026 [19]. The skills log follows the wording of the standard the provider uses.
- **US.** A Registered Apprenticeship combines a paid job with rising wages, structured on-the-job learning with a mentor, classroom education and a nationally recognised credential, approved by the Department of Labor or a State Apprenticeship Agency [20].
- **EU and elsewhere.** Schemes are national. Point to the national apprenticeship authority and the training provider.
- I design the skills log, weekly plan, mentor guide and reviews. Funding, levy, pay, contracts and assessment decisions go to the provider, the official pages and an adviser.

### Required training

- **Great Britain:** employers must give adequate information, instruction, training and supervision, with particular care for new recruits, people changing jobs or taking on extra duties, young workers and safety representatives (HSE page updated 6 June 2025) [22].
- **US:** many OSHA standards contain specific training requirements; required training must be provided and paid for by the employer; OSHA advises keeping a record of all safety training; some standards require training in a way the employee understands [23].
- **EU:** the 1989 Framework Directive requires adequate safety and health training for each worker, and costs of safety measures may not be passed to workers [24].
- **EU AI literacy:** Article 4 of the AI Act has applied to organisations that provide or use AI systems since 2 February 2025, and national authorities can enforce it from 2 August 2026. No certificate is required; keeping internal records of training is suggested. The Commission's suggested minimum: a general grasp of AI and how the organisation uses it, whether the organisation is a provider or a user (deployer), the risks of the systems in use, and training fitted to staff knowledge and context. The 2026 Digital Omnibus kept the duty but removed a required "sufficient" level (Commission Q&A updated 20 October 2026) [21].
- I design required training from the client's own rules and the official pages. A competent person in the client's business signs off safety content.

### Evaluation

- Plan it at the start: what result the sponsor expects, which behaviours drive it, how and when each level is measured [1].
- Use Level 1 questions about relevance and intent to use, not enjoyment. Use Level 2 checks that ask people to decide or perform, not recall [5].
- Check behaviour within weeks, not months, using observation, work samples, manager check-ins or system data [1].
- Use the full Phillips ROI only for large, costly programmes where a sponsor needs money figures, and say how the effect was isolated [4].

### AI in L&D

- LinkedIn's 2025 report found 71% of L&D professionals exploring, experimenting with or already using AI in their work [9]. AI speeds up drafting; it does not know the client's SOPs, risks or people. Everything it writes is checked against the client's material.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a manager asks for "a course on X", ask what people should do differently and how you would see it, because a topic is not a goal and cannot be evaluated.
- If the gap comes from tools, process, unclear rules or workload, say so and suggest the non-training fix, because training cannot fix what people already know how to do [7].
- If a sponsor wants proof of results, agree the Level 4 measure and its baseline before design starts, because results cannot be shown without a before [1].
- If you can only measure one thing, measure behaviour on the job, because that is where learning turns into results [1].
- If a check only asks people to recall facts, add a decision or task question, because recall does not show people can do the work [5].
- If the programme is large and the board asks for money figures, use the ROI Methodology with fully loaded costs and a stated isolation method; otherwise report return on expectations [1][4].
- If an SOP contains a safety warning, keep it word for word before the step it covers and have a competent person sign off the training [22][23].
- If a trainee's programme follows a published standard, write the skills log in the standard's words [17][18].
- If a question is about funding, levy, pay, contracts or whether training is legally required, point to the official page and an adviser, and do not answer it.
- If a skills matrix shows a skill held by one person, rank it as a risk and plan cross-training before other gaps of the same size.
- If a workshop runs long, cut content before cutting practice time.
- If the client uses AI systems in the EU, include AI literacy in the plan and keep a record of who was trained on what [21].
- If ratings come from named people, keep them out of any shared report and show team-level results only.

## 6. Common mistakes

- **The catalogue plan.** Spot it: the plan lists courses from a provider's brochure with no business goal next to each. Fix: tie every programme to one goal and one counted gap.
- **Training as the answer to everything.** Spot it: no one asked why people are not doing the task now. Fix: run the "is it a training problem?" check first.
- **Happy sheets as proof.** Spot it: the only data is end-of-session satisfaction scores. Fix: add relevance and intent questions, a performance check and a behaviour check [1][5].
- **Evaluation planned at the end.** Spot it: no baseline exists. Fix: agree measures before design.
- **Talk-heavy sessions.** Spot it: most of the agenda is slides. Fix: practise on real cases for at least half the time.
- **SOP gaps filled with guesses.** Spot it: steps in the training that are not in the SOP. Fix: list gaps for the owner.
- **No manager role.** Spot it: managers find out about the training afterwards. Fix: a briefing before and check-in questions after [6].
- **Onboarding as paperwork.** Spot it: week one is forms and videos, with no real task and no buddy [11].
- **Skills matrix vanity.** Spot it: 60 skills per role and everyone rated 3 out of 4. Fix: fewer skills, plain level meanings, evidence for each rating.
- **Myth numbers.** Spot it: forgetting-curve or 70:20:10 percentages with no study behind them [25][26].
- **Sign-off on attendance.** Spot it: a signature means "was there", not "can do it". Fix: watched attempts against a pass line.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Does each programme name the business goal, the gap it fixes (with its count and source) and the behaviour that should change?
2. Is every step, name, number and warning taken from the client's material, with gaps listed?
3. Is at least half the time spent practising, with feedback?
4. Do the timings add up and fit the hours and busy seasons the client gave?
5. Is there an evaluation plan for each programme across the four levels, with owners and dates?
6. Are named people's ratings and reviews kept out of shared documents?
7. Are legal, funding, pay and safety sign-off points flagged for the right person?
8. Would a shift lead or first-time manager be able to run it from the page, in plain words?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Formats.** Tables for plans, calendars, matrices and evaluation plans; numbered steps for trainer guides; one-page job aids; timed agendas with a running clock; sign-off forms with a pass line for each step.
- **Goals.** Written as actions you could watch, such as "close the slicer safely and log it", never "understand food safety".
- **Tone.** Practical, warm and direct. Respect for frontline staff: short sentences, the words they use on shift, no jargon.
- **Evidence.** Every number shows its source. Every gap shows its count.
- **Hand-offs.** Each plan ends with "Open questions" for the client: missing SOPs, unclear owners, costs to confirm, legal or safety sign-offs.

## 9. Glossary

- **L&D:** learning and development, the function that builds the skills an organisation needs.
- **TNA (training needs analysis):** finding which gaps exist, who has them, and whether training is the fix [6].
- **Performance gap:** the difference between what people do now and what the job needs.
- **Kirkpatrick levels:** reaction, learning, behaviour and results, the four levels of training evaluation [1].
- **Return on expectations (ROE):** success measures agreed in advance with the sponsor [1].
- **ROI Methodology:** the Phillips approach that adds a fifth level, return on investment [4].
- **BCR (benefit-cost ratio):** programme benefits divided by programme costs [4].
- **Isolation:** working out how much of a change came from the programme rather than other causes [4].
- **LTEM:** Thalheimer's eight-tier Learning-Transfer Evaluation Model [5].
- **Transfer:** using what was learned on the job [6].
- **Skills matrix:** a grid of skills per role with a rating scale and ratings per person.
- **Single point of failure:** a skill only one person holds.
- **Career ladder:** levels in a job family described by scope, impact and evidence.
- **SFIA:** Skills Framework for the Information Age, a skills and levels framework for digital roles [13].
- **ESCO:** the EU's free classification of occupations and skills [15].
- **SOP:** standard operating procedure, the written way a task is done.
- **Job aid:** a one-page checklist or guide used at the moment of work.
- **Sign-off:** a record that a person was watched doing a task to the pass line.
- **Job Instruction (TWI):** the prepare, demonstrate, try, follow-up method of on-the-job training [27].
- **Four Cs:** compliance, clarification, culture and connection in onboarding [11].
- **Apprenticeship standard:** the published description of an occupation an English apprenticeship trains for [18].
- **Registered Apprenticeship:** the US Department of Labor's approved apprenticeship model [20].
- **AI literacy:** the EU AI Act duty to give staff enough understanding of the AI they use [21].
- **1-2-4-All:** a Liberating Structures format that moves from solo thinking to pairs, fours and the whole group [12].
