# Skills matrix and career ladder

## When to use

- A team of roughly 10 to 150 people needs to see which skills it has, which it needs and where it depends on one person.
- People ask "how do I get promoted?" and nobody can answer with evidence.
- A training needs analysis needs skill data by role and level.
- A manager wants six-month development plans that are more than "go on a course".

Do not use it to set pay or pay bands, run performance reviews, or decide whether a named person is promoted. Those decisions belong to the client, with HR and, where contracts change, an employment adviser. Check this with local rules before any promotion process goes live.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** job descriptions, team lists with role and level, existing frameworks, rating exports (names replaced with IDs where possible).
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** build the matrix and rating sheet as a draft spreadsheet or form; draft skills fields or competency records in the HR or learning system without publishing them.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send the approved rating form to the approved team, update skills fields for the people named in the limits, and publish the approved ladder where staff find it. Anything affecting pay, grade or contract needs a new yes each time. Log every action.

## What you need

1. Roles in scope, team size, and why the client wants the matrix (gaps, cover, promotion, hiring).
2. Job descriptions or a short list of what each role does in a normal week.
3. Any framework already in use: a provider standard, SFIA for digital roles, ESCO or O*NET occupation pages.
4. For a ladder: the job family, current levels and two or three real examples of work at each level.
5. For a gap report: the ratings, ideally both self and manager, with names replaced by IDs.

## Steps

### 1. List the skills per role

Keep to 10 to 15 per role, mixing job skills ("reconcile a supplier statement") and ways of working ("explains a fix to a customer"). Write each as something you could watch. Borrow wording from the client's framework, or from SFIA, ESCO or O*NET, so the words are consistent; adapt rather than copy whole lists.

### 2. Write the scale

Four steps, each with a plain meaning and an example:
1. **Learning:** knows what it is; does it only with help.
2. **Doing with checks:** does it; someone checks the result.
3. **Doing alone:** does it reliably without checks, including unusual cases.
4. **Teaching:** can teach it, check others and improve the method.

Ask for one piece of evidence for every rating of 3 or 4.

### 3. Build the matrix and rating sheet

Rows are people (IDs), columns are skills, cells are ratings. Add a required level per role. The rating sheet asks the person first, then the manager, then a short conversation where they differ by two steps or more.

### 4. Read the ratings

- **Thin spots:** skills where few people reach the required level.
- **Single points of failure:** skills where only one person is at 3 or 4.
- **Over-rating signs:** everyone at 3 with no evidence.
- **Ready to teach:** people at 4 who could coach others.

Rank fixes by risk: a single point of failure on a critical task comes first.

### 5. Draft development plans

Per person, two or three skills for six months. For each: how to build it on the job (shadow, do with checks, take over a task), who coaches, and what evidence shows progress. Courses only where practice alone will not do.

### 6. Draft a career ladder (if asked)

For one job family, levels from entry to senior. Each level states scope (what they own), impact (who relies on their work), the skills at their required levels, behaviours, and two examples from the client's own work. If the client uses SFIA, align level descriptions with its levels of responsibility.

### 7. Draft promotion criteria (if asked)

The evidence needed to move up, who decides, how often, and how a person asks. Mark every rule HR should check, and leave pay out.

## Checks

- Each skill is observable, and each role has 10 to 15.
- Scale steps have plain meanings and examples.
- Ratings of 3 or 4 have evidence.
- No names in shared reports; team-level views only.
- Single points of failure are listed and ranked.
- Pay, grades and contract changes are out of scope and flagged.
- "Check this with local rules" appears on promotion criteria.
- At level 3, only the approved people and fields were changed, and the log is updated.

## Output format

1. Skills list per role with required levels.
2. Rating scale with meanings and examples.
3. Matrix template and rating sheet (paste-ready table or CSV).
4. Gap report: thin spots, single points of failure, ranked fixes.
5. Development plans (one table per person, IDs only in shared copies).
6. Career ladder and promotion criteria, if asked, with "Open questions for HR".

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a plumbing supplies firm, customer support team of 14 across three levels. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Skill 7:** Handles a delivery dispute end to end (checks the proof of delivery, offers the policy options, logs the outcome).
> Required: Level 1 role at 2, Level 2 at 3, Level 3 at 4.
>
> **Gap report:** Only one person (ID S-04) rates 4 on "trade account credit checks", and S-04 is the only person who trains it. Risk: high, because trade orders stop when S-04 is away. Fix 1: S-09 and S-11 shadow S-04 for two weeks, then do credit checks with S-04 checking, aiming for level 3 by March. Evidence: 10 checked cases each with no errors.
>
> **Ladder, Level 2 (Senior advisor):** Owns complex disputes and coaches new advisors on the phone. Others rely on their notes for escalations. Example from your work: "resolved the double-billing complaint for a trade account and wrote the fix into the FAQ".
>
> *Promotion criteria are a draft. Check this with local rules and your HR lead before use.*
