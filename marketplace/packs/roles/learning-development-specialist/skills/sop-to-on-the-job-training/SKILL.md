# SOP to on-the-job training

## When to use

- A business has a written SOP and new or moved staff who must learn it on the floor, not in a classroom.
- Shift leads train people but were never shown how, so training differs by who is on shift.
- The client needs proof that someone was watched doing a task to standard before working alone.

Do not use it to write the SOP from scratch, to approve licensed, regulated or safety-critical training without the client's qualified person, or to judge named staff for pay or discipline. Required safety training follows the client's own rules and the official guidance where they work; check this with local rules.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the SOP, station guides, incident or error notes, existing training records.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the module, trainer guide, sign-off form and job aid as drafts; set up a draft course or checklist in the learning system without assigning it.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved module, assign it to the named new starters, and record sign-offs that a named trainer has confirmed, within the client's limits. Changing a past training record or deleting one needs a new yes. Log every action.

## What you need

1. The SOP, in full, with its version date.
2. Who trains (role), where (busy floor, quiet room, vehicle), and how long they can spend.
3. Who learns: new or moved staff, their language level, any reading or language needs.
4. The mistakes people make now, or the ones the SOP warns about.
5. Who signs off, how many watched attempts the client wants, and what happens if someone is not ready.
6. The name of the client's competent person who approves safety content.

## Steps

### 1. Read the SOP line by line

Mark each step as **do**, **see** (check something) or **decide**. Copy every warning, caution and legal line word for word, and note which step it belongs to. List gaps: missing steps, steps out of order, unclear terms, equipment named in one place and not another. Send the gap list to the SOP owner; do not invent fixes.

### 2. Set 2 to 4 goals people can show

Each is a whole task someone could be watched doing, with its standard: "close down the slicer safely and log it on the cleaning sheet". Not "understand slicer safety".

### 3. Write the demonstration

For each step in the SOP's order and words: what the trainer does, and one line on why it matters. The warning comes before the step it covers, said and shown. Break the job into its important steps and the key points within each, as Job Instruction does: what, how and why.

### 4. Write practice tasks

The learner does the task while the trainer watches. Build each from a real mistake: "the slicer guard is left up", "the log is filled in before the clean is finished". Start with the normal case, then one awkward case. Include what good looks like.

### 5. Write the trainer guide

What to say at each step, what to watch for, the common slips and how to correct them kindly ("show me the next step again; what does the SOP say comes first?"). Add how to pace it on a busy shift: which steps can be taught in a quiet ten minutes.

### 6. Build the sign-off form

Each step as something the trainer can see, a pass line for each ("guard down and locked before wiping"), space for two or three watched attempts with dates, the trainer's and learner's initials, and a "not ready yet" route: more practice, a new date and who decides.

### 7. Write a short knowledge check

Five to ten questions on the decisions and warnings, with answers and the SOP section each tests. Prefer "what do you do if..." over "what is...".

### 8. Make the job aid

The SOP cut to one page for the station: steps in order, the warnings, the check at the end, and who to call. Large type, short lines, pictures described for the client to photograph.

### 9. Final check against the SOP

Every step is in the SOP, nothing is added, the order matches, warnings come first, and the words suit the staff. Mark the module with the SOP version it was built from.

## Checks

- Every step and warning traces to the SOP version named on the cover.
- No steps were added or reordered; gaps are listed for the owner.
- Every goal is a task you can watch, with a standard.
- Sign-off needs watched attempts against a pass line, not attendance.
- The client's competent person is named for safety sign-off.
- Plain words, short sentences, readable on a phone or a laminated sheet.
- At level 3, only the named people were assigned or signed off, and the log is updated.

## Output format

1. Cover: SOP name and version, goals, time needed, trainer, approver.
2. SOP gap list for the owner.
3. Demonstration script (table: step, trainer does, says why, warning).
4. Practice tasks with what good looks like.
5. Trainer guide.
6. Sign-off form.
7. Knowledge check with answer key and SOP sections.
8. One-page job aid.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a corner deli, closing-down SOP v3 (12 May), new part-time staff trained by shift leads. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Goal 2:** Clean and close the meat slicer safely and record it on the cleaning sheet.
>
> | Step | Trainer does | Says why | Warning (from SOP, word for word) |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 4 | Turns the slicer off and unplugs it at the wall | "Off at the switch is not enough; unplugged means it cannot start." | "Unplug before any cleaning. Never rely on the on/off switch." |
> | 5 | Sets the blade guard and thickness dial to zero | "Zero closes the gap so the blade edge is covered." | none |
>
> **Practice task 2 (from a real slip):** The slicer is still plugged in and the cleaning cloth is ready. Learner closes it down. Good looks like: unplugs first without a prompt, sets dial to zero, says why.
>
> **Sign-off line for step 4:** Unplugs at the wall before touching the blade area, on 2 of 2 watched attempts.
>
> **SOP gap for the owner:** Step 7 says "sanitise" but does not name the product or contact time. Please add before training starts.
