# Training evaluation plan

## When to use

- Before a programme is designed, so the measures and baseline exist.
- When a sponsor or board asks what a programme achieved.
- When the only evidence so far is end-of-session satisfaction scores.
- When deciding whether to keep, change or stop a programme.

Do not use it to rate named individuals for pay or discipline, or to claim a programme alone caused a business change when other causes were not checked.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** programme outline, business measures the client already tracks, survey and quiz exports, system usage or quality data, with names replaced by IDs where possible.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** build the surveys, quizzes and observation forms as drafts in the client's survey or learning tool; draft the report.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send the approved surveys to the approved groups on the agreed dates, send reminders, close them, and pull the agreed reports, within the client's limits. Messaging a new group or sharing results beyond the named audience needs a new yes. Log every action.

## What you need

1. The programme: who attends, goals, format, dates.
2. The sponsor's expectation in their words: what should be different, by when.
3. The business measures already tracked that relate to it, with recent values.
4. Who can observe behaviour on the job (managers, quality checkers, system data).
5. The minimum group size for reporting survey results, set before any survey goes out.
6. If ROI is needed: costs (fully loaded) and how the client values the business measure.

## Steps

### 1. Start at Level 4: results

Agree with the sponsor the result they expect, the measure, the baseline and the date ("refund handling time, now about 9 minutes, target 5 by end of Q2"). Add one or two leading indicators that move sooner. Write these down as the return on expectations: what success looks like to the people paying.

### 2. Name the critical behaviours: Level 3

Two to four things people must do on the job for the result to move, each observable ("uses the refund checklist on every call over £50"). For each: the support they get (job aid, manager check-in, coaching), how it will be seen (observation, call review, system log), who checks, and when. Check within the first two to six weeks, not only at 90 days.

### 3. Design Level 2 checks that test doing

For each goal, a check that asks people to decide or perform: a scenario question, a role-play scored against a rubric, a task in a test account. Add two confidence and commitment questions ("How confident are you to use this on your next shift?", "What will you do first?"). Recall questions alone do not show people can do the work.

### 4. Write Level 1 questions about relevance

Three to five questions: how relevant it was to their job, what they will use, what got in the way, what to cut. Avoid "did you enjoy it" as the main question. Collect them at the end and, for longer programmes, mid-way.

### 5. Add ROI only if it is needed

If the sponsor needs money figures and the programme is large: say how the effect will be isolated (comparison group, trend line, or estimates by participants and managers with a confidence adjustment), convert the result to money using values the client gives, count fully loaded costs, then report the benefit-cost ratio and ROI with the intangible benefits beside them. If isolation is not possible, report return on expectations instead and say why.

### 6. Plan a success-case follow-up

Pick a few people who used it well and a few who did not. Ask what helped and what blocked them. This explains the numbers and finds blockers in the workplace, not in the training.

### 7. Build the timeline and owners

One table: level, measure, method, when, who collects, where it is stored.

### 8. Report results honestly

When data arrives: results by level, against baseline, with group sizes. Say what the evidence shows, what else could explain it, and what you recommend: keep, change, scale or stop. Never report groups below the minimum size.

## Checks

- Level 4 measure, baseline and date agreed with the sponsor before design.
- Every critical behaviour is observable and has an owner and a date.
- Level 2 includes decision or task checks, not only recall.
- Survey groups meet the minimum size; no individual is identifiable.
- Claims about cause match the method used.
- Costs and money values come from the client.
- At level 3, surveys went only to the approved groups, and the log is updated.

## Output format

1. One-paragraph summary: expectation, measures, timeline.
2. Evaluation table across the four levels (measure, method, timing, owner).
3. Level 1 questions; Level 2 checks with rubric; Level 3 observation form.
4. ROI plan (if needed) with isolation method.
5. Success-case interview questions.
6. Report template, then the report when data arrives.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Quillmoor Home Insurance, 45-minute refund handling session for 30 new support agents.*

> | Level | Measure | Method | When | Owner |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | 4 Results | Average refund handling time (baseline about 9 min); refund complaints per month (baseline 14) | Phone system report; complaints log | Monthly, Feb to Jun | Support manager |
> | 3 Behaviour | Uses the refund checklist on calls over £50; offers both refund options | 5 recorded calls per agent scored with the checklist | Weeks 2 and 6 | Team leads |
> | 2 Learning | Handles 3 scenario calls correctly; confidence 1 to 5 | Role-play scored with rubric; two questions | End of session | Trainer |
> | 1 Reaction | Relevance to the job; what blocks use | Four questions | End of session | Trainer |
>
> **Return on expectations (agreed 14 Jan):** handling time under 6 minutes by end of Q2, with no rise in complaints.
>
> **Not claimed:** the new refund policy launched the same month, so any change in complaints may come from both. The report will show both lines.
