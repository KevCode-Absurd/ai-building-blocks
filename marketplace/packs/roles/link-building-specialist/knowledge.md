## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires me.** Small brands with real know-how or data but few links: a local service business, an online shop in a crowded category, a software company with usage data, a professional firm with expert staff. Often the SEO work on the site is done and pages still sit behind competitors with more and better links. Sometimes the owner has been offered a "guest post package" and wants a second opinion.

**What good work looks like.**
- Links come from real sites with real readers, on topics close to the client's, because an editor chose to link.
- Each campaign starts from a story or resource someone would cite even if links passed no ranking credit.
- Outreach is small, personal and polite, and goes only to people the client approved.
- Paid placements, if any, are labelled for readers and marked `rel="sponsored"` for search engines.
- Reports show what was earned, what was lost and what it means, without inflating the numbers.

**How the work is judged.**
- Relevant referring domains won, by campaign, and the pages they point to.
- Coverage and brand mentions, linked or not, and referral visits from them.
- Movement in search visibility for the linked pages over months, read with care because many things move rankings.
- Zero manual actions for unnatural links, and no links that would embarrass the client if a journalist looked.

## 2. The canon

**Sergey Brin and Larry Page, "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" (1998).** Links as votes weighted by the importance of the page that casts them, and anchor text as a description of the target. Google still lists link analysis and PageRank among its core ranking systems, while saying PageRank has evolved a lot since launch [9].

**Google Search Central documentation.** The rules of the game. The spam policies define link spam [1]; "qualify your outbound links" says how paid and user-generated links must be marked [2]; the link best practices page covers anchor text [10]. When a practitioner blog and Google's own page disagree, Google's page wins for what Google does.

**Matt Cutts, "The decay and fall of guest blogging for SEO" (2014).** Writing on his own blog, the man who headed Google's webspam team for many years declared guest blogging for links "done" because it had become too spammy, while keeping a place for guest posts written for exposure and readers [27]. Google repeated the point in 2017 for large-scale article campaigns [6].

**Brian Dean, the Skyscraper Technique (Backlinko, 2013).** Find content that has already earned many links, make something clearly better, then tell the people who linked to the original [26]. Still a useful frame, with a caveat: "better" must mean more useful to readers, not just longer.

**Eric Ward and Garrett French, *Ultimate Guide to Link Building* (2013).** A practitioner's case for links as the result of relevance and relationships: find the people who care about a topic and give them a reason to cite you. Credited as reading.

**The FTC's native advertising guide (2015) and endorsement guides (revised 2023).** Readers must be able to tell an ad from editorial content, and paid or gifted relationships must be disclosed clearly [18][19]. These shape every paid placement and every product sent to a blogger.

## 3. How the work is done now

### What Google calls link spam (as of 6 November 2026)

Google treats links made mainly to manipulate rankings as link spam [1]. Its list includes:
- buying or selling links for ranking purposes, including money, goods or services, or a free product in return for a write-up with a link;
- excessive link exchanges and partner pages made only to cross-link;
- automated programs or services that create links;
- requiring a link in terms of service or a contract without letting the publisher qualify it;
- advertorials or native ads with links that pass ranking credit, and optimised anchor text in articles, guest posts or press releases on other sites;
- low-quality directory and bookmark links, keyword-rich links hidden in widgets, and widely distributed footer or template links;
- forum comments with optimised links, and low-value content made mainly to build links [1].

Google also says buying and selling links is a normal part of advertising, and such links are fine when marked `rel="nofollow"` or `rel="sponsored"` [1].

**How Google enforces it.** Its link spam updates aim to nullify unnatural links rather than only punish them: the December 2022 update used SpamBrain to detect sites buying links and sites used to pass outgoing links, and any credit those links passed is lost [5]. Sites that publish or acquire excessive sponsored or guest posts without the right link marking can also get manual or algorithmic actions [4]. Spam updates are frequent: in 2026 Google started them on 24 March, 24 June, 18 August and 24 September [11].

### Marking links

- `rel="sponsored"` for ads and paid placements; `rel="ugc"` for comments and forum posts; `rel="nofollow"` when neither fits. Values can be combined [2].
- Since September 2019 Google treats all three as hints, and from 1 March 2020 nofollow also became a hint for crawling and indexing. Existing nofollow links did not need changing [3].
- Affiliate links should carry `rel="sponsored"` [4].
- Anchor text should be descriptive, concise and relevant, without cramming keywords [10]. When pitching, never demand a particular anchor; editors choose their own words.

### Site reputation abuse and expired domains

- **Site reputation abuse:** third-party pages published on a strong site mainly to borrow its ranking signals, such as a sponsored payday loan review on an educational site [1]. Wire services, syndicated news, user forums, columns and properly handled affiliate links are not abuse in themselves [1]. In November 2024 Google clarified that first-party involvement or oversight does not change the third-party nature of such content [7].
- **EEA change:** from 30 August 2026, inside the EEA Google may separate an affected section so it ranks on its own, instead of applying a manual action's effect; outside the EEA manual actions still apply [8]. The change followed discussion with the European Commission [8].
- **Expired domain abuse:** buying an expired domain and reusing it mainly to rank low-value content, such as casino pages on a former school site [1].
- For link builders: never sell or arrange "parasite" pages on other people's strong sites, and never buy expired domains to redirect their links.

### Guest posts

Guest articles are fine when they inform another site's readers. They become a problem when the main aim is building links at scale: many sites, writers who don't know the topic, reused articles and keyword-rich links [6][27]. If a client writes guest posts, links in the author bio or body should be few and natural, and the host decides how to mark them.

### The tactics that work within the rules

- **Digital PR from a story.** Original data, a survey, a useful tool or an expert view, shaped around one headline a journalist could write. The pitch leads with the finding, explains why it matters to that outlet's readers, and offers the data, a quote or an image. Media lists and embargoes are in the PR specialist kit.
- **Expert comment.** Journalist request services send daily queries from reporters looking for sources. HARO, now shown as powered by Featured, lets sources subscribe free [28]. Answer only where the client has real expertise, quickly and briefly.
- **Unlinked mentions.** Set Google Alerts for the brand, products and founders [16], and check a backlink tool's mentions report. Where an article names the client without a link, a short, friendly note asking for one is reasonable. Many editors will say no; that is fine.
- **Broken link outreach.** Find pages on relevant sites that link to dead resources, look up what the dead page said with the Wayback Machine [17], and offer the client's page only where it genuinely covers the same thing.
- **Resource and "useful links" pages.** Associations, councils, universities and local groups often list helpful resources. A free guide or tool can earn a place on merit.
- **Local links.** For local businesses, Google says prominence, which includes how many websites link to the business, is one of three factors in local ranking, and there is no way to pay for a better local ranking [15]. Local news, suppliers, sponsorships of real local events, trade associations and chambers of commerce are the natural sources. A sponsorship link that comes with payment should be marked sponsored.
- **Skyscraper-style improvement.** Improve on a resource that already earned links, then tell those who linked to it [26]. Use it where the client can make something clearly more useful.

### Measuring links

- Google Search Console's Links report shows top linked pages, top linking sites and top linking text. It is a sample, not every link; tables stop at 1,000 rows and exports at 100,000; and it does not say whether a link is nofollow [14].
- Third-party tools (Ahrefs, Semrush, Majestic) crawl the web on their own and give their own scores. Ahrefs' Domain Rating, for example, is Ahrefs' own 0 to 100 logarithmic score of backlink strength, not a Google metric; it ignores nofollow links, can be inflated by spam and can fall without any lost links [25]. Use such scores to compare and sort, never as a target or a promise.

### Clean-up: manual actions and disavow

- If Search Console shows a manual action for unnatural links to the site, download the links, check them against the spam policy, ask site owners to remove or qualify the bad ones, document the attempts, disavow only what can't be removed, and request a review [13].
- Google says most sites will not need the disavow tool. Use it only with a considerable number of spammy, artificial or low-quality links that have caused or will likely cause a manual action [12]. A new upload replaces the old list, and changes take weeks [12]. Disavowing natural links can do harm, and disavowing everything without trying removal may get a review request rejected [13].

### Disclosure of paid placements (not legal advice)

- **US.** The FTC's native advertising guide says paid content must be recognisable as advertising, with plain labels such as "Ad" or "Sponsored Advertising Content", placed in front of or above the headline; "Promoted" is ambiguous and a logo alone is not enough [18]. Its endorsement guides cover gifted products and paid posts by bloggers and creators: material connections must be disclosed clearly and close to the endorsement [19]. In 2016 Lord & Taylor settled FTC charges that it deceived consumers through a paid article in an online fashion magazine and paid Instagram posts by 50 "fashion influencers"; the final order was approved on 23 May 2016 [20].
- **UK.** The ASA and CAP say payment or another reciprocal arrangement brings consumer protection law into play, and brand control over the content brings the CAP Code too. "Advertisement Feature" or "Ad" upfront is acceptable; "Sponsored" alone is not recommended [21].
- **EU.** The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive covers misleading marketing, and the Commission's guidance notice of 17 December 2021 covers influencer marketing and endorsements [22]. Check the member state's rules.
- In every region, the link in a paid placement also carries `rel="sponsored"` [2]. Disclosure to readers and marking for search engines are two separate duties.

### Outreach email rules (not legal advice)

- **US.** CAN-SPAM covers commercial email, with no exception for business-to-business messages: accurate headers and subject lines, a postal address and opt-outs honoured within 10 business days [24]. A pure editorial pitch may not be "commercial", but a pitch that promotes a product should be treated as if it is.
- **EU.** A journalist's or site owner's name and work email are personal data. Legitimate interest can be a legal basis only when the interest is real, the processing is necessary, and it doesn't override the person's rights and reasonable expectations [23]. Keep only what the campaign needs and remove anyone who asks.
- **UK.** Check the Information Commission's guidance on direct marketing by email before cold outreach that promotes a product [29]. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)).

### Case study: Backlinko's skyscraper post

In 2013 Brian Dean published a long list post on Google ranking factors, then emailed sites that linked to similar content. He reports sending 160 emails and earning 17 links, and that organic traffic to the whole site roughly doubled within 14 days [26]. These are his own figures from one campaign by a well-known SEO writer; they show the method, not a rate to expect.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client has nothing new to say (no data, tool, expertise or story), build the asset before planning outreach, because pitches without a story are spam to editors.
- If a link would be worth having even if it passed no ranking credit (real readers, close topic, could send visits), pursue it; if its only value is ranking, drop it.
- If anyone offers or asks for money, goods or services in return for a followed link, refuse, because that is link spam under Google's policy [1].
- If the client wants a paid placement for exposure, prepare it at level 2 with a clear ad label and `rel="sponsored"`, and get a fresh yes before any money is committed [2][18][21].
- If a product is sent to a blogger or creator for review, require disclosure of the gift in the content, because the FTC treats it as a material connection [19].
- If a prospect site sells "guest post packages", lists prices for links, or publishes on every topic, score it as skip, because those are signs of link selling.
- If a site mentions the client without a link, ask once, politely, and accept no.
- If outreach would go to someone not on the approved list, stop and ask, even at level 3.
- If a pitch demands specific anchor text, remove the demand, because optimised anchors in articles are on Google's link spam list [1].
- If the backlink profile shows a sudden spike of links from unrelated sites, check for a negative attack or an old vendor before disavowing anything, and disavow only when a manual action is present or likely [12].
- If an editor asks for payment in return for a followed link, decline and record the site as a seller.
- If a Google spam update starts, wait until it finishes before reading ranking changes [11].

## 6. Common mistakes

- **Buying "high DA" guest posts.** Spot it: sudden links from unrelated sites with exact-match anchors, all with "write for us" pages.
- **Treating third-party scores as Google's.** Spot it: goals written as "DR 50+ links" with no relevance check [25].
- **Mass outreach.** Spot it: the same email to hundreds of addresses, no personal line, follow-up after follow-up.
- **Pitching without a story.** Spot it: the email asks for a link but never says why readers would care.
- **Unlabelled paid content.** Spot it: a paid article with no ad label and followed links [1][18].
- **Free products with no disclosure.** Spot it: reviews of gifted products that never mention the gift [19].
- **Disavowing by reflex.** Spot it: huge disavow files built from a tool's "toxic" score with no manual action [12].
- **Counting links instead of value.** Spot it: monthly reports with totals but no relevance, page or referral data.
- **Ignoring lost links.** Spot it: no list of links lost after site changes, often from redirects that were never set.
- **Expired domains and redirects.** Spot it: a plan to buy old domains and point them at the client's site [1].

## 7. Quality bar

Before handing over, an expert checks:

- Every campaign has one sentence a journalist could use as a headline, and the data or expertise behind it.
- Every claim in a pitch comes from the client's own data or a cited source, and every number is checkable.
- The prospect list shows why each site fits (topic, readers, editorial standards) and flags any seller signs.
- Outreach goes only to the approved list; contact details come from the client's tools or the site's published contact page.
- Each pitch is personal, short, offers something useful and asks for no specific anchor.
- Paid placements, if any, are approved, labelled for readers and marked `rel="sponsored"`.
- Reports separate followed, nofollow, sponsored and unlinked mentions, and show lost links worth chasing.
- Nothing promises coverage, links or rankings.

## 8. How this role writes

- **Asset plan:** working headline, the finding or resource, the data needed, effort, the kinds of sites likely to cover it and why.
- **Prospect sheet:** site, page, topic fit, reader signal, editorial signal, seller signals, score (pitch, maybe, skip), contact source, notes.
- **Pitch email:** subject that states the finding; first line on why it matters to that outlet's readers; the proof; an easy offer (data, quote, image); a polite close with no demand for a link. Under 150 words.
- **Follow-up:** one, short, adding something new (a local cut of the data, a quote), not "just bumping this".
- **Monthly report:** links won by campaign and page, link type, mentions without links, lost links worth chasing, what worked, what to repeat, three next steps.
- **Tone:** plain, honest, never pushy with editors and never inflated with clients.

## 9. Glossary

- **Backlink:** a link from another site to yours.
- **Referring domain:** a distinct site that links to you; ten links from one site count as one referring domain.
- **Anchor text:** the clickable words of a link [10].
- **Followed link:** a link with no qualifying rel value, which may pass ranking credit.
- **rel="sponsored":** marks a paid or advertising link [2].
- **rel="ugc":** marks a link in user-generated content [2].
- **rel="nofollow":** asks search engines not to associate your site with the target; now a hint [3].
- **Link spam:** links made mainly to manipulate rankings [1].
- **Manual action:** a penalty applied by a Google reviewer, shown in Search Console [13].
- **Disavow:** a file asking Google to ignore certain links, for manual-action cases only [12].
- **SpamBrain:** Google's AI-based spam detection system [5][9].
- **Site reputation abuse:** third-party content placed on a strong site mainly to borrow its rankings [1][7].
- **Expired domain abuse:** reusing an expired domain mainly to rank low-value content [1].
- **Digital PR:** earning coverage and links with stories, data and expert comment.
- **Unlinked mention:** an article that names you without linking.
- **Broken link outreach:** suggesting your page to replace a dead link on someone else's page.
- **Linkable asset:** a piece of content made to be worth citing, such as a study or tool.
- **Domain Rating and similar vendor scores:** a backlink tool's own score of a site's link strength, such as Ahrefs' DR; not a Google metric [25].
- **Advertorial:** paid content styled like editorial; must be labelled as an ad [18][21].
- **Material connection:** a payment, gift or relationship that must be disclosed in an endorsement [19].
