# Link-worthy asset plan

## When to use

- A site needs links and has nothing yet that other sites would cite.
- The client has data, expertise or a tool but has never turned it into something public.
- A planned campaign has a topic but no angle a journalist would use.
- The product looks dull and someone needs to find the story in it.

Reading the client's site, data and coverage needs level 1. Drafting the asset page in the client's website as unpublished needs level 2. Publishing the asset page needs level 3, within the site sections the client named. Commissioning a paid survey or paid data needs the client's fresh yes, and the client pays.

## What you need

1. The business: what it sells, to whom, where, and the pages it wants links to.
2. What it has that others don't: anonymised data, customer surveys it is allowed to use, staff expertise, a calculator or tool, a strong opinion, local knowledge.
3. Who covers its field: trade press, local news, consumer sites, blogs, newsletters, associations.
4. Past coverage or campaigns, and what happened.
5. Limits: data that can't be shared, legal or regulatory topics to avoid, people who can and can't be quoted.

## Steps

### 1. Inventory the raw material

List every possible source of a story: datasets (orders, bookings, support tickets, claims, usage), staff with deep knowledge, tools, internal reports, customer surveys, local ties, and any strong view the founder holds. For each, note whether it can be shared, anonymised and aggregated safely. Personal data never leaves the client; only aggregated findings do.

### 2. Write the headline first

For each idea, write the headline a journalist could run, in their words: "Dog owners in the North East claim for injuries twice as often as Londoners". If the headline is dull, needs three caveats, or could be written without the client's data, drop or rework the idea. The test: would an editor care if no link were involved?

### 3. Choose the format

- **Data study:** a finding from the client's own data or a survey, with a clear method.
- **Tool or calculator:** something people use and others link to as a resource.
- **Expert guide:** a reference page that answers a question better than what exists.
- **Local angle:** a cut of national data by city or region, or a genuinely local story.
- **Opinion with evidence:** a contrarian view backed by the client's experience and numbers.
- **Improved resource:** something clearly more useful than a page that already earned many links (the skyscraper approach).

### 4. Check it is real and safe

Every number must come from the client's data or a cited source, with a short method note: sample, dates, how it was collected. If a survey is needed, say who would run it and that the client approves and pays. Avoid topics where a wrong claim could cause harm (health, money, safety) unless an expert checks it. Never make up a statistic or a quote.

### 5. Match outlets and reasons

For each idea, list three to five kinds of sites likely to cover it (national consumer press, a trade title, local news, niche blogs, association resource pages) and the reason their readers would care. Name example publications only if the client confirms they fit.

With web search connected, I also check whether the same story ran recently and who covered it, since a story already told needs a new angle. Without it, I ask the client for recent coverage in the field and mark the idea "freshness not checked".

### 6. Score effort and fit

For each idea: effort (low, medium, high) with what drives it (data cleaning, design, a survey, a developer for a tool), the link target page it supports, and the main risk (data too thin, topic too crowded, legal sensitivity). Don't attach a promised number of links.

### 7. Recommend and plan

Pick one idea to start, with a short build plan: data work, page draft (level 2), review by the client and any expert, publish (level 3), then hand over to outreach with the `outreach-pitch-and-follow-up` skill.

### 8. Say so when there is no story

If nothing passes step 2, say it plainly and suggest how to create one: a short customer survey, a local cut of public data, a free tool, an expert column. Outreach without a story wastes the client's reputation with editors.

## Checks before handing over

- Every idea has a headline a journalist could use, written before the plan.
- Every claimed number has a source in the client's data or a cited public source.
- No personal data leaves the client; only aggregated findings.
- Each idea names the page it supports and why that page matters.
- Effort and risk are stated; no promised link counts or rankings.
- Paid surveys or data purchases are flagged for the client's approval and payment.
- The recommended idea fits the client's real expertise.

## Output format

1. Verdict: is there a story now? One paragraph.
2. Idea table: headline, format, raw material, data needed, effort, target page, outlet types and why, main risk.
3. Recommended idea and build plan.
4. Questions for the client.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: "Pawsure", a UK pet insurer with five years of anonymised claims data.*

> **Verdict:** yes, there is a story. The claims data is unusual and covers the whole UK.
>
> | Headline | Format | Data needed | Effort | Target page | Outlets and why | Risk |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | "Spring is the riskiest season for dogs: claims for eating something toxic peak in April" | Data study | Claims by month and cause, 2021 to 2025 | Medium | Seasonal safety guide | Pet blogs and national consumer pages: timely advice for owners | Needs vet review of the safety advice |
> | "Which UK cities have the most cat road accidents?" | Local cut | Claims by postcode area | Medium | Cat insurance page | Regional news: a local ranking readers share | Small samples in some areas; publish only areas above a minimum count agreed with the client |
> | "What a vet visit really costs: a calculator" | Tool | Average claim costs by treatment | High | Pricing guide | Money and pet sites: a useful reference | Costs date fast; needs yearly updates |
>
> **Recommended:** the spring study. It fits a news moment, uses data nobody else has and supports a page that already converts.
>
> **Questions:** can we name the vet who reviews the advice? Who signs off the method note?
