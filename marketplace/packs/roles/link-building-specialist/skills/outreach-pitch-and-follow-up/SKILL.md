# Outreach pitch and follow-up

## When to use

- An asset is published (a study, tool or guide) and the approved prospect list is ready.
- The client is named in articles that don't link to it.
- Pages in the client's field link to a dead resource the client's page could replace.
- A journalist request matches the client's expertise.

Writing pitches needs no access. Saving them as drafts in the client's mailbox or outreach tool needs level 2. Sending them needs level 3, and only to the list the client approved for this campaign, within the agreed daily limit and dates. A quote attributed to a named person needs that person's approval of the exact words each time. Any offer of payment, goods or a free product in return for coverage needs the client's fresh yes, and a gifted product must be disclosed by the recipient.

## What you need

1. The asset: its headline finding, the URL, the method note and any images or data files.
2. The approved list for this campaign: site, contact, angle from the `link-prospect-scoring` skill.
3. Who the email comes from (a named person at the client) and their role.
4. Who can be quoted, and the approved quotes.
5. For unlinked mentions: the article URL and the exact mention.
6. For broken links: the page with the dead link, the dead URL, what it used to say (from the Wayback Machine) and the client's matching page.

## Steps

### 1. Lead with the story, not the ask

The main pitch, under 150 words:
- **Subject:** the finding in plain words, no clickbait, no "quick question".
- **Line 1:** the finding, with the number.
- **Line 2:** why it matters to this outlet's readers.
- **Line 3:** the proof (how the data was gathered, sample, dates).
- **Offer:** the full data, a quote from the named expert, a chart or image they can use.
- **Close:** a polite line; no request for a link, no anchor text, no fake urgency.

### 2. Write two angle variations

Different site types need different reasons: a local news version leads with the local cut; a trade version leads with what the finding means for practitioners. Same facts, different first two lines.

### 3. Personalise each send

One line per recipient showing you know their work: a recent article on the topic and why this adds to it. If you can't write that line honestly, the site probably belongs on the maybe list.

### 4. Unlinked mention version

Short and friendly: thank them for the mention, say where readers could find more (the page URL), and offer anything useful (an updated figure, an image). Ask once. Accept no.

### 5. Broken link version

Tell them the specific link is dead (page and link text), say what it used to cover, and suggest the client's page only if it covers the same thing. Offer an alternative from another site too, if one exists; it builds trust.

### 6. Journalist request answers

Answer only where the client has real expertise. Lead with the direct answer, then two or three lines of specific experience, the expert's name, role and approved credentials. Keep to what was asked and the deadline.

### 7. One follow-up

One follow-up after a few working days, adding something new (a regional figure, an approved quote, a chart). Never "just bumping this". No second follow-up unless the client agrees.

### 8. Check before sending

- Every number comes from the client's data or a cited source.
- No payment, gift or link swap is offered for a followed link. If the client wants paid exposure, that is a separate, labelled ad with `rel="sponsored"` and the client's fresh yes.
- If a product is sent for review, the email asks the recipient to disclose the gift.
- US: if the email promotes a product, follow CAN-SPAM: accurate sender and subject, the client's postal address, and a working way to opt out, honoured within 10 business days.
- EU and UK: keep only the contact details the campaign needs, have a reason the contact would reasonably expect the email, and remove anyone who asks. Check the local regulator's guidance for promotional emails.

### 9. Send within the limits (level 3)

Confirm the action list once with the client ("these 25 contacts, from this address, on these dates, one follow-up each"). Then send only to the approved list, within the daily limit. Log each send and reply. Stop and ask on a bounce storm, an angry reply, a request for payment or a request to be removed (remove them at once).

### 10. Track

Tracking sheet: outlet, contact, angle, date sent, follow-up date, reply, outcome (coverage, link type, no), URL. Feed results into the `monthly-link-report` skill.

## Checks before handing over

- Each email is under 150 words and leads with the finding.
- No email asks for a link, a followed link or specific anchor text.
- Every claim is sourced; quotes are approved by the person quoted.
- Recipients are all on the approved list.
- Payment, gifts and disclosure are handled as above.
- Opt-out and removal requests are honoured and logged.

## Output format

1. Main pitch with five subject lines.
2. Two angle variations.
3. Unlinked mention and broken link versions, if relevant.
4. Follow-up.
5. Send plan: list, sender, dates, daily limit, for the client's yes.
6. Tracking sheet layout.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a UK pet insurer; the asset is a study of five years of anonymised claims. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Subject:** Dog claims for eating something toxic peak in April, five years of claims show
>
> Hi Sam,
>
> Your piece last month on spring garden dangers for dogs got me thinking you might find this useful. Our anonymised claims data from 2021 to 2025 shows claims for dogs eating something toxic peak every April, at almost twice the January level.
>
> For your readers, that's the month to check the garden and the Easter treats. The full monthly breakdown, the method and a chart are here: [domain]/spring-study. Our in-house vet, Dr A. Example, can comment by phone this week.
>
> Happy to send the raw monthly figures or a regional cut if that helps.
>
> Best,
> Jo, [Business]
