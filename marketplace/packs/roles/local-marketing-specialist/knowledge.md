# Local marketing specialist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-07. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Google changes Business Profile and Local Services Ads features often, so every platform fact below says when it was true. The Traction pack's local variant (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/mega/traction/variants/local.md`) covers how a local growth lead picks between all 19 channels; this kit covers the local craft itself.

## 1. What this role is for

A local business hires this role to turn nearby people into calls, bookings and visits. The clients are trades (plumbers, electricians, roofers, cleaners), salons and spas, gyms and studios, pet groomers and walkers, clinics, farm shops and cafés: firms with one to a few sites, an owner who does the work, and marketing squeezed into the gaps.

What good work looks like:

- **The map listing is right.** The Google Business Profile has the real name, the right categories, accurate hours and service areas, real photos, and a booking or contact route that works.
- **Reviews arrive steadily and honestly.** Every customer is asked the same way, nobody is paid or filtered, and every review gets a specific reply.
- **The website backs up the listing.** One useful page per main service, with prices or price ranges, real photos and the areas served, written from facts the owner supplied.
- **Offers follow the trade's calendar.** Busy months are protected, quiet weeks get offers with a margin check, a minimum job size and an end date.
- **Every lead has a source.** Calls, messages, forms and walk-ins are tagged so the owner can see which channel books jobs and what each booked job costs.

How it is judged: booked jobs or visits from new customers per week, cost per booked job by source, how many new reviews arrive each month and the rating they hold, and the calls, direction requests and website clicks the profile produces. Clicks and likes are not the measure.

## 2. The canon

Local marketing has few books but a clear rulebook and a small body of research. Practitioners lean on these.

**Google's own account of local ranking [1].** Google says local results depend mainly on three things: *relevance* (how well a profile matches the search), *distance* (how far the business is from the searcher or the place searched) and *prominence* (how well known it is, including links from other sites and review count and rating). It also says plainly that no one can pay for a better local ranking. Every local tactic works on one of these three; anything that claims to work another way is a red flag.

**Google's guidelines for representing your business [2] and the Maps content policy [13].** These are the field's rules of play. The guidelines say what a profile may contain; the content policy says what counts as fake engagement. Breaking them risks removed reviews, suspended profiles and lost months. A specialist knows them better than the owner and better than most agencies.

**Michael Luca, "Reviews, Reputation, and Revenue: The Case of Yelp.com" (Harvard Business School, 2011, revised 2016) [29].** Matching Yelp ratings to restaurant revenue data from the Washington State Department of Revenue, and using Yelp's rounding of star ratings, Luca found that a one-star increase was associated with a 5 to 9% rise in revenue for independent restaurants, with no measurable effect for chains. The lesson for local firms: reviews matter most where customers know least about you, which is exactly the independent local business.

**BrightLocal's yearly Local Consumer Review Survey [30].** A long-running survey of how US consumers use reviews to choose local businesses. Treat it as direction, not law: it is a vendor survey of about a thousand people a year. The 2026 edition (summarised by a third party, because BrightLocal's site blocks automated reading) points the same way as earlier years: people read recent reviews and notice whether the owner replies.

**Consumer law on reviews.** In the US, the FTC's 2024 rule on fake reviews [24] and its 2022 case against Fashion Nova for hiding negative reviews [25]; in the UK, the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act regime and the CMA's fake reviews guidance [26][27]; in the EU, the rules on fake reviews in the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive as amended [28]. These turn "be honest" from good manners into a legal duty.

**Traction's local playbook.** Gabriel Weinberg and Justin Mares's Bullseye method, applied to local firms in the Traction pack's local variant, is where a growth lead decides whether the map listing, reviews, Local Services Ads, postcards or referral swaps come first. It also has sourced early-traction stories from real local businesses.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 Google Business Profile: the rules (as of November 2026)

- **Eligibility.** A business must either have a location customers can visit or travel to customers. Mailing addresses and unstaffed virtual offices are not eligible [2].
- **Name.** The name must match what the business uses in real life, on signs and stationery. Adding services, towns, taglines or keywords to the name is not allowed [2]. This is the most common shortcut and the most common reason for trouble.
- **Address and service areas.** Service-area businesses that do not serve customers at their premises hide the address and list service areas [2][3]. Hybrid businesses can show both [3]. Service areas are set by city, postcode or area, not a radius; up to 20 are allowed, and the overall area should be no more than about two hours' drive from the base [3].
- **Categories.** Use as few as possible and as specific as possible, describing what the business *is*, not every service it offers [2]. Services are listed separately.
- **Phone and website.** Prefer a local number to a central call centre; the number must be under the business's direct control and must not redirect people elsewhere [2]. The guidelines do not mention call-tracking numbers specifically, so any tracking set-up on the profile is a judgement call (section 3.7).
- **Hours.** Show regular customer-facing hours and special hours for holidays [1][2].
- **Practitioners.** Doctors, lawyers and similar public-facing professionals may have their own profiles under set conditions; support staff may not [2].
- **Verification.** Google chooses the methods available: video recording, live video call, phone or text, email or postcard. Reviews take up to five business days. A postcard usually arrives within 14 days and its code expires after 30; editing the name, address or category while waiting can break it [4].
- **People and access.** Profiles have a primary owner, owners and managers. Managers can edit information, reply to reviews, post and manage photos, but cannot add or remove users or delete the profile. New users must wait seven days before they can transfer ownership or remove others [5]. There is no read-only role, which matters for access levels (see access.md).

### 3.2 Profile features that come and go

- **Posts.** Updates, offers (with dates and an optional code) and events. Posts older than six months are archived unless they carry a date range; posts with an unverified phone number may be rejected; Google reviews each post after it is published [7].
- **Chat and call history.** Removed on 31 July 2024. Eligible profiles can show text or WhatsApp contact options instead [11].
- **Questions and answers.** Google discontinued the Q&A API on 3 November 2025 [12]. Industry reports say the public Q&A section has also been removed from many profiles and partly replaced by AI answers, but I found no Google help page confirming the full change; check live profiles before planning Q&A work.
- **Performance data.** Views, search terms, calls (clicks on the call button), messages, direction requests, website clicks and bookings. Views count each person once a day; search terms update monthly; only verified profiles have data [6].

### 3.3 Local ranking in practice

Work through the three factors [1]:

- **Relevance:** the right primary category, a full services list, a plain description, website pages that match the services.
- **Distance:** cannot be changed, only described honestly. Service areas define where you serve, not where you rank; a firm rarely shows well far from its base, and stuffing towns into names or pages to fake reach breaks the rules [2][20].
- **Prominence:** review count and rating, and links from other sites such as local organisations, press and directories [1]. Customers also notice how recent reviews are and whether the owner replies [30].

Google offers no paid route to the map pack [1]. Local Services Ads and search ads sit above or beside it as paid placements, not as ranking help.

### 3.4 Service and area pages

The website gives Google and customers the detail the profile cannot. Write one page per main service with what is included, prices or ranges, real photos of the work, how booking works, and the areas served. Area pages are worth writing only when there is real local substance: jobs done there, local conditions (old housing stock, hard water, parking), travel times. Google's spam policies name city pages that funnel visitors to one page as doorway abuse, and mass-produced pages made to rank, including with AI tools, as scaled content abuse [20]. A town-name template copied twenty times is exactly that.

AI answers in Google Search (AI Overviews and AI Mode) draw on pages that are indexed and eligible for a snippet; Google says no special files or markup are required [21]. For "near me" questions, the same basics apply: accurate profile, clear service pages, real reviews.

### 3.5 Reviews: asking, replying, and the rules

**How to ask (Google).** Ask every customer, the same way, with a short link or QR code from the profile [8]. The ask works best at a natural end point: the job finished, the class done, the pet collected. Train staff to say it in one line, and follow up once by text or email where the business has the customer's contact details and the right to message them.

**What is banned (Google, as of November 2026)** [8][13]:

- offering anything in return for a review, a changed review or a removed review (discounts, free services, entry into a draw);
- asking only happy customers ("review gating"), or discouraging negative reviews;
- setting staff quotas for reviews or asking for particular wording;
- pressing customers to review while they are on the premises;
- reviews from staff, family or anyone with a conflict of interest;
- posting on competitors' profiles.

Fake engagement is removed and accounts can be suspended [13].

**Yelp is different.** Yelp asks businesses not to ask for Yelp reviews at all, and its software tends not to recommend reviews it thinks were solicited [23]. So the review link goes to Google; Yelp gets a "find us on Yelp" sign at most.

**The law (dated).**

- *US:* the FTC's rule, announced 14 August 2024, bans fake reviews (including AI-written ones), rewards tied to positive or negative sentiment, undisclosed reviews by insiders, and suppressing reviews through threats or intimidation; knowing violators face civil penalties [24]. In January 2022 Fashion Nova agreed to pay $4.2 million after the FTC said it had held back reviews under four stars for four years, and the FTC warned 10 review management firms that filtering out negative reviews breaks the law [25].
- *UK:* under the CMA's guidance (4 April 2025), fake reviews, concealed incentivised reviews, misleading review information and writing or commissioning fake reviews are banned; businesses that publish reviews need a policy and reasonable checks [27]. In January 2025 Google agreed with the CMA to put warning alerts on UK profiles that boosted ratings with fake reviews, switch off their reviews, and delete repeat offenders' reviews for six months or more [26].
- *EU:* traders that publish reviews must say how they check reviews come from real customers, and must not alter reviews or pay for fake ones [28].

This is general practice, not legal advice. Note the difference between Google and the law: Google bans *all* incentives for reviews [8][13], while the FTC rule targets rewards tied to sentiment [24] and the UK targets *concealed* incentives [27]. Follow the strictest rule that applies, which on Google is Google's.

**Replying.** Only verified profiles can reply. Replies are checked against policy (usually within minutes, sometimes much longer), appear as the business, and the reviewer is told [9]. Keep replies short, specific and not promotional; for complaints, apologise where right, protect the reviewer's privacy, and move the detail to a private channel [8]. Reviewers can change their review after a good reply [9].

**Removing reviews.** Report reviews that break policy from the profile or the Reviews Management Tool; one appeal of up to ten reviews is allowed. Google does not remove reviews for being negative or disputed [10]. A calm public reply usually does more than a removal request.

### 3.6 Local Services Ads (as of November 2026)

Local Services Ads put verified providers in a separate unit at the top of Google results. Customers call, message or (in the US and Canada) book directly, and the business pays per lead, not per click [14][15].

- **Availability:** listed service categories only, in the US, Canada, the UK, Ireland, Germany, Austria, Switzerland, France, Italy, Spain and Belgium (the country versions of Google's help page); some categories are limited to certain US states [14].
- **The badge:** one "Google Verified" badge now covers every advertiser that passes screening, and it does not affect ranking. Google's page gives no start date. The Money Back Guarantee tied to the old Google Guarantee badge is discontinued; reimbursement applies only to bookings made before 7 December 2025 [17]. Businesses can get leads before the badge, but appear below verified providers [14].
- **What counts as a lead:** answered or returned calls, missed calls in business hours where the caller stays on the line for more than 20 seconds, messages, voicemails and booking requests. Repeat contact from the same number or email within 15 days is not charged again [15].
- **Budget:** an average weekly budget sets a monthly maximum; when it is reached, ads stop for the month [15].
- **Credits:** Google reassesses charged leads for 30 days and may credit them automatically. Lead credits and call recordings are not available in Europe, and some US health and tax categories have no credits [15].
- **Ranking:** bid, responsiveness (missed calls hurt), relevance of services and business bio, lead types switched on, and profile quality: rating, review count, response time, images and completed checks [16].

The practical rule: Local Services Ads reward a business that answers the phone. Before switching them on, check who answers calls and how fast.

### 3.7 Other places customers look

- **Apple Maps.** Apple Business Connect now redirects to Apple Business, where a business can claim its Apple Maps place, add logo, hours and images, set custom actions for bookings, and buy Apple Ads on Apple Maps; availability varies by country [22].
- **Bing Places, Yelp, Facebook, Nextdoor and trade directories.** Keep name, address, phone and hours identical everywhere, and update them all when anything changes.

### 3.8 Local lead tracking

Most local customers call or walk in, so the phone and the front desk are the tracking system.

1. **A lead source tracker.** A sheet or booking-system field where staff note, for every new enquiry: date, how they contacted us, "How did you hear about us?", service, booked (yes or no), job value. This is the backbone; the Traction local variant has the full formula set.
2. **Profile data.** Download Business Profile performance monthly: calls, directions, website clicks, bookings [6].
3. **Tagged website link.** Add UTM parameters to the profile's website link (for example source google, medium organic, campaign gbp), lowercase, so profile visits show separately in GA4 [19].
4. **Calls from the website and ads.** Google forwarding numbers on the website show which ads and keywords led to calls, where the country is supported [18]. Specialist call-tracking tools do the same for other channels.
5. **Calls from the profile itself.** Google's guidelines want a number under the business's direct control that represents the location [2]. Some practitioners put a tracking number in the profile and keep the main number as an additional number; Google's guidelines do not address this directly, so treat it as a judgement call, keep the tracking number local and owned by the business, and never change the number while verification is pending.
6. **Local Services Ads leads.** The Leads page shows each lead's status (charged, not charged, in review, credited) [15]; match them to bookings in the tracker.

From these, compute cost per booked job by source, every week.

### 3.9 Seasons, offers and posts

Every local trade has a calendar: heating and cooling seasons, January gym rushes, wedding seasons, school holidays, harvest weeks. Map demand by month for each service, then give each season a job: book maintenance before the rush, catch emergencies during it, fill quiet weeks after it, and ask for reviews when volume is high. Offers in quiet weeks need a margin check (cost of the offer against the job's gross profit), a minimum job size and a real end date. Add value before cutting price: an add-on, a priority slot, a loyalty step. Business Profile offer posts carry the dates and terms [7].

### 3.10 Working in the client's tools

Agents in this role work in the Business Profile, Local Services Ads, the website and booking tools once the owner grants access: level 1 (read), level 2 (read and draft) or level 3 (read, write and publish within set limits). Because Business Profile has no read-only role [5] and edits and posts go live once Google has reviewed them [3][7], the level is an agreement written down in "Access I have". access.md gives the details.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these for judgement, not as targets.

- **Reviews and revenue:** a one-star rise on Yelp associated with 5 to 9% more revenue for independent restaurants, none for chains (Washington State revenue data) [29]. *Caveat:* one city, one category, one platform; a correlation identified through rating rounding, not an experiment run by a business.
- **Consumer habits (US, February 2026, 1,002 adults, via a summary of BrightLocal's survey):** 41% always read reviews when choosing a local business (29% in 2025); 74% look for reviews from the last three months; 31% will only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or more (17% before); 80% are more likely to use a business that replies to all reviews; 50% are put off by generic replies [30]. *Caveat:* a vendor survey, read through a secondary summary; check BrightLocal's report before quoting it to a client.
- **Google's own limits:** up to 20 service areas within about two hours' drive [3]; verification reviews up to five business days, postcard codes valid 30 days [4]; posts archived after six months [7]; LSA missed calls count as leads after 20 seconds on the line, repeat contacts free within 15 days, 30-day lead reassessment [15].

No trustworthy cross-industry figure exists for "good" call rates, review velocity or cost per booked job. Use the client's own first month as the baseline.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the profile name has keywords or towns added, fix it to the real-world name first, because it breaks the guidelines and puts the profile at risk [2].
- If the business serves customers only at their homes, hide the address and set service areas, because showing an address customers cannot visit breaks the rules [2][3].
- If reviews are slow, fix the asking habit (same words, every customer, a link or QR code) before anything else, because Google bans every shortcut [8][13].
- If an owner wants to reward reviews or ask only happy customers, say no and offer the honest version, because Google removes such reviews and US, UK and EU law may apply [13][24][27][28].
- If a negative review is genuine, reply calmly and take it offline; report only reviews that break policy [9][10].
- If the client wants Yelp reviews, do not ask for them; point the review ask at Google and let Yelp grow on its own [23].
- If nobody answers the phone reliably, fix call handling before switching on Local Services Ads, because missed calls lower LSA ranking and waste leads [16].
- If the business is in Europe, plan LSA budgets without counting on lead credits, which are not available there [15].
- If a service page would only differ from another by the town name, do not publish it; write it only when there is real local detail [20].
- If the client cannot say where new customers came from, start the lead source tracker this week, before any paid test.
- If a quiet-week offer would cut below the job's gross profit, change the offer (add value, set a minimum job size) or drop it.
- If profile verification is pending, change nothing on the name, address or category until it completes [4].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Keyword-stuffed names.** Spot it: "Smith Plumbing Leeds Emergency Plumber 24/7" on the profile, "Smith Plumbing" on the van.
- **Wrong or too many categories.** Spot it: a list of every service as categories; the primary category is not what the business mainly is [2].
- **Address shown for a home-based trade.** Spot it: a residential street on the map pin for a mobile service [2].
- **Review gating.** Spot it: a feedback form that sends happy customers to Google and unhappy ones to a private form [13].
- **Reviews for rewards.** Spot it: "10% off your next visit for a review" on a counter card [8].
- **Generic replies.** Spot it: the same "Thanks for your review!" under every review [8][30].
- **Doorway town pages.** Spot it: twenty area pages with identical text and a swapped town name [20].
- **Untracked profile traffic.** Spot it: the website link on the profile has no UTM tags, so GA4 shows it as plain organic search [19].
- **Missed calls.** Spot it: call-button clicks in profile data far above calls answered in the phone log.
- **Out-of-date hours.** Spot it: no holiday hours set; complaints about turning up to a closed door.
- **Offers that lose money.** Spot it: no minimum job size, no end date, no cost worked out.
- **Treating Yelp like Google.** Spot it: a "review us on Yelp" card at the till [23].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does the profile follow the guidelines: real name, right categories, address or service areas set correctly, local number, accurate hours [2][3]?
2. Is every claim (licence, insurance, warranty, years in business, prices) supplied by the owner, not assumed?
3. Does the review ask go to every customer, with no reward, no filter and no quota [8][13]?
4. Does each review reply speak to that review, avoid promotion and protect privacy [8]?
5. Does each service page carry real detail and real photos, with no copied text across towns [20]?
6. Is there a working lead source tracker, and are the profile link and campaign links tagged [19]?
7. Does every offer have a price, what is included, a minimum job size where needed, an end date and a margin check?
8. Are LSA budgets, lead handling and region rules on credits understood before launch [15][16]?
9. Is the access level for each tool written down, with level 2 and level 3 steps marked?
10. Would the owner be comfortable if a customer, a competitor or a regulator read every word?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Profile audit:* each field, current state, the rule it must meet, the fix, the access level the fix needs.
- *Profile text:* a plain description (what you do, where, how fast, how you price), the services list, and a month of posts.
- *Review kit:* the counter or end-of-job line, the text and email follow-up, the QR card wording, reply templates by star level, and the house rules (ask everyone, no rewards).
- *Service and area pages:* each with the facts used and the photos needed.
- *Seasonal calendar:* month by month, the job of the season, the offer with its margin check, and the posts, emails and print pieces.
- *Weekly lead sheet and monthly review:* booked jobs and cost per booked job by source, new reviews and rating, profile actions.

**Tone and conventions.** Warm, local and plain. Write as the owner speaks to customers. Specific beats clever: the street, the job, the time it took. Prices and end dates in numbers. No "best in town", no licence, insurance or warranty claims unless the owner supplied them, and no health, safety or legal promises. Notes to the owner are separate from anything customers will see.

## 9. Glossary

- **Google Business Profile (GBP):** a business's listing on Google Search and Maps.
- **Map pack:** the group of local business results shown with a map in Google Search.
- **Relevance, distance, prominence:** the three things Google says drive local results [1].
- **Primary category:** the one category that best describes what the business is [2].
- **Service-area business (SAB):** a business that serves customers at their location and hides its address [2][3].
- **Hybrid business:** a business with premises customers visit that also travels to customers [3].
- **Verification:** Google's check that you manage the business [4].
- **Manager:** a Business Profile user who can edit and reply but cannot manage users or delete the profile [5].
- **Profile actions:** calls, direction requests, website clicks, messages and bookings from the profile [6].
- **Review gating:** asking only happy customers for reviews, or filtering who gets the link [13].
- **Incentivised review:** a review given in return for a reward; banned on Google [8].
- **Review velocity:** new reviews per month.
- **Local Services Ads (LSA):** Google's pay-per-lead ads for local service providers [14].
- **Google Verified badge:** the single LSA badge for providers that pass Google's screening [17].
- **Lead credit:** a refund of an LSA lead charge for an invalid lead; not available in Europe [15].
- **Doorway page:** a page made to rank for a place or phrase that funnels people elsewhere [20].
- **UTM parameters:** tags on a link that tell analytics where a visit came from [19].
- **Call tracking number:** a number that forwards to the business and records which source the call came from [18].
- **Lead source tracker:** the sheet or booking field recording how each new customer found you.
- **Cost per booked job:** channel spend plus staff time, divided by booked jobs from that channel.
- **NAP:** name, address and phone, kept identical across every listing.

Re-check at each refresh:

- **Business Profile features:** posts, Q&A, messaging options, verification methods and performance metrics, on Google Business Profile Help [4][6][7][11] and the API deprecation schedule [12].
- **Business Profile and Maps policies:** the guidelines and the fake engagement policy [2][13], and any new enforcement such as warning labels [26].
- **Local Services Ads:** categories and countries, lead rules, credits, ranking inputs and the badge [14][15][16][17].
- **AI answers for local searches** in Google Search, and how they cite businesses [21].
- **Review law:** FTC enforcement under the 2024 rule [24], CMA action under the DMCC Act [26][27], and EU guidance [28].
- **Apple Business** features and country availability [22].
- **Consumer review surveys,** re-issued yearly; read the original report where possible [30].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Stock hype words.** Profile descriptions and posts with "Elevate your home", "seamless service" or "game-changer". Write as the owner speaks to customers.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "Friendly, reliable, quality service" that every business in town also says. Use the street, the job and the time it took, as section 8 asks.
- **Headline formula clichés.** "Your trusted local experts" or "Looking for a plumber? Look no further." Lead with the service, the area and one specific fact.
- **Fake urgency.** "Book now, spots filling fast" on a quiet-week offer with no real limit. Give a real end date and a real limit, or none.
- **Generic stock-style images.** Stock photos of smiling workers or generic houses on service pages and posts. Use the client's real photos of real jobs.
- **Town-swapped page template.** Service pages built from the same centred hero, three benefit cards and FAQ, with only the town name changed. Build each page around real local detail, or do not publish it (section 5).
