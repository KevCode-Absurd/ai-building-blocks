# Job and service costing

## When to use

- The owner asks "which jobs (or clients) made money last year?" or "why are we busy but not profitable?"
- Quotes or fixed fees are set by habit and nobody knows the true cost of an hour.
- A job ran over and the owner wants to know where, before quoting the next one.
- A service firm wants utilisation, realisation and effective rates by client and service.

Do not use it to set prices for the owner, to judge individual staff, or to decide how revenue or work in progress goes into the statutory accounts. This is general management accounting practice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant before job figures go into your accounts, a tax return or a lender pack.

## What you need

1. A timesheet export for the period: person, date, job or client, task, hours, billable flag.
2. Bills and supplier invoices coded to jobs (materials, subcontractors, hire), or a job cost report from the job software.
3. The invoice list: job or client, date, amount, and any credits or write-offs.
4. Payroll summary by person or by role: gross pay and employer costs (employer taxes, pension, benefits). Roles are enough if pay by name is sensitive.
5. Contracted hours and how leave, sickness and training are recorded.
6. The overhead costs for the period (rent, vehicles, admin staff, software, insurance) from the profit and loss.
7. For quoted work: the original estimate or fee for each job.

Access: level 1 (read) is enough for all of this. Exports work just as well as a connection.

## Steps

### 1. Check the data

Every hour and every bill should point to a job. List those that do not, with totals, and ask before assigning them. List hours with no rate, invoices with no job, and jobs with no invoice. Never guess hours that were not recorded.

### 2. Work out the true labour cost per hour

For each person or role: (gross pay + employer costs) / available hours, where available hours = contracted hours minus leave, public holidays, sickness and training. Show the sum for at least one person. If the business only gives wage rates, say the result will understate cost and by roughly which items.

### 3. Set an overhead rate

Total overhead for the period divided by a driver. Labour hours suit most service and trade firms; machine hours suit workshops; orders suit online sellers. Show the rate and the inputs so the owner can change them. If jobs differ a lot in the support they need (site visits, admin, project management), split the biggest overhead pool and cost it per hour of that activity instead of one blanket rate.

### 4. Build the job table

One row per job: revenue, labour (hours x cost per hour), materials, subcontractors, other direct costs, overhead share, profit, margin. Add totals and tie revenue and direct costs to the profit and loss for the period. Any difference is shown, not hidden.

### 5. For service firms, add the service view

- Capacity per person = contracted hours minus leave.
- Utilisation = billable hours / capacity, by team and month.
- Effective rate = fees billed / hours worked, by client, service and project, next to the standard rate.
- Write-offs and over-budget hours grouped by client, service and type of work, not by person.

### 6. Review the worst jobs

For the bottom three to five jobs by profit, compare estimate to actual line by line (hours by task, materials, subcontractors, variations). Name the likely cause from the data: under-estimated hours, unbilled variations, rework, scope creep, price of materials. If the data does not show the cause, write the question.

### 7. Turn findings into quoting rules

Examples: "price labour at the true cost per hour plus the overhead rate, then margin"; "quote site visits as a line, not free"; "fixed fees for this service need a change-order clause". Each rule names the number it should move.

### 8. Optional: set up the job cost sheet

A sheet to track each live job weekly: budget hours and costs, actual to date, forecast to finish. At level 2 I can create this as a draft sheet or a new unshared report in the client's tool; at level 3, and only within the limits the client set, I can add tracking categories or classes for jobs in the accounting software. I log every action.

## Checks

- Every hour and bill is assigned to a job, or listed as unassigned with totals.
- Labour cost per hour includes employer costs and paid time not worked, with the sum shown.
- Overhead rate shown with its inputs; changing one cell updates the table.
- Job table totals tie to the profit and loss for the period.
- No individual is ranked or named in a performance sense; utilisation is shown by team.
- Estimated costs are marked as estimates.
- The report carries the line: *General management accounting practice: check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant before job figures go into your accounts or a lender pack.*

## Output format

1. Summary: three jobs or clients that made the most and three that lost the most, and the one change that would move profit most.
2. Method box: labour cost per hour by role, overhead rate and driver, period.
3. Job table (or client and service table) with totals tied to the profit and loss.
4. Service view (if relevant): utilisation by team, effective rate by client and service, write-offs by type of work.
5. Estimate-versus-actual review for the worst jobs.
6. Quoting rules, each with the number it should move.
7. Data gaps and questions; questions for the accountant.

Made-up business for illustration: Ridgeway Joinery, a 9-person fit-out firm.
