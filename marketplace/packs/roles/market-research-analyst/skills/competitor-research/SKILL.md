# Competitor research

## When to use

- Before launching a product, entering a market or changing a price.
- When sales say "we keep losing to X" and nobody knows why.
- To find an angle rivals miss.
- As a monthly change log of rivals' prices, offers and messages.

Do not use it to obtain confidential information, to copy rivals' copyrighted material, to agree prices with competitors, or to make claims about rivals that the evidence cannot support. Comparative claims in ads have their own legal rules; ask a qualified adviser before publishing one.

## What you need

1. The client's buyer, the decision the comparison serves, and the rivals the client names.
2. Rivals' public material, pasted or uploaded: home and pricing pages, terms, product pages, ads (from public ad libraries), emails received as a subscriber, public reviews, job ads, press releases and filings.
3. The client's own page set and price list, for the same rows.
4. Won and lost deal notes, and sales or support notes that mention rivals.
5. Dates for everything: rivals change pages often.

Access: level 1 (read) is enough. I work from what is pasted, uploaded or publicly visible. Buying from a rival as an ordinary customer is fine; posing as someone else to extract confidential terms is not.

## Steps

### 1. Define the field

Name 3 to 6 rivals: direct rivals, the closest substitute, and "do nothing" or "do it by hand". Confirm with the client which ones buyers actually compare.

### 2. Collect and date the evidence

One folder or table: rival, item, URL or source, date captured. Save a copy or screenshot, since pages change. Note gaps (for example "no price shown; quote only").

### 3. Build one grid

Same rows for every rival and for the client: target buyer, job it promises, offer and packages, price and price model, proof (numbers, logos, reviews), main message, channels, guarantees and terms, weak points. Quote short lines and label the source for each cell. Leave a cell empty with "not public" rather than guessing.

### 4. Mine reviews

For each rival, read public reviews by star rating. Pull the top three praise points and top three complaints, with counts and two or three short labelled quotes. Separate what the rival claims from what customers say.

### 5. Find the gaps

- Claims no rival makes that buyers care about.
- Complaints no rival answers.
- Buyer groups every rival ignores.
- Price points or models nobody offers.
Check each gap against the client's real strengths. A gap the client cannot fill is not an opportunity.

### 6. Score honestly

Score each rival and the client on the factors buyers say matter most (from interviews, reviews or deal notes), with the evidence beside each score. Include the client's weak points.

### 7. Recommend

One position to own, in a sentence, with the proof that supports it. Two small changes for this month (a page, a price display, an offer, a guarantee). One thing to stop doing because a rival does it better.

### 8. Keep a change log (monthly)

Date, rival, what changed (price, offer, message, new product, ads), source, and whether it needs a response. At level 2, I can save the log and briefs as drafts in the client's workspace; posting changes to the client's own pages or ads needs level 3 within agreed limits, after the client confirms the action list. I log every action.

## Checks

- Same rows for every rival and the client.
- Every cell sourced and dated, or marked "not public".
- No private numbers guessed (rival revenue, margins, customer counts) unless a filing or the rival's own statement gives them.
- Customer quotes are short, labelled and real.
- Substitutes and "do nothing" included.
- Recommendation fits the client's proven strengths.
- Nothing obtained by deception, fake accounts, scraping that breaks site terms or someone else's login.

## Output format

1. Summary: the position to own, two changes, one thing to stop.
2. Competitor grid with sources and dates.
3. Review themes per rival, with counts and quotes.
4. Gap list, each checked against the client's strengths.
5. Scores with evidence.
6. Evidence log and gaps in evidence.
7. Monthly change log (if running monthly).

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real business: an e-bike shop comparing itself with three local rivals. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Position to own:** "Same-week repairs for any e-bike, wherever you bought it." Proof: 4.8 average on 160 public reviews mentioning repairs; no rival offers repairs for bikes bought elsewhere (pricing and service pages, captured 2 November 2026).
>
> | Row | The shop | Rival A | Rival B | Online-only brand |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Price, entry e-bike | 1,450 | 1,390 | 1,600 | 1,290 + delivery |
> | Repairs on other brands | Yes | No ("own-brand only", service page) | Not public | No |
> | Top complaint in reviews | Wait for stock (14 mentions) | Repair waits of weeks (22) | Pushy sales (9) | Hard to get parts (31) |
>
> **Changes this month:** put the repair promise on the home page; show stock levels online. **Stop:** price-matching the online brand, which wins on price but loses on service.
