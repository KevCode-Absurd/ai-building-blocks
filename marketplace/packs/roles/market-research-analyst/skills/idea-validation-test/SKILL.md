# Idea validation test

## When to use

- An idea is about to get money, time or a loan, and nobody has tested whether people will pay.
- A founder has "lots of positive feedback" but no commitments.
- A business plan needs a stress test before it goes to a lender or partner.
- Results from a test are in and someone has to call go, change or stop.

Do not use it to guarantee success, to give investment or loan advice, or to collect money for something that may never exist without the safeguards below.

## What you need

1. The idea: who has what problem, what is offered, how it makes money.
2. What is already known: interviews, sales, waitlist sign-ups, market size work, rivals.
3. Budget and time available for testing.
4. How the client can reach potential buyers (lists, communities, ads, partners).
5. For a results review: the test design, the pass line set beforehand, and the raw results.

Access: level 1 (read) to plan and read results. Level 2 to build a test page, form or ad as an unpublished draft. Level 3, within agreed limits (budget cap, dates, audience), to publish the page, run the ads or send invitations, after the client confirms the action list. I log every action and stop if spend or results look wrong.

## Steps

### 1. One sentence

"[Buyer] who [situation] struggle with [problem]; we offer [solution] for [price], reached through [channel]."

### 2. Map the assumptions

List every assumption under customer, problem, solution, price and channel. Write each as a statement that could be false: "Salon owners lose at least one booking a week to no-shows."

### 3. Pick the riskiest

Score each assumption on chance of being wrong (from the evidence so far) and cost if wrong (how much of the plan collapses). The top one is tested first. Usually it is the problem or the willingness to pay, not the technology.

### 4. Choose the cheapest valid test

- **Problem interviews:** recent, specific past behaviour, no pitching. Good for "is the problem real and frequent?"
- **Landing page or smoke test:** a real offer, price and call to action; measures clicks and sign-ups.
- **Pre-order or deposit:** the strongest signal; follow the local rules for taking money for something not yet available. In the US, the FTC's mail and internet order rule sets conditions for dry-testing and shipping and refund duties; elsewhere, ask a qualified adviser first.
- **Concierge or manual version:** deliver the result by hand to a few customers and charge for it.
- **Price test:** two real prices to comparable visitors, with full prices shown.

### 5. Set the pass line before the test

Write the metric, the sample needed, the time box and the pass and fail lines before any data comes in. Base the line on what the business needs to work (for example the sign-up rate the plan assumes), not on a generic benchmark. Write what "change" would mean as well as go and stop.

### 6. Run it honestly

Real offer, real price, clear words about what exists and what does not. No fake reviews, invented user counts or names, or claims the product cannot back. Keep research conversations apart from sales pitches.

### 7. Read the result

Compare with the pass line. Say go, change or stop, and why. Do not move the line after the data arrives. If the test was flawed (too little traffic, wrong audience), say so and redesign rather than reading noise.

### 8. Pre-mortem for plan reviews

"It is a year from now and this failed. Why?" List the top reasons, link each to an assumption and check whether it has been tested.

### 9. Next single step

Name the next test or build step and its cost.

## Checks

- Assumptions written as statements that could be false.
- Riskiest assumption chosen with reasons.
- Pass and fail lines, sample and time box written before the test.
- The test offer is real and honest; any money taken follows local rules.
- Results read against the original line; no moved goalposts.
- Weak results reported plainly.

## Output format

1. One-sentence idea.
2. Assumption map with scores.
3. Test plan: type, audience, metric, sample, time box, pass and fail lines, cost, what is needed from the client.
4. Results read-out (when results exist): numbers against the line, go, change or stop, reasons.
5. Pre-mortem (for plan reviews).
6. Next step and cost.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a meal prep business testing weekly ready meals for shift workers at one hospital. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Riskiest assumption:** night-shift staff will pay 45 a week for five meals delivered to the staff entrance.
>
> **Test:** a landing page shared in two staff groups (with the groups' admins' permission) offering a 10 deposit for the first week, refundable in full if the service does not start by 1 December. Run for 10 days. The plan needs 60 paying customers to break even on one delivery run, so the pass line is 30 deposits; fewer than 12 is stop; 12 to 29 is change (test a lower price or a different pick-up point).
>
> **Result:** 260 page visits from the two groups, 9 deposits. Traffic was enough to read the result. **Call: stop at this price and place.** Three comments asked for meals at the main canteen instead; test that before anything else.
