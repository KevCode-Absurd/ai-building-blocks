# Market sizing

## When to use

- A founder or owner asks "how big is this market?" before spending money.
- A business plan, grant application or lender needs a market size that can be checked.
- Someone quotes a large figure from a report headline and the owner wants to know if it holds.
- Choosing between segments, regions or countries on size and reach.

Do not use it to value a company, forecast the client's sales as a promise, or give investment advice. Sizes are estimates with stated assumptions.

## What you need

1. The question in one sentence: what is sold, to whom, where, at what price, over what period.
2. Anything the client already has: sales data, customer counts, prices, pipeline, past surveys, reports they own.
3. Official data for the region: in the US, Census County Business Patterns and Nonemployer Statistics for business counts, the Consumer Expenditure Surveys for household spending, the American Community Survey for population and income; in the UK, ONS releases such as Family Spending; in the EU, Eurostat structural business statistics.
4. Trade or association figures, public company filings and price lists, with dates.
5. How the client can reach buyers today (channels, sales team, partners, a website).

Access: level 1 (read) is enough. Official data is public; client data can come as exports.

## Steps

### 1. Restate the question

One sentence: "Annual spending by [buyer] in [region] on [job or product] at around [price], [year]." Ask for the buyer, region, price and time frame if missing. Write the decision the size feeds.

### 2. List and tag inputs

Table: input, value, unit, year, geography, source and date, tag. Tags: [given] from the client, [source n] from a named publisher, [derived] with the sum shown, [guess]. Convert everything to one currency, one year and one geography, and show each conversion.

### 3. Top-down

Start from a published total (industry revenue, household spending on a category, number of businesses in a NAICS or NACE code). Narrow it step by step to the part that matches the job, with each filter's source. Never jump from a total to "we take 1%".

### 4. Bottom-up

Number of reachable buyers x share with the problem now x price x purchases per year. Count buyers from official counts or the client's own lists; take price from the client's price or real price lists; take frequency from the client's data or a survey. Show the formula and the result.

### 5. Reconcile

Compare the two. If they are far apart, find the input that causes the gap (usually buyer count or frequency) and say which estimate you trust more and why. Do not average two numbers you do not trust.

### 6. TAM, SAM, SOM

- TAM: all spending on the job in the region and year.
- SAM: the part the client's product, channels and region can serve.
- SOM: what the client can realistically win in a stated time, from reach (people the channels can contact), conversion seen in the client's own data or a test, and capacity. If there is no conversion evidence, give SOM as a range marked [guess] and say what test would fix it.

### 7. Cases and sensitivity

Low, likely and high for each. Change one assumption at a time and name the three that move the answer most.

### 8. Segments

Split SAM into segments that buy differently (need, situation, size, channel). Size each one, check they add up to the total, and say which one the client can reach first and why.

### 9. Write it up

Answer first, then working, then risks, then the next test. At level 2, I can save the model as a draft sheet in the client's drive; sharing it with others or publishing it in a plan needs level 3 within agreed limits. I log every action.

## Checks

- One currency, one year, one geography; every conversion shown.
- Every input tagged; every source has a publisher and date.
- Top-down and bottom-up both shown; gap explained.
- Segments add up to the total; no double counting.
- No figure from a report headline treated as verified unless the client has the report.
- SOM tied to reach, conversion evidence and capacity, or marked as a guess.
- The three most sensitive assumptions named, with the test for the biggest one.

## Output format

1. Answer box: TAM, SAM, SOM (low, likely, high), confidence, decision it supports.
2. Inputs table with tags and sources.
3. Top-down and bottom-up workings.
4. Reconciliation note.
5. Sensitivity: the three assumptions that matter most.
6. Segment table and first segment to target.
7. Next test, its cost and what result would change the answer.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up business for illustration, and every figure below is illustrative too: Fernway Pet Care, planning a mobile dog-grooming van in one county.

> **Answer.** Likely SAM 2.1m a year (low 1.4m, high 2.9m) for mobile grooming of dogs in the county; likely SOM in year one 95,000 (about 1,460 visits), capped by one van's capacity, not demand. Confidence: medium.
>
> | Input | Value | Tag |
> |---|---|---|
> | Households in county | 182,000 | [source: official census estimate, 2025] |
> | Share with a dog | 24% | [source: client-supplied national pet survey, 2025] |
> | Share who pay for grooming | 45% | [guess] |
> | Visits a year | 4 | [given: client's salon records, median] |
> | Price per visit | 65 | [given] |
>
> Bottom-up TAM: 182,000 x 24% x 45% x 4 x 65 = 5.1m. SAM keeps owners within a 25-minute drive of base and willing to pay a mobile premium: 41% [guess] = 2.1m.
>
> **Moves the answer most:** share who pay for grooming, the mobile share, visit frequency.
> **Next test:** a two-week waitlist page in two postcodes with a stated price, before buying the van.
