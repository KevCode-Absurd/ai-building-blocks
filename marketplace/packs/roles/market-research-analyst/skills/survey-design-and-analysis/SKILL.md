# Survey design and analysis

## When to use

- A decision needs numbers from buyers: which need matters most, which segment to serve, which package or price range is acceptable.
- Someone has survey results and wants to know what they show and how far to trust them.
- A pricing question needs a stated-preference range before a live test.

Do not use a survey to predict exact sales, to sell (no discount codes or sales calls attached to research), or to research children without parental consent. Privacy rules differ by country; ask a qualified adviser if the survey collects sensitive data or crosses borders.

## What you need

1. The decision and the two or three questions the survey must answer.
2. Who should answer (the population) and how the client can reach them: customer list, website visitors, a panel, social followers.
3. Interviews or open feedback already gathered, to write answer options from real words.
4. For pricing: the product description, current or planned price, rivals' prices the client knows.
5. The survey tool (Google Forms, Microsoft Forms, Typeform, SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics or similar).
6. For analysis: the raw response export, the questionnaire as fielded, dates and how people were invited.

Access: level 1 (read) for analysis. Level 2 to build the survey as an unpublished draft in the client's tool. Level 3, within the limits the client sets (which list, how many invites, which dates), to publish the survey or send invitations. I confirm the action list before the first send and log every action.

## Steps

### 1. Fit the method to the decision

If the question is "why" or "how", interviews first. If it is "how many" or "which", a survey. For price: Van Westendorp for an acceptable range, Gabor-Granger for demand at set price points, or both; a best-worst (MaxDiff) exercise if packages are still open.

### 2. Write the questionnaire

- Screener first: confirm the person is in the population (for example bought the category in the last 12 months).
- Order: behaviour (what they did last time), then needs, then reactions, then price, then profile.
- Forced choices instead of agree-disagree; one idea per question; options that cover all answers without overlap; randomise unordered lists; keep ordered scales in order.
- Price block, Van Westendorp: four questions on the prices that feel too cheap, a bargain, getting expensive and too expensive, after a short neutral description. Gabor-Granger: "would you buy at [price]?" at three to five price points set from the client's facts, starting at a random point or going high to low consistently.
- Add one attention check and one open question.
- Aim for a survey most people finish in a few minutes; cut anything that does not feed the decision.

### 3. Consent and privacy

Opening text: who is running the research, its purpose, roughly how long it takes, that taking part is voluntary, what personal data is collected and how long it is kept, and a contact. No sales follow-up unless people opt in separately. Collect only the profile questions the analysis needs.

### 4. Sample

State the target number of completes and why. For a percentage from a random sample, about 1,067 completes gives roughly plus or minus 3 points at 95%; most small-business surveys use customer lists or opt-in panels, which are not random, so report results as "among respondents" and give no margin of error unless a model justifies one. Plan subgroup sizes if subgroups will be compared.

### 5. Pretest

Run it with a handful of people from the population, watch where they hesitate, fix wording, then launch.

### 6. Clean the data

Remove and count: incomplete responses, speeders (far faster than the median), straight-liners, failed attention checks, duplicate or nonsense open answers, and impossible combinations. Report how many were removed for each reason. Be most careful with surprising rare answers, which bogus respondents inflate.

### 7. Analyse

- Counts and percentages with bases on every table.
- Cross-tabs only where groups are big enough; merge or suppress small cells so no one can be identified.
- Van Westendorp: cumulative curves for the four questions, the crossing points and the acceptable range.
- Gabor-Granger: share who would buy and expected revenue index at each price.
- Open answers coded into themes with counts and short labelled quotes.

### 8. Report

Answer first, then the tables, then the method box: who was surveyed, how invited, dates, completes, removed responses, weighting (if any), quality checks and limits. Say what the survey cannot show (for example real purchase behaviour) and the live test that would confirm it. If AI helped code open answers or draft questions, say so and how results were checked. Never mix synthetic responses with real ones.

## Checks

- Every question maps to the decision; nothing is there "for interest".
- No leading, double-barrelled or agree-disagree items.
- Consent text present; research and sales kept apart.
- Removals counted by reason.
- Every percentage has its base; small cells merged or suppressed.
- Margin of error only for random samples, or with the model explained.
- Price results labelled as stated preference, with a live test proposed.
- AI or synthetic input disclosed.

## Output format

1. Questionnaire (with screener, consent text and logic notes), or
2. Findings report: answer, key tables, price range if asked, themes from open answers, method box, limits, next test.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up business for illustration: Brightwell Bookkeeping, testing a monthly fixed-fee package for sole traders.

> **Answer.** Among 214 usable respondents (sole traders on the client's newsletter list, 3 to 14 October 2026), the acceptable monthly range was 38 to 61, with most "getting expensive" answers clustering near 55. This is stated preference from an opt-in list, not a random sample, so no margin of error is given.
>
> **Removed:** 9 speeders (under a third of the median time), 6 failed attention checks, 3 duplicates.
>
> **Gabor-Granger (would buy):** 45: 62% | 55: 54% | 65: 22%. Revenue index (price x share): 27.9, 29.7, 14.3, so it peaks at 55.
>
> **Top need (open answers, 187 coded):** "know what I owe in tax before the deadline" (71 mentions).
>
> **Next test:** offer the package at 49 and 55 to two halves of new enquiries for four weeks; compare sign-ups.
