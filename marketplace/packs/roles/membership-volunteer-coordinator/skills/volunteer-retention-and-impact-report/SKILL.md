# Volunteer retention and impact report

Groups lose most volunteers quietly: a shift missed, a message not answered, a move to a new job. This skill plans the year so people feel useful and thanked, catches them before they drift, learns from those who leave, and reports what volunteers did in numbers a funder can trust.

## When to use

- Many sign-ups, few still active after a few months.
- No regular thank-yous, or thanks that reach the same few people.
- A board, funder or annual report needs volunteer hours, people and impact.
- A volunteer problem is building and nobody knows how to raise it.

Not for: employment or benefits advice, disciplinary action against paid staff, or ending someone's volunteering without the client's decision. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified adviser where needed.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read sign-in sheets, the volunteer list, past thank-yous and any survey answers. Level 2 to draft messages, a calendar and the report in the group's tools. Level 3 to send approved thank-yous and check-ins to approved volunteer lists, and to log hours and thank-you dates, within the client's limits. Ending someone's volunteering, and formal replies to complaints, need a fresh yes every time.

- Sign-in sheets or hours logs for the period, and the roles people did.
- Start dates (for anniversaries) and who has stopped, with any reasons given.
- What the group can give: time from a named person, small items, events, training.
- Two or three true stories, with each person's consent to share.
- The funder's or board's reporting template, if any.

## Steps

1. **Read the drop-off.** From the records, count active volunteers by month of joining and see when people stop (after the first shift, after three months, after a year). Note what changed for those who left, if known.
2. **Map the moments.** For the year, list: welcome, first-shift check-in, one-month check-in, start-date anniversary, Volunteers' Week (1 to 7 June), International Volunteer Day (5 December), the group's own big days, and a leaving moment.
3. **Write a message for each moment.** Short, from a named person, saying one specific thing the volunteer or team made possible. Thank teams as well as individuals so no one feels ranked. Ask nothing in a thank-you.
4. **Plan check-ins that offer options.** NCVO's 2023 survey found less time from changing circumstances was the top reason people expect to stop, and satisfied volunteers were far more likely to stay. Each check-in asks how it's going and offers a lighter, shorter or remote option before people drift.
5. **Keep gifts inside the rules.** Small milestone items only (a card, a certificate, a mug, a team breakfast). No money, vouchers, valuable items or regular gifts, which can look like pay and affect tax or benefits. Expenses stay receipted.
6. **Write the exit question.** Two or three questions: what worked, what got in the way, and would they come back for a lighter role. Make it optional and anonymous if the group can.
7. **Set the problem route.** A short, separate volunteer process: raise it early, get the facts, hear everyone, agree a fix and a review date. Anything about harm goes straight to the safeguarding lead.
8. **Build the impact report.** Count hours and people from the sheets, by role and month. Add outputs the group records (meals packed, visits made). If the client wants a money value for time, use a named figure with its year and say how it was made (for example, Independent Sector's US figure of $36.14 per hour for 2025, a preliminary national average released 21 April 2026). Never invent a value. Add two or three consented stories and a method note: source, period, gaps, rounding.
9. **Prepare the send list.** At level 3, list the messages, dates and lists, and get one yes before the first send.

## Checks

- Every number in the report traces to a sheet or log, with gaps stated.
- Any money value names its source, year and method, or is left out.
- Each story has the person's consent on record.
- Thanks reach every volunteer at least once in the year, not only the most visible.
- No gift is money, a voucher, valuable or regular.
- Check-ins offer a lighter option; exit questions are optional.
- The plan carries "Template: check this with local rules before use."

## Output format

1. **Drop-off summary:** active volunteers by joining month and the main stopping points.
2. **Year calendar:** moment, who, channel, message, owner, date.
3. **Messages** for each moment, plus the exit question.
4. **Problem route** on half a page.
5. **Impact report:** headline numbers, table by role and month, stories, method note.
6. **Send list and questions for the client.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Riverbend Befriending is a made-up charity, used only to show the format. Names and numbers are invented.*

**Drop-off summary:** 31 people joined in the year; 12 were still active in October. Most who stopped did so after their first visit (9) or around month three (7). The month-three group often mentioned new work hours.

**One-month check-in (text, from Lena):** "Hi Sam, it's a month since you started visiting Mrs Patel. How's it going? If weekly visits are getting hard, a fortnightly visit or a phone call works too. Just reply and we'll sort it."

**Impact report extract:**

| Role | Volunteers | Hours (sign-in sheets) |
|---|---|---|
| Home visits | 14 | 612 |
| Phone calls | 9 | 238 |
| Total | 23 | 850 |

**Method note:** hours from weekly sign-in sheets, April to September; three weeks of August sheets are missing, so the total is likely low. No money value is shown, because the funder did not ask for one. Template: check this with local rules before use.
