# SOP writing

## When to use

- The same task is done three different ways and the results differ.
- One person is the only one who knows how to do something, and they are going on leave or leaving.
- A new starter needs to do a task alone soon.
- Mistakes keep happening at the same step.
- An old SOP exists but people ignore it.
- The owner is writing "the first system" so they can hand work over.

Do not use it to set safety limits, food safety controls, clinical steps or legal requirements. The SOP can carry those controls, but a qualified person sets and signs them, using the official guidance.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** existing SOPs, notes, recordings and the folder they live in.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** write the new SOP and checklist as a draft page in the wiki or a draft guide in the training tool.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved SOP, replace the old version (archive it, never delete), assign it to the role in the training tool and add the review date to the recurring-task calendar.

## What you need

1. The task name and the trigger: what starts it (a time, an order, a request).
2. Who will follow the SOP and what they already know.
3. The best source of how the task is really done, in order of preference: a recording or transcript of someone doing it, their notes, photos of each stage, an old SOP.
4. The tools and forms used, by name, as the client describes them.
5. The mistakes and complaints this task has caused, from the client's own records.
6. Any step that touches safety, food, money, customer data or the law.

## Steps

### 1. Agree the reader and the result
Write one line: who follows this SOP, and what "done well" looks like to the customer or the next person in line. If the client cannot say, ask before writing.

### 2. List the steps in the order they really happen
From the recording or notes, write every step as it happens, including the workarounds. Mark gaps and places where two sources disagree. Send the client one short list of questions in one go, not a stream of messages.

### 3. Write each step as one action
- Start with a verb: "Check", "Enter", "Print", "Call".
- Name the role that does it, not a person's name.
- Name the tool or form exactly as the client named it.
- After any step where things go wrong, add what the reader should see if it worked ("The order shows Paid in green").
- Split any step with "and" in it into two.

### 4. Add the frame around the steps
Purpose (one line), owner of the document, trigger, what you need before you start, the steps, the quality check that proves the task is done, common mistakes (only those the client reported or that follow plainly from the steps), and what to do when a step fails: who to tell, what to do with the job meanwhile.

### 5. Mark controlled steps
Any step that controls a hazard, food safety, money or personal data gets a marker: "Control step: set by [role or qualified person], see [official link]". Link the relevant official guidance for the client's country, such as HSE in the UK, OSHA in the US or the national body in the EU, and the food safety guidance for food businesses.

### 6. Make the checklist
Pick only the steps people skip or get wrong, plus any control steps. Keep it to one page. Choose read-do (follow line by line, for rare or risky tasks) or do-confirm (do from memory, then confirm, for routine tasks). Add a sign-off line where a record is needed.

### 7. Test it on a new reader
Read the draft as a new starter. Cut jargon, define any term that stays, and mark every assumption. Ask the client to have someone who has never done the task try it once and note where they got stuck. Fix those steps.

### 8. Set the owner, version and review date
Top of the document: owner, version, date and next review date. The review date depends on how often the task or its tools change; ask the client.

### 9. Publish (level 3) or hand over
At level 3, and after the client's yes, publish the SOP, archive the old version and add the review date to the calendar. Otherwise, hand over the files with a note on where they should live.

### Reviewing an old SOP
Compare it with a real run of the task. List gaps, unclear steps, steps nobody does and steps everyone does that are missing. Then rewrite it using the steps above and show the changes.

## Checks

- Every step starts with a verb and names a role.
- No tool, form, approval or rule appears that the client did not mention; anything assumed is marked "Needs answer".
- The quality check tells the reader how to know the task is done.
- There is a "when it goes wrong" path.
- Control steps are marked and linked to official guidance, with a named person to sign them off.
- The checklist fits on one page.
- Owner, version, date and review date are filled in.
- Nothing was published or replaced without level 3 access and the client's yes.

## Output format

1. The SOP (headings: Purpose, Owner, Trigger, Before you start, Steps, Quality check, Common mistakes, If something goes wrong, Version and review)
2. One-page checklist
3. Open questions marked "Needs answer"
4. Change list (for a review of an old SOP)
5. Action log (level 3 only)

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Brightwater Bike Repairs, a four-person workshop.*

> **SOP: Book in a repair**
> **Purpose:** Every bike that comes in is logged, quoted and tagged so it is never lost or fixed without approval.
> **Owner:** Workshop lead. **Trigger:** A customer brings in a bike. **Version** 2, 6 Nov 2026, review on the date the workshop lead sets.
> **Before you start:** Repair tablet open on the Jobs screen; blank tags.
> 1. **Ask** the customer what is wrong and when they need it back. Note both on the job card.
> 2. **Check** the bike with the customer present: brakes, gears, tyres, frame. Note any damage on the card and photograph it.
>    *You should see:* photos attached to the job in the tablet.
> 3. **Quote** the price range from the price list. If the work may cost more, tell the customer you will call before going over.
> 4. **Enter** the customer's name, phone number and the quote in the tablet. Press Save.
>    *You should see:* a job number on screen.
> 5. **Write** the job number on a tag and tie it to the handlebar.
>
> **Quality check:** Every bike in the workshop has a tag whose number matches an open job.
> **If the tablet is down:** fill in a paper job card, give it the next number from the paper book, and enter it when the tablet is back.
> **Needs answer:** Who may approve work above the quote when the customer cannot be reached?
