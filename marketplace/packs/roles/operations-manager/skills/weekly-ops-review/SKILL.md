# Weekly ops review

## When to use

- Every week, as the operating rhythm of a small business.
- The owner says "things keep slipping" but cannot say which things.
- Fixes agreed in meetings fade within a month.
- A first look at a business: an operations health check of its core routines.

Do not use it to judge individual staff performance, make pay or staffing decisions, or replace a safety or compliance specialist.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the weekly numbers (orders, jobs, complaints, refunds, overdue tasks, hours), last week's review and the task tool.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the review, the action list as tasks in a private project, and the calendar as tentative entries.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** post the review in the agreed channel, create and assign the actions in the task tool, set recurring tasks and reminders, and close actions that are done, within the client's limits.

## What you need

1. The numbers the business already tracks each week, with last week's and this week's figures. If none exist, start with four: output (orders or jobs finished), lead time or overdue work, errors or complaints, and hours worked.
2. Short notes from the owner or team leads: what went well, what went wrong.
3. Last week's action list and what happened to each action.
4. The recurring-task calendar, if one exists.

## Steps

### 1. Check last week's actions first
For each action: done, not done, or done but did not work. An action not done twice in a row is either dropped or given to someone with time for it. Say which.

### 2. Read the numbers
Compare this week with last week and with the usual range. For each number that moved, say whether the move is real or noise (one unusual order, a bank holiday). Pair each number with a guard: output with errors, speed with complaints, sales with refunds.

### 3. Sort the problems
Group problems by type: people (cover, skills, workload), process (steps, hand-offs), tools (systems, equipment), stock or supplies, and information (someone did not know). Most repeat problems are process or information.

### 4. Find the cause behind each
For each problem that matters, ask why until you reach something the team can change. If the cause is unclear, the action is to find out (count it, watch it), not to guess a fix.

### 5. Choose the fixes
Rank fixes by effect and effort. Choose only as many as the team can really finish next week. For each: one owner, one date and how you will know it worked.

### 6. Update the recurring-task calendar
Add any fix that must become a routine: a weekly check, a monthly stock count, a quarterly review. Every entry has an owner, a backup and a day.

### 7. Write the watch list
Two to five things that could go wrong next: a supplier running late, a staff holiday, a seasonal rush, a contract renewal. Each with the early warning sign.

### 8. Publish or hand over
At level 3, and within the agreed limits, post the review in the agreed channel, create and assign the actions and update the calendar. Otherwise hand over the one-page review and the action list.

### First-time health check
For a new client, score each core routine (taking orders, delivering, invoicing and chasing payment, stock, customer follow-up, admin and records) as working, shaky or broken, with the evidence. Name the five biggest fixes and start the weekly rhythm from there.

### Roles and backups chart
List every recurring task, who does it, who backs them up and what is missing. Tasks with no backup go on the watch list.

## Checks

- Every action from last week has a status.
- Every number has a source and is compared with something.
- Every fix has one owner, one date and a measure.
- The action list is short enough to finish.
- Causes name processes, not people's character.
- Guesses are labelled as guesses.
- Nothing was posted or assigned without level 3 access.

## Output format

One page:
1. Last week's actions: status
2. Numbers table: this week, last week, change, guard number, note
3. What worked
4. What slipped, and the cause
5. Fixes for next week: owner, date, measure
6. Calendar changes
7. Watch list

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Hollybank Cleaning Co, 14 cleaners and two office staff.*

> **Last week's actions:** Key log at the office: done. Text customers the day before: not done (second week); moved to Priya, who has Friday admin time.
>
> | Number | This week | Last week | Guard | Note |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Cleans completed | 212 | 205 | Complaints: 6 (last week 3) | Complaints doubled |
> | Late arrivals | 19 | 11 | Cleans cancelled: 2 (2) | 12 of 19 on Monday |
>
> **What slipped:** Late arrivals on Monday. Why? Teams waited for keys. Why? Keys for Monday jobs are collected on Monday morning. Why? No rule to prepare them on Friday.
> **Fix:** Bag Monday's keys by team on Friday afternoon. Owner: Priya. Date: Friday 14 Nov. Measure: Monday late arrivals (baseline 12).
> **Calendar:** Friday 3 pm, "Bag Monday keys", owner Priya, backup Tom.
> **Watch list:** Two cleaners off from 1 Dec (sign: open shifts on the rota by 20 Nov).
