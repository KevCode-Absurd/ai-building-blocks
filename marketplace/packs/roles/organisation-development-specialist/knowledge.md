# Organisation design specialist: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general organisation design and change practice. It is not legal advice. Restructures, role changes and redundancies are governed by employment law that differs by country, state and province, and they can need formal consultation. Where a step turns on those rules, I point to the official guidance and say when to ask HR or an employment lawyer. Check this with local rules before you act on any people decision.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires an organisation design specialist.** Founders and leadership teams of firms growing past about 100 people, groups merging two teams or companies, and organisations where every decision still goes through one or two people. The person asking is usually the chief executive, chief operating officer or HR lead. They feel the symptoms: slow decisions, work falling between teams, managers with too many or too few people, duplicated roles after a merger, and staff unsure who owns what.

The agents built on this kit deliver:
- a map of today's structure, as it really works, with spans, layers and the pain points people report;
- two or three org chart options with honest trade-offs and a recommendation;
- a decision rights table for the key decisions;
- role profiles for new or changed roles, describing roles, not people;
- a phased transition plan with owners, communication and risks;
- a staff announcement and FAQ;
- team health checks before and after the change.

**What good work looks like.**
- Structure follows strategy. The design starts from what the organisation must do better, not from a favourite model [3].
- Trade-offs are visible. Every option says what it fixes, what gets harder and what could go wrong.
- Roles come before names. Design the work and the roles; people decisions come later, with HR and legal advice.
- Decision rights are explicit, with one decider per decision [6].
- The people side is planned as carefully as the chart: what people lose, how they hear, who supports them [14].
- Legal steps are flagged early, so consultation and notice periods shape the timeline instead of breaking it [18][21].

**How the work is judged.**
- Do decisions move faster, and do fewer of them go to the top?
- Do teams know what they own, and does less work fall between them?
- Are spans and layers manageable for the managers in place [7]?
- Did the transition run without avoidable legal or trust damage?
- Do team health checks show clarity and safety holding or improving after the change [8][10]?

**What this role never does.** Choose which named people stay, move or go; give redundancy, consultation or employment law advice; set pay for named people; or handle personal details beyond role and team. Those decisions sit with the client, their HR lead and an employment lawyer.

**Related kits.** Hiring, policies, contracts and records are in the `hr-specialist` kit; staff surveys, change announcements and manager packs in `employee-engagement-specialist`; training for new or changed roles in `learning-development-specialist`. This kit points to them rather than repeating them.

## 2. The canon

**Jay Galbraith, the Star Model.** Five design policies leaders control: strategy (direction), structure (where decision power sits), processes (how information and decisions flow), rewards (motivation and incentives) and people (selecting and developing staff). All five must line up, and structure is only one of them [3]. Practical reading: a new org chart without matching processes, rewards and skills rarely changes behaviour.

**Melvin Conway, Conway's law (1968).** An organisation that designs a system produces a design that copies its own communication structure [4]. Practical reading: if you want two products or services to work together, put the people who build them in close contact.

**Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais, Team Topologies.** Four team types: stream-aligned (owns a flow of work end to end), enabling (helps other teams build missing skills), complicated subsystem (deep specialist work) and platform (internal services that speed up others). Three ways teams interact: collaboration, X-as-a-Service and facilitation. Overloaded teams decide badly and move slowly, so team cognitive load is a design limit [5]. Written for technology firms, it travels well to any team that delivers a service.

**Bain & Company, RAPID.** Five decision roles: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide. Ideally each decision has one decider, and "Agree" is used sparingly, mainly for legal or regulatory checks [6]. RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) is the more common grid; both work when the "one decider" rule holds.

**Amy Edmondson, psychological safety (1999).** In a field study of 51 work teams, a shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks was linked to learning behaviour, which in turn was linked to team performance [9].

**Google, Project Aristotle.** Across 180 Google teams, five dynamics set effective teams apart, in this order: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning and impact. How the team worked together mattered more than who was on it [8].

**Change models.** John Kotter's eight steps run from creating a sense of urgency and building a guiding coalition to generating short-term wins and instituting change [12]. Prosci's ADKAR model tracks individual change through awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement, and finds where a person is stuck [13]. William Bridges separates change (the outside event) from transition (the inner process) and names three phases: endings, the neutral zone and new beginnings [14].

**CIPD on organisation development.** CIPD defines organisation development as a planned, systematic approach to sustained performance through involving people, built on alignment with strategy, behavioural science and continual improvement. Its four intervention types are human process (coaching, facilitation), techno-structural (process redesign), human resource (performance, reward, surveys) and strategic (culture and transformation). When change is broad and interconnected, development becomes design; the two are complementary but distinct [1].

**Reading list (credited, not quoted).** Kurt Lewin's work on group change; Henry Mintzberg, *Structure in Fives*; Jay Galbraith, *Designing Organizations*; Amy Kates and Jay Galbraith, *Designing Your Organization*; Edgar Schein, *Organizational Culture and Leadership*; Amy Edmondson, *The Fearless Organization*; J. Richard Hackman, *Leading Teams*; William Bridges, *Managing Transitions*; John Kotter, *Leading Change*. Together they push one habit: design the whole system, not just the boxes, and plan for how people experience the change.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Diagnose before designing
- Read the strategy, the org chart, headcount by team and any staff interview notes. Write down what the structure must make easier: faster decisions, clearer customer ownership, less duplication.
- Draw today's structure as it really works, not as the old chart shows it. Mermaid flowcharts turn a text list into a diagram any AI tool or wiki can render; use top-down direction and subgraphs for departments, and avoid the lowercase word "end" in box text [28].
- Count spans (direct reports per manager) and layers (levels from front line to chief executive). List pain points with how often each came up in interviews.

### Spans and layers
- Gallup's 2026 analysis found the average number of direct reports per manager rose from 10.9 in 2024 to 12.1 in 2025, while the median stayed at about five or six; 13% of managers had 25 or more [7]. Gallup's conclusion is that manager talent matters more than span alone, and wide spans work only when managers have the time and support to lead [7].
- So judge span by the work: how similar the roles are, how experienced the people, how much the manager also does hands-on work, and how often people need feedback. Do not apply a single "ideal" number.

### Design options
- Common shapes: functional (by expertise), divisional (by product, customer or region), matrix (two reporting lines), and team-based or self-managing structures.
- **Real example:** Buurtzorg, the Dutch home-care organisation founded in 2006, organises nurses in self-managing neighbourhood teams of twelve that decide how to organise their own work. It reports growing to over 10,000 nurses in 850 teams in its first ten years and to more than 14,000 professionals today [16]. Self-management works there because the work is local, the teams are small and the professionals are highly trained; copy the conditions, not just the chart.
- For each option, write what it fixes, what gets harder, what it costs (new roles, more layers, lost focus) and the risks. Check it against all five points of the Star Model [3] and against Conway's law [4].
- Recommend one, and show it fairly against the others.

### Decision rights
- List the decisions that matter most (hiring, pricing, budgets, product changes, customer exceptions). For each, name who recommends, who must agree, who gives input, who decides and who carries it out [6].
- One decider per decision. If two people both think they decide, the table has failed.

### Role profiles
- For each new or changed role: purpose, outcomes, key decisions, reporting line, and the essential functions. SHRM suggests testing each task by whether it is a requirement, how often it is done, what happens if it isn't, other ways to do it, and whether it could be reassigned; in the US this also helps with disability accommodation requests [29].
- Profiles describe roles, never named people. Pay grades go to HR.

### Transition and change
- Plan in phases with owners, dates, communication and risks. Expect people to move through endings, a neutral zone and new beginnings at their own pace [14]; managers need help to hold those conversations.
- Use change models as checklists, not scripts: is there a clear case for change, a guiding group, early wins [12]? Where is each affected group on awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement [13]?
- CIPD notes that change initiatives are often unsuccessful and that poorly managed change can harm performance and wellbeing [2]. Plan the people side with the same care as the structure.

### Team health
- Run a short, facilitated health check before and after a change. Atlassian's Health Monitor covers eight attributes, suits 3 to 11 people, takes about 90 minutes, uses a simultaneous thumbs vote, picks one to three areas to improve and repeats quarterly [10].
- Spotify's Squad Health Check (2014) uses traffic lights and trend arrows on ten indicators, warns it is a support tool, not a judgement tool, cautions against comparing teams, and says an online survey loses the conversation that gives most of the value [11].
- Use the results to fix how teams work. Never use them to rate managers or to choose people for a restructure.

### Legal steps by region (flag, never decide)
- **UK.** Employers should consult staff on changes, and must by law in some cases, including collective redundancies of 20 or more and TUPE transfers [18]. Changes to contract terms must be agreed, and employers should avoid "fire and rehire" [17]. For 20 or more proposed redundancies within 90 days at one establishment, GOV.UK sets minimum consultation of 30 days (20 to 99) or 45 days (100 or more) and an HR1 notice [21]. Acas's redundancy guidance (updated 7 April 2026) starts with checking alternatives such as redeployment and pausing hiring [19]. When a business or service moves to a new employer, TUPE may apply [20][22].
- **EU.** Directive 2002/14/EC requires information and consultation on decisions likely to lead to substantial changes in work organisation, in undertakings of at least 50 or establishments of at least 20 employees, as each country chooses [23]. Directive 98/59/EC sets collective redundancy consultation "with a view to reaching an agreement" and notice to the public authority [24]. National law adds detail.
- **US.** The WARN Act requires advance notice of qualifying plant closings and mass layoffs [25]; check your state labour department as well. Role profiles that define essential functions also matter for disability accommodation [29].
- In every case: name the step, point to the official page, and tell the client to ask HR or an employment lawyer before acting. Check this with local rules.

### Other countries: where to check
I give no detail on these countries' rules. Start with the official source and a qualified adviser locally.
- **India:** the Ministry of Labour and Employment; its site blocked automated reading on 6 November 2026, so it is not in sources.json. State labour departments may also apply.
- **Canada:** Employment and Social Development Canada for federally regulated employers [26]; other employers check their province's or territory's employment standards office.
- **Australia:** business.gov.au's employer pages [27] and the Fair Work Ombudsman, whose site blocked automated reading, so it is not in sources.json.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client can't say what the structure must make easier, agree that first, because structure follows strategy [3].
- If a design changes boxes but not processes, rewards or skills, add those or expect little to change [3].
- If two teams must build things that fit together, put them close in the structure or give them a clear interface, because designs copy communication lines [4].
- If a decision has two "deciders", pick one and make the other an input or an agree role [6].
- If a manager's span is wide, check their hands-on workload and the support they get before adding layers [7].
- If a team owns too many unrelated things, split or move work, because overloaded teams decide badly [5].
- If the client asks you to design around who they want to keep or remove, design roles first and leave people decisions to them and their adviser.
- If a change may alter people's contract terms, roles or job security, flag consultation and tell them to ask HR or an employment lawyer before any announcement [17][18]. Check this with local rules.
- If 20 or more UK roles could go at one site within 90 days, put the collective consultation and HR1 steps in the timeline before any decision is final [21].
- If a business, team or service is moving between employers, ask whether TUPE or a national equivalent applies [22][24].
- If staff will lose something (a manager, a team, status), plan the "endings" conversation, not just the new chart [14].
- If a health check shows low psychological safety, fix it before a big change, not after [8][9].
- If health check results would be used to judge or select people, refuse that use [11].

## 6. Common mistakes

- **The new chart first.** Spot it: options drawn before today's structure is mapped. Fix: draw the current state and pain points first.
- **Structure only.** Spot it: no change to processes, rewards or skills [3].
- **Designing around people.** Spot it: boxes shaped to fit, or remove, named individuals. Fix: roles first, people decisions with HR.
- **Flat for its own sake.** Spot it: removing team leads leaves one manager with more people than they can coach, and one-to-ones quietly stop. Wide spans need manager time and support [7].
- **Two deciders.** Spot it: a RACI with two "A"s, or "the leadership team" as decider [6].
- **Matrix without rules.** Spot it: two bosses and no rule for who wins a conflict.
- **Announce, then consult.** Spot it: a staff email before the legal steps were checked [18][21].
- **Treating change as an event.** Spot it: a launch date and nothing after it [14].
- **Myth numbers.** Spot it: "70% of change fails" in the business case [15].
- **Health checks as league tables.** Spot it: team scores compared in a leadership pack [11].
- **Too many personal details.** Spot it: names, pay or health notes in design documents. Fix: roles, teams and counts only.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Is the design's purpose written in one or two lines, tied to strategy?
2. Is today's structure mapped, with spans, layers and counted pain points?
3. Does each option have "fixes", "gets harder" and "risks", and is the recommendation fair to the others?
4. Does every key decision have exactly one decider?
5. Do role profiles describe roles, not people, with no pay for named people?
6. Does the transition plan include legal and consultation steps flagged for HR or an employment lawyer, before any announcement?
7. Does every team and number come from the client's brief?
8. Is the people side planned: who is affected, what they lose, how they hear, who supports them?
9. Are personal details limited to role and team?
10. Does every document carry "check this with local rules" where people decisions are involved?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Formats.** Today's structure as a Mermaid diagram; each option as a diagram with a three-column table (what it fixes, what gets harder, risks); a decision rights grid; role profiles on one page each; a phased transition table (phase, actions, owner, date, communication, risks).
- **Tone.** Reflective, measured and fair. Set out choices honestly; recommend one without overselling it.
- **Sentences.** Considered and balanced, with a "but" where the trade-off sits.
- **Words to avoid.** "Rightsizing", "efficiencies" as a euphemism, "dead wood", "flat is always better", and "agile" as a cure-all.
- **Hand-offs.** Every deliverable ends with open questions, decisions for the leadership team, and the legal steps for HR or an employment lawyer.

## 9. Glossary

- **Organisation design:** shaping structure, processes, roles and decision rights to fit strategy [1][3].
- **Organisation development (OD):** planned, people-involving change to improve how an organisation works [1].
- **Star Model:** Galbraith's five aligned design policies: strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people [3].
- **Span of control:** the number of people reporting directly to one manager [7].
- **Layers:** the number of management levels from front line to the top.
- **Functional structure:** teams grouped by expertise, such as finance or engineering.
- **Divisional structure:** teams grouped by product, customer or region.
- **Matrix:** a structure where people report on two lines.
- **Stream-aligned team:** a team that owns a flow of work end to end [5].
- **Cognitive load:** how much a team must hold in mind to do its work [5].
- **Conway's law:** systems copy the communication structure of the organisation that builds them [4].
- **Decision rights:** who recommends, agrees, gives input, decides and acts on a decision [6].
- **RAPID:** Bain's decision roles: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide [6].
- **RACI:** Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed.
- **Role profile:** purpose, outcomes, decisions and reporting line of a role.
- **Essential functions:** the core tasks a role exists to do [29].
- **Psychological safety:** a shared belief that it is safe to take interpersonal risks in a team [9].
- **Team health check:** a facilitated self-assessment of how a team works [10][11].
- **Transition:** the inner process people go through during change [14].
- **ADKAR:** awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement [13].
- **Collective consultation:** required consultation with representatives before large-scale redundancies [21][24].
- **TUPE:** UK rules protecting employees when a business or service moves to a new employer [22].
