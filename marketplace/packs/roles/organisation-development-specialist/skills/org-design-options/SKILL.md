# Org design options

## When to use

- The current structure is mapped and design criteria are agreed.
- Leaders want choices, not a single answer.
- Two teams or companies are merging.
- A new strategy (new market, product line or service) doesn't fit today's structure.

Do not use it to decide which named people get which roles, to plan redundancies, or to set pay.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the current structure map, design criteria, strategy notes.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draw options on a new board; write role profiles as draft pages.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved option and role profiles to the page the client named, within their limits. Sharing options with staff, or anything that affects a named person's job, needs a fresh yes each time.

## What you need

1. The current structure map and its pain points (from the `current-structure-map` skill).
2. The agreed design criteria.
3. Constraints: budget for new roles, locations, union or works council agreements, timing.
4. Any options leaders already favour.

## Steps

### 1. Choose the main grouping for each option
Pick a different primary logic per option, so they are real alternatives:
- **Functional:** grouped by expertise. Strong depth; slower cross-team work.
- **Divisional:** grouped by product, customer or region. Clear ownership; duplicated skills.
- **Matrix:** two reporting lines. Flexible; needs clear rules for who wins a conflict.
- **Team-based:** small teams that own a flow of work end to end (stream-aligned teams), with enabling or platform teams around them. Fast delivery; needs mature teams and clear interfaces.

### 2. Draw each option in Mermaid
Same style as the current map, roles and headcount only. Show which teams move and which roles are new or changed.

### 3. Test against the criteria and the Star Model
For each design criterion: does this option meet it (yes, partly, no) and why? Then check the other four points: what processes, rewards and skills must change for this structure to work? An option that needs a reward change the client won't make is weaker than it looks.

### 4. Check Conway's law and cognitive load
Do teams that must build things together sit close? Does any team own too many unrelated things? Overloaded teams decide badly and move slowly.

### 5. Count spans and layers
For each option, the new spans per manager role and the number of layers. Note where a manager would have too many people to coach, or too few to justify the layer.

### 6. Write the three-column table
For each option: **What it fixes** (tie to pain points), **What gets harder** (always something), **Risks** (transition risks, single points of failure, cost of new roles). Add a cost line: new roles, roles removed or changed, in counts only.

### 7. Recommend one, fairly
Say which option best meets the criteria and why, and what would make you choose another. Don't oversell: name its biggest weakness and how to manage it.

### 8. Write role profiles
For each new or changed role in the recommended option: purpose (one line), outcomes (three to five), key decisions it owns, reporting line, main relationships, and essential functions. For each task, note whether it is essential, how often it is done, what happens if it isn't done and whether it could be reassigned; in the US this also helps with disability accommodation. No names, no pay; grades go to HR.

### 9. Flag people and legal steps
List where the recommended option changes reporting lines, roles, locations or job numbers. Say plainly that these may need consultation, contract agreement or notice, and that the client should ask HR or an employment lawyer before anything is shared with staff. Check this with local rules.

## Checks

- Options use genuinely different groupings.
- Every option has "fixes", "gets harder", "risks" and a cost line in counts.
- Each design criterion is scored for each option.
- Star Model points beyond structure are covered.
- Spans and layers are counted for each option.
- The recommendation names its own weakness.
- Role profiles describe roles only; no names, pay or ratings.
- People and legal steps are flagged for HR or an employment lawyer, not decided.

## Output format

1. Options summary (one paragraph).
2. Per option: Mermaid chart, three-column table, criteria scores, Star Model notes, spans and layers.
3. Recommendation with its weakness and what would change the choice.
4. Role profiles for new or changed roles.
5. People and legal flags.
6. Open questions for leaders.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a software firm, 140 staff. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Option B: customer-aligned teams**
>
> | What it fixes | What gets harder | Risks |
> |---|---|---|
> | One team owns each customer group from sale to renewal (8 of 10 interviews) | Specialist sales skills spread across three teams | Customer success lead role changes; three new team lead roles to fill |
> | Pricing exceptions decided within each team | Consistent pricing across teams needs a shared rule | Founders must let go of discount decisions |
>
> **Role profile: Customer team lead (new, 3 roles)**
> Purpose: own revenue and retention for one customer group.
> Outcomes: renewal rate, expansion revenue, customer satisfaction for the group.
> Decides: discounts within the agreed range; team priorities each quarter.
> Reports to: CEO. Essential functions: weekly pipeline and renewal review (essential, weekly); coaching team members (essential, ongoing).
>
> **Flag:** the customer success lead's role would change substantially. Speak to HR or an employment lawyer before discussing it with anyone affected.
