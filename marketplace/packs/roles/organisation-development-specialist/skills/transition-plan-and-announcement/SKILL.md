# Transition plan and announcement

## When to use

- Leaders have chosen a structure and need to move to it.
- Two teams are merging after an acquisition.
- A change will alter reporting lines, roles, locations or team membership.
- Staff need a clear, honest message and answers to their questions.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the chosen option, role profiles, headcount by team, timelines, any union or works council agreements.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** write the plan, briefing notes, announcement and FAQ as drafts; build an unsent team health survey.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** within the client's limits, publish approved non-personal pages (the plan's milestones, the decision rights table), send approved team health surveys and book approved meetings. Every staff message about the change, every consultation or at-risk letter, and every record change for a named person needs a fresh yes each time, after HR or legal review.

## What you need

1. The chosen option, role profiles and decision rights table.
2. Which roles, teams and locations change, in counts.
3. Where staff work (countries, states, provinces) and any employee representatives.
4. The target date and why.
5. Who leads the change and who briefs managers.
6. The client's HR lead or employment lawyer contact.

## Steps

### 1. Put legal checkpoints first
List what the change touches: contract terms, roles, locations, possible job losses, transfers between employers. For each, name the official source to check (Acas and GOV.UK in the UK, EU national rules under the information and consultation and collective redundancy directives, the US Department of Labor and state agencies, and the official sources for other countries) and mark it "HR or employment lawyer to confirm". Consultation periods and notices can set the earliest dates, so build the timeline around them. Check this with local rules.

### 2. Map who is affected
A stakeholder table by group, not by name: what changes for them, what they may lose (a manager, a team, status, a familiar way of working), what they need to know, and who tells them.

### 3. Plan the phases
Typical phases: prepare (legal checks, manager briefing), inform and consult (as required), decide and confirm, move (new lines, handovers), settle (support and review). For each: actions, owner role, dates, communication, risks and how to spot them early.

### 4. Brief managers for transition, not just change
Managers hold the hard conversations. Give them: the reasons, what is decided and what isn't, what they can and can't say, and the three phases people go through (endings, a neutral zone where the old has gone and the new isn't settled, then new beginnings). Add a short ADKAR check: does each group have awareness, desire, knowledge, ability and reinforcement?

### 5. Write the announcement
Plain words, roles and teams, no names. What is changing and why; what is not changing; what happens next and when; how people will be supported; where to ask questions. Avoid euphemisms like "rightsizing". If consultation is required, say that proposals are subject to consultation and nothing is final.

### 6. Write the FAQ
The questions people will really ask: Will my job change? Who will my manager be? Will my pay change? Do I have to move? What if I disagree? Answer honestly, and where the answer isn't known yet, say when it will be. Route individual questions to HR.

### 7. Plan team health checks
Run a short, facilitated team health check before the change and again a few months after, in the teams most affected. Use results to fix how teams work, never to judge people. Report only above the minimum group size.

### 8. Set the review
Name what success looks like (faster decisions, clear ownership, team health holding), how it will be checked, and when leaders will review the structure.

## Checks

- Legal checkpoints come before any staff communication, each marked for HR or an employment lawyer.
- No named people anywhere in the plan, announcement or FAQ.
- Every phase has owners, dates, communication and risks.
- Managers have a briefing with what they can and can't say.
- The announcement says what isn't changing and how people will be supported.
- FAQ answers are honest; unknowns say when they'll be known.
- Health checks are for improvement only, above the minimum group size.
- Every staff message and personal record change is marked "fresh yes required".

## Output format

1. Legal and consultation checkpoints table (item, official source, who confirms, earliest date).
2. Stakeholder table by group.
3. Phased transition table.
4. Manager briefing notes.
5. Staff announcement (draft) and FAQ.
6. Team health check plan.
7. Success measures and review date.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Harrowgate Home Services, merging two customer service teams of 40 and 25 after buying a competitor.*

> | Checkpoint | Official source | Who confirms | Earliest date |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | Do the acquired team's staff move to a new employer? | GOV.UK and Acas pages on TUPE | Employment lawyer | Before any announcement |
> | Do any contract terms change (hours, location)? | Acas: changing an employment contract | HR lead | Before consultation starts |
>
> **Announcement (extract):** "We're bringing our two customer service teams together into one team of 65, organised by customer type. Your pay, hours and location are not changing as part of this proposal. Over the next two weeks your manager will meet you to talk it through, and we'll publish answers to your questions every Friday."
>
> **Fresh yes required:** sending this announcement; any change to a named person's manager in the HR system.
