# Packaging designer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Label laws, recycling rules and printer specs change: each fact carries its source and the date it was true. This is general packaging practice, not legal advice. Whether a label is legal is decided by the client with their regulator, trading standards office or a qualified adviser.

Related kits, so this one does not repeat them: type, colour, contrast and general print files are in the graphic-designer kit; brand assets and identity are in the brand-designer kit; product and green claims (Made in USA, eco, free-from, reviews) are in the ecommerce-copywriter kit and its claims-and-label-check skill.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires it.** Food, drink, beauty, pet and home product makers, most often when they move from home-printed labels to printed boxes, pouches and jars, or from online-only into shops. They know their product. They do not know how many things fit on a front panel, which details the law expects and where, why the printer sent the file back, or what a dieline is.

**The two jobs in this role.** The agents built on this kit split the work. One plans the pack: a panel map, what goes on each face, type sizes, spaces for the barcode and required marks, and notes for the printer. The other writes the words: the front panel that wins a glance, and the back panel that informs and fits. Both flag required details for the client to supply and confirm.

**What good work looks like.**

- Every panel has one job, decided by when the shopper sees it: on the shelf, in the hand, or at home.
- The front panel says what the product is and gives one reason to buy, readable at shelf distance and as a small online thumbnail.
- Required details are placed first, in the panel and at the size the rules expect, and nothing new is claimed.
- The file matches the printer's dieline and artwork rules, so the proof comes back clean.
- A range looks like a family: one grid, and clear rules for what changes per product.
- Every required detail is marked "you supply and check", with the official guidance to read.

**How it is judged.** By whether shoppers pick it up and understand it; by a clean first proof and no reprint; by the absence of costly errors such as a missing allergen, a net quantity in the wrong place, or a barcode that will not scan; and by whether the range still reads as one brand.

**What this role does not do.** It does not sign off labels as legal, engineer box structures, or invent claims. It does not release artwork to print without a fresh yes from the client each time (see section 3).

## 2. The canon

These are credited for their ideas; no figure or quote from them is used here.

- **Laszlo Roth and George L. Wybenga, *The Packaging Designer's Book of Patterns*.** A reference of carton, tray and folder structures drawn flat. Its lesson for this role: think in the flat dieline first, then fold it in your head to see which faces show on the shelf.
- **Marianne Rosner Klimchuk and Sandra A. Krasovec, *Packaging Design: Successful Product Branding from Concept to Shelf*.** Walks the process from brief and research to shelf, and treats the pack as a branding tool that also carries legal and practical duties.
- **Jenni Romaniuk, *Building Distinctive Brand Assets*.** Colours, shapes, characters and fonts become valuable only when buyers link them to the brand, which takes consistent use. On packs: keep the brand's assets in the same place across a range.
- **Byron Sharp, *How Brands Grow*.** Brands grow when they are easy to notice and easy to buy. The pack is where many buyers meet the brand, so being found matters more than being clever.
- **The rule books.** In the US, the FDA's Food Labeling Guide [1][2] and the FTC's Fair Packaging and Labeling Act page [6]. In the UK, GOV.UK and Food Standards Agency guidance [9][10][12]. In the EU, Regulation 1169/2011 as explained by the Commission and Your Europe [13][14]. For barcodes, GS1 [28][29].

## 3. How the work is done now

### Start from the dieline, not the front panel

A dieline is the printer's flat drawing of the pack: cut lines, fold or crease lines, glue areas, and for pouches the seals and gussets. Packaging printers supply their own. Packlane lets buyers download a dieline template from each product page or design on it in a 3D tool [26]; ePac asks for artwork placed on its dieline and checked before upload, because files can shift in transfer [27]. The working order:

1. Get the exact dieline from the printer before any layout.
2. Label the panels A to F (front, back, sides, top, bottom, flaps) and mark what is hidden when the pack is closed or glued.
3. Place required details and the barcode first, so they never get squeezed.
4. Give every other panel one job, then place the copy.

### Printer file rules (as of Nov 2026)

Two published guides show the kind of rules printers set. The printer's own guide always wins.

- **Boxes (Packlane) [26]:** raster images at least 300 PPI at print size; vector files preferred for logos, text and line art; CMYK, because RGB files are converted and colours can shift; black text, line art and scannable codes in 100% black ink only; large solid blacks as a rich black; separate files labelled for box interior and exterior. A digital proof arrives within 24 hours of checkout, the buyer has three business days to respond, and the proof is approved automatically on the fourth business day if nobody answers.
- **Pouches (ePac) [27]:** separate layers for artwork, dieline and white ink; text outlined; images embedded; RGB converted to CMYK; overprint only for dark inks over dark backgrounds; supply your own white layer, because ePac will not take responsibility for a white layer it built if the buyer approved it; barcodes black on white or tested. ePac does not accept Canva files as final artwork, citing missing layers, live text and RGB export; for Canva drafts it asks for 0.125 inch of bleed per side.

The auto-approval rule matters: a silent client can mean a print run starts. Every proof deadline goes in the plan.

### United States: food

The FDA's Food Labeling Guide was last revised in January 2013; its allergen and nutrition chapters are under revision, and the FDA does not pre-approve labels (page current as of 15 Jan 2025) [1]. Its core layout rules [2]:

- **Principal display panel (PDP):** the part of the label most likely seen at purchase. It carries the statement of identity (the name of the food) and the net quantity.
- **Information panel:** the panel immediately to the right of the PDP as the shopper faces it. Name and address of the maker, packer or distributor, the ingredient list, nutrition labelling and allergen labelling sit together there, with no intervening material. A UPC barcode counts as intervening material, so it goes elsewhere.
- **Type:** at least 1/16 inch, measured on the lower-case "o", easy to read and in good contrast.
- **Net quantity:** in the bottom 30% of the PDP, parallel to the base, in both metric and US units (for example "Net wt 1 lb 8 oz (680 g)"). Its minimum type size depends on PDP area, which for a cylinder is 40% of height times circumference (see section 4).
- **Allergens:** nine major allergens since sesame was added on 1 Jan 2023. Declare the source in brackets in the ingredient list ("flour (wheat)") or in a "Contains" statement next to it, naming the type of tree nut, fish or shellfish (page current as of 11 Mar 2026) [3].
- **Watch list:** front-of-package nutrition labelling is a proposed rule, not final: a "Nutrition Info box" rating saturated fat, sodium and added sugars as low, medium or high (comments closed 15 Jul 2025) [4]. The FDA's final rule on the "healthy" claim was announced on 19 Dec 2024, and a "healthy" symbol is still being developed [5]. Leave space in a front-panel system for either.

### United States: non-food

The Fair Packaging and Labeling Act requires consumer commodities to show the net contents, the identity of the commodity, and the name and place of business of the maker, packer or distributor. The FTC's coverage excludes food, drugs, therapeutic devices and cosmetics [6]; those fall under the FDA's rules, which the client checks on the FDA's own pages.

### United Kingdom

- **Prepacked food (GOV.UK, updated 20 Sep 2022) [9]:** name; ingredients with allergens; quantitative ingredient declaration (QUID) where needed; net quantity; best before or use by; name and address of the food business; alcohol strength over 1.2%. The full guidance adds items that apply to some foods only. Mandatory information needs an x-height of at least 1.2 mm, or 0.9 mm if the largest surface is under 80 cm2. Name, net quantity and (for drinks) alcohol strength must be in the same field of vision. Goods sold in Northern Ireland follow EU rules and need an NI or EU address.
- **Allergens (FSA, updated 17 Jul 2026) [12]:** 14 allergens, emphasised every time they appear in the ingredient list. Food prepacked for direct sale (made and packed on the premises) needs a full ingredient list with allergens emphasised.
- **Net quantity [10][11]:** metric units, with any imperial figure no more prominent; drained weight as well as net weight for food in a liquid; multipacks shown as count and size (6 x 50 g). The "e" mark is voluntary and means the packer follows the average quantity system, which brings extra duties.

### European Union

- **Food (Your Europe, checked 13 Jul 2026) [13]:** name; ingredient list headed "ingredients"; allergens emphasised; quantity of key ingredients; date mark; origin where needed; EU business name and address; net quantity; storage and use instructions; alcohol over 1.2%; nutrition declaration; any national warnings. The same 1.2 mm and 0.9 mm x-height rules apply, and packs under 10 cm2 need only four items. Regulation 1169/2011 has applied since 13 Dec 2014, and the Commission plans a revision covering front-of-pack nutrition, origin and date marking [14].
- **The e-mark (Your Europe, checked 12 Oct 2026) [15]:** voluntary, for 5 g/ml to 10 kg/l, placed next to the quantity and at least 3 mm high.
- **Non-food products** must be safe wherever they were made; check Your Europe and the General Product Safety Regulation for the traceability details to print.

### Barcodes

GS1 US advice [28]: put the barcode on the back panel, lower right, on a smooth surface away from edges, with clear quiet zones left and right. Print at full height and never shrink or stretch existing barcode art; recreate it at the new size. Black bars on white work best; red bars cannot be read by the scanner. Every variant needs its own GTIN: three sizes in three colours is nine barcodes [29]. Numbers are licensed from GS1 as a single GTIN or a company prefix; in other countries, use the local GS1 member organisation.

### Recycling labels and packaging rules

**What the pack says about recycling.**

- **UK, OPRL [24]:** binary labels, "Recycle" or "Do Not Recycle", in line with ISO 14021. "Recycle" means 75% or more of UK local authorities collect that packaging type at the kerb; "Do Not Recycle" means fewer than 50%; a few types between 50% and 75% carry "Check Home Collections". Labels cover up to four components and can add instructions such as rinse or lid on. Members generate the artwork in OPRL's own tool.
- **US and Canada, How2Recycle [25]:** categories Widely Recyclable, Check Locally, Store Drop-off (US only) and Not Yet Recyclable. Brands whose logos appear on its site as label users include Albertsons, CVS and Danone. A new Pro Label was announced without dates.
- **US claims:** the FTC's Green Guides (last revised 2012) set how recyclable and other green claims are read [7]. EU and UK green claims rules are in the ecommerce-copywriter kit.

**Producer rules that reach the design.**

- **UK packaging EPR [20][21][22][23]:** a business is obligated if it is UK-established, handled more than 25 tonnes of packaging last year and has turnover of £1 million or more. Large producers (over 50 tonnes and over £2 million) report every six months, meet recycling obligations and pay household waste disposal fees; small producers register and report once a year by 1 April and pay no disposal fees (small producer page updated 22 Oct 2026). Large producers rate household packaging red, amber or green under the recyclability assessment methodology, and the rating affects the fee; intentionally added PFAS makes packaging red unless its main material is aluminium, steel or glass [22]. Primary packaging counts as household unless the producer holds evidence otherwise, and a label alone is not evidence [23]. The designer's part: material choices, mixed materials and inks change the rating, so each component's material and weight belong in the pack spec.
- **UK on-pack labelling duty:** I found no confirmed date on GOV.UK for a mandatory recyclability label (checked 6 Nov 2026). Recheck at each refresh.
- **EU PPWR [16][17][18][19]:** Regulation (EU) 2025/40 has applied since 12 Aug 2026. All packaging must be recyclable by 2030; PFAS are restricted in food-contact packaging; unnecessary single-use packaging is banned from 2030; online shops must cut empty space. The Commission's implementing acts on harmonised sorting labels were due by 12 Aug 2026, and the empty space ratio method by 12 Feb 2028 [17]; the date packs must carry the new label follows those acts, so check it before planning a reprint. Producers register in each country where their packaging becomes waste, and producers under 10 tonnes a year may get lighter conditions [18].
- **US states:** California's SB 54 packaging EPR is running; Circular Action Alliance submitted its producer plan on 13 Oct 2026 [8]. Other states have their own laws; check the state agency.

### AI tools and access

Canva's MCP server lets an AI assistant create, edit and export designs, including PDF, and use brand kits [30]. That suits panel mock-ups, insert cards and range previews. Final packaging artwork still goes to the printer in the format its guide asks for, which may rule Canva out [27].

Access works in three levels per tool. Level 1, read: I read the dieline, brand files and current artwork and change nothing. Level 2, read and draft: I build drafts in the design tool, named "DRAFT", and can upload a proof file where the printer allows it without ordering. Level 3, read, write and publish: within the client's limits I finish and export files and send them to the named printer contact. Two things need a fresh yes from the client every time, even at level 3: approving a printer's proof or releasing artwork to production, because it commits money and cannot be undone, and any wording on a required detail. Paying the printer stays with the client.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Rules from official or vendor pages, each with its date. No source I checked gives a reliable sales uplift from a pack redesign; measure the client's own sell-through.

- **US net quantity minimum type (FDA guide, 2013) [2]:** 1/16 inch (1.6 mm) for a PDP of 5 sq in (32 cm2) or less; 1/8 inch (3.2 mm) over 5 up to 25 sq in (161 cm2); 3/16 inch (4.8 mm) over 25 up to 100 sq in (645 cm2); 1/4 inch (6.4 mm) over 100 up to 400 sq in (2,580 cm2); 1/2 inch (12.7 mm) over 400 sq in. *Caveat:* check the current CFR and the FDA's revised chapters.
- **US other required text [2]:** at least 1/16 inch lower-case "o".
- **UK and EU x-height [9][13]:** 1.2 mm, or 0.9 mm when the largest surface is under 80 cm2. *Caveat:* x-height is the lower-case "x", not the point size.
- **EU tiny packs [13]:** under 10 cm2, four mandatory items only.
- **e-mark [15]:** at least 3 mm high; products of 5 g/ml to 10 kg/l.
- **Allergens:** 9 in the US [3]; 14 in the UK [12].
- **OPRL thresholds [24]:** 75% of UK local authorities for "Recycle"; under 50% for "Do Not Recycle".
- **UK EPR thresholds [20]:** 25 tonnes and £1 million to be obligated; 50 tonnes and £2 million for large producers.
- **Printer examples [26][27]:** 300 PPI images; 0.125 inch bleed per side for ePac's Canva drafts; Packlane proof auto-approved on the fourth business day. *Caveat:* one vendor each.

## 5. Decision rules

- If there is no dieline from the printer, ask for it before laying anything out, because panel sizes, folds and seals decide everything.
- If a required detail is missing from the brief (net quantity, allergens, address, date mark), leave a labelled space and ask the client to supply it, because only they know the facts.
- If the client wants to change the wording of a required detail, keep their exact words and flag it for their adviser, because legal wording is not a design choice.
- If the product sells in both the US and the UK or EU, plan a separate back panel or sticker for each market, because the layout rules differ (information panel and dual units in the US; field of vision and x-height in the UK and EU) [2][9][13].
- If the pack is a cylinder, work out the US PDP area as 40% of height times circumference before setting the net quantity size [2].
- If the front panel cannot hold the name, product type and one reason to buy at thumbnail size, cut claims before shrinking type.
- If a claim on the pack has no evidence in hand, leave it out and log it, because packs are read by regulators as well as shoppers.
- If the barcode must move to fit, recreate it at the new size and keep it out of the US information panel cluster [2][28].
- If the client asks for a recycling message, find out which scheme they belong to (OPRL, How2Recycle) or whether a claim can be backed, before drawing any symbol [7][24][25].
- If materials or inks change, update the component list and tell the client it may change their EPR data or rating [22].
- If a proof has an auto-approval deadline, put it in the plan and remind the client, because silence can start a print run [26].
- If a step would send artwork to print or approve a proof, stop and ask for a fresh yes, even at level 3.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Designing the front before the dieline.** Spot it: a lovely flat mock-up with no folds, glue flap or seal areas marked.
- **Required details squeezed in last.** Spot it: net quantity in small type near the top of a US front panel; allergens not emphasised; ingredients at 5 pt.
- **Breaking the US information panel.** Spot it: a barcode or a story paragraph sitting between the address and the ingredients [2].
- **Field of vision missed (UK and EU).** Spot it: the name on the front, the weight on the back [9].
- **Too many front-panel claims.** Spot it: six badges and a flavour name that disappears at thumbnail size.
- **Barcode trouble.** Spot it: barcode across a fold or curve, red bars, scaled art, no quiet zone [28]; one barcode reused for two sizes [29].
- **Printer file faults.** Spot it: RGB file, live fonts, no white layer on a clear or metallised pouch, low-resolution images, rich black in small text [26][27].
- **Recycling symbols added for decoration.** Spot it: a chasing-arrows mark or "recyclable" with no scheme or evidence behind it [7][24].
- **Range drift.** Spot it: logo in a different place on each flavour, three green tones, net weight moving around.
- **Missed proof deadline.** Spot it: no date on the plan for the proof reply [26].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Built on the printer's current dieline, with panels labelled and hidden areas marked.
2. Each panel has one job; the front reads at shelf distance and at thumbnail size.
3. Every required detail for each market is present, in the right panel, at or above the minimum size, and supplied and confirmed by the client.
4. US: identity and net quantity on the PDP; net quantity in the bottom 30%, metric and US units; information panel cluster unbroken [2].
5. UK and EU: name, net quantity and alcohol in one field of vision; allergens emphasised; x-height met [9][12][13].
6. Barcode: correct GTIN for this variant, placed and printed to GS1 advice [28][29].
7. Recycling label from the client's scheme, or none; claims matched to evidence.
8. File matches the printer guide: layers, white, colour mode, resolution, black values, bleed [26][27].
9. Component list with material and weight for EPR data, where the client is obligated [20][22].
10. Proof reviewed against the copy deck line by line; proof approval and print release confirmed by the client with a fresh yes.

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** A panel map labelled A to F; one table per panel (zone, content, size, notes); a required details list with each item tagged "you supply and check" and the guidance to read; a copy deck by panel with character counts and the space available; print notes (dieline version, bleed, safe area, colour mode, white, finishes, material); a component list for EPR; and a proof checklist.

**Tone.** Plain words, short sentences, measurements in the printer's units with the other in brackets. Panels described by when the shopper sees them. Suggestions marked as suggestions. Required wording never rewritten.

## 9. Glossary

- **Dieline:** the printer's flat drawing of the pack with cut, fold and glue lines [26][27].
- **Panel map:** a labelled plan of every face of the pack and what goes on it.
- **PDP (principal display panel):** the US term for the face most likely seen at purchase [2].
- **Information panel:** the US panel immediately right of the PDP that holds the grouped required details [2].
- **Statement of identity:** the common name of the product on a US label [2].
- **Net quantity:** the amount in the pack by weight, volume or count [2][10].
- **Intervening material:** anything not required that sits between required details on the US information panel [2].
- **x-height:** the height of a lower-case "x"; the UK and EU minimum size measure [9][13].
- **Field of vision:** the faces that can be seen at once without turning the pack [9].
- **QUID:** quantitative ingredient declaration, the percentage of a key ingredient [9].
- **e-mark:** the voluntary symbol showing the average quantity system is used [15].
- **GTIN:** the number behind a barcode, unique to each product variant [29].
- **Quiet zone:** the clear space either side of a barcode [28].
- **Bleed:** artwork that runs past the cut line so no edge shows [27].
- **White layer:** a separate white ink plate under colours on clear or metallic film [27].
- **Overprint:** printing one ink on top of another without knocking out [27].
- **Rich black:** black mixed with other inks for large solid areas; not for small text [26].
- **Gusset:** the folded side or base panel of a pouch that lets it stand or expand.
- **Proof:** the printer's file or sample for sign-off before production [26].
- **EPR:** extended producer responsibility; producers pay for and report on their packaging [20].
- **RAM:** the UK recyclability assessment methodology, red, amber or green [22].
- **PPWR:** the EU Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation, (EU) 2025/40 [16].
- **OPRL:** the UK on-pack recycling label scheme [24].
- **How2Recycle:** the US and Canadian on-pack recycling label [25].

## 10. What changes fast

Recheck at each refresh:

- **FDA:** the revised allergen and nutrition chapters, the front-of-package proposal, and the "healthy" symbol [1][3][4][5].
- **UK:** FSA and GOV.UK labelling pages, any new on-pack recyclability labelling duty, EPR fees and thresholds, and the yearly RAM update [9][12][20][21][22].
- **EU:** PPWR implementing acts on labels and their start date, the empty space ratio method, the small-business proposals, and the revision of Regulation 1169/2011 [14][16][17][18].
- **US states:** California SB 54 and other state packaging laws [8].
- **Recycling schemes:** OPRL rules and How2Recycle's Pro Label rollout [24][25].
- **Printers and tools:** each printer's artwork guide and proof terms, and Canva's MCP features [26][27][30].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Product on a gradient.** A front panel with a product render floating on a soft colour gradient. It looks like every AI packshot; build the panel from the brand's own colours, photography or illustration.
- **Purple or blue gradients.** Gradient fills as the default background for "modern" or "tech" products. Use the brand's palette, and check the printer can reproduce it.
- **System fonts in place of the brand font.** Inter, Geist or a default sans on the front panel because it was to hand. Use the brand's typefaces, licensed for packaging.
- **Cream for "premium".** Cream or kraft backgrounds swapped in to signal premium when the brand never chose them. Keep the brand's colours, and ask whether premium is really the brief.
- **Rounded badges everywhere.** Every claim in a rounded pill or burst, the pack version of rounded cards everywhere. Give each panel one job (section 7) and let type carry the hierarchy.
- **Emoji-style icons.** Generic leaf, heart and tick icons for claims and features. Use the client's scheme symbols or no icon; claims need evidence anyway (section 5).
- **Stock hype words.** "Elevate your routine", "seamless" or "game-changer" on the pack. Name the product type and one real reason to buy.
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "Premium quality" or "made with love". Use the specific difference the client can back up.
- **Rule-of-three claim lists.** Three claim bullets on every front panel by habit. The front holds name, product type and one reason to buy; cut claims before shrinking type (section 5).
