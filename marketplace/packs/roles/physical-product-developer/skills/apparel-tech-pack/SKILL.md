# Apparel tech pack

## When to use

- A first production run of a garment: hoodie, T-shirt, leggings, jacket, uniform.
- A factory asks for a tech pack before quoting.
- A sample came back wrong and the tech pack needs fixing, with comments for the factory.
- Moving a style to a new factory.

Not for drawing flats or CAD, children's wear safety sign-off, or finding factories.

## What you need

1. The garment type, a photo or sketch, and a sample you like if you have one.
2. Measurements of that sample in one size, or your size chart.
3. Sizes to make and the base size.
4. Fabric (weight, composition as stated by the supplier), colours, trims, labels and packing wishes.
5. Quantities and target factory or region.
6. For a fix: the current tech pack, the sample's measurements and photos, and your notes.

**Access:** level 1 to read your files. Level 2 to write the tech pack in a copy or in Techpacker, and draft the factory email. Level 3 to update the live tech pack and send it, or sample comments, to factory contacts on your list, within your limits. Sample orders and production orders are purchase orders: a fresh yes every time.

## Steps

### 1. List what is confirmed and what is not

A short table at the top: item, value, confirmed by (client, supplier, sample), or [to confirm]. Fibre content, origin and any certification are [to confirm] unless the client gives the supplier's document.

### 2. Write the flat sketch brief

Describe the front and back in words with numbered callouts: neckline, pockets, seams, closures, hems, labels, prints. The designer or factory turns this into flats if needed.

### 3. Build the measurement chart

| POM code | Point of measure | How to measure | Base size value | Tolerance (+/-) |
|---|---|---|---|---|

- Define every point of measure precisely: "Chest width: 2.5 cm below armhole, edge to edge, garment flat."
- Use one unit throughout (cm or inches), stated in the header.
- Tolerances from the client or factory; mark [to confirm] if neither gave them.

### 4. Build the grading table

Increments from size to size for each point of measure, labelled "suggested, confirm with pattern maker". Every point of measure appears in the grading table.

### 5. Write the bill of materials

| Item | Description | Supplier or ref | Colour | Placement | Quantity per garment |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Fabric, lining, interlining, thread, zips, buttons, drawcords, elastic, labels (main, size, care, composition), hang tags, polybag, carton. Unknowns marked [to confirm].

### 6. Write construction notes by area

Seam types and stitch types by area (shoulders, side seams, hems, neckline), seam allowances where given, finishing, pressing, and label positions with measurements from a fixed point.

### 7. Add labelling questions by market

Labels depend on where the garment is sold. In the EU, textile products of at least 80% textile fibres by weight carry a fibre composition label using EU fibre names, in the languages of each country of sale, and show any non-textile parts of animal origin. In the US, labels show fibre content by generic name and percentage, country of origin and the company name or RN number, with care instructions required by a separate rule; garments with a neck carry the origin label inside the centre back neck. For the UK, check GOV.UK guidance at the time. The client's adviser confirms the wording.

### 8. Write the factory email and the sample review sheet

The email lists what is attached, quantities, the delivery term and place, the questions (price per size range or colour, minimums, sample cost and time, lead time, payment terms) and the reply date. The sample review sheet:

| POM | Spec | Measured | Difference | Within tolerance? | Photo | Comment for factory |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

### 9. For a sample fix

Measure the sample against the chart, fill the review sheet, then write numbered comments: what is wrong, by how much, the fix requested, and whether the tech pack changes or the factory missed the spec.

## Checks

- One unit throughout; every number has it.
- Every point of measure has a how-to-measure line, a tolerance and a grading row.
- BOM quantities are per garment; nothing missing for labels and packing.
- No fibre, origin, organic, recycled or certification claims the client did not give.
- Grading marked as suggested until a pattern maker confirms it.
- Unknowns listed at the top.
- Factory comments are measurable and polite.

## Output format

1. Cover: style name, version, date, sizes, base size, units.
2. Confirmed and [to confirm] table.
3. Flat sketch brief with callouts.
4. Measurement chart.
5. Grading table.
6. Bill of materials.
7. Construction notes.
8. Labels and packing, with market questions.
9. Factory email.
10. Sample review sheet.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a clothing brand's heavyweight hoodie, S to XXL, base size M, units cm. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> | POM code | Point of measure | How to measure | M | Tolerance |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | A | Chest width | 2.5 cm below armhole, edge to edge, flat | 61.0 | +/- 1.0 [to confirm with factory] |
> | B | Body length | High point shoulder to hem, flat | 72.0 | +/- 1.0 [to confirm] |
> | C | Sleeve length | Shoulder seam to cuff edge, flat | 63.5 | +/- 1.0 [to confirm] |
>
> **Grading (suggested, confirm with pattern maker):** A +5.0 per size; B +2.0; C +1.5.
>
> **BOM line.** Fleece body fabric, weight 400 gsm (client's figure), composition [to confirm from mill certificate], colour Charcoal, 1.6 m per garment [to confirm with factory marker].
>
> **Sample comment 1.** Body length on size M measured 69.5 cm against 72.0 cm spec (2.5 cm short, outside tolerance). Please correct the pattern; tech pack unchanged.
