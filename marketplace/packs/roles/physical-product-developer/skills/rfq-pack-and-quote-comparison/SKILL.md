# RFQ pack and quote comparison

## When to use

- You are ready to ask factories for prices.
- You have two or more quotes that do not line up: different quantities, currencies, delivery terms or scope.
- You need cost per unit at several order sizes for pricing or a funding plan.

For purchase orders and lead-time tracking after you choose, see the supply chain planner kit.

## What you need

1. The requirements document or tech pack (see the `product-requirements-and-prototype-plan` or `apparel-tech-pack` skill).
2. Quantities to quote, for example a first batch and two larger runs.
3. Packaging, labelling and where you want the goods delivered.
4. The supplier list you have approved, and any quotes already received.
5. Your target landed cost or retail price.

**Access:** level 1 to read quotes and emails. Level 2 to draft the pack, emails and comparison sheet. Level 3 to send quote requests and follow-up questions to suppliers on your list, and update the live sheet, within your limits. Accepting a quote as binding, purchase orders and contracts need your fresh yes every time; tooling and deposit payments stay with you.

## Steps

### 1. Build the RFQ pack

- Cover note: who you are, the product, the quantities and the date you need a reply.
- Spec or tech pack, drawings or photos, materials and finishes [to confirm items marked].
- Packaging and labelling requirements.
- Delivery: an Incoterms 2020 rule and a named place, for example "FCA Shenzhen factory" or "DAP our warehouse, Leeds". Incoterms 2020 has eleven rules that split tasks, costs and risks between seller and buyer; name both the rule and the place.
- A reply table for the supplier to fill in.

### 2. Ask the questions that change the price

| Question | Why |
|---|---|
| Unit price at each quantity | Price breaks |
| Minimum order quantity, per colour or size if relevant | Can you afford the first batch? |
| Tooling cost, what it covers, who owns it, where it is kept, how many units it lasts | Hidden cost and lock-in |
| Sample cost and lead time; is it credited against the order? | Development budget |
| Production lead time from approval | Timeline |
| Payment terms: deposit, balance, when | Cash |
| What is included: packaging, labels, inspection, export documents | Scope |
| Quality checks they run, and whether a third-party inspection is allowed | Risk |
| Certificates or test reports they already hold for these materials | Lab planning (verify, do not assume) |

### 3. Normalise the replies

Put each quote on the same basis before comparing:
- Same quantities (ask again if a supplier quoted different ones).
- Same currency, with the exchange rate and its date.
- Same delivery term: add freight, insurance and fees to get each quote to the same named place.
- Same scope: add packaging, labels or inspection where a quote left them out, marked as estimates with their source.

### 4. Spread tooling and one-off costs

Tooling per unit = tooling cost / units the tool will make in the period you plan for. Show it at each quantity. Do the same for sample and setup fees if they are not credited.

### 5. Work out landed cost per unit

| Line | Supplier A @ 1,000 | Supplier A @ 3,000 | Supplier B @ 1,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Unit price | | | |
| Tooling per unit | | | |
| Packaging and labels | | | |
| Freight and insurance to named place | | | |
| Duty (rate, code, date checked) | | | |
| Other fees | | | |
| **Landed cost per unit** | | | |

Duty: find the commodity code and rate in the official tool for the destination (UK Trade Tariff, EU TARIC, US HTSUS) on the day, write the date next to it, and note that customs makes the final decision. Rates can change at short notice.

### 6. Compare beyond price

Lead time, payment terms and cash needed up front, tooling ownership, minimums, quality process, communication so far, and red flags (refusing inspection, vague answers, bank detail changes). Score only on facts in the quotes and emails.

### 7. Recommend and list what needs a yes

A short recommendation with reasons, the open questions to settle before ordering, and the decisions for the owner: which supplier, which quantity, the sample order, and any contract or tooling agreement.

## Checks

- Every number comes from a quote, the client or an official tool, with a date.
- All quotes compared at the same quantity, currency, delivery term and scope.
- Tooling and one-off costs shown separately and per unit.
- Delivery terms name an Incoterms rule and a place.
- Duty has a code, rate, source and date, or is marked [to check].
- Sums are shown so the owner can follow them.
- Nothing commits the client without a fresh yes.

## Output format

1. RFQ cover email and reply table.
2. Question list.
3. Normalised quote table.
4. Landed cost per unit at each quantity, with sums.
5. Non-price comparison and red flags.
6. Recommendation, open questions, decisions for the owner.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a homeware brand's wooden phone stand. Quotes from two suppliers, US sale, figures supplied by the client. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Normalised to 1,000 units, USD, FCA factory, plain kraft box included.**
>
> | Line | Supplier A | Supplier B |
> |---|---|---|
> | Unit price | $3.10 | $2.60 |
> | Tooling (A: none; B: $1,200 jig, owned by the brand) | $0.00 | $1.20 ($1,200 / 1,000) |
> | Box (B quoted without box; A's box price used as estimate) | included | $0.35 (estimate) |
> | **Cost per unit before freight and duty** | **$3.10** | **$4.15** |
>
> At 3,000 units A quoted $2.95 and B kept $2.60. B's tooling falls to $0.40 per unit, so B costs $3.35 ($2.60 + $0.40 + $0.35) against A's $2.95. On these figures A is cheaper at both quantities. Freight and duty to add once the forwarder quotes and the HTSUS rate is checked (date it).
>
> **Open questions.** Ask B for a boxed price and a 3,000-unit price break before ruling it out.
>
> **Needs your yes.** A sample order from A (you pay the sample fee).
