# Guest booking and release

Guests make or break an interview show, and a missing release can take an episode down after it is live. This skill finds the right guests, asks them well, gets them ready, and makes sure the paperwork is done before anything goes out.

## When to use

- A show needs guests for the season.
- A guest has said yes and needs confirmation and prep.
- An episode is edited and the release is not on file.
- A guest asks how the recording will be used, or asks for changes after recording.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the guest list, calendar and past pitches. Level 2 to save pitches and confirmations as email drafts, add tentative holds, and set up a recording session that is not yet shared. Level 3 to send pitches, confirmations, calendar invites and studio links to the guests the client approved, within agreed dates. Sending a release or any agreement as binding needs a fresh yes each time.

- The show summary, audience, format, length and recording method.
- The client's list of wanted guests, or permission to research (with web search connected I check each guest's recent appearances and public contact route; without it I work from what the client pastes).
- The release form the client's lawyer wrote or approved. If there is none, I list what the client should ask a lawyer to cover; I do not write binding terms.
- Calendar availability and the booking tool the client uses.

## Steps

1. **Score the shortlist.** For each guest: fit with the audience, a fresh angle (what they have not said elsewhere), the story they can tell, reach, and ease of booking. Sort into "pitch now", "later" and "skip". A smaller list of good fits beats a long list of big names.
2. **Find the right contact route.** Use the guest's public booking page, agent or publicist, or a warm introduction from the client. Never guess personal email addresses, and treat any contact details as personal data kept where the client keeps other contacts.
3. **Write the pitch.** Short enough to read on a phone: who the host is, why this guest and this show, the specific angle, format, length, recording method, two or three possible dates, and what the guest gets (the audience, a link, clips). One follow-up if there is no reply, then stop.
   **Approval point:** the client approves the list of guests to pitch. At level 3 I send only to that list.
4. **Book and confirm.** Send the booking link or invite, confirm time zone, platform, length, what the guest needs (headphones, quiet room, a wired connection if possible), and whether video is recorded.
5. **Send the prep pack.** Show summary, audience, the angle, three sample questions (not a script), logistics, how the episode will be used (audio, video, clips, transcript, newsletters), the release form, and a contact for the day.
6. **Check the release.** Before release day, confirm the signed form is on file. Compare it against this checklist and flag gaps to the client:
   - permission to record, edit and publish on all the platforms and formats the show uses, including video;
   - use of name, image, bio and voice in clips, promos and transcripts;
   - whether the guest is offered a review, and what kind (facts only, or approval);
   - topics that are off limits;
   - what happens if the guest asks for removal later;
   - whether AI tools may be used for translation, dubbing or voice repair, and that no synthetic version of the guest's voice will be made without separate written consent.
7. **Get consent on tape.** The run sheet opens with the host asking everyone to confirm they agree to be recorded and published. Rules on recording calls differ by country and by US state; the client checks the local rules that apply to them.
8. **Handle changes after recording.** If the guest asks to remove a line, tell the client at once. Offer to cut it if it does not change the meaning; never rewrite their words. If they withdraw entirely, hold the episode and let the client decide with their lawyer.
9. **Thank and share.** After release, send the guest the link, clips and suggested posts.

## Checks

- Every pitch names a specific angle for that guest.
- Pitches and follow-ups only went to the approved list.
- The release is signed and filed before the episode is scheduled.
- Release gaps are flagged to the client, not papered over.
- No promise of audience size or download numbers in any pitch.
- Guest quotes used in promotion match the transcript exactly.

## Output format

1. Scored shortlist (table): guest, angle, fit, reach, ease, decision.
2. Pitch and one follow-up per guest.
3. Confirmation message and prep pack.
4. Release checklist with each item marked "covered", "missing" or "ask lawyer".
5. Booking and send log.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real show or guest: a weekly show for owners of small joinery and building firms, pitching a guest who wrote about keeping apprentices. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Pitch**

> Subject: [Show]: how you fixed apprentice drop-out?
>
> Hi Dana, I produce [Show], a weekly show for owners of small joinery and building firms. Your post about keeping apprentices past their first winter got passed round our listeners. Would you tell that story on the show: what was going wrong, what you changed, and what it cost? It's a 40-minute remote conversation, audio and video, recorded on Riverside. Could any of these work: 12, 14 or 19 March, mornings UK time? You'd get the episode, three clips and a link to your firm in the notes.

**Release checklist (extract)**

| Item | Status |
|---|---|
| Record, edit, publish on podcast apps and YouTube | Covered |
| Clips and promo use of name and image | Covered |
| Guest review | Missing: form is silent. Ask lawyer whether to offer a facts-only check |
| AI translation or dubbing | Missing: ask lawyer to add, or do not use AI on this episode |
