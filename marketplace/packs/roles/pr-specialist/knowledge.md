# PR specialist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

## 1. What this role is for

A small business hires a PR specialist to get talked about by people it does not pay: reporters, editors, podcast hosts, award judges and event organisers. That is earned media. The same person helps the business speak clearly and honestly when something goes wrong.

Who hires it:

- Founders and small teams with real news (a launch, a hire, funding, a partnership, a result) and no agency.
- Experts, consultants and authors who want to be quoted, booked on podcasts or invited to speak.
- Firms entering industry awards or calls for speakers.
- Independent artists, bands and DJs who need a press kit and booking pitches.
- Any business facing a product fault, outage, data incident, complaint storm or bad press.

What good work looks like:

- An honest read of whether there is a story at all, and a sharper angle when there is.
- A release a reporter can lift facts from in a minute, with approved quotes and every number checked.
- Pitches written for one reporter or host at a time, about what their audience needs.
- Embargoes and exclusives handled so no one is burned.
- A crisis response built only on confirmed facts, out early, consistent across staff, customers and the public, and updated when promised.
- Reports that show what coverage did for the business, not a made-up "advertising value".

How it is judged: coverage in the outlets and shows the business named as targets; whether the key message and spokesperson appear in that coverage; what followed (visits, enquiries, invitations, bookings); relationships with reporters that last; and, in a crisis, whether trust held. The measurement field's own body, AMEC, asks practitioners to judge PR by outcomes, not by advertising value equivalents [6][7].

The agents in this role each lead with one specialty: press releases and pitches, crisis communications, awards and speaking, podcast guesting, and music publicity. They share this knowledge.

## 2. The canon

**Professional ethics codes [1][2].** The PRSA Code of Ethics (US, first developed in 1950) asks for honesty and accuracy, quick correction of errors, disclosure of who sponsors a cause, and gifts to journalists that are modest and rare. It calls front groups with hidden sponsors improper [1]. The CIPR Code of Conduct (UK) adds that a practitioner never hides the fact that they represent a client, and checks information before sharing it [2]. For a small-business PR agent these are working rules, not decoration: an invented quote, a hidden sponsor or a false "first" can end a reporter relationship for good.

**Crisis and Emergency Risk Communication (CDC) [3][4].** CERC is built for public health emergencies, but its six principles fit any business crisis: be first, be right, be credible, express empathy, promote action, show respect [3]. Accuracy includes saying what is known, what is not known, and what is being done to find out. CERC also splits a crisis into phases (preparation, initial, maintenance, resolution) and says what to do in each. In the initial phase: express empathy, explain the risk, tell people what to do, and describe the response [3]. The manual has chapters on plans, spokespeople and working with the media [4].

**PESO (Gini Dietrich) [5].** Paid, earned, shared and owned media work together. Earned coverage is stronger when the business can share it on social media, host it on its own site and, where useful, put paid reach behind it. Dietrich launched the model in 2014 with her book *Spin Sucks* [5].

**Measurement: the Barcelona Principles and AMEC [6][7].** The Barcelona Principles, now in their fourth version and first agreed in 2010, ask for measurement that is meaningful, transparent and focused on outcomes [6]. AMEC's campaign against advertising value equivalents (AVEs) has run since 2017; its supporters call AVEs an outdated and flawed metric that measures cost, not value [7].

**The inverted pyramid and the news test.** Newsrooms read the top first. The headline states the fact; the first paragraph answers who, what, when, where and why; detail follows in falling order of importance. A story needs at least one reason to run now: it is new, it affects many people, it is surprising, it involves a known name, it is local to the reader, or it has a human story. If none applies, there is no release to write.

**Reading list (credited, not summarised).** Practitioners also rely on these. This kit takes no specific claims from them.

- *The Associated Press Stylebook* (updated every year): the house style most US newsrooms use.
- James Grunig and Todd Hunt, *Managing Public Relations* (1984): models of how organisations talk with their publics.
- W. Timothy Coombs, *Ongoing Crisis Communication*: the idea that a response should fit how much responsibility the public assigns to the organisation.
- David Meerman Scott, *The New Rules of Marketing and PR*: publishing direct to buyers, not only through the press.
- Chip Heath and Dan Heath, *Made to Stick*: why some ideas are remembered.

## 3. How the work is done now

**Where audiences get news (2026).** The Reuters Institute's Digital News Report 2026 (published 16 June 2026, 97,520 people in 48 markets) found trust in news at 37%, the lowest since it began measuring in 2015. Social media and video networks (54%) overtook news organisations' own websites and apps (51%) as a news source for the first time. Weekly use of AI chatbots for news rose to 10%, from 7% a year earlier, and 27% got some news from news-focused creators [8]. In the US, trust was 25% [8]. The practical result: a good story now needs a plan for reporters, creators, podcasts and the business's own channels, and the facts must survive being summarised by an AI tool.

**Finding the story.** Start with five questions: what happened, who is affected, why now, who cares, and what proof exists. Then test the angle against a named outlet: would its readers care this week? A product update is rarely news; a product update that fixes a problem a known group faces, with a customer who will talk and a number to prove it, can be. Data the business owns (a survey of its customers, anonymised usage figures, prices it tracks) is one of the most reliable ways to create a story.

**The press release.** A release is a fact source, not an advert. Structure: dateline or "for immediate release" line (or the embargo line), a plain headline that states the fact, an optional subhead, a first paragraph with the five Ws, supporting facts with sources, one or two quotes that add opinion or meaning (never facts the body should state), a boilerplate about the business, and a media contact. Quotes come only from words the spokesperson said or approved. Every number links to its source or is marked for checking. Releases go on the business's own newsroom page, to reporters, and sometimes over a paid wire.

**Wires and search.** Paid wire distribution puts a release in front of newsrooms and on syndication sites, and for listed companies it is one accepted way to make news public (see below). It is not a ranking trick: Google lists links with optimised anchor text in press releases distributed on other sites as link spam [13]. Paid or sponsored links should carry `rel="sponsored"` (or `nofollow`) [14]. Write links in releases as plain brand or page names.

**Pitching.** A pitch is a short email to one reporter or host about one story, written for their audience. It says what the story is, why now, why them (their recent work), one line of proof, and one ask (an interview, an exclusive, data under embargo). It names what is available: spokesperson, customer, data, images. One polite follow-up, with something new, is the norm; a string of "just checking in" emails is not. Muck Rack's annual State of Journalism survey (2026 edition, nearly 1,100 journalists) studies what makes a pitch land; its figures sit behind a form, so check the latest edition before quoting any number [25].

**Media lists.** Build lists from what reporters have published recently, not from job titles alone. Keep each list small and specific to the story. Record who covered what, who asked not to be pitched, and every reply. Journalists' names and contact details are personal data in the UK and EU, so keep only what the work needs and honour every opt-out.

**Embargoes and exclusives.** An embargo is an agreement that information will not be made public before a set date and time [9]. It binds only people who agreed to it. EurekAlert!, the science news service, recognises embargoes only from legitimate content owners and makes reporters sign an agreement first [9]. Its agreement asks reporters to hold the news until release time, share it with no one, and not upload embargoed material to AI tools; a breach can suspend the reporter and their whole outlet [10]. Practical rules for a small business:

- Ask first ("Would you like this under embargo until Tuesday 09:00 UK time?") and send the material only after a yes.
- State the embargo in the subject line and at the top of the release, with date, time and time zone.
- Embargo only what is truly ready. If the news leaks, lift the embargo for everyone at once.
- An exclusive gives one outlet the story first. Do not offer the same exclusive twice, and say plainly when other outlets will get it.

**Public companies.** In the US, Regulation FD (effective 23 October 2000) says that when a listed company shares material nonpublic information with market professionals or holders likely to trade on it, it must make the same information public at the same time (if deliberate) or promptly (if not) [11]. A press release through a widely circulated wire service or a Form 8-K filing counts as public disclosure; posting on the company website alone generally does not [11]. In the UK, listed issuers must disclose inside information as soon as possible and may delay only under set conditions, then tell the FCA [12]. Market-sensitive news is therefore the company's securities lawyers' call, not PR's. Never pitch it, embargo it or tease it without their sign-off.

**Gifts, trips and creators.** Gifts to journalists should be modest and rare [1]. In the US, free products and payment are material connections that creators and reviewers generally must disclose clearly [15]. The UK's influencer guide from the ASA and CMA reached its fourth edition on 23 October 2026, adding fake reviews and the DMCC Act [16]. The FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (in effect from 21 October 2024) bans fake reviews, undisclosed insider reviews, review suppression and fake follower counts [17]. When a campaign mixes press and creators, make every paid or gifted link clear.

**Podcasts, awards and speaking.** These follow the same rules as press: fit first, then a specific pitch.

- *Podcasts.* Score shows on audience fit, format, how often they publish and recent guests. Pitch topics the host's listeners need, with a one-sheet (bio, three topics with hooks, sample questions, past appearances). Prepare stories with numbers and one call to action for listeners.
- *Awards.* Read the entry rules. Answer each criterion in order, within its word limit, leading with the result and attaching evidence for every claim. A true smaller number beats an inflated one.
- *Talks.* Sell one takeaway in the title. Write the abstract for the audience. Keep speaker bios in three lengths.
- *Music.* An electronic press kit holds bios in several lengths, the story of the release, notable shows, press quotes the artist supplies, links and contacts. Booking pitches say why the act fits that venue, the draw, dates and a clear ask.

**Crisis basics.** The order of work:

1. *Fact log.* Three columns: confirmed, not confirmed, unknown. Every message draws only on the confirmed column.
2. *Severity and roles.* Who decides, who speaks, who approves, who tells staff, who watches the news and social media. The FTC's data breach guide gives the same advice: one point person for releasing information [18].
3. *Holding statement.* What happened, who is affected, what you are doing, what people should do, and when you will update. Express care without guessing at causes [3].
4. *Sequence.* Staff first, then directly affected customers, then the public and press, with the same facts in every version.
5. *Legal duties first.* Some crises carry reporting duties that come before any press work. In the EU, a personal data breach must be reported to the data protection authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless it is unlikely to put people at risk, and people must be told without undue delay when the risk is high [19]. The UK GDPR has the same 72-hour duty, reported to the Information Commission [26]. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)). All US states have breach notification laws [18]. Product recalls have their own regulator processes: the CPSC in the US [20] and market surveillance authorities in the UK, with the PAS 7100 code of practice [21]. The business's lawyer and data or safety lead own these duties; PR writes the words around them.
6. *Spokesperson.* Key messages, the hardest questions with honest answers, and phrases to avoid. "No comment" sounds like hiding; "We don't know yet, and we'll update you at 4pm" does not.
7. *Recovery.* After the issue, say what changed, and keep the promises the statements made.

**Two public examples.** On 19 July 2024 a faulty CrowdStrike content update for Windows went out at 04:09 UTC and was reverted at 05:27 UTC [22]. Microsoft estimated about 8.5 million Windows devices were affected, less than one percent of all Windows machines [23]. CrowdStrike published a preliminary post-incident review on 24 July 2024, explained the cause, and committed to staggered rollouts, customer control over updates, independent reviews and a public root cause analysis [22]. On 18 November 2025 Cloudflare had a major outage from about 11:20 UTC; it published a detailed account the same day, said plainly that it was not an attack (its team had first suspected one), apologised, and listed the fixes it would make [24]. Both show the shape a technical crisis statement now takes: a timeline, a plain cause, an apology where due, and specific next steps.

**AI in PR work.** AI tools draft releases, pitches, Q&As and one-sheets quickly. The rules: use only facts and quotes the client supplied or approved; mark anything that needs checking; never invent a quote, a statistic, an award, a client or a reporter's interest; and keep embargoed or market-sensitive material out of any tool the client has not approved. Because some audiences now meet news through AI answers [8], put the key facts in plain sentences on the business's own newsroom page so that summaries quote them correctly.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to set expectations, never as targets. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **News habits (Reuters Institute, 16 June 2026, 48 markets):** trust in news 37%; social and video networks 54% vs news websites and apps 51%; AI chatbots for news 10% weekly; news creators 27%; news podcasts 11% [8]. *Caveat:* online survey across many countries; national figures differ widely (US trust 25%).
- **Disclosure timing for US listed companies (Regulation FD):** unintentional selective disclosure must be made public promptly, meaning by the later of 24 hours or the start of the next NYSE trading day [11]. *Caveat:* a securities lawyer applies this, not PR.
- **EU data breach reporting:** 72 hours to notify the authority, unless the breach is unlikely to pose a risk [19]. *Caveat:* other rules and sectors add their own duties.
- **Scale of one recent crisis:** about 8.5 million devices in the July 2024 CrowdStrike incident [23]; fix reverted 78 minutes after release [22]. *Caveat:* used here only as a dated example of a public timeline.

No reliable public benchmark gives a typical pitch reply rate, coverage rate per release, or the "right" number of pitches for a small business. Annual journalist surveys (Muck Rack, Cision) publish preference data, often behind a form [25]. Use the client's own history as the baseline: replies per pitch, coverage per story, and what coverage led to.

## 5. Decision rules

- If there is no answer to "why now, and who cares?", do not write a release; suggest an angle, a data story or a different channel, because a release without news wastes the client's credibility with reporters.
- If a quote has not been said or approved by the named person, mark it as a placeholder and do not send it, because invented quotes break the ethics codes [1][2].
- If the news could move a listed company's share price, stop and get the securities lawyer's sign-off before any pitch, embargo or tease [11][12].
- If a reporter has not agreed to an embargo, do not send embargoed material, because an embargo binds only those who accepted it [9].
- If an embargo breaks, lift it for everyone at once and tell the other reporters, because fairness keeps relationships.
- If you are pitching an exclusive, offer it to one outlet at a time, and move on only after a clear no or the deadline you stated.
- If a pitch would work for any outlet, rewrite it for one, because a generic pitch shows you have not read their work.
- If a reporter asks you to stop, remove them from every list that day.
- If a crisis is unfolding, publish a holding statement built only on confirmed facts and promise a time for the next update, because CERC's first principle is to be first [3].
- If a crisis involves personal data, injury, a product safety risk or a legal claim, bring in the lawyer and the data or safety lead before any public word, because reporting duties may come first [18][19][20][21].
- If you do not know something in a crisis, say so and say when you will know more, because accuracy includes the unknowns [3].
- If a release goes out on a wire, write links as plain names with no keyword anchor text [13].
- If gifts or paid trips are involved, keep them modest and make sure any creator coverage carries a clear disclosure [1][15][16].
- If the client asks for coverage value in "ad equivalency", explain why AVEs are not accepted and report outcomes instead [7].
- If an award entry claim cannot be proved, cut it or use the smaller number that can be.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **No news.** Spot it: the headline needs adjectives to be interesting.
- **Hype words.** Spot it: "leading", "revolutionary", "world-class" with no proof.
- **Quotes that state facts.** Spot it: the quote repeats the first paragraph.
- **Spray-and-pray lists.** Spot it: hundreds of contacts, no notes on recent coverage.
- **Mail-merge personalisation.** Spot it: the only personal line is the reporter's first name.
- **Burned embargoes.** Spot it: embargoed material sent before a yes, or no time zone stated.
- **Market news leaked through PR.** Spot it: financial results or deal terms in a pitch with no lawyer sign-off.
- **SEO-stuffed releases.** Spot it: keyword-rich links scattered through the release [13].
- **Crisis guesswork.** Spot it: statements that name causes the fact log marks "not confirmed".
- **Silence or "no comment".** Spot it: hours pass with no holding statement and no time for the next update.
- **Inconsistent messages.** Spot it: staff, customers and press hear different versions.
- **AVE reporting.** Spot it: a dollar "value" of coverage with no outcomes [7].
- **Inflated award entries.** Spot it: claims with no evidence attached.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is there a real reason to run now, and is it in the headline and first paragraph?
2. Is every fact, number and name checked against a source, with anything unchecked marked?
3. Are all quotes approved, in the speaker's voice, adding meaning rather than facts?
4. Is the release free of hype words, unproven firsts and bests?
5. Does each pitch say why this reporter or host, stay short enough to read at a glance, and make one ask?
6. Are embargo terms, time zone and contacts clear on every page and email?
7. Has anything market-sensitive, legal or safety-related had the right professional sign-off?
8. Are boilerplate, contact details, images and links correct and working?
9. In a crisis: does every message use only confirmed facts, say what people should do, and give the next update time?
10. Are paid, gifted or sponsored links and relationships disclosed?
11. Does the report measure outcomes the client cares about, not AVEs?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Newsworthiness check:* the angle, what is missing, how to strengthen it, and a frank "no story yet" when that is true.
- *Press release:* usually one page (300 to 500 words is this role's house range, not an industry rule); headline, first paragraph, facts, quotes, boilerplate, contact.
- *Pitch:* subject line that states the story; three short paragraphs; one ask; one follow-up line.
- *Media list:* outlet, reporter, recent relevant piece, angle for them, status, notes.
- *Fact sheet and Q&A:* the facts in bullets; likely questions with honest answers.
- *Crisis pack:* fact log, holding statement, staff note, customer message, social replies, media Q&A, update schedule.
- *Spokesperson prep:* key messages, the hardest questions with answers, phrases to avoid.
- *Award entry or talk proposal:* criteria answered in order, word counts, evidence list.
- *One-sheets and press kits:* bios in three lengths, topics or release notes, links, contacts.
- *Coverage report:* what ran, where, reach where known, message pull-through, outcomes, next steps.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain statements of fact. Short paragraphs. Numbers with sources. Dates with time zones. No hype. Placeholders in [SQUARE BRACKETS] for anything the client must supply or approve. Notes to the client kept apart from anything a reporter will see. In a crisis: calm, human, specific, and never defensive.

## 9. Glossary

- **Earned media:** coverage the business does not pay for, such as articles, interviews and podcast appearances [5].
- **PESO:** paid, earned, shared and owned media planned together [5].
- **Press release:** a factual announcement written for reporters to use.
- **Pitch:** a short, personal email offering one story to one reporter or host.
- **Boilerplate:** the standard "about us" paragraph at the end of a release.
- **Inverted pyramid:** writing with the most important facts first.
- **Embargo:** an agreement not to publish before a set date and time [9].
- **Exclusive:** a story given to one outlet before anyone else.
- **On the record / off the record / on background:** whether a source can be quoted and named; agree the terms before speaking.
- **Wire service:** a paid service that distributes releases to newsrooms and syndication sites.
- **Media list:** the reporters and outlets chosen for one story.
- **One-sheet:** a one-page summary of a guest, speaker, artist or release.
- **Electronic press kit (EPK):** an artist's or company's media materials in one place.
- **Holding statement:** a first short statement in a crisis, issued before all facts are known.
- **Fact log:** a record of what is confirmed, not confirmed and unknown.
- **Spokesperson:** the one person authorised to speak for the business on a topic.
- **Material nonpublic information:** information that could move a listed company's share price and is not yet public [11].
- **Regulation FD:** the US rule against selective disclosure by listed companies [11].
- **AVE:** advertising value equivalent, a discredited way of valuing coverage [7].
- **Barcelona Principles:** the PR measurement principles promoted by AMEC [6].
- **Message pull-through:** whether the key message appeared in the coverage.
- **Share of voice:** a business's share of coverage compared with named competitors.
- **Material connection:** a payment, gift or relationship that must be disclosed in endorsements [15].

- **Disclosure duties for listed clients:** SEC and FCA pages [11][12].
- **Crisis duties:** data breach rules (EDPB, Information Commission, US state laws) [18][19][26]; recall processes (CPSC, OPSS) [20][21]; CERC updates [3][4].

- **Tools and prices:** media databases, wires and podcast tools change plans often; check each vendor's pricing page before advising.
