# Crisis response

In a crisis, people decide within hours whether to trust what a business says. The work is to be first, be right and be credible, to show care, and to tell people what to do, as the CDC's crisis communication principles put it. This skill turns a messy situation into consistent messages built only on what is confirmed.

This is communications support, not legal advice. Anything with legal, safety, regulatory or personal data risk goes to the client's lawyer or responsible lead before it goes out.

## When to use

- Something has gone wrong: a product fault or recall, an outage, a data incident, an accident, a viral complaint, a damaging story, a staff issue that has become public.
- Something might go wrong and the client wants a plan: likely scenarios, roles, approvals and template statements.
- A crisis has passed and the client needs a review and a plan to rebuild trust.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the facts, timeline, coverage and social posts. Level 2 to save statements, emails and replies as drafts in the client's tools. Level 3 to publish or send, but every crisis statement, apology and reply to a formal complaint needs the client's fresh yes before it goes out, even at level 3, and legal review where there is any legal, safety or data angle.

- What happened, when it was found, who is affected, what is being done.
- Who decides, who speaks, who approves, who tells staff.
- Coverage, posts and customer messages so far.
- Contacts for the lawyer, the data protection or safety lead, and any regulator relationship.

## Steps

1. **Start the fact log.** Three columns: confirmed, not confirmed, unknown. Add the source and time for each fact. Every message draws only on the confirmed column.
2. **Check for duties that come first.** Ask: is personal data involved? Is anyone at risk of harm? Is a product unsafe? Is the company listed? Is there a legal claim? If yes, the lawyer and the responsible lead decide on reporting duties (for example, data breach reporting deadlines or product recall processes) before any public word. Record their decisions.
3. **Set severity and roles.** A simple scale (low, medium, high) based on harm, number affected and public attention. Name the decider, the spokesperson, the approvers and the person who watches news and social media.
4. **Write the holding statement.** Five parts: what happened (confirmed facts only); who is affected; what the business is doing; what people should do now; when the next update will come. Express care plainly. Do not guess at causes or blame anyone.
5. **Sequence the messages.** Staff first (so they hear it from the business), then directly affected customers, then the public, press and social. Same facts, adapted to each audience.
6. **Write the pack.** Staff note; customer email or text; website notice or FAQ in plain language; social post and reply lines; media Q&A.
7. **Prepare the spokesperson.** Three key messages; the hardest questions with honest answers; phrases to avoid ("no comment", "isolated incident" when unknown, anything that blames customers); a line for steering back to what people should do.
8. **Get approval.** Show each statement to the decider, and to the lawyer where needed. Log who approved what and when.
9. **Publish and update.** Keep every promised update time, even if the update is "no new facts yet". Update the fact log as things are confirmed, and correct any earlier statement that turns out wrong, openly.
10. **Watch and respond.** Track coverage and social replies. Answer questions with facts and the next update time. Escalate new claims to the fact log.
11. **Review and recover.** After the issue: what happened, how the response went, what people said, what to fix, and a plan of follow-up messages that show what changed.

## Checks

- Every sentence traces to the confirmed column.
- The statement says what people should do.
- The next update time is stated and realistic.
- Staff got the message before the public.
- Legal, data and safety duties were checked with the right professional and recorded.
- No speculation about cause, no blame, no "no comment".
- All versions (staff, customer, public, social) carry the same facts.
- Each public statement had the client's fresh yes, logged.

## Output format

1. Fact log (table).
2. Severity, roles and approvals.
3. Holding statement.
4. Staff note; customer message; website notice or FAQ; social post and reply lines.
5. Media Q&A and spokesperson prep.
6. Update schedule and monitoring plan.
7. Items for the lawyer or responsible lead, marked [LEGAL REVIEW] or [DATA LEAD].

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: an online booking service whose site went down. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Fact log (extract)**

| Confirmed | Not confirmed | Unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Booking site down from 08:12 to 10:40 UTC today (status page) | A database change caused it (engineering's first view) | How many bookings failed |
| Card payments were not taken for failed bookings (payments provider report) | | Whether any customer data was exposed [DATA LEAD] |

**Holding statement (for approval):**

"This morning our booking site was down from 08:12 to 10:40 UTC. We're sorry: we know many of you were trying to book and couldn't. The site is working again. If your booking didn't finish, you were not charged; please try again or reply to this email and our team will book it for you. We're still checking what caused the problem and whether anything else was affected, and we'll post an update here by 16:00 UTC."

**Hardest question:** "Was customer data exposed?" **Honest answer:** "We don't have any sign of that, but we're checking properly, and we'll tell you by 16:00 UTC what we've found." [DATA LEAD: confirm wording]
