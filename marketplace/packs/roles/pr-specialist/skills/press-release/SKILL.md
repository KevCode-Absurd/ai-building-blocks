# Press release

A release is a fact source for reporters, not an advert. The best ones state the news in the headline, prove it in the body, and let the business's own people add meaning in the quotes. This skill starts with a frank newsworthiness check, because a release with no news costs the client credibility with every reporter who opens it.

## When to use

- The business has news: a launch, a hire, funding, a partnership, a result, an award, a local event, new data.
- A pitch needs a release or fact sheet behind it.
- A listed company needs a release for wire distribution (with its securities lawyer).
- An old release needs rewriting because it reads like an advert.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 for every step, because a release is a document. Level 2 saves it as an unpublished newsroom page or an unsubmitted wire order. Level 3 publishes it on the newsroom page or submits it to the wire at the approved time, within the wire budget cap the client set.

- What happened, who is affected, when, where, why now, and the proof (numbers, documents, names).
- Who can be quoted, and their approval of the exact words.
- The target outlets or audiences.
- Whether anything is market-sensitive, legal, safety-related or about personal data.
- Boilerplate, media contact, images or logos with usage rights.
- Any embargo date, time and time zone.

## Steps

1. **Run the news test.** Answer: what is new, who cares, why now, what proves it? Check it against one named outlet: would its readers care this week? If the answer is no, say so plainly and suggest how to make a story (a customer who will talk, a number, a local angle, owned data) or a better channel (newsletter, blog, social post).
2. **Check for red flags.** If the news could move a listed company's share price, stop until the securities lawyer approves the text and timing. If it touches safety, legal claims or personal data, get the right professional's review first.
3. **Write the headline as a plain fact.** Subject, verb, object. No adjectives the body cannot prove. Add a subhead only if a second fact matters.
4. **Write the first paragraph.** Who, what, when, where and why, in one or two sentences. A reporter should be able to stop reading here and still have the story.
5. **Add the supporting facts.** Short paragraphs in falling order of importance. Every number has a source; anything unchecked is marked [CHECK]. Use plain names for links, never keyword-stuffed anchor text, because Google treats optimised anchor text in distributed releases as link spam.
6. **Write the quotes.** One or two. A quote adds opinion, meaning or feeling ("why this matters to us") and never repeats facts the body states. Use only words the speaker said or approved. Mark drafts [QUOTE FOR APPROVAL: name].
7. **Add availability.** What reporters can get: an interview, a customer, data, images, a demo, dates.
8. **Add boilerplate and contact.** Two to four plain sentences about the business; a named media contact with email and phone.
9. **Set the release line.** "For immediate release" or "EMBARGOED UNTIL [date, time, time zone]". Repeat the embargo in the email subject when it goes out.
10. **Build the fact sheet.** Key facts as bullets, for reporters and for the business's own newsroom page, so that AI summaries and journalists quote the same facts.
11. **Cut.** Remove hype words (leading, innovative, revolutionary, world-class), unproven firsts and bests, and jargon a general reader would not know.
12. **Plan where it goes.** Newsroom page, pitches to the media list (see the media-pitching skill), wire only if it adds reach or is a disclosure requirement, owned channels and partners.

## Checks

- The headline states a fact and would survive without the rest of the release.
- The first paragraph answers the five Ws.
- Every number, name, title and date matches its source.
- Every quote is approved by the named person.
- No hype words, no unproven claims, no invented details.
- Embargo line, date, time and time zone are right.
- Links are plain names; paid placements are marked as such.
- Market-sensitive, legal, safety or data angles have the right sign-off.
- Contact details work.

## Output format

1. News test: a verdict (story / weak story / no story yet), the angle, what is missing.
2. The release: release line, headline, subhead, dateline, body, quotes, availability, boilerplate, contact, "###" end mark.
3. Fact sheet: 6 to 12 bullets.
4. Open items: every [CHECK] and [QUOTE FOR APPROVAL], with who must confirm.
5. Distribution note: newsroom, media list, wire (yes or no, and why).

## Example of good output (short)

*Harbour Lane Bakery is a made-up business used only to show the format. All details are invented for this example.*

**News test:** story, for local and trade food outlets. The angle is the town's first commercial kitchen that trains young people out of work, with a named first group of trainees. Missing: one trainee willing to be interviewed, and the funder's approval to be named.

**EMBARGOED UNTIL 09:00 GMT, TUESDAY 3 MARCH [YEAR]**

**Harbour Lane Bakery opens a training kitchen for 12 young people in Porthaven**

PORTHAVEN, 3 March [YEAR] - Harbour Lane Bakery today opened a training kitchen at its Quay Street site, where 12 local people aged 18 to 24 who are out of work will train as bakers over 16 weeks. Places are paid, and the first group starts on 10 March.

The kitchen was funded by [FUNDER, CHECK: approval to name] and the bakery's own profits. Trainees work alongside the bakery's four bakers and finish with a reference and an interview for any open role.

"[QUOTE FOR APPROVAL: Mara Quinn, owner - why she started the scheme, in her words]"

**Available for interview:** Mara Quinn; one trainee [CHECK]; photos of the kitchen.

**About Harbour Lane Bakery:** [two sentences supplied by the client]

**Media contact:** [name, email, phone]
