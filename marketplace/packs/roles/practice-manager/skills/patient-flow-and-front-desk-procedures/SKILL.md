# Patient flow and front desk procedures

## When to use

- The waiting room backs up at the same times each day.
- The phone rings out while someone is checking a patient in.
- Late arrivals, payments or rebooking cause arguments or lost bookings.
- A new receptionist starts and there is nothing written down.
- The owner is still the person who knows how the desk works.

Do not use it to decide triage, urgency or what to tell a patient about symptoms. The procedure carries the practice's own emergency script, word for word, set by the clinicians.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** a de-identified schedule export, the current procedures, the floor plan, the rota.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the procedures in the practice's document folder or wiki.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved procedures, replace old versions (archive, never delete) and set a review date.

## What you need

1. Practice type, clinicians, rooms, hours and who is on the desk each session.
2. The booking system and how payments are taken.
3. The busiest times, from a schedule export or the desk's own account.
4. What goes wrong now, in the desk team's words.
5. The practice's written policies on late arrivals, cancellations, payments and emergencies.
6. Who decides clinical questions, and the emergency script if one exists.

## Steps

### 1. Map the flow

Write the patient's path as a short list: book, confirm, arrive, check in, wait, see the clinician, check out, pay, rebook or set a recall, follow-up. Mark who owns each step. For a vet practice, add the animal's records and consent forms where they sit.

### 2. Find the busiest ten minutes

From the export or the desk team's account, find the window when arrivals, departures and calls collide. Count what happens in it: how many arrivals, check-outs and calls. Show the counts. That window shapes the procedures more than any average.

### 3. Name the pinch points

Typical ones: phone during check-in; late arrivals; payment queues at check-out; patients leaving without a next booking; forms not done before arrival; clinicians running late with nobody telling the waiting room. List each with what happens now and what it costs (a missed call, a lost slot, a complaint).

### 4. Decide who does what in the window

One person owns the phone and one owns the door during the busy window. If there is only one person, decide which waits and say so in the procedure. Use the practice system's tools where they help: online forms before arrival, self check-in, payment at booking, a wait list for freed slots.

### 5. Write each procedure

For each pinch point, one procedure:
- **Purpose:** one line.
- **Who:** the role, not the person's name.
- **Steps:** numbered, one action each.
- **What to say:** an italic line where the desk speaks to a patient or caller.
- **When it goes wrong:** the fallback and who to tell.

Late arrivals follow the written policy and the clinician's call on whether to see the patient. Rebooking happens before payment, so the next step is set while the patient is still at the desk. Recalls are set from the interval the clinician recorded; if none is recorded, ask the clinician, never guess.

### 6. Add privacy habits

- Call patients by first name or as the practice prefers; no reasons for the visit spoken aloud at the desk.
- Screens face away from the waiting room; the system locks when the desk is left.
- Phone messages: practice name, number and what is needed to confirm. Reminder and voicemail wording is in the front desk coordinator kit.
- Each staff member uses their own login.

### 7. Test with the team

Read each procedure with the person who will follow it. Run the busiest window once with the new roles. Change what does not fit. Then set one measure for each change (calls missed in the window, patients leaving without a next step) and check it after a month.

## Checks

- Every step has one action and a role.
- Every patient-facing step has a "what to say" line.
- No step asks the desk to judge symptoms, urgency or treatment.
- Policies quoted match the practice's written versions, with dates.
- Privacy habits are in the procedures, not a separate memo.
- Each change has a measure and a review date.
- The procedures carry the line: *Clinical steps are set by the clinicians; check this with local rules for privacy and fees.*

## Output format

1. Patient flow map with owners and pinch points
2. The busiest ten minutes: counts and who does what
3. Procedures (purpose, who, steps, what to say, when it goes wrong)
4. Privacy habits at the desk
5. Measures and review date
6. Open questions for the owner and clinicians

## Example of good output (short)

> **Illustrative structure, not a real business: a physio practice with four physios and two receptionists. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.**
>
> **Busiest ten minutes:** 8:50 to 9:00 on Mondays. From the last four Mondays: 6 arrivals, 2 check-outs and 7 calls on average.
>
> **Procedure: phone during check-in (8:45 to 9:15)**
> Who: Receptionist 2 (phones). Receptionist 1 owns the door.
> 1. Receptionist 2 answers every call by the third ring.
> 2. If the caller wants to book, book now; if it will take long, take a name and number and call back by 9:30.
>    *What to say: "I'd love to help. Can I call you back by half nine so I can give you my full attention?"*
> 3. If the caller describes pain or an injury, read the clinic's script and pass the call to the duty physio. Do not give advice.
> When it goes wrong: if both are busy, the call goes to voicemail with the callback promise; Receptionist 2 clears voicemail by 9:30.
