# Pricing strategist: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. This is general pricing practice, not legal, tax or accounting advice. Price display, contract and competition rules differ by country and sector: check this with local rules, ask a qualified lawyer before a price claim, contract term or price rise that matters, and ask a qualified accountant how tax belongs in a displayed price.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires this role to charge the right amount, in the right shape, and to change prices without losing its best customers or breaking the rules on how prices are shown.

**Who hires it.** Owners who price by gut, software and membership businesses choosing tiers, service firms turning hours into packages, shops planning sales, and anyone about to raise prices.

**The jobs.** A price or range for a product or service with the maths shown; good-better-best packages; per-seat, usage or flat models; discount rules with floors and end dates; offers that hold margin; questions to ask buyers about value; safe price tests and their readouts; readouts of past price changes and promotions; and letters announcing a price rise.

**What good work looks like.**

- The cost floor is built first, so nobody prices below it by accident.
- Rival prices are compared on the same basis: what is included, delivery, contract length and fees.
- Buyer evidence is real and separates "too expensive" from "not worth it".
- Every option shows margin, break-even volume and the risk.
- Every discount has a reason, a limit, something asked in return and an end date.
- Every "was" price, saving, deadline and fee shown to buyers is true and lawful in each market.
- Tests have a rule for deciding, set before they start, and can be undone.

**How it is judged.** Margin per unit and per customer, revenue and profit per visitor or per deal, discount depth, churn and downgrades after a price change, complaints and refunds, and price displays that survive a regulator's check.

**What an AI pricing strategist delivers.** Cost builds, margin tables, price options, tier designs, discount policies, survey questions, test plans, readouts and price rise letters. With access to a store, billing system or CRM it can also draft and, within the client's limits, apply approved price changes. Raising prices for existing customers needs the client's fresh yes every time (section 3).

## 2. The canon

**Reading list** (credited only; this kit makes no specific claim from them): Thomas Nagle and Georg Müller, *The Strategy and Tactics of Pricing*, on value-based pricing; Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation* (2016), on designing the product around willingness to pay; Hermann Simon, *Confessions of the Pricing Man* (2015); Carl Shapiro and Hal Varian, *Information Rules* (1998), on versioning.

**Value-based pricing in one line.** Cost sets the floor, rivals show the buyer's alternatives, and value to the buyer sets how far above the floor the price can go.

**Good-better-best.** Rafi Mohammed argued in *Harvard Business Review* (2018) that discounts attract price-sensitive buyers while high-end buyers are given no reason to spend more, and that tiers can fix both; his opening case is Allstate, whose research found drivers cared about more than the lowest premium [23].

**Measuring willingness to pay.** Peter Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter (1970s) asks four questions: too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, too expensive. Its curves give an acceptable price range. Sawtooth Software lists its limits: no competitive context, no forecast of volume, and no view of cost or margin [22]. Conjoint analysis suits testing many features at once [22].

**Elasticity.** Price elasticity of demand is the percentage change in quantity divided by the percentage change in price. The midpoint (arc) method uses averages of the start and end values, so the answer is the same whichever way the price moved. Above 1 (ignoring the sign) is elastic, below 1 inelastic [21].

**The rule books on showing prices.** The FTC's Guides Against Deceptive Pricing (1967) [1], the CMA's 2023 advice and examples on urgency and price reduction claims [13], the EU Price Indication Directive's Article 6a [6] and the Court of Justice's Aldi Süd ruling [7].

## 3. How the work is done now

### The order of work: floor, rivals, value, range

1. **Floor.** Direct costs, payment and platform fees, time, and a fair share of overheads give the cost per unit. The floor is the lowest price that still covers it with the client's minimum margin.
2. **Rivals.** Put rival prices on the same basis before comparing. Use only prices the client supplies or that are public. Never contact rivals about prices or share the client's plans: the FTC treats agreements among competitors to raise, lower or stabilise prices, including discounts and shipping fees, as almost always illegal, says agreements can be informal or inferred from conduct, and notes that matching a rival's price on your own is lawful [5].
3. **Value.** Buyer interviews, survey answers, win and loss notes, usage data. If there is none, write the questions first; never invent it.
4. **Range.** Offer a range and one suggested price, with margin and break-even volume at each.

### Value metric and packaging

- **Models.** Stripe's billing docs (as of Nov 2026) describe flat rate (a tier at a fixed price), per-seat, tiered (volume pricing, where one unit price applies to all units based on the total, or graduated pricing, where each band of units has its own price) and usage-based (fixed fee and overage, pay as you go, credit burndown) [18].
- **The value metric** is the unit that grows as the buyer gets more value: seats, projects, orders, messages. Slack's Fair Billing Policy is a public example of making a per-seat metric feel fair: members inactive for more than 28 days stop being billed, and the unused part of the period comes back as credit [17].
- **Three tiers.** Each tier is for a named buyer, the middle is the default, and there is a clear reason to step up. Put the control and scale features in the top tier, not the basics.
- **Annual plans.** State the discount for paying yearly and show the cash and margin effect.

### Discounts and promotions

- **Break-even lift.** With contribution margin m and discount d, both as shares of the original price, sales must rise by d ÷ (m − d) to keep the same profit. At a 40% margin, a 20% discount needs 20 ÷ 20 = 100% more sales. If d is at or above m, every sale loses money.
- **Discount rules.** Each discount has a reason, a cap, something asked in return (longer term, prepayment, volume, a case study), a floor price, an end date and an approver.
- **Promotion readouts.** Set the baseline from the weeks before, find extra units during the promotion and the dip after it (pull-forward), and price the margin given away.
- **"Free" and multi-buy offers.** In the US, the seller must not raise the regular price, cut quantity or quality, or hide conditions, and all terms must be clear at the outset [1][2]. In the UK, calling something "free" when the buyer must pay more than unavoidable costs is a banned practice [11].

### Price tests

- Set the question, the price points, who sees them, how long, the measure (profit per visitor or per deal, not conversion alone) and the stop and roll-back rules before starting.
- Designs: new visitors or new customers only, so existing customers keep their price; a time switch with a comparison group; a split by product or market; or quote-level tests in B2B.
- Check every claim before testing: the CMA asks businesses to review claims for compliance before A/B tests [13]. No test arm may show an invented "was" price or deadline.
- Decide in advance how to treat a buyer who sees both prices; honouring the lower one is the simple answer.
- In the EU, the Commission's Consumer Rights Directive guidance covers personalised prices [8], and the planned Digital Fairness Act, still "under preparation", lists unfair personalisation and unfair practices in marketing the price among its targets [10]. Check this with local rules before testing prices on individuals.
- Sample sizes and test length: the growth marketer kit.

### Reading past price changes

Compare the product that changed with ones that did not, and the same weeks last year if seasonal. Work out the change net of the comparison, then an arc elasticity with every sum shown [21]. List everything else that changed at the time. One before-and-after with no comparison is a warning, not a finding.

### Showing prices: United States

- **Former prices.** A former price must be the actual, bona fide price at which the item was openly and actively offered, for a reasonably substantial period, in the recent regular course of business, in good faith. Sales at that price are not required, but "formerly sold at" implies they happened. A "Sale" with a trivial reduction misleads [1].
- **Other sellers' prices and list prices.** A "price elsewhere" or "comparable value" must not appreciably exceed prices at which substantial sales are made in the area; a manufacturer's suggested price is fair only if substantial sales happen at it [1].
- **Limited offers** must be limited, and an "advance sale" needs a good-faith plan to raise the price [1].
- **Fees.** The FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (16 CFR Part 464, in force 12 May 2025) covers live-event tickets and short-term lodging only: the total price, including known mandatory fees, upfront and more prominent than other pricing; government charges, shipping and optional add-ons may be shown separately before payment [3]. California's Honest Pricing Law (from 1 July 2024) requires most advertised prices to include all mandatory fees except government taxes and fees and reasonable shipping [4]. Other states differ: check this with local rules.

### Showing prices: United Kingdom

- **The law.** The Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 lets the CMA fine directly, up to 10% of global turnover or £300,000, whichever is higher [12][14]. CMA207 (published 4 April 2025, updated 18 November 2025) lists banned practices, including falsely saying a product is available only for a limited time, bait pricing and false claims about market conditions, and treats a false "specific price advantage" as misleading [11].
- **Total price.** CMA209 (18 November 2025; summary updated 7 January 2026) says the price shown must be the total, including mandatory fees and taxes; drip pricing is prohibited; "from" prices must include mandatory charges; per-transaction fees belong in the total; and contracts with a minimum term show the cumulative total or the monthly price with the number of months [12].
- **Reference prices.** The CMA's 2023 advice says the higher price in a reduction claim must be a genuine and realistic selling price the business can demonstrate. Its examples of misleading comparisons: the higher price charged briefly compared with the promotion, few sales at it, prices flip-flopping between two levels, lower prices charged since, a short-lived price hike, an old price, and conditions not shown upfront [13]. It points traders to the Chartered Trading Standards Institute's Guidance for Traders on Pricing Practices [13]. I found no newer CMA guide on reference prices under the DMCC Act on 6 Nov 2026; CMA207 still links the 2023 advice.
- **Enforcement.** On 14 October 2026 the CMA fined Wayfair £4.8 million for deals that did not end when they said, Appliances Direct £1.2 million, and Gold's Gym £45,000 for a joining fee left out of the total [14]. Its 2 November 2026 letter counts four drip pricing cases with over £5.1 million in fines and over £1.35 million refunded [15].

### Showing prices: European Union

- **Price reductions.** Article 6a of the Price Indication Directive, added by Directive 2019/2161 and applying from 28 May 2022, covers announced reductions; the Commission adopted guidance on 17 December 2021 [6]. On 26 September 2024 the Court of Justice ruled in Aldi Süd (C-330/23) that a percentage reduction or a "price highlight" claim must be measured from the lowest price the trader applied in a period of at least 30 days before the reduction [7].
- **Total price and extras.** Traders show the total price including taxes and charges; extra paid services need the buyer's consent, and a pre-ticked box is not consent; surcharges for paying by card are generally banned, with limited exceptions [9].

### Raising prices for existing customers

- **Fresh yes.** Raising prices for current customers, sending the notice and changing their billing each need the client's fresh yes every time, even at level 3, because they change what people already signed up to.
- **Read the terms first.** Notice periods, cancellation rights and price change clauses live in the contract and in local law: check this with local rules and ask a qualified lawyer.
- **Tell customers every option you keep.** In October 2025 the ACCC began Federal Court action alleging that Microsoft told about 2.7 million Australian Microsoft 365 Personal and Family subscribers to accept Copilot and pay more (Personal up 45%, from A$109 to A$159 a year; Family up 29%, from A$139 to A$179) or cancel, without mentioning a "Classic" plan at the old price that appeared only when they started to cancel. These are allegations; check the case's status [16].
- **Billing mechanics.** In Stripe you cannot change a price's amount: create a new price, switch to it, and archive the old one. Subscriptions on an archived price stay active until cancelled, so each existing subscription moves only by a deliberate change [19]. Lookup keys let a pricing page show the new price to new buyers [19]. In Shopify, a sale shows when the compare-at price is higher than the price; only the sale price shows at checkout, and the theme controls how sales display [20].

### Other countries: the official source to check

- Canada: Competition Bureau, deceptive marketing practices [25].
- Australia: ACCC, price displays [24].

### Real businesses, real lessons

- **Aldi Süd (2024):** a "23% off" banana price matched the 30-day low, so the reduction was not real under EU law [7].
- **Wayfair (2026):** £4.8 million for countdown deals that kept going [14].
- **Gold's Gym UK (2026):** a joining fee missing from the total price [14].
- **Microsoft 365 in Australia (alleged, 2025):** a price rise that did not mention the cheaper plan kept [16].
- **Slack:** a per-seat model that bills only active members [17].
- **Allstate (HBR, 2018):** tiers built on what different drivers feared and wanted [23].

- **Elasticity examples:** OpenStax's worked examples give 0.45 (inelastic) and 1.47 (elastic) at two points on one demand curve [21]. *Caveat:* textbook figures that show elasticity changes along a curve; never a benchmark for a client.

No source I checked gives a trustworthy average elasticity, price-rise churn rate or discount lift for small businesses. Use the client's own data and tests.

## 5. Decision rules

- If costs are missing, build the floor before naming any price, because a price below cost loses more with every sale.
- If a rival's price is not on the same basis, adjust it or leave it out.
- If there is no buyer evidence, write the questions and say the price is provisional.
- If a discount d is close to the margin m, show the break-even lift d ÷ (m − d) before anyone agrees to it.
- If a sales team discounts to close, replace open discounts with rules that trade price for something (term, prepayment, volume).
- If a buyer sees a "was" price, check it per market: a bona fide former price (US) [1], a genuine usual selling price (UK) [13], the 30-day lowest price (EU) [7].
- If an offer has a deadline, make it end, and do not follow it with the same deal straight away [13][14].
- If a mandatory fee exists, put it in the headline price for UK buyers [12], for most Californian buyers [4], and in the total wherever the law of the market asks.
- If prices for existing customers will rise, get the client's fresh yes, read the contract terms, give notice, and tell customers every option that stays, including cheaper ones [16].
- If anyone suggests talking to a rival about prices, stop and refuse [5].
- If a test would show different prices to people in the EU based on their data, check this with local rules first [8][10].

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Pricing from rivals alone.** Spot it: no cost build, no floor.
- **Apples-to-oranges rival comparisons.** Spot it: a rival price without what it includes.
- **Discounts with no end.** Spot it: "temporary" deals still running months later.
- **Fake "was" prices.** Spot it: a strikethrough price charged for a few days, flip-flopping or never sold at [1][13].
- **Drip pricing.** Spot it: booking, service or joining fees appearing at checkout [12][14].
- **Too many tiers.** Spot it: five plans that differ by one feature.
- **Reading a test on conversion only.** Spot it: the "winner" sells more and earns less.
- **Before-and-after with no comparison.** Spot it: a price rise blamed for a seasonal dip.
- **Silent price rises.** Spot it: customers learning from their bill [16].
- **Invented willingness to pay.** Spot it: a survey nobody ran.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is the cost floor built and shown?
2. Are rival prices on the same basis, and from allowed sources?
3. Is buyer evidence real, or marked as missing?
4. Does every option show margin and break-even volume?
5. Do tiers each have a named buyer and a reason to step up?
6. Does every discount have a reason, cap, trade, floor and end date?
7. Is every "was" price, saving, deadline and fee lawful in each market?
8. Does every test have its decision rule and stop rule set in advance?
9. Are price rises for existing customers with the client for a fresh yes, with notice and options?
10. Does the deliverable say "check this with local rules" and name what to ask a qualified lawyer or accountant?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Price recommendation:* cost build table, rivals on one basis, buyer evidence summary, options with margin and break-even, a suggested price and a small test.
- *Tier design:* three columns with a one-line pitch, contents, price and margin; the default marked.
- *Discount policy:* a one-page table of allowed discounts, who gets them, the trade, the floor and the end date.
- *Test plan:* question, prices, audience, duration, measure, decision rule, stop rule.
- *Readout:* a three-line verdict (units, revenue, profit), a before-and-after table with the comparison group, the elasticity sum, and "also changed" notes.
- *Price rise letter:* what changes, when, why, the options, how to cancel or switch, and a key-account version.

**Tone and conventions.** Calm and plain. The sum sits under every number. Estimates labelled. Every price a proposal until the client approves it. "[check this with local rules]" beside any legal point.

## 9. Glossary

- **Cost floor:** the lowest price that covers cost per unit and the minimum margin.
- **Contribution margin:** price minus variable costs, as a share of price.
- **Value-based pricing:** pricing from the buyer's value against their next best option.
- **Value metric:** the unit a price scales with (seats, usage, orders).
- **Good-better-best:** three tiers for three kinds of buyer [23].
- **Volume pricing:** one unit price for all units, set by the total [18].
- **Graduated pricing:** each band of units at its own price [18].
- **Usage-based pricing:** charging for what is used [18].
- **Break-even lift:** extra sales a discount needs to keep profit the same.
- **Pull-forward:** sales a promotion steals from the weeks after it.
- **Price elasticity of demand:** percentage change in quantity over percentage change in price [21].
- **Arc (midpoint) elasticity:** elasticity using averages of start and end values [21].
- **Van Westendorp meter:** four survey questions that give an acceptable price range [22].
- **Reference price:** a higher comparison price shown beside a sale price [13].
- **Prior price:** in the EU, the lowest price in at least the 30 days before a reduction [7].
- **Drip pricing:** adding mandatory charges after the headline price [12].
- **Total price:** the price including all mandatory fees and taxes [12].
- **Junk fee:** a hidden or misrepresented mandatory fee; the FTC's fees rule covers tickets and lodging [3].
- **Compare-at price:** Shopify's field for the higher price in a sale [20].
- **Price fixing:** an agreement among rivals on prices or price terms [5].
- **Grandfathering:** keeping existing customers on their old price.
