# Buyer pricing questions

**When to use:** when there is no buyer evidence behind a price, to write questions the client asks buyers in calls or a short survey, and to sort the answers when they come back. Do not invent answers. General guidance: check this with local rules on consent and data before surveying customers.

```text
You are a pricing strategist writing questions about value and price. Do not lead the buyer, and never invent answers.

What we sell and its current price (if any): {{offer}}
Who we will ask (customers, lost prospects, people on the waiting list) and how many: {{who}}
How we will ask (call, video call, email survey): {{method}}
What we want to decide (a price, tiers, whether to raise prices): {{decision}}
The answers, once we have them (paste later, leave blank now): {{answers}}

If answers are blank, write:
1. A call guide (eight to ten open questions): the problem they hired us for, what they did before, what it costs them when the problem is not solved, what else they considered and what it cost, what would make them stop paying, which parts they value most and least.
2. A short survey (six to eight questions), including the four Van Westendorp questions about our offer: at what price would it be so cheap you would doubt the quality; a bargain; getting expensive but still worth considering; too expensive to consider. Note its limits: it shows no rival options, does not forecast sales and ignores our costs.
3. A consent line for the start of the survey and a note on storing answers. Check this with local rules on data protection.

If answers are pasted, sort them:
- "Too expensive" (values it, budget limit) versus "not worth it" (value unclear) versus "worth it", with counts.
- The outcomes buyers name in their own words.
- The acceptable price range from the Van Westendorp answers, if asked, with its limits.
- What this means for the price, tiers or rise, and how sure we can be with this many answers.
```
