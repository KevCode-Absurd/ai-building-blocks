# Price test and readout

## When to use

- Before trying a new price, to design a test that can be undone.
- When a price test has finished and needs a clear call.
- When an owner asks "did our price rise hurt?" or "was that sale worth it?"

This is general analysis practice, not legal advice. Check this with local rules before showing different prices to different people (especially in the EU), and ask a qualified accountant if a cost or tax figure is in doubt.

## What you need

**For a test plan:**
1. The question (which price, which product or plan, for whom).
2. Weekly volume (visitors, enquiries, bookings or orders) and current conversion.
3. Costs, so profit per visitor or per deal can be measured.
4. How prices can be shown: the store, billing tool, test tool or quotes.
5. Markets.

**For a readout:**
1. Weekly units, prices and revenue for the product that changed, for the weeks before and after (or the test arms).
2. The same for products that did not change, and for the same weeks last year if seasonal.
3. Unit costs.
4. Everything else that changed in the period: ads, packaging, stock-outs, season, rival moves.

**Access:** level 1 to read sales, orders and test results. Level 2 to build the test unstarted in the test tool, store or billing. Level 3 to start and stop the approved test within its limits. Showing a new price to existing customers, or any new "was" price, needs a fresh yes.

## Steps: planning a test

### 1. Write the question and the decision rule

"If [new price] gives higher [profit per visitor or per deal] than [current price] over [period], we keep it; if it is lower, we roll back." Write it before starting, with the client's approval.

### 2. Choose the design

- **New buyers only:** the new price shows to new visitors or new enquiries; existing customers keep theirs.
- **Time switch with a comparison:** change the price for a period and compare against products or regions that did not change.
- **Split by product or market:** when showing two prices at once is not possible or not wanted.
- **Quote-level (B2B):** alternate price points across new proposals and track win rate and margin.

### 3. Check the volume

Say plainly whether the weekly volume can show a difference in the time available. Use the growth marketer kit's sample-size method; if volume is too small, choose a longer test or a before-and-after with a comparison group, and say how sure the answer can be.

### 4. Check the claims

Every price, saving or deadline in each arm must be true for each market. No arm may show an invented "was" price. The CMA asks businesses to review claims for compliance before A/B tests. In the EU, check this with local rules before personalising prices, as the Commission's guidance covers personalised prices.

### 5. Set the stop rule and the fairness rule

Stop early if a set guardrail breaks (complaints, refunds or cancellations rising, profit per visitor falling for a set number of weeks the client agrees). Decide how to treat a buyer who sees both prices: honouring the lower one is the simple answer.

## Steps: reading out a test or a past change

### 1. Check the data

Weeks covered, missing weeks, stock-outs, and the other changes listed. Report problems before any maths.

### 2. Compare with something that did not change

Use the midpoint method for each percentage change: (after − before) ÷ the average of after and before.

- Units change for the changed product.
- Units change for the comparison group.
- Net units change = changed product − comparison.
- Price change, same method.
- Arc elasticity = net units change ÷ price change. Above 1 (ignoring the sign) means demand is elastic; below 1, inelastic.

### 3. Work out revenue and profit

Weekly revenue and weekly contribution (price − unit cost, times units), before and after. Lead with profit.

### 4. For promotions

Baseline from the weeks before, extra units during, the dip after (pull-forward), margin given away, and whether it paid back.

### 5. Write the verdict

Three lines: units, revenue, profit. Then how sure we can be, and what else changed. Label the elasticity as an estimate for these products only.

## Checks

- The decision rule was written before the test started.
- A comparison group is used, or its absence is flagged.
- Every percentage uses the midpoint method, with the sum shown.
- Profit, not only units or conversion, decides.
- Other changes in the period are listed under the result.
- No test arm shows a false price claim.
- Existing customers' prices did not change without the client's fresh yes.
- The deliverable says to check this with local rules.

## Output format

**Test plan:** question, design, prices, audience, period, measure, decision rule, stop rule, fairness rule, claim check, what needs approval.

**Readout:** three-line verdict; before-and-after table with the comparison group; elasticity sum written out; revenue and profit table; "also changed" list; next step; spreadsheet formulas using the client's column names.

## Example of good output

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a granola maker. The 500 g granola rose from $8.50 to $9.75 on 1 June. Fifteen weeks either side. Two flavours stayed at $8.50. Unit cost $4.10. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

**Verdict:** units down 10%, revenue up 4%, profit up 16% a week.

| | Before | After | Change (midpoint) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Granola, units a week | 420 | 380 | −10.0% |
| Comparison flavours, units a week | 300 | 291 | −3.0% |
| Net units change | | | −7.0% |
| Price | $8.50 | $9.75 | +13.7% |

**Arc elasticity:** −7.0% ÷ 13.7% = −0.51, so demand was inelastic for this product over this period (an estimate).

| A week | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $3,570 | $3,705 |
| Contribution | $1,848 | $2,147 |

**Also changed:** new shelf placement at one stockist in July; no stock-outs.

**Next:** hold the price; read again after the holiday season against the same weeks last year.

Check this with local rules before showing the new price with any "was" price, and ask a qualified accountant if the unit cost has changed.
