# Discovery and first-version scope

The most expensive mistake in product work is building the wrong thing well. This skill puts the problem and its evidence first, then cuts the idea to the smallest version that answers the riskiest question. It never invents research: where evidence is missing, it says so and plans a cheap way to get it.

## When to use

- A new product or feature idea, before a PRD.
- A founder with a fixed budget asking what to test first.
- A team with a long feature list and no clear first release.
- Feedback or tickets that need turning into a problem worth solving.

## What you need

- The idea in the client's words, and who it is for.
- Evidence: interview notes, tickets, reviews, sales notes, usage data. Names removed unless agreed.
- Budget, people and time available.
- What they do today and what competitors offer.
- **Research mode:** with web search connected, check competitors, public pricing and reviews live and date each finding. Without it, work from what the client supplies and list the unknowns.
- **Access I have:** level 1 is enough. Saving the brief as a draft page needs level 2; publishing it to the shared space needs level 3.

## Steps

1. **Write the problem, not the solution.** Who has it, in what situation, how often, how costly it is, and what they do today (including workarounds and doing nothing). Quote real evidence with its source; label anything else "assumption".
2. **Size the evidence honestly.** How many people, from which source, how recent. If there is none, stop and plan the cheapest way to get some (five conversations, a landing page, a concierge test) before scoping.
3. **State the idea in one sentence:** for [who], who [situation], our [product] [does what], unlike [current way].
4. **Check the four big risks** (Cagan): value (will they buy or use it?), usability (can they work it out?), feasibility (can it be built with the time and skills?), viability (does it work for sales, legal, money and brand?). Rank them; the riskiest goes first.
5. **Name the one thing the first version must prove,** and the result that means go, adjust or stop. Agree it before building. Example: "At least [number agreed with the client] of the first [number] invited users pay for a second month."
6. **List every feature the client mentioned,** then sort into must, later and never. A must is only something the test cannot run without. Everything else is later or never, with a one-line reason.
7. **Pick the cheapest way to deliver each must:** by hand (a person does it behind the scenes), a spreadsheet or form, a no-code tool, or a small build. Give rough steps and who does each. Prefer faking it by hand where the test allows.
8. **Set the appetite.** How much time is the test worth? Fit the scope to it rather than estimating the full idea (Shape Up). If the musts do not fit, cut again.
9. **Write the test plan:** who is recruited and how (see the customer-insights-researcher kit for consent), what is measured, for how long, and who reads the result.
10. **List risks and open questions,** each with the person who must answer it.
11. **Access steps.** Level 2: save the brief as a draft page. Level 3: publish it to the agreed space and create a single discovery epic or project, within the limits.

## Checks

- The problem is written before any feature.
- Every piece of evidence has a source; assumptions are labelled.
- One thing to prove, with go and stop results agreed in advance.
- Every must is needed for the test.
- The plan fits the budget and the appetite.
- No invented users, quotes or numbers.

## Output format

1. **Access I have.**
2. **Problem brief (one page):** problem, who, evidence with sources, today's workaround, why now, options considered.
3. **Four risks table:** risk, what we know, what we assume, how we test.
4. **The one thing to prove,** with go, adjust and stop results.
5. **Scope table:** feature, must, later or never, reason, cheapest way to deliver.
6. **Test plan** and rough timeline.
7. **Risks and open questions** with owners.

## Example of good output (short)

*Pawsitter is a made-up business used only to show the format: a founder with a small budget wants an app matching dog sitters with owners in one city.*

**Problem:** Owners who travel for work struggle to find a trusted sitter at short notice. Evidence: 9 of 12 owners the founder spoke to in October described a last-minute scramble (founder's notes). Assumption: owners will pay a booking fee.

**One thing to prove:** owners will pay a booking fee for a vetted sitter. Go: the founder's agreed number of paid bookings from the first invited owners within six weeks. Stop: fewer than half that.

| Feature | Sort | Reason | Cheapest way |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sitter profiles | Must | Owners need to choose | A shared page of 10 vetted sitters |
| Booking and payment | Must | The test is payment | Form plus a payment link; founder confirms by hand |
| Reviews | Later | No bookings yet to review | - |
| In-app chat | Never (for now) | Text messages work | - |
| Matching algorithm | Later | 10 sitters can be matched by hand | Founder matches |

**Test plan:** invite owners from two local dog groups; measure paid bookings and repeat bookings; founder reads results weekly.
