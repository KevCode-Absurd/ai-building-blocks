# Product marketing manager: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

**Where neighbouring knowledge lives.** This kit covers product positioning, messaging, launches, pricing pages, competitor comparisons and the rules on comparative claims, and the handoff to sales. Other kits go deeper on the next step and are pointed to, not repeated: page and email copy in the conversion copywriter kit; brand-level positioning, naming and voice in the brand strategist kit; competitor and market research methods in the market research analyst kit; battlecards, playbooks and rep training in the sales enablement specialist kit; channel tests and unit economics in the growth marketer kit.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires a product marketing manager when it has a product or feature that buyers do not yet understand, cannot tell apart from rivals, or have not heard about. The work is to decide who the product is for, what it should be compared with, why it wins for those buyers, how to say that in words the whole team repeats, and how to bring it to market so the first weeks teach something.

Who hires it: founders whose homepage lists features but no reason to choose them; software teams shipping a feature nobody notices; small firms entering a crowded market where every rival sounds the same; owners about to launch a first or second product with a small list and a small budget; sales teams asking "what do we say when they mention the cheaper rival?"

What good work looks like:

- **A positioning statement on one page**: the competitive alternative, the capabilities only this product has, the value they create, the buyers who care most, and the market frame that makes that value obvious [1].
- **A message sheet** the whole team uses: one core line, three pillars each with proof, the buyer's own words, and words to avoid.
- **A rival map** with the date each claim was captured and the gaps nobody owns.
- **A claims file**: every objective or comparative claim matched to the evidence the business holds before it is published [8][14].
- **A launch plan** built backwards from the date, with one audience first, owners on every task, first-week measures and a plan for the week after.
- **A pricing page** where tiers, totals and any savings claims are clear and lawful in every market the business sells to.
- **A sales handoff**: talk track, objections, proof and "what we can and cannot claim", delivered before launch day, not after.

How it is judged: whether buyers repeat the core message back in calls and reviews; win rate against the named alternative; launch-week numbers against the plan; whether sales and support use the materials; and whether any claim gets challenged by a rival or a regulator.

Agents in this role differ in scope, not method: positioning strategists produce the positioning statement, message sheet, rival map, homepage check and buyer profile; launch managers run the demand test, beta, launch plan, message pack and post-launch review.

## 2. The canon

Each idea is summarised in my own words and credited. None of these authors has reviewed or endorsed AI Blocks.

**April Dunford, *Obviously Awesome* (2019) and *Sales Pitch* (2023) [1][2].** Positioning has five parts that depend on each other: competitive alternatives (what customers would do if the product did not exist, often a spreadsheet, a manual process or doing nothing), the capabilities that differ from those alternatives, the value those capabilities create, the customers who care most about that value, and the market category that makes the value obvious. Work them in that order, starting from the real alternatives, not from the features. Beware "phantom competitors" that buyers never actually consider, and treat creating a new category as a risky choice that usually loses to starting in one buyers already know [1]. *Sales Pitch* turns positioning into an eight-step pitch for sales teams [2].

**Al Ries and Jack Trout, *Positioning: The Battle for Your Mind* (1981).** Positioning happens in the buyer's mind, not in the product. In a crowded market a brand can own one simple idea, and the clearest way in is often relative to something the buyer already knows. Credited here as a reading-list classic.

**Geoffrey Moore, *Crossing the Chasm* (1991).** Early buyers and mainstream buyers want different things, so a new product wins one narrow segment completely before it widens. His positioning statement template (for whom, who need what, our product is a what, that does what, unlike which alternative) is a common one-line format. Credited here as a reading-list classic.

**Clayton Christensen, *Competing Against Luck* (2016), and Bob Moesta, demand-side selling [3].** People "hire" a product to make progress in a situation. Buying starts with a struggle: nothing happens until the current way stops working. Four forces act on a switch: the push of the problem and the pull of the new solution, against anxiety about the new and the habit of the present. Buying moves through a timeline from first thought, to passive and active looking, to deciding, onboarding and ongoing use. Learn it by interviewing real recent buyers about what happened, not by inventing personas [3].

**Madhavan Ramanujam and Georg Tacke, *Monetizing Innovation* (2016).** Talk about willingness to pay early, while the product can still change, and design the packaging around what different segments value and will pay for. Credited here as a reading-list classic.

**Peter Van Westendorp's Price Sensitivity Meter (1970s) [4].** Four questions (too cheap to trust, a bargain, getting expensive, too expensive) give a rough acceptable price range. It ignores rivals, the theory behind its line crossings is weak, and it does not predict volume, so use it for a first range on a new product, not as the answer [4].

**Kate Moran and Taylor Dykes, Nielsen Norman Group (2024) [5].** Comparison tables help when buyers weigh several features across a few options. The most common failure is inconsistent content, not design: every row needs a comparable value for every column [5].

**Spiegel Research Center (2017, page updated 2025) [6].** Reviews change buying: the first few matter most, and near-perfect averages are trusted less than good ones [6]. Proof from customers outweighs adjectives from the seller.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 Raw material first

Positioning is found, not invented. Gather, in this order: customer reviews (yours and rivals'), notes or recordings of recent sales calls, win and loss reasons, support tickets, switching interviews with five to ten recent buyers, and the current homepage and sales deck. Quote buyers' own words and keep the source of each quote.

Two working modes:

- **With web search connected:** the agent reads rivals' current pages, pricing and public reviews live, and records the date and URL of each capture, because rival claims change.
- **Without web search:** the agent works only from what the client pastes or uploads (rival pages, screenshots, exports, review text), says so at the top of the work, and marks every claim about a rival "as supplied on [date]".

Never invent a quote, a review, a rival's price or a market figure in either mode.

### 3.2 From positioning to message sheet

Run Dunford's order [1]:

1. **Alternatives:** list what buyers actually used before, from the interviews and lost deals. The status quo is usually on the list.
2. **Differences:** the capabilities this product has that those alternatives lack. Strip anything rivals also say.
3. **Value:** for each difference, "so what?" in the buyer's terms: time, money, risk, status. Attach proof: a number from the client's data, a named customer quote with permission, a third-party test.
4. **Best-fit buyers:** who cares most about that value, described by situation and trigger, not by age bracket.
5. **Market frame:** the category that makes the value obvious. Prefer a category buyers already search for; take on a new one only with budget to teach it.

Then write the positioning statement and the message sheet: the core line, three pillars (each a claim plus its proof), a short "why now", objections with honest answers, words to use (the buyer's) and words to avoid (jargon and claims you cannot prove), and how the emphasis shifts by audience (economic buyer, daily user, technical reviewer) and by channel. Brand voice belongs to the brand strategist kit; page copy to the conversion copywriter kit.

### 3.3 Rival maps and comparisons

A rival map lists five or more alternatives, including the status quo, with each one's main claim, price model, target buyer and the proof they show, plus the capture date. Claims that many rivals share are weak ground; the gap worth owning is a true benefit that matters to a clear buyer and that few rivals state.

Public comparisons come in three shapes: a feature table on the pricing page, a "vs" page about one named rival, and an "alternatives to" page. For tables, keep to about five options, use the same attributes with comparable values for every option, offer a way to show only the differences, and translate specs into everyday terms [5]. Google's guidance for review and comparison content asks for evidence and first-hand testing, measurable comparisons, what sets each option apart and who each suits [7]. A page that names a rival is advertising, so the claims rules below apply in full.

### 3.4 Claims, comparisons and substantiation, by region (as of November 2026)

**US.**
- Advertisers need a reasonable basis for every objective claim, stated or implied, *before* it runs. A claim that names its proof ("tests show") needs at least that level of proof [8].
- The FTC has encouraged truthful comparisons that name rivals since 1979, with a clear basis of comparison; truthful disparagement is allowed, deception is not [9].
- Fine print cannot fix a misleading headline, qualifiers belong next to the claim, testimonials rarely prove objective claims, and agencies that help make the ad can share liability [10].
- Customer quotes and employee posts follow the Endorsement Guides: disclose material connections in the endorsement itself, and if a testimonial shows unusual results, say what people generally get [11].
- A rival can sue over false statements about its goods or yours in commercial advertising under the Lanham Act, section 43(a) [12]. It can also file a challenge with the National Advertising Division, an industry body that reviews national advertising and refers advertisers who will not comply to the FTC; its fast track aims to decide single-issue cases within 20 business days [13].

**UK.**
- The CAP Code requires documentary evidence for objective claims before publication (rule 3.7) and allows only obvious exaggeration that does not mislead (3.2). Comparisons with an identifiable competitor must not mislead about either product, must compare products that meet the same need, must objectively compare material, relevant, verifiable and representative features (price can be one), and must not cause confusion, denigrate a rival or take unfair advantage of its reputation. An unqualified superlative such as "the best" is read as a comparison with every rival [14].
- Three ASA rulings show how this plays out:
  - *LanguageNut* (Aug 2025): rival Sanako complained about a comparison blog post on LanguageNut's own site. The ASA found claims on content, ease of use and price unproven and gave readers no way to check them. Upheld [15].
  - *Vodafone* (Sep 2024): BT complained about "the same broadband" for less. The small print was not clearly tied to the claim, the report relied on did not support it, and there was no evidence that "millions" of BT customers were switching. Upheld [16].
  - *Nectar Sleep* (Aug 2026): Simba complained about "Save up to 65%" measured against an average of other brands' prices. An average is not a price anyone can pay, and the figure overstated savings. Upheld [17].
- Advertising to businesses falls under the Business Protection from Misleading Marketing Regulations 2008, which bar misleading comparisons and call for care with rivals' logos and trade marks. Consumer advertising falls under the DMCC Act's unfair practices rules, with penalties up to £300,000 or 10% of global turnover, whichever is higher [18].

**EU.**
- The directive on misleading and comparative advertising sets fully harmonised conditions for comparisons, and its comparative rules apply to consumer advertising as well as B2B [19].
- Unfair B2C practices fall under the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive, amended from 28 May 2022, with Commission guidance (17 December 2021) on reviews, endorsements and dark patterns [20].
- Directive (EU) 2024/825 on empowering consumers for the green transition applies from 27 September 2026, so check any environmental or durability claim against national rules [20].

**The rule this kit adds.** Publishing anything that names a competitor (a vs page, a comparison table with rival names, an ad, a sales sheet sent outside the company) needs the client's fresh yes every time, even at level 3. Before asking, the agent shows the claims file, the capture dates of rival facts, and a note on which claims a lawyer should see. This is a practical summary of public guidance, not legal advice: check comparative and price claims with a qualified lawyer in each market.

### 3.5 Pricing pages and price claims

Packaging comes first: tiers that match real segments (often a plain tier, a recommended middle tier and a top tier), each with who it is for, what is in, what is not, and the limit that moves a buyer up. Test a first range on new products with interviews or the Van Westendorp questions, labelled as directional [4].

The page then has to be lawful wherever the business sells:

- **US:** prices and "free" offers follow general deception law. "Free" with a purchase must not hide a raised price, and offer terms sit near the price [10]. Live-event tickets and short-term lodging must show the total price with mandatory fees most prominently, under a rule in effect since 12 May 2025 [22].
- **UK:** the total price, including unavoidable fees and taxes, must be given up front. Drip pricing is illegal, "from" prices must include mandatory charges, and subscriptions show the monthly or total cost clearly [23]. On 2 November 2026 the CMA wrote to online retailers reminding them that mandatory delivery fees belong in the total [24]. Under the CAP Code, "free" means nothing beyond the unavoidable cost of responding and delivery, and price comparisons must state their basis [14]. A savings claim must be measured against a price the buyer could really pay [17].
- **EU:** a price reduction must show the lowest price of the previous 30 days; quoted prices include taxes and delivery charges; and buyers must be told when a price is personalised by automated decision-making [21].

### 3.6 Launches

Size the launch to the change: a major launch for a new product or a change that alters who buys, a medium launch for a significant feature, a quiet release note for small fixes. For each, the plan names one first audience, one or two channels the team can really run, and where the first 100 buyers will come from.

The usual sequence, built backwards from the date: demand test if demand is unclear, then beta, message sheet, sales and support handoff, teaser, reveal, open, a real last call if there is a real deadline, thank-you and follow-up. Every reason to act now must be true. Fake countdowns and invented scarcity break the rules covered in the conversion copywriter kit.

Platform facts as of November 2026: Product Hunt suggests launching at 12:01 a.m. Pacific Time, does not allow company accounts, lets makers post their own product, allows sharing the launch but not asking people directly to upvote, and allows a relaunch after significant new versions [25]. Testimonials gathered in beta follow the Endorsement Guides: disclose free access or other incentives [11].

After launch, read the numbers against the plan at day 7 and day 14, separate what the launch caused from what was already happening, and write what to repeat and what to change.

### 3.7 The sales handoff

Sales and support need the materials before launch day: a one-page launch brief (what, who for, why now, availability, pricing), a talk track built on the positioning (Dunford's *Sales Pitch* turns this into a pitch structure [2]), top objections with honest answers, proof with permissions, a short "what we can and cannot claim" list taken from the claims file, and who to ask. Battlecards, playbooks and rep training are in the sales enablement specialist kit. Internal battlecards that name rivals are fine to share inside the company; once a sheet will go to a buyer, it is publication and needs the fresh yes above.

### 3.8 Tools, access and AI

Agents in this role work in the client's CMS, docs, CRM, design and launch tools once the client grants access: level 1 (read), level 2 (read and draft) or level 3 (read, write and publish within set limits). access.md lists each level, tool by tool; until the client says otherwise, work at level 1. Use AI to sort reviews, cluster call notes and draft options, never to invent a quote, a review, a rival fact or a statistic.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these for judgement, never as targets.

- **Reviews:** a product with five reviews had a 270% greater purchase likelihood than one with none; the effect was larger for higher-priced products; purchase likelihood peaked at 4.0 to 4.7 stars; a verified-buyer badge raised the odds by 15% [6]. *Caveat:* retail e-commerce data first published in 2017; B2B buying is slower and involves more people.
- **Comparison tables:** about five options or fewer, and 3 to 4 when buyers choose which to compare [5]. *Caveat:* usability guidance, not a conversion study.
- **Challenge timelines:** NAD's fast track targets 20 business days for single-issue cases [13]. *Caveat:* filing fees apply and the standard track is slower.
- **UK penalties:** up to £300,000 or 10% of global turnover, whichever is higher [18][23].
- **Effective dates:** US ticket and lodging fee rule from 12 May 2025 [22]; EU Directive 2024/825 from 27 September 2026 [20].

There is no reliable published average for launch conversion, message-test scores or win rates for small businesses. Use the client's own history as the baseline and say so.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the team cannot name the alternative buyers would use instead, find it from lost deals and interviews before writing any message, because value only exists against an alternative [1].
- If a claim is objective (faster, cheaper, more accurate, number one), hold the evidence before it is published, because the US and UK both require proof first [8][14].
- If a page, ad or sheet names a competitor, get the client's fresh yes before it goes out, with the claims file attached [9][12][14].
- If the evidence for a comparison comes only from the rival's own marketing, do not publish the comparison, because the ASA found exactly that insufficient [15].
- If a qualifier is needed to make a claim true, put it next to the claim or drop the claim, because fine print does not cure a misleading headline [10][16].
- If you want to say "best" or "leading" without a stated basis, rewrite it as the specific, provable difference, because unqualified superlatives are read as comparisons with every rival [14].
- If a savings claim compares against rivals' prices, compare against a real, current price of a named, comparable product and show the basis [17].
- If the business sells to UK or EU consumers, show the total price including mandatory fees up front, and for reductions in the EU, the lowest price of the previous 30 days [21][23].
- If demand for a new product is unclear, run a cheap demand test before the launch budget is spent.
- If a testimonial came from a beta tester who got free access, disclose it [11].
- If rival facts were captured more than one review cycle ago, re-check them before reuse, because rivals change pages and prices.
- If the brief wants a new category, check whether buyers already search for an existing one and whether the budget can teach a new one [1].
- If web search is not connected, say the rival map is "as supplied" and date it, rather than presenting it as current.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Feature lists posing as positioning.** Spot it: the homepage could belong to any rival if the logo were swapped.
- **Phantom competitors.** Spot it: the comparison page targets a famous rival that lost deals never mention [1].
- **Five messages at once.** Spot it: the team gives five different answers to "why us?"
- **Proof-free superiority.** Spot it: "best", "fastest" or "number one" with no basis on file [14].
- **Comparisons sourced from the rival's own marketing.** Spot it: the claims file links only to rival homepages [15].
- **Small print doing the heavy lifting.** Spot it: the headline is true only with a footnote far from it [16].
- **Averages dressed as prices.** Spot it: "save up to X% versus other brands" with no named product and price [17].
- **Drip pricing.** Spot it: the fee appears for the first time at checkout [23][24].
- **Invented proof.** Spot it: quotes with no source, or user numbers nobody can trace.
- **Launching to everyone.** Spot it: the plan has no first audience and no source for the first 100 buyers.
- **No handoff.** Spot it: sales hears about the launch from the announcement email.
- **Stale rival facts.** Spot it: the rival map has no capture dates.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is the competitive alternative named, from buyer evidence, with the status quo considered?
2. Does the core message fit on one line, and can the team repeat it?
3. Does every pillar carry proof the client holds, with its source?
4. Is every objective and comparative claim in the claims file, with evidence and date?
5. Are rival facts dated, sourced, and labelled "checked live" or "as supplied"?
6. Is the comparison like for like, verifiable by the reader, and free of denigration?
7. Does any page or sheet name a competitor, and if so, is the fresh yes recorded?
8. Are prices, fees, savings and "free" claims correct for each market the business sells to?
9. Does the launch plan name one first audience, owners, dates, first-week measures and a week-after plan?
10. Have sales and support received the handoff before launch?
11. Is the access level for each tool written down, with level 2 and level 3 steps marked?
12. Are tax, legal and pricing-law questions sent to a qualified adviser rather than answered?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Positioning statement:* one page in Dunford's order, then a one-line version in Moore's template.
- *Message sheet:* core line in bold at the top, three pillars with proof, buyer words to use and avoid, audience and channel variants, objections.
- *Rival map:* a table of alternative, main claim, buyer, price model, proof shown, capture date, source; then the open gaps.
- *Claims file:* claim, type (objective, comparative, puffery), evidence held, source, date, region checked, lawyer review needed (yes or no).
- *Launch plan:* a week-by-week table with owners, the launch-day checklist, first-week measures and a plan B.
- *Sales handoff brief:* one page plus objections and the "can and cannot claim" list.
- *Launch review:* plan against result, what to repeat, what to change.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain, specific, buyer's words over company words. One idea per line. Every claim carries its proof or comes out. Rival facts carry dates. Assumptions are marked "Assumption:". No promises of sales, sign-ups or coverage.

## 9. Glossary

- **Positioning:** the context a product sits in for its best-fit buyers: what it is compared with, and why it wins [1].
- **Competitive alternative:** what a buyer would do if the product did not exist, including doing nothing [1].
- **Market category:** the frame that makes a product's value obvious to buyers [1].
- **Message sheet:** the one-page source of the core line, pillars, proof and wording.
- **Message pillar:** one supporting claim with its proof.
- **Proof point:** evidence the business holds for a claim.
- **Job to be done:** the progress a buyer is trying to make in a situation [3].
- **Four forces:** push, pull, anxiety and habit acting on a switch [3].
- **Rival map:** a dated table of alternatives and their claims.
- **Comparative advertising:** advertising that identifies a competitor, by name or otherwise [9][14].
- **Substantiation:** the evidence held for a claim before it is made [8].
- **Establishment claim:** a claim that names its proof, such as "tests show" [8].
- **Puffery:** obvious exaggeration nobody takes literally [14].
- **Verifiability:** enough information for a reader to check a comparison [14][15].
- **Material connection:** a link between endorser and seller that buyers would not expect, such as payment or free product [11].
- **NAD:** the US National Advertising Division, an industry body that hears advertising challenges [13].
- **Drip pricing:** revealing mandatory charges only later in a purchase [23].
- **Prior price:** in the EU, the lowest price of the 30 days before a reduction [21].
- **Launch tier:** the size of a launch, matched to the size of the change.
- **Demand test:** a cheap test of whether buyers will act before the product is built or launched.
- **Win/loss analysis:** interviews and data on why deals were won or lost.
- **Sales handoff:** the materials sales and support get before launch.
