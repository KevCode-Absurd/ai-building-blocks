# Product launch plan

## When to use

- A product, feature, plan or relaunch has a date, or needs one.
- The team wants a "big launch" but nobody knows where buyers will come from.
- Demand is unclear and the budget is small.
- After launch, to read results against the plan.

## What you need

1. What is launching, the problem it solves, the price, and the buyer.
2. The positioning statement and message sheet (skill `product-positioning-and-message-sheet`), or enough to write a one-line positioning first.
3. Audience sizes you control: email list, customers, social followers, partners, community.
4. The date, the budget, and who can do the work each week.
5. Past launch numbers, if any.
6. The markets and whether buyers are consumers, businesses or both.

**Access:** level 1 to plan. Level 2 to build drafts: unsent emails, unpublished pages and posts, a paused or scheduled campaign, a launch hub page. Level 3 to publish and send on launch days within the limits you set (which lists, which channels, which pages, budget cap). Posting from accounts tied to one person, such as your Product Hunt maker profile, stays with that person.

## Steps

### 1. Size the launch

- **Major:** new product, or a change to who buys or what it costs. Full plan.
- **Medium:** a significant feature for existing buyers. Email, in-product, blog, sales handoff.
- **Minor:** fixes and small improvements. Release note.
Match effort to size; a small team cannot run a major launch every month.

### 2. Check demand if unsure

If nobody has paid or pre-ordered yet, run a one-week test first (prompt `demand-test-design`): a simple page, one message, one action that costs the buyer something (a deposit, a booked call, a waitlist with a real question), and one number that means go. Taking money for something not yet available has rules; see the market research analyst kit and ask a qualified adviser before charging.

### 3. Write the one-line positioning

"For [who] who [problem], [product] is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [alternative]." Everything else hangs on it.

### 4. Pick one audience and the first 100

Choose the audience most likely to buy first, usually existing customers or the list. Name where the first 100 buyers will come from, with the numbers: "list of 3,200 at a 30% open rate gives about 960 readers". Label every rate as the client's own history or an assumption.

### 5. Plan a beta if it helps

Ten to thirty testers who match the best-fit buyer, a feedback form, a weekly check-in, and a written rule for when to launch ("8 of 10 testers complete the core task unaided"). Beta testers who got free access and later give testimonials must say so.

### 6. Build the calendar backwards

From launch day: teaser, reveal, open, real last call (only if a real deadline exists), thank-you, follow-up, and the week after. Each row: day (relative to launch day, such as "Day -7", unless the brief gives dates), channel, audience, message, owner, asset, status, access level needed. Add the launch-day checklist: page live, links tested, tracking firing, pricing correct for each market, support briefed, rollback plan.

Standard safety gates are part of the plan by default, never an option or an extra: for a release to existing users, a staged rollout (a small share first, widened when error and support numbers hold), a way to undo, and a go or no-go call with its rule. Any work the plan adds names who does it: if the launch will raise support tickets, say who covers them, on which days and hours.

### 7. Write the messages

Announcement email, two to four social posts, page outline, partner note, in-product note, sales email. One voice, one core line. Every reason to act now is true: a real date, a real limit, a real price change. No fake countdowns, no invented scarcity, no made-up testimonials or user numbers. Anything naming a competitor goes through `competitor-comparison-and-claims-check` and needs a fresh yes.

### 8. Hand off to sales and support

One-page launch brief, talk track, top objections with answers, the "can and cannot claim" list, FAQ, and who to ask. Deliver at least a few working days before launch (prompt `sales-launch-handoff-brief`).

### 9. Set measures and plan B

Three first-week numbers tied to the goal (for example sign-ups, activations, paid orders), each with a target the client can justify from past results or a stated assumption. Plan B: what changes if results are below half the target by day 4 (a second message, a different audience, a direct outreach push).

### 10. Go live

At level 2, the client publishes from the drafts. At level 3, show the action list and limits, get one yes, then publish and send within limits and log each action. Stop and ask on errors, complaints or policy warnings.

### 11. Review at day 7 and day 14

Plan against result for each measure; what the launch caused versus what was already happening (compare with the weeks before); feedback themes; what to repeat, change and stop. Write it as a dated decision log.

## Checks

- Launch size matches the change.
- Demand evidence exists, or a test is planned first.
- One first audience, with the first-100 sum shown.
- Every task has an owner and a day; every live step shows its access level.
- A staged rollout, an undo plan and a go or no-go rule are in the plan as defaults; added work, such as support cover, names who does it.
- Every urgency claim is true; no invented proof.
- Pricing and fees display correctly in each market.
- Sales and support are briefed before launch.
- Measures, targets and plan B are written before launch.

## Output format

1. One-line positioning and launch size.
2. Demand test (if needed) with go and no-go numbers.
3. Calendar table: day, step, channel, audience, message, owner, access level, status.
4. Message pack.
5. Launch-day checklist, with the rollout stages, undo plan, go or no-go rule and support cover.
6. Measures, targets and plan B.
7. Review template for day 7 and day 14.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a printable planner shop launching a $29 freelancer planner, list of 3,200, Instagram 9,000 followers, six weeks out. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.

> **Size:** medium. **One line:** For freelancers who lose track of invoices, the [Business] Freelancer Planner is a printable planner that puts work, invoices and tax dates on one page each week, unlike a general diary.
>
> **First 100:** list 3,200 x 32% open (own last 5 sends) x 10% click x 30% buy, about 31 buyers; existing customers' early-access email, about 40 (assumption: half last year's repeat rate); Instagram, the rest. Gap of about 30: add a partner newsletter swap.
>
> | Week | Step | Owner | Access |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | -4 | Beta: 15 freelancers, form, launch rule "12 of 15 use it 2 weeks running" | Owner | L1 |
> | -2 | Teaser email and post drafts | Agent | L2 |
> | 0 | Open: email, posts, page live | Agent | L3, within list and channel limits |
> | +1 | Real last call: launch price ends Friday 23:59 | Agent | L3 |
> | +2 | Review | Agent | L1 |
>
> **Plan B:** if under 50 sales by day 4, send the beta stories (with disclosure of free copies) to non-openers.
