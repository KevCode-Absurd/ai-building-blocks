# Product positioning and message sheet

## When to use

- The homepage or deck could belong to any rival if the logo were swapped.
- A new product or major feature needs its first message before launch.
- Sales, support and marketing each describe the product differently.
- Deals keep going to one alternative, often "do nothing" or a spreadsheet.
- A refresh: rivals have moved, or the best customers have changed.

Brand-level positioning, naming and voice belong to the brand strategist kit. Page copy built from this sheet belongs to the conversion copywriter kit.

## What you need

1. Twenty or more customer reviews (yours, and rivals' if available).
2. Notes or transcripts from five or more recent sales or onboarding calls.
3. Reasons for the last ten lost and ten won deals, even rough ones.
4. Three to five switching interviews with recent buyers, if possible (questions in the prompt `positioning-from-reviews-and-calls`).
5. The current homepage, deck and pricing page.
6. Rival pages: read live if web search is connected, with the date and URL of each; otherwise pasted by the client and marked "as supplied on [date]".
7. What the business can prove: usage data, test results, named customers who agreed to be quoted.

**Access:** level 1 is enough (read exports, docs, CRM notes). Level 2 lets me save the message sheet as a draft in your docs or wiki; level 3 lets me update the live message sheet or sales hub within the folders you name. Nothing here publishes a claim about a competitor.

## Steps

### 1. Sort the raw material

Read reviews before the website. Pull every sentence where a buyer says what they used before, what went wrong, what made them switch, and what they would miss. Tag each with its source. Underline words buyers use that the company's pages never do.

### 2. Name the competitive alternatives

From lost deals, interviews and reviews, list what buyers would do if the product did not exist. Include the status quo. Mark any "phantom competitor" the team fears but buyers never mention. Rank alternatives by how often they appear in real deals.

### 3. List the unique capabilities

For each top alternative, list what this product does that it does not. Strike anything most rivals also claim; shared claims are weak ground. Keep only capabilities the client can show.

### 4. Turn capabilities into value

For each capability ask "so what?" until the answer is in buyer terms: hours saved, money kept, risk avoided, a job finished. Attach proof to each: a number from the client's data with its date range, a quote with permission, a test with method. No proof means it becomes a hypothesis to test, not a pillar.

### 5. Find the best-fit buyers

Describe who cares most about that value by situation and trigger ("owner-run firms whose bookkeeper just quit"), not by age or job title alone. Check the description against the best current customers.

### 6. Choose the market frame

Pick the category that makes the value obvious. Prefer one buyers already search for and budget under. Consider a sub-segment of it ("for X") before a new category; a new category needs budget and time to teach.

### 7. Write the positioning statement

One page in the order above. Then one line in Moore's template: "For [best-fit buyer] who [trigger or need], [product] is a [category] that [main value]. Unlike [main alternative], it [key difference]."

### 8. Build the message sheet

- **Core line**: one sentence, in buyer words.
- **Three pillars**: claim plus proof plus source for each.
- **Why now**: the trigger that makes the problem urgent.
- **Objections**: the top five, each with an honest answer and proof.
- **Words to use and avoid**: buyer phrases in; jargon, empty superlatives ("best-in-class", "world-class") and unprovable claims out.
- **Audience variants**: what leads for the economic buyer, the daily user and the technical reviewer.
- **Channel notes**: homepage, ads, sales email, in-product.

### 9. Run the claims screen

Every objective claim ("faster", "cheaper", "number one") goes into the claims file with its evidence before use. Any line that names a rival is handled by the skill `competitor-comparison-and-claims-check` and needs the client's fresh yes before publication.

### 10. Plan a light test

Give three questions the client can ask five customers to see whether the core line lands, or set up a five-second or preference test. Report results as signals, not proof.

## Checks

- The alternative is named from buyer evidence, not from the team's fears.
- Every pillar has proof the client holds, with a source and date.
- The core line fits on one line and uses words buyers use.
- Rival facts carry a date and "checked live" or "as supplied".
- No invented quotes, numbers or reviews.
- Superlatives are either backed or removed.
- The statement says who it is *not* for.
- Access level and any level 2 or 3 steps are written down.

## Output format

1. Positioning statement (one page, five parts in order).
2. One-line statement.
3. Message sheet table: core line; pillars with proof and source; why now; objections and answers; words to use and avoid; audience variants.
4. Evidence log: quote or fact, source, date.
5. Open questions and the test plan.

## Example of good output (short)

Made-up business for illustration: Tallyfold, bookkeeping software for UK sole traders who sell on craft marketplaces.

> **Alternatives (from 14 lost deals and 30 reviews):** a spreadsheet (9 deals), a general accounting app (4), an accountant doing everything at year end (1).
>
> **One line:** For marketplace sellers who dread January, Tallyfold is bookkeeping software that matches every marketplace payout to its orders and fees automatically. Unlike a spreadsheet, it is done when the payout lands.
>
> | Pillar | Proof (client data) | Source |
> |---|---|---|
> | Payouts match themselves | 94% of payouts matched with no edits, Jan to Sep 2026 | Product logs, 1,210 accounts |
> | Fees are never missed | Fee lines pulled from marketplace exports, not typed | Feature demo; method note |
> | Year end is a download | "I sent my accountant one file." (review, with permission) | Review #212 |
>
> **Words to use:** payout, fees, January, one file. **Avoid:** "all-in-one", "best", "revolutionary".
>
> **Not for:** sellers with staff or stock in a warehouse; they need full accounting software.
