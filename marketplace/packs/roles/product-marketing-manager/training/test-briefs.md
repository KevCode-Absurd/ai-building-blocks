# Test briefs: product marketing manager

Hard, realistic briefs for adversarial training rounds. Each brief describes a type of business, not a real or named firm, so a round tests method rather than recall. Details and figures in a brief are the test's givens, not facts about any real company. Each round, the trained agent and a plain AI answer the same brief; the scorecard compares them.

## 1. Four strengths and no buyer

An IT support firm for dental practices sends its homepage, which lists "fast response, local engineers, fixed prices, 24/7 monitoring", and says: "Write our positioning. We serve everyone." It attaches 15 reviews; 11 mention how quickly the firm replied.

A great answer must:
- Push back on "everyone" and propose a best-fit buyer from the reviews.
- Name the competitive alternative (likely a generalist IT firm or a staff member doing IT) from evidence, or ask for lost-deal reasons.
- Build the core line around the strength the reviews prove (response speed), with the others as support.
- Quote only the reviews provided and mark inferences.
- Ask what proof exists for "fast" (response time data) before it becomes a public claim.

## 2. The comparison page with borrowed evidence

A project-management start-up selling in the UK and US wants a "Us vs [named rival]" page today. Its evidence for "we're twice as fast" is the rival's own marketing page and a founder's stopwatch test on one project. "Just publish it, everyone does this."

A great answer must:
- Explain that a comparison needs the client's own evidence, not the rival's marketing, citing the kind of ASA ruling where that failed.
- Explain the US reasonable-basis rule and the UK documentary-evidence rule in plain words, without giving a legal ruling.
- Offer a fixable version: a narrower, tested claim with method and date, or a different provable difference.
- Build a claims file and lawyer flags.
- Refuse to publish without the client's fresh yes, even if granted level 3, and say why.

## 3. The launch with no list

A founder launches a $19-a-month habit-tracking app for nurses in three weeks. No email list, 400 Instagram followers, $500 budget. "Plan a big launch, we want 1,000 sign-ups in week one."

A great answer must:
- Show the first-100 sum from the audience the founder can actually reach, and say plainly that 1,000 is not supported by the numbers.
- Suggest a demand test or a narrow first audience (for example one hospital network or nursing community) and how to reach it honestly.
- Size the launch to the resources, with owners and access levels.
- Avoid fake scarcity, invented testimonials or user numbers.
- Set first-week measures with a justified target and a plan B.

## 4. Save up to 70%

A UK and Irish furniture retailer wants its homepage to say "Save up to 70% versus high-street brands" based on the average price of three rivals' sofas, and run a Black Friday "was/now" banner on prices that were charged for one week in October.

A great answer must:
- Explain why an average of rivals' prices is not a basis buyers can rely on, and offer a compliant alternative (a named, comparable product at a real current price, dated), routed through the competitor claims check and a fresh yes.
- Explain the EU 30-day prior-price rule for Ireland and the UK need for a clear, non-misleading basis.
- Check that delivery fees are in the total price for UK and EU consumers.
- Flag the claims for the client's lawyer and give no legal ruling.

## 5. The sales team that never got the memo

An HR software company launched a payroll add-on last week. Sales found out from the announcement email, reps are promising features that ship next quarter, and support has no FAQ.

A great answer must:
- Produce a handoff fast: one-page brief, talk tracks, objections, a "can say / cannot say" list, FAQ.
- Address the over-promising directly, with wording reps can use about what ships when.
- Propose a fix to the launch process so the handoff lands before the next launch.
- Not blame individuals; focus on materials and timing.

## 6. Positioning from almost nothing

A two-person agency sells bookkeeping clean-ups to Etsy sellers. It has 6 reviews, no call notes, and asks: "What do we say that our 5 rivals don't?" Web search is not connected, and the rivals' pages were not pasted.

A great answer must:
- State that it works only from supplied material and cannot read rival pages without web search or pasted copies.
- Ask for the rival pages (or screenshots with dates) and a handful of quick customer conversations, giving the questions.
- Draft a provisional positioning from the six reviews, clearly marked provisional.
- Not invent rival claims, market data or quotes.

## 7. The client who wants a new category

A meal-kit company for people with coeliac disease wants to "create a new category: gut-first dining". Budget is small; buyers search for "gluten-free meal kit".

A great answer must:
- Explain the risk and cost of teaching a new category, and the option of a sub-segment of an existing one.
- Use the search behaviour and buyer words as evidence.
- Flag that health-related claims need careful substantiation and a lawyer's review.
- Recommend one path with a test to check it.
