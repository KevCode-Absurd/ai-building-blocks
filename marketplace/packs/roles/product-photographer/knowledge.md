# Product photographer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Marketplace image rules and AI rules change often; each fact says when it was true. Copyright, model releases and AI disclosure are covered as general practice, not legal advice; the client checks those with a qualified adviser.

**What this kit leaves to other kits.** The graphic designer kit covers social graphic sizes and safe zones (Instagram, Stories, Pinterest), contrast, text on images and print files. The Amazon marketplace manager kit covers listing copy, A+ Content and Amazon's testing tool. The Shopify specialist kit covers how images load on a store. Load those kits when a job turns on them.

## 1. What this role is for

A seller hires a product photographer when the photos are holding sales back: grey, yellow or dull images, a catalogue that looks like ten people shot it, listings rejected by a marketplace, or buyers asking questions the photos should answer. Small sellers often take the photos themselves on a phone; bigger ones hire a freelancer for a shoot day. Either way, they need someone to decide what to shoot, how it should look, and what each channel accepts.

**Who hires it.** Shopify, Etsy and Amazon sellers shooting at home; brands launching a new range who need a full image set from one shoot day; shops whose freelancer and in-house photos do not match; sellers starting to use AI tools on their product photos.

**The work.**

- **Plan:** where every image will be used, the shot list (each shot with one job), the brief a photographer can quote from, and the day schedule.
- **Set the look:** a style guide (light, background, colour, angles, props, crop) and a home or studio set-up that repeats it.
- **Edit:** an edit recipe that keeps colour true and looks the same across hundreds of products.
- **Spec:** sizes, ratios, background rules, file names and alt text per channel.
- **Check:** every image is true to the product, meets each channel's rules, keeps any AI marking, and has clean rights.

**What good work looks like.**

- The image set answers the buyer's questions: what it is, how big, what it is made of, what is inside, how it is used, what comes in the box, each colour.
- Colour in the photo matches the product in the hand.
- Main images pass each marketplace's rules the first time.
- The whole catalogue looks like one shop: same light, angle family, background and crop.
- AI tools are used where they help (cleaning a background, extending a canvas) and never to change what the product looks like; AI-made images keep their markings.
- The client owns or has a written licence for every image, and every person shown signed a release.

**How it is judged.** Listings approved without image errors; fewer "not as pictured" returns and questions about size or colour; a consistent catalogue; and results of tests on the store's busiest products.

**What an AI agent delivers.** It does not hold a camera. It writes the shot list, brief, schedule, style guide, set-up, edit recipe and channel spec; checks images the client shares; and with level 2 or 3 access organises, renames, adds alt text and uploads images inside the client's tools.

## 2. The canon

**Fil Hunter, Steven Biver, Paul Fuqua and Robin Reid, *Light: Science & Magic* (6th edition, Routledge, 2021) [25].** The standard text on photographic lighting. It explains light as reflection: diffuse and direct reflection, the "family of angles" from which a light source reflects straight back to the lens, and how to light surfaces, metal, glass, liquids, white-on-white and black-on-black subjects [25]. The working lesson: with shiny products, you control what the product reflects, not just how bright it is. Move the light or a large white or black card, and the surface changes.

**Jakob Nielsen, Nielsen Norman Group, "Photos as Web Content" (2010, reviewed 2026) [21].** Eye-tracking showed that people study photos that carry information, such as product photos, and ignore decorative stock imagery. Users ask for bigger product photos and study them; Nielsen advises that an enlarged view be at least twice the size of the original [21].

**Katie Sherwin, Nielsen Norman Group, *UX Guidelines for Ecommerce Product Pages* (2019) [22].** One product view rarely answers buyers' questions; shoppers value several views, close-up detail and the product in use, and an enlarged view is a must-have [22].

**Baymard Institute product page research (2017 and 2020) [23][24].** Large usability studies of product pages. Many shoppers try to judge size from the photos, and many first explore the images when they land on a product page; sites that lack scale shots or enough resolution lose buyers to doubt [23][24]. Numbers are in section 4.

**W3C, Images Tutorial (updated April 2026) [19].** How to write alt text: a short description of what an informative image shows; empty alt text for decoration; and the words themselves for an image of text [19].

**Provenance standards.** IPTC's Digital Source Type vocabulary names how an image was made, from a plain camera capture to "edited using generative AI" [10]. The C2PA standard, Content Credentials, records the origin and edits of a file [11]. Marketplaces now read these marks.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Plan from the channels backwards

List every place an image will be used before writing a single shot: store product page, marketplaces, Google Shopping, ads, social, email. Each has its own rules on size, background and what may appear in the frame. A shot that suits none of them does not make the list.

### Channel image rules (as of Nov 2026)

| | Main image | Size | Other rules | AI rules |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Amazon** [1] | Pure white background (RGB 255,255,255); the product fills 85% or more of the frame; the actual product, no illustrations; no props not included | At least 1,000 pixels on the longest side, for zoom | JPEG, TIFF or PNG; RGB or CMYK; file name includes the product identifier; no added text, graphics or watermarks | Nothing found in the public guidance I could open |
| **Shopify** [2][3] | Your store's choice; keep one aspect ratio across collection pages | Up to 5000 x 5000 pixels or 25 megapixels, under 20 MB; 2048 x 2048 suggested for square images | PNG suggested, then JPEG; WebP, HEIC and others accepted | Built-in AI editing marks generated images with an invisible watermark [3] |
| **Google Merchant Center** [5][6][7] | A clean, unobstructed view of the exact item; product fills 75% to 90% of the image; one variant per image | At least 500 x 500 pixels for all products from 31 January 2027; 1500 x 1500 or more recommended; up to 64 megapixels and 16 MB | No promotional overlays, watermarks, added logos, borders or placeholders; up to 10 additional images; up to 5 lifestyle images at 600 x 600 or more | Generative AI images must keep metadata marking them as AI-made, such as IPTC DigitalSourceType [5][6][7] |
| **Etsy** [9] | First photo landscape or square, at least 635 pixels each side | At least 2000 pixels each side recommended | Later photos match the first photo's shape | At least one unedited photo of the actual item on a real background; edits may not hide flaws [9] |

The Amazon row comes from a post by an Amazon staff account on Amazon's seller forum, undated (about late 2025), because Seller Central's help pages need a login [1]. Re-check it at every refresh.

Google's Merchant Center can try to remove promotional text, watermarks and logos from flagged images if the seller switches on automatic image improvements; it is off by default [8]. It is better to upload clean images in the first place.

### The image set

A standard set for one product, each shot with one job:

1. **Main image:** the product alone, as each channel's main-image rule requires.
2. **Angles:** front, back, side, top, as the product needs.
3. **Scale:** in a hand, on a person, or next to a familiar object. Baymard found many shoppers judge size from images, and that cut-out images alone lead to wrong conclusions about size [23].
4. **Detail:** texture, stitching, ports, ingredients, finish, at enough resolution to zoom [24].
5. **In use:** the product doing its job, in a real setting [22].
6. **What's in the box** and **packaging**, where buyers ask.
7. **Variants:** each colour or size, one per image for Google [5].
8. **Lifestyle:** context and mood, kept to the slots that allow it (for Google, the lifestyle image attribute) [7].

Read the client's reviews, returns and pre-sale questions first. A question that keeps coming up ("does it fit an A4 folder?") is a missing shot.

### Light, colour and consistency

- **Light first, kit second.** One large, soft light (a window or a diffused lamp) and a white card to fill shadows solves most home set-ups. Shiny products need large, even sources and black or white cards to shape reflections [25].
- **Lock the set-up.** Same light position, distances, camera height, lens and settings for every product in a range, written down so it can be rebuilt next month.
- **Lock focus and exposure** on the phone or camera, and set white balance once with a grey card in the first frame of each session.
- **Colour space.** Export in sRGB for web and marketplaces unless a channel asks otherwise. Browsers treat images with no colour profile as sRGB, so an image saved in a wider space without its profile shifts colour on screen [20].
- **Check against the product.** Hold the product next to the screen in daylight. If the photo and the product disagree, fix the photo, not the description.

### Editing and retouching: where the line is

- **Fine:** removing dust and lint, cleaning or replacing a plain background, straightening, cropping, correcting colour to match the real product, combining exposures for even light.
- **Not fine:** changing colour, shape, size, texture or finish; hiding flaws or damage; adding parts, accessories or results the buyer will not get. Etsy says this outright [9].
- **The law looks at the whole picture.** The FTC judges an ad by its overall message, including pictures, and expects proof for implied claims [13]. UK guidance under the DMCC Act judges whether the overall presentation misleads [14]. EU law forbids misleading by action or omission [15]. An edited photo that makes a product look better than it is can be a misleading claim. This is general guidance, not legal advice.

### AI-edited and AI-generated product images (as of Nov 2026)

- **Google Merchant Center** requires images made with generative AI to keep metadata that marks them as AI-generated, such as the IPTC DigitalSourceType tag, and says not to strip it, including from images made with its own Product Studio tool [5][6][7].
- **Shopify's** file editor can replace a background with a colour or transparency, change a background, setting, lighting or style from a text prompt, and extend a canvas. Output is about 1 megapixel, below Shopify's own suggested 2048 pixels, and generated images carry an invisible watermark that cannot be removed. Shopify tells sellers to review anything an AI tool creates or changes [3][4].
- **Etsy** allows AI tools for lighting, colour, cropping, resizing and backgrounds, but every listing needs at least one unedited photo of the actual item on a real background, edits may not hide flaws, and Etsy suggests noting edits in the description [9].
- **Amazon's** guidance I could open asks for the actual product with no illustrations in the main image and says nothing about AI [1]. Until Amazon says more, use only real photographs as main images.
- **The marks.** IPTC's vocabulary separates "digital capture" (a camera), "edited using generative AI" (for example a generated background), "created using generative AI" (fully generated) and "algorithmically enhanced" (sharpening or noise reduction that does not change content) [10]. Keep these tags when exporting; some export settings strip metadata. C2PA Content Credentials can record the edit history; its steering committee includes Adobe, Amazon, Google and Meta [11].
- **EU AI Act, Article 50**, applying from 2 August 2026: providers of AI image tools must mark output as AI-made in a machine-readable way (with an exception for assistive standard editing that does not substantially change the input), and anyone publishing a deep fake (content that resembles real people, objects or places and would falsely appear authentic) must disclose it [12]. A realistic AI scene around a real product may need a label in the EU; the client checks with an adviser.
- **Ownership.** The US Copyright Office concluded in January 2025 that prompts alone do not make an AI output protectable, while human creative arrangement or modification can [16]. A fully generated product scene may be something the client cannot own.

### Rights and releases (general practice, not legal advice)

- **Commissioned photos (US).** A freelancer's work is a "work made for hire" only in narrow cases: an employee in their job, or a work in one of nine listed categories with a written agreement signed by both sides [17]. Otherwise, write down who owns the photos or what licence the client gets.
- **UK.** An owner can license use or sell (assign) the copyright; a sale needs a signed document [18].
- **People and places.** Anyone recognisable in a photo signs a model release; private locations need permission. Props with other brands' logos are avoided.

### Delivery

- **File names:** one pattern per catalogue, such as SKU, shot type and number. Amazon wants the product identifier in the file name [1].
- **Alt text:** a short description of what each informative image shows, written for a person who cannot see it [19].
- **Masters and exports:** keep the full-size, unedited masters; export sized copies per channel.
- **For Google:** a changed image needs a new URL; re-using the same URL can take weeks to update [7].

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to frame expectations. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Shoppers look at images first:** 56% of users' first action on a product page was to explore the images (Baymard, 2020) [24]. *Caveat:* lab study on large retail sites.
- **Size from images:** 42% of users tried to judge a product's size from its images, and 28% of 60 large sites tested had no in-scale images (Baymard, 2017) [23]. *Caveat:* best-selling products on big sites, nine years ago.
- **Image gaps:** 25% of sites had images that did not support visual exploration; 14% had low-resolution images; 11% lacked enough zoom (Baymard, 2020) [24]. *Caveat:* benchmark of large sites.
- **Enlarged views:** at least twice the size of the original image (NN/g) [21]. *Caveat:* advice from 2010, reviewed in 2026.
- **Platform numbers (checked 6 Nov 2026):** Amazon 1,000 pixels on the longest side and 85% fill [1]; Shopify up to 5000 x 5000 pixels, 2048 x 2048 suggested [2]; Shopify AI output about 1 megapixel [3]; Google 500 x 500 minimum from 31 January 2027, 1500 x 1500 recommended, 75% to 90% fill [5]; Etsy 2000 pixels recommended [9]. *Caveat:* platforms change these without notice.

I found no trustworthy published figure for how much better photos raise sales for a small shop. Test on the shop's busiest products and compare like with like.

## 5. Decision rules

- If you do not know where the images will be used, ask before writing the shot list, because each channel's rules decide what to shoot [1][2][5][9].
- If a channel publishes an image rule, follow it over any general rule here, because their systems decide what is approved.
- If an image will be a marketplace main image, use a real photograph of the actual product, not a render or AI scene, because Amazon asks for the actual product and Etsy needs at least one unedited photo [1][9].
- If AI was used to make or change an image, keep its metadata and C2PA data on export, because Google requires the AI marking to stay [5][10][11].
- If an edit would change the product's colour, size, shape or finish, or hide a flaw, do not make it, because it can mislead buyers and break platform rules [9][13][14].
- If buyers ask about size, add a scale shot before anything else, because many shoppers judge size from images [23].
- If an image will be zoomed, shoot and export at enough resolution for an enlarged view at least twice the displayed size [21][24].
- If the product is shiny or clear, plan the light around reflections, with large sources and cards, because that is where most home set-ups fail [25].
- If a Shopify AI edit is used for a main product image, check its output size against the channel's minimum and suggested sizes, because the tool outputs about 1 megapixel [2][3].
- If a person, a private place or another brand appears, get a signed release or permission first, or leave it out.
- If a freelancer is shooting, agree in writing who owns the photos or what licence the client gets, before the shoot [17][18].
- If a step would upload, replace or publish images, check the access level first: draft at level 2, and change live listings only with level 3 access and within the client's limits.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Grey or yellow photos.** Spot it: white backgrounds that look grey or cream; mixed daylight and warm bulbs. Fix white balance with a grey card and one light source.
- **Colour that isn't true.** Spot it: "colour not as pictured" in returns or reviews; saturation pushed in the edit.
- **Off-white or busy main images.** Spot it: a cream backdrop, props, or a product filling a small part of the frame on a marketplace main image [1][5].
- **Text badges on main images.** Spot it: "Best seller" or "Free shipping" written on a Google or Amazon image [1][5].
- **No scale shot.** Spot it: questions about size, or returns for "smaller than expected" [23].
- **Ten different looks.** Spot it: a collection page where angles, light and crop change from product to product.
- **AI changing the product.** Spot it: a generated scene where the product's shape, label or colour shifted, or parts float [3].
- **Stripped AI metadata.** Spot it: an export preset that removes all metadata from AI-edited images [5][10].
- **Low-resolution zoom.** Spot it: images that blur when enlarged on a phone [24].
- **Rights left open.** Spot it: no written agreement with the photographer; models without releases [17].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Is there a channel list with each channel's current image rules, dated?
2. Does every shot have one job and at least one channel that needs it?
3. Does the set answer the top buyer questions: size, detail, in use, contents, each variant?
4. Does colour match the product in the hand, checked in daylight?
5. Does each main image meet its marketplace's rules: background, fill, no text, real product [1][5][9]?
6. Are sizes and formats right for each channel, with masters kept [2][5]?
7. Are AI edits limited to background, light and canvas, with the product unchanged and metadata kept [3][5][10]?
8. Does the catalogue look consistent: same light, angle family, crop and background?
9. Are file names, alt text and gallery order done [1][19]?
10. Are ownership, licences and releases in writing [17][18]?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Channel spec sheet:* each channel's sizes, ratios, background and AI rules, with the date checked and the source.
- *Shot list:* a table of product, shot, job, channel, background, props, crop and notes.
- *Photographer brief:* goal, look in plain words, light, colour accuracy, must-have shots, file specs, delivery date and rights terms.
- *Style guide:* light, background, colour, angles, props and crop, with do and don't pairs.
- *Set-up plan:* the set described from where the person stands, with distances and settings as numbers.
- *Edit recipe:* the editing steps and values in order, saved as a preset.
- *Image check:* pass or fix per image, with the rule each check comes from.

**Tone and conventions.** Plain, calm and exact. Every setting is a number with a unit, never "a bit brighter". Set-ups are described from the person's position ("window on your left"). Every platform rule carries its source and date. Suggestions are labelled as suggestions.

## 9. Glossary

- **Main image:** the first image a marketplace shows in search and on the listing.
- **Fill:** the share of the frame the product takes up [1][5].
- **Pure white:** RGB 255,255,255, the background Amazon asks for on main images [1].
- **Scale shot:** an image that shows size against a hand, person or familiar object [23].
- **Lifestyle image:** the product in a real-world setting [7].
- **Promotional overlay:** text, badges, prices or logos added on top of a product image [5].
- **Diffuse reflection / direct reflection:** light scattered evenly by a surface, versus a mirror-like reflection of the light source [25].
- **Family of angles:** the angles from which a light source will reflect directly into the lens [25].
- **White balance:** the camera setting that makes white look white under a given light.
- **Grey card:** a neutral card photographed to set white balance and exposure.
- **sRGB:** the standard colour space browsers assume for images with no colour profile [20].
- **Colour profile:** data in an image file that says which colour space it uses [20].
- **Master file:** the full-size, unedited original kept for future exports.
- **Alt text:** a text alternative that tells people who cannot see an image what it shows [19].
- **IPTC Digital Source Type:** a metadata field that records how an image was made, including by generative AI [10].
- **Content Credentials (C2PA):** a standard for recording a file's origin and edit history [11].
- **Deep fake (EU AI Act):** AI-generated or altered content resembling real people, objects or places that would falsely appear authentic [12].
- **Work made for hire:** US term for work owned by the employer or commissioning party from the start, in narrow cases [17].
- **Model release:** a signed permission from a person to use their image.
- **Outpainting:** using AI to extend an image beyond its original edges [3][10].

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Product on a gradient.** Lifestyle and ad images that float the product on a soft colour gradient. It reads as an AI packshot; use a real set, the brand's background or a real place.
- **Purple or blue gradient backdrops.** Gradient backgrounds as the default "modern" look. Pick backgrounds from the brand's style guide (section 8).
- **Cream for "premium".** Cream or beige backdrops chosen to signal premium. Use the brand's own colours unless the style guide picks cream.
- **Generic stock-style scenes.** In-use and lifestyle shots that look like stock, with staged smiles and empty marble counters. Shoot the product where the client's buyers really use it.
- **Every ad concept in one look.** Ad and lifestyle concepts all shot with the same light, angle and props. Keep the catalogue consistent (section 7), but give each ad concept its own look.
- **Hype text overlays.** Infographic and ad images with "Elevate", "Seamless" or "Game-changer" in the overlay. Use the plain fact the image proves, such as the size or what is in the box.
