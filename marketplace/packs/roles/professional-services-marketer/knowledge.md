# Professional services marketer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked.

This kit points to the official rules for each profession. It does not say what those rules allow in any one case. For that, ask your regulator or a qualified adviser.

## 1. What this role is for

Firms that sell expertise hire this role to win and keep clients: law firms, accountants and bookkeepers, insurance agencies and brokers, coaches and consultants, and architecture and design studios. Their buyers cannot judge the work before they buy it, so they buy on trust: a recommendation, a clear page, a quick and calm reply, proof that feels real, and a sense that the firm plays by its profession's rules.

Who hires it:

- Small and mid-size firms with no marketing team, where partners or owners do the selling.
- Solo coaches and consultants who want a steadier flow of clients than referrals alone.
- Firms whose website, intake and client emails have grown up piece by piece and confuse people.
- Firms that want content (explainers, newsletters, project stories) without breaking advertising rules.

What good work looks like:

- One clear sentence on who the firm helps and the change it makes.
- Service pages that start with the client's problem, explain the process, show what it costs (or how fees work), and say what happens next.
- An intake path that answers fast, gathers the right facts, and never gives advice before the firm has agreed to act.
- Proof that is true and fairly presented: reviews asked for honestly, testimonials that do not promise the same result, case and project stories with permission.
- Client updates, reminders and renewals that people understand the first time.
- Every claim checked against the profession's advertising rules, with a named person at the firm signing off.

How it is judged: enquiries and booked consultations from the right people; the share of enquiries that become clients; referrals and repeat work; review volume and quality; fewer "what happens next?" calls; and zero regulator complaints about the firm's marketing.

The agents in this role each lead with one specialty: coaches and consultants, architecture and design studios, law firm intake, accounting firms, and insurance agencies. They share this knowledge.

## 2. The canon

**The trust equation (Charles H. Green, *The Trusted Advisor*, 2000) [1].** Trust rises with credibility (can we believe what you say?), reliability (do you do what you said?) and intimacy (do people feel safe telling you things?), and falls with self-orientation (are you focused on yourself?) [1]. The book was co-written with David Maister and Robert Galford. For marketing, this means pages and emails that talk about the client's situation, show credentials plainly, promise only what the firm controls, and then keep those promises.

**Google's people-first content and YMYL [2].** Google gives extra weight to experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust on "Your Money or Your Life" topics: health, financial stability, safety and similar [2]. Legal, tax, insurance and money pages fall here. Google asks for accurate bylines and warns that made-up authors or false credentials are deception [2]. In practice: a named, qualified author, a review date, and sources for any rule or figure.

**The profession's own advertising rules.** These are the true canon for this role, and they differ by country and profession (section 3). They share a few ideas: be accurate, do not mislead even with true statements, do not create expectations of results, do not knock competitors without proof, respect limits on cold approaches, and publish the information clients need to compare.

**Reading list (credited, not summarised).** This kit takes no specific claims from these.

- David Maister, *Managing the Professional Service Firm* (1993): how expertise firms win and keep work.
- Blair Enns, *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto*: selling expertise without free work.
- Alan Weiss, *Million Dollar Consulting*: positioning and fees for consultants.
- Marcus Sheridan, *They Ask, You Answer*: answering buyers' real questions, including price.

## 3. How the work is done now

**How clients choose (UK legal data, 2026).** The Legal Services Consumer Panel's 2026 tracker surveyed 3,802 people in England and Wales who used legal services in the previous two years (fieldwork 23 February to 13 April 2026) [3]. Findings:

- 42% shopped around, similar to 2025 (44%) and well above levels before 2022 (22% to 30%).
- Price (57%) and reputation (52%) were the factors most often compared.
- The most common ways of choosing were having used the provider before (21%) and a recommendation from family or friends (18%).
- 91% said it mattered that the provider was regulated, the top factor.
- 74% found cost information easy to find, but only 56% found information on complaints and professional indemnity insurance easy [3].

This is one country and one profession, but it matches what most expertise firms see: past clients and referrals bring much of the work, and newer buyers compare price, reputation and regulated status online. The UK's competition authority found in 2016 that the legal market was not working well for individuals and small businesses and called for more transparency [4]; the SRA's price transparency rules followed (below).

**Positioning in one sentence.** "We help [who] with [problem] so they can [result]." If a firm cannot say this, fix it before writing pages. A narrow, true focus (a type of client, a type of problem, a place) makes every page, referral and talk easier.

**Service pages.** Lead with the client's problem in their words. Then: who the service is for, how the process works step by step, what the client sends, what they get, how fees work (and the actual prices where rules require it), who does the work and their qualifications, regulatory details, and the next step. Label general information as general information. For YMYL topics, add the author, their qualification and a review date [2].

**Intake.** Speed and calm matter. A good intake path:

1. Acknowledges every enquiry quickly, says when a person will reply, and sets expectations.
2. Collects only the facts the firm needs to decide whether it can help, in an order a stressed person can answer.
3. Says plainly what the person answering cannot do (for example, give legal or tax advice before the firm agrees to act).
4. Books a consultation or explains why the firm cannot help, with a pointer elsewhere where possible.
5. Treats what enquirers share as confidential. Lawyers have specific duties to prospective clients and conflict checks to run, so the firm's own process decides what to ask and when.

**General-information content.** Newsletters, explainers and guides build trust and keep the firm in mind. Rules of thumb: use only sources the firm provides or approves; say who it applies to (country, state, type of client) and when it was last checked; end with "speak to us about your own situation"; avoid promises of savings, refunds, outcomes or cover; and route anything that reads as advice to a qualified person at the firm.

**Reviews and testimonials.** The same consumer rules apply as for any business, plus profession rules.

- In the US, the FTC's rule on consumer reviews and testimonials (in effect from 21 October 2024) bans fake reviews, incentives that require a positive or negative review, undisclosed insider reviews and review suppression [24]. Its Endorsement Guides say a testimonial showing better-than-usual results must make the generally expected results clear [23].
- Google removes incentivised reviews, reviews from owners or staff, and reviews gathered by asking only happy clients or discouraging negative ones. Asking every client for an honest review, with no incentive, is fine [25].
- In the UK, the CAP Code's misleading advertising section covers testimonials and substantiation [26], the ASA and CMA's influencer guide (fourth edition, 23 October 2026) adds fake reviews and the DMCC Act [27], and the CMA's DMCC guidance on unfair commercial practices has a separate guide on fake reviews [28].
- For lawyers, profession rules add caution: California's Rule 7.1, built on the ABA model, says past results and testimonials can mislead if they suggest others will get the same result, and that a guarantee of an outcome is misleading [5].

**Cold outreach.** Rules differ more here than anywhere else. Solicitors in England and Wales may not make unsolicited approaches to members of the public, except current or former clients [6]. The ABA's model framework has a separate rule (7.3) for direct, real-time or targeted contact with potential clients [5]. ICAEW's code forbids promotion that amounts to harassment [10]. Check the profession's rule before any cold email, call, direct message or targeted letter.

**AI in this work.** AI drafts pages, intake scripts, explainers and emails well. The limits: never invent authors, credentials, results, reviews or case details [2]; never paste confidential client information into tools the firm has not approved; keep a qualified person's review on anything that touches advice; and never let an AI intake tool or chatbot give advice before the firm has agreed to act.

### Rules by profession (pointers, not rulings)

Where to look for the advertising rules. Read the current text, and ask your regulator or a qualified adviser before relying on any reading of it.

**Lawyers.**
- *US:* the ABA Model Rules of Professional Conduct 7.1 to 7.3 (communications about a lawyer's services, advertising, and solicitation) are a model; each state adopts its own version. California's Rule 7.1 (effective 1 November 2018) is one example: no false or misleading communication, no truthful statement that misleads, no guarantees, care with past results and comparisons [5]. Some state bars run their own advertising review or filing rules, so check the state bar where the lawyer is licensed. (The ABA's website blocks automated reading, so this kit cites California's adopted text.)
- *England and Wales:* the SRA Code of Conduct for Solicitors (current version in effect from 11 April 2025) requires publicity to be accurate and not misleading (8.8), the best possible information on pricing (8.7), and no unsolicited approaches to members of the public except current or former clients (8.9) [6]. The SRA Transparency Rules require price information for listed services such as residential conveyancing, uncontested probate, employment tribunal claims and debt recovery, plus the complaints procedure and the SRA number and digital badge on the website [7].
- *EU:* each national bar or law society sets its own conduct rules. The CCBE's Model Code (adopted 8 October 2021) is a model, not a uniform enforceable code; its separate Code of Conduct covers cross-border practice [8].

**Accountants and tax advisers.**
- *International and UK:* the IESBA Code is used in more than 130 jurisdictions [11]; its current handbook was published on 24 September 2026 [12]. ICAEW's Code of Ethics (in force from 1 July 2026) says marketing must not bring the profession into disrepute, must be honest and truthful, and must not make exaggerated claims or disparaging or unsubstantiated comparisons; if in doubt, consult the professional body [9][10]. ACCA's Rulebook (1 January 2026 edition) includes its Code of Ethics and Conduct [13].
- *US:* CPAs follow the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct and their state board of accountancy. Attorneys, CPAs and enrolled agents who practise before the IRS also follow Circular 230 [14]. (The AICPA's online code did not load for automated reading, so check it directly.)

**Insurance agencies and brokers.**
- *US:* insurance is regulated by each state. The NAIC's Unfair Trade Practices Act (Model 880) is a model that states adopt or modify; it bans misrepresenting a policy's benefits or terms and untrue, deceptive or misleading insurance advertising in any medium [15]. Check your state insurance department and each carrier's rules on approved wording.
- *UK:* the FCA says all financial promotions, including insurance, must be clear, fair and not misleading whatever the medium [16]. Its 2024 social media guidance (FG24/1) covers balance and influencers [17], and the Consumer Duty sets high standards for retail customers [18].
- *EU:* the Insurance Distribution Directive sets conduct and information rules, including the product information document for non-life cover; it is minimum harmonising, so national rules can add more [19].

**Architects and design studios.**
- *UK:* only people on the Architects Register may use the title "architect"; anyone may offer architectural services, and terms like "architectural consultant" are not protected [22]. The 2025 Architects Code is in effect [20], and its Standard 1.2 asks architects to promote their services responsibly and accurately [21].
- *US:* state licensing boards control the title "architect"; check the board in each state where the studio works. Interior designers face title or practice rules in some states.

**Coaches and consultants.** Most have no single regulator, so general advertising law applies: the FTC in the US (including disclosure and typical-results rules for testimonials [23][24]), the CAP Code in the UK [26], and consumer protection law in each EU country. If a coach's work touches health, therapy, money or investments, those fields may have their own regulators. Income and outcome claims need proof and fair presentation.

**Other countries.** For India, Canada and Australia, check the profession's own regulator before publishing: for lawyers, the Bar Council of India, the law society of the relevant Canadian province or territory, and the legal profession regulator of the relevant Australian state or territory; for accountants, ICAI, CPA Canada with the provincial CPA body, and CPA Australia or CA ANZ; for insurance, IRDAI, the relevant Canadian provincial regulator, and ASIC.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these to set expectations, never as targets. Each has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Shopping around for legal services (England and Wales, 2026):** 42% of users shopped around; 21% chose a provider they had used before; 18% chose on a recommendation; 91% said regulated status mattered [3]. *Caveat:* one country, consumers only (not businesses), online panel.
- **Information gaps (same survey):** 74% found cost information easy to find; 56% found complaints and indemnity insurance information easy [3]. *Caveat:* only those who shopped around answered.
- **Reach of the international accountants' code:** used in more than 130 jurisdictions [11]. *Caveat:* each body adapts it; check the local version.
- **UK architects:** over 42,000 on the Register [22]. *Caveat:* changes daily; check the Register for any one person.

No reliable public benchmark gives a typical enquiry-to-client rate, cost per enquiry or review count for small expertise firms across countries. Use the firm's own numbers as the baseline: enquiries by source, consultations booked, clients won, and referrals.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the firm cannot say who it helps and with what in one sentence, fix that before writing any page, because every later piece depends on it.
- If a claim describes results ("we win", "you'll save", "guaranteed"), cut it or present it with the context the rules require, because guarantees and implied outcomes are what advertising rules target [5][10][23].
- If a sentence could read as advice to a specific person, mark it for review by a qualified person at the firm, because marketing copy must not stand in for advice.
- If a profession requires price or regulatory information on the website (for example SRA-regulated firms for listed services), put it where a buyer would look, because it is required and buyers compare price first [3][7].
- If you are about to send a cold email, call, message or letter, check the profession's solicitation rule first, because some forbid it outright [5][6][10].
- If a testimonial shows an unusual result, add the typical result or drop it, and check the profession's rules on testimonials [5][23].
- If you ask for reviews, ask every client, with no incentive and no screening for happy ones [24][25].
- If content covers rules, deadlines or rates, use only dates and sources the firm supplies, state the jurisdiction and the date checked, and end with "speak to us about your own situation".
- If an insurance message describes cover, use the carrier's approved wording and say "check your policy documents"; never say what is covered for a specific person [15][16].
- If a studio's team includes people who are not registered architects, describe them accurately, because the title is protected in the UK [22].
- If the firm's compliance lead has not signed off a piece that needs it, do not publish, even at level 3.
- If enquiries arrive and wait, fix response speed before buying more traffic, because a slow reply wastes the enquiries you already have.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Vague positioning.** Spot it: the home page could belong to any firm in the town.
- **Outcome promises.** Spot it: "we always win", "maximum refund", "you're covered".
- **Results without context.** Spot it: one big case result or client win at the top of the page, with no note that results vary.
- **Hidden fees.** Spot it: "contact us for a quote" where rules or buyers expect a price or fee basis.
- **Advice in marketing.** Spot it: a blog post that tells "you" what to do in a specific legal or tax situation.
- **Cold outreach that breaks profession rules.** Spot it: bought lists, unsolicited messages to members of the public.
- **Review gating.** Spot it: a form that asks for a star rating and only sends five-star clients to Google [25].
- **Fake or borrowed proof.** Spot it: stock "client" photos, unattributed quotes, invented authors [2].
- **Misused titles.** Spot it: "architect" for an unregistered designer; "specialist" or "expert" where rules restrict it.
- **Slow, cold intake.** Spot it: enquiries answered days later with a generic form.
- **Out-of-date rules content.** Spot it: deadline or rate pages with no date and no jurisdiction.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does the piece start with the client's situation in their words?
2. Is every claim true, provable and free of implied guarantees?
3. Have the profession's advertising rules for this country or state been checked, and has the firm's named reviewer signed off?
4. Is required information present (prices or fee basis, regulatory number or badge, complaints route) where the rules ask for it?
5. Is general information labelled, with jurisdiction, date checked and "speak to us about your own situation"?
6. Are testimonials and reviews genuine, permitted, fairly presented and gathered without incentives or screening?
7. Are the author and their qualifications real and correct?
8. Is the next step obvious, and does intake promise a reply time the firm can keep?
9. Is any confidential client detail removed or used with written permission?
10. Would a stressed first-time client understand it on one read?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Positioning line and offer summary:* who, problem, result, how, price basis.
- *Service or practice-area page:* problem, who it is for, process, what to send, what you get, fees, people, regulatory details, next step.
- *Intake script and form:* greeting, what we can and cannot do on this call, questions in order, next step, privacy note.
- *Enquiry replies and follow-ups:* fast acknowledgement, booking link, reminder, polite close.
- *Explainers and newsletters:* one topic, general information, jurisdiction and date, sources, "speak to us".
- *Review and testimonial requests:* honest ask to every client, no incentive, permission wording.
- *Case or project stories:* the brief, the approach, the result as fact, with permission and credits.
- *Client updates:* what happened, what comes next, what we need from you, when you will hear from us.
- *Reminder and renewal sequences:* date, what to send, how to send it, what happens if late, one action per email.
- *Proposals and fee letters:* services by stage, what is included, what is extra, fees the firm supplies, next steps.

**Tone and conventions.** Calm, plain and exact. Short sentences, one idea at a time. Define a term the first time it appears. No hype, no guarantees, no fear tactics. Name the jurisdiction. Date anything rule-based. Mark every line for review that could read as advice, a promise or a comparison, with [REVIEW].

## 9. Glossary

- **Professional services:** work sold on expertise, such as law, accounting, insurance broking, consulting, coaching and design.
- **Practice area:** a type of legal (or other professional) work a firm offers.
- **Intake:** the steps from first enquiry to deciding whether to take on a client.
- **Prospective client:** someone who has asked a firm for help but has not yet hired it.
- **Conflict check:** a firm's check that taking on a client will not clash with existing duties.
- **Engagement letter:** the document that confirms a firm has agreed to act, on what terms.
- **General information:** content that explains a topic for everyone, not advice for one person.
- **Solicitation:** a direct approach to a potential client, such as a call or targeted message [5][6].
- **Testimonial:** a client's statement about their experience, used in marketing [23].
- **Material connection:** a relationship (payment, gift, employment) that must be disclosed with an endorsement [23].
- **Review gating:** asking only happy clients for public reviews; not allowed on Google [25].
- **Price transparency:** publishing prices or fee bases so buyers can compare [7].
- **Digital badge (SRA):** the SRA's website badge showing a firm is regulated [7].
- **Financial promotion:** a communication that invites people to take up a financial product, including insurance in the UK [16].
- **Clear, fair and not misleading:** the FCA's core standard for promotions [16][17].
- **IPID:** the insurance product information document required in the EU for non-life cover [19].
- **Protected title:** a job title only registered people may use, such as "architect" in the UK [22].
- **YMYL:** "Your Money or Your Life" topics where Google weighs trust more heavily [2].
- **E-E-A-T:** experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trust [2].
- **Trust equation:** credibility, reliability and intimacy divided by self-orientation [1].
- **Referral partner:** another professional who sends clients, such as an accountant referring to a lawyer.

## 12. Defaults to avoid

An AI model with no context falls back on these choices, so they make work look like everyone else's. Avoid each one unless the client's captured brand or the agreed plan chooses it on purpose, and then say why. The full shared list is in tools/ai-default-patterns.md.

- **Template firm site.** Centred hero, three benefit cards, "How it works in 3 steps", testimonials, FAQ and a final CTA, for every firm. Start from the client's situation in their words (section 7) and the order a buyer needs: problem, fees, people, next step.
- **Generic stock-style images.** Handshakes, gavels, skylines and smiling teams. Use real photos of the real people, with permission.
- **"Trusted by" rows with no logos.** Client or award logo strips with placeholders. Use only logos the firm may show.
- **Stock hype words.** "Unlock your potential", "seamless service", "game-changer". This kit's tone is calm, plain and exact (section 8).
- **Benefits any competitor could claim.** "Client-focused", "experienced team", "tailored solutions". Say who the firm helps and with what, in one sentence (section 5).
- **Headline formula clichés.** "Your trusted partner in..." Lead with the client's problem and the firm's specific service.
- **Fake urgency.** "Act now" or deadline pressure that is not real. It sits close to the fear tactics this kit rules out (section 8); use real dates only.
