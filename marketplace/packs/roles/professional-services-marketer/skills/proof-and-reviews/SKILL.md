# Proof and reviews

Proof is what turns a careful buyer into a client: real reviews, a story like theirs, a project they can picture. It is also where expertise firms most often break the rules, with gated reviews, results that imply a promise, or stories that reveal who a client is. This skill builds proof that is both persuasive and clean.

This skill follows general consumer rules and points to profession rules. It does not decide what a profession allows in a given case. Ask your regulator or a qualified adviser, and have the firm's reviewer sign off.

## When to use

- A firm has few reviews, or reviews only from years ago.
- The firm wants to use testimonials, client results or case studies on its site.
- A design studio or consultant needs portfolio and project stories.
- New reviews need replies, including critical ones.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read reviews and existing proof. Level 2 to draft request emails, stories and replies. Level 3 to send review requests to the client lists the firm approved, publish signed-off stories, and post review replies that follow the approved reply rules. Replies that touch a client matter, complaint or legal issue need a fresh yes every time.

- The profession and jurisdiction, and the firm's rules on testimonials and client confidentiality.
- The list of recent clients the firm is willing to ask (all of them, not a chosen few).
- Client permissions already given, and how they were recorded.
- Project or case details the client agreed to share, with credits (photographers, collaborators).

## Steps

1. **Check the rules.** General rules: no fake or bought reviews, no incentives tied to positive reviews, no undisclosed reviews from staff or family, no suppression of negative reviews (US FTC rule); no review gating and no incentives on Google; testimonials must not mislead (UK CAP Code). Profession rules may add more: some legal rules treat past results and testimonials as misleading if they suggest others will get the same result. Ask your regulator or a qualified adviser where unsure.
2. **Ask every client, the same way.** One short request after the work ends, to every client in the agreed list, with a direct link and no incentive. Do not ask only happy clients, and do not route unhappy ones elsewhere.
3. **Ask for testimonials separately, with permission.** Three easy questions (what was the situation, what did we do, what changed), plus a clear permission line: where it will appear, with which name or initials, and how to withdraw.
4. **Present results fairly.** If a testimonial or case shows an unusually good result, add context ("every case is different; this result depended on [facts]") or leave it out. Never use a result as a promise.
5. **Write case or project stories as fact.** The brief or situation, the approach, the result, the people and credits. Use the client's words only with permission. Remove details that could identify a client who has not agreed. For design studios, use correct areas, materials, credits and titles.
6. **Write review replies.** Thank the reviewer. For critical reviews: acknowledge, do not argue, do not confirm the person was a client or reveal any detail, and offer a private route. Example: "We take all feedback seriously. Please contact [name] at [email] so we can look into this."
7. **Hold risky reviews.** Any review mentioning a legal matter, a complaint, a regulator or a safety issue goes to the firm before any reply.
8. **Record permissions.** Who agreed, to what, when, and where the record is kept.
9. **Get sign-off and publish.** At level 3, publish only signed-off stories and send requests only to the approved lists.

## Checks

- Every client in the agreed list was asked, with no incentive.
- No gating, no staff or family reviews, no fake proof.
- Each testimonial has recorded permission and fair context.
- No result is presented as a promise.
- Stories reveal nothing a client has not agreed to share.
- Replies confirm no client details.
- Profession rules on testimonials checked for this jurisdiction.

## Output format

1. Rules checked (general and profession), with open questions for the reviewer.
2. Review request email and text.
3. Testimonial request with permission wording.
4. Case or project stories with credits and permission notes.
5. Review reply templates (positive, neutral, critical, hold-for-firm).
6. Permission log columns.

## Example of good output (short)

*Fernhollow Studio is a made-up architecture practice, used only to show the format. All details are invented for this example.*

**Review request (to every client at handover):**
"Thank you for working with us on [project]. If you have a minute, we'd be grateful for an honest review of your experience, good or bad: [link]. It helps other people decide whether we're right for them. There's no obligation."

**Testimonial permission line:**
"May we quote your words on our website and in proposals, with your first name and town? You can ask us to remove it at any time by emailing [address]."

**Project story (extract):**
"The brief: a cold 1930s semi with a dark kitchen, for a family of five. We opened the back of the house to the garden with a steel-framed extension and moved the stairs to free the middle of the plan. [CHECK: floor area and completion date with project file.] Photography: [credit]. Shared with the clients' permission."

**Critical review reply:**
"Thank you for taking the time to write this. We'd like to understand what happened. Please contact our practice manager at [email]."
