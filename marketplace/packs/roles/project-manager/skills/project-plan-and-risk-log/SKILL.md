# Project plan and risk log

## When to use

- A new project has a goal and a deadline but no plan.
- A project has drifted: dates keep moving and nobody can say when it will finish.
- The owner needs to know whether a deadline is possible before promising it.

Do not use it for construction or safety-critical schedules that need a licensed planner, or to promise dates the plan cannot support.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the brief, notes, the existing project or board, team calendars and time off.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** build the plan as a private project or board with tasks not yet assigned, and the risk log as a draft page.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** create and assign tasks, set dates and dependencies, share the project with the team and set the review meetings, within the client's limits.

## What you need

1. The goal, in the client's words.
2. The finish line: how everyone will know it is done.
3. The deadline and whether it is fixed (an event date) or a target.
4. The limits: budget, people and their available time, tools.
5. Who decides on scope, money and dates.
6. Anything already done, and any past project like this one with what it really took.

## Steps

### 1. Restate the project in two sentences
Goal, finish line, deadline and limits. Ask for anything missing before going on. If the client's goal and deadline already look incompatible, say so now.

### 2. Break the work down
List the deliverables (the things that will exist at the end), then split each into tasks small enough to have one owner and a clear end. Include the work people forget: setup, content, approvals, testing, training, launch, fixes after launch, and the client's own tasks (supplying content, signing off, granting access).

### 3. Add owners, effort and dependencies
- Owner: one role or name the client gave; never a whole team.
- Effort: a low and high range, from the client's past work where possible; label every guess.
- Dependencies: what must finish before this can start.

### 4. Find the critical path
Lay the tasks out by dependency and find the longest chain. That chain sets the earliest finish. If it ends after the deadline, say so plainly and offer options: cut or simplify scope, start something earlier, run tasks in parallel, or move the date. Each option with what it costs.

### 5. Set milestones and review points
Milestones mark deliverables and decisions (designs signed off, content in, launch). Add a review point at a steady rhythm, weekly or fortnightly, to compare progress with the plan.

### 6. Write the risk and assumption log
Run a short pre-mortem: imagine the project has failed and list why. For each risk: likelihood, effect, the warning sign that it is starting, the first response and an owner. List the assumptions the plan rests on (for example "the client supplies all content by the agreed date") with who confirms each.

### 7. Hold contingency in one place
Rather than padding each task, hold a shared buffer before the deadline and say how big it is and why. Show how much of it is used at each review.

### 8. Write the summary
One paragraph: goal, finish line, deadline, whether the plan fits, the biggest risk and the next decision needed.

### 9. Set it up (level 3) or hand over
At level 3, and after the client's yes to the action list, create the project, tasks, owners, dates and dependencies in the tool and book the review meetings. Otherwise hand over the table and the steps to load it.

### Re-planning a drifted project
Mark each task done, in progress or not started, re-estimate the remaining work as ranges, find the new critical path and give a new date range. Show what changed since the last plan and why.

## Checks

- Goal, finish line, deadline and limits are written and agreed.
- Every task has one owner, a range and its dependencies.
- The client's own tasks are in the plan with dates.
- The critical path is shown, and any gap with the deadline is stated plainly.
- Every risk has a warning sign, a first response and an owner.
- Contingency is held in one place and its size explained.
- Guesses are labelled; nothing is promised that the plan cannot support.
- Nothing was set up live without level 3 access and the client's yes.

## Output format

1. Summary paragraph
2. Milestones (date, deliverable or decision, owner)
3. Task table: task, deliverable, owner, effort range, depends on, start, finish
4. Critical path and fit with the deadline, with options if it does not fit
5. Risk and assumption log
6. Review rhythm and contingency
7. Action log (level 3)

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Ridgeway Physio, moving its clinic to a new building.*

> **Summary:** Move the clinic to 14 Mill Lane so patients are seen there from Monday 2 February, with no lost appointments, within the agreed budget. The plan fits with about a week of buffer. Biggest risk: the internet line install, which the provider will only date after the lease is signed. Decision needed: sign the lease this week.
>
> | Task | Owner | Effort | Depends on | Finish |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Sign lease | Owner | 1 day | Solicitor review | 14 Nov |
> | Order internet line | Practice manager | 1 day | Lease signed | 17 Nov |
> | Line installed | Provider | 4 to 8 weeks (guess, confirm with provider) | Order | 15 Dec to 12 Jan |
> | Tell patients new address | Reception lead | 2 days | Move date confirmed | 9 Jan |
>
> **Critical path:** lease, internet order, install, booking system test, move weekend.
> **Risk:** install slips. Sign: no install date by 1 Dec. First response: order a 4G router as backup. Owner: practice manager.
