# Website redesign and migration

## When to use

- A business is redesigning its site, moving to a new platform or theme, or changing domain.
- A previous redesign lost search traffic and the owner wants it done safely this time.
- A redesign is stalled on content and sign-offs.

Do not use it to design or build the site, run a full technical SEO audit, or write all the new copy. It plans, coordinates and checks.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** a crawl or sitemap export, analytics and Google Search Console data, the current CMS page list.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** draft the inventory, page list, brief and redirect map in a shared sheet or doc; draft pages in the new CMS's staging site if given access.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** load approved redirects through the method the developer or host has agreed, run launch checks on the live site, and submit sitemaps in Search Console, within the client's limits. DNS and domain changes always need a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. Why they are redesigning, who the site is for, and what success looks like after launch.
2. The platform now and next, the budget and the deadline.
3. A crawl or sitemap export of every current URL.
4. Traffic per page from analytics, and pages with links from other sites or sales, if available.
5. Who writes content, who signs off and who builds.

## Steps

### 1. Agree the why
Goals, users, must-keep features and the limits, in half a page. Get it signed off before the inventory.

### 2. Take the content inventory
List every URL from the crawl or sitemap: title, purpose, traffic, whether it has outside links or drives sales or leads, owner, and last updated.

### 3. Call each page
Keep, update, merge or cut. Protect pages that bring traffic, links, leads or sales. Pages to cut still need a decision on where their visitors go (a related page, or a "gone" status if nothing fits).

### 4. Write the new page list
Each page gets one job, the content it needs, an owner and a due date. Group into a sitemap.

### 5. Write the designer brief
Goals, users, the page list, must-keep features, brand rules, examples the client likes and dislikes, accessibility target (WCAG 2.2, level AA unless the client's adviser says otherwise), budget and dates.

### 6. Build the redirect map
For every old URL that matters, the best new home. Rules from Google's guidance: server-side permanent redirects (301 or 308), each old URL straight to its final page, no long chains, and no dumping many old pages on the home page. Include images and files that get traffic. Keep redirects for as long as possible, at least a year.

### 7. Plan the content move
Who writes, who checks, the deadline per page, and the date after which late content means the page launches in its old form or not at all. Late content often makes redesigns slip, so put it on the critical path.

### 8. Write launch day checks
Redirects tested (a sample of top pages plus every high-traffic URL), no broken internal links, forms send to the right inbox, analytics and tags firing, the new sitemap submitted, robots rules allow crawling, the old sitemap removed, and for a domain change the Change of Address tool used. At level 3, run these on the live site and log results.

### 9. Write the first-month watch list
Traffic by page against the old site, Search Console coverage and errors, 404s from old links, form submissions and sales. Expect some wobble in rankings for a few weeks; investigate drops on key pages at once.

### 10. Flag the risks
Late content, slow sign-offs, missing redirects, a launch date set before content is in, and accessibility left to the end. Each with a warning sign and first response.

### Accessibility by market
Budget accessibility checks into design and build, not after. WCAG 2.2 is the current W3C standard. In the EU, accessibility requirements apply to many products and services, including online shops, placed on the market after 28 June 2025, with an exemption for service businesses under 10 staff and EUR 2 million turnover. In the US, the DOJ points to WCAG as helpful guidance under the ADA. In the UK, all service providers must make reasonable adjustments under the Equality Act 2010. Whether a site meets the law is for the client's adviser.

## Checks

- Every current URL is in the inventory with a call.
- Every page with traffic, links, leads or sales has a redirect to a relevant page.
- Redirects are permanent, server-side and direct.
- Every new page has a job, an owner and a content date.
- Launch checks cover redirects, forms, tracking, sitemaps and robots rules.
- Accessibility is in the brief, the budget and the launch checks.
- No live change, redirect upload or DNS change happened without level 3 access, the developer's agreement and the client's yes.

## Output format

1. The why (half a page)
2. Content inventory with calls
3. New page list and sitemap
4. Designer brief
5. Redirect map (old URL, new URL, type, notes)
6. Content plan with owners and dates
7. Launch day checklist and first-month watch list
8. Risk list
9. Action log (level 3)

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a veterinary practice moving from an old CMS to a new one. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> | Old URL | Visits (last 12 months) | Call | New URL | Redirect |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | /services/dog-vaccinations.html | 4,210 | Update | /dogs/vaccinations | 301 |
> | /services/cat-vaccinations.html | 2,980 | Merge | /cats/vaccinations | 301 |
> | /news/2019-open-day | 12 | Cut | /about | 301 (closest relevant page) |
> | /prices.pdf | 1,540 | Update | /prices | 301 |
>
> **Launch day:** test all 38 redirects with visits over 100; submit the new sitemap; send a test booking from each form.
> **Watch list, weeks 1 to 4:** visits to /dogs/vaccinations against the old page's weekly average; 404s in Search Console; bookings per week.
