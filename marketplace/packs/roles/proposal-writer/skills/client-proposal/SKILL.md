# Client proposal

A good proposal makes the buyer's decision easy: they see their problem in their own words, a plan they believe, a scope with clear edges and a price they can compare. This skill writes that document, and never promises what the client cannot deliver.

## When to use

- After a sales or discovery call, when the buyer asks for a proposal or quote.
- A freelancer is answering a bigger client directly, beyond a short marketplace bid.
- The client sends proposals often and wants a reusable template.
- A draft proposal needs checking against the buyer's brief before it goes out.

Not for: formal RFPs and public tenders with scoring rules (use the tender-and-rfp-answers skill), or legal contract terms (the client's lawyer reviews those).

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read call notes, the brief, past proposals and the price list. Level 2 to build the proposal as a draft in the client's proposal tool (such as PandaDoc or Proposify), document folder or CRM. Level 3 to move it through the client's internal approval steps within their limits. Sending a proposal the client treats as binding, or sending it for signature, needs a fresh yes every time.

- **The buyer's words:** call notes, emails or the brief, including goals, deadline, constraints, budget hints and who decides.
- **What the client will deliver:** services, team, method, timing.
- **Prices:** the client's price list or rates, any minimum, and what may be discounted. Never invent a price.
- **Proof the client may use:** past projects, numbers and quotes with permission.
- **Terms:** payment terms, how long the offer stays open, the client's standard terms if any.

## Steps

1. **Pull out the buyer's words.** List goals, pains, deadline, constraints and success measures as the buyer said them. Note who decides and who influences.
2. **Write the summary first in draft, last in final.** Three or four sentences: "You told us [goal, in their words]. You need it by [date] because [reason]. We propose [plan]. You'll see [result they care about] by [checkpoint]."
3. **Set scope as deliverables.** Things the buyer receives, not activities ("A 12-page site with booking, built and tested on mobile", not "web design work"). Add quantities, rounds of revisions and acceptance criteria.
4. **Write the not-included list.** Everything the buyer might assume is included and is not: copywriting, stock photos, hosting, extra revision rounds, travel, third-party fees.
5. **Timeline with dependencies.** Phases with dates or week numbers, and what the buyer must supply at each one (content, access, sign-off). State what happens to dates if inputs are late.
6. **Build options from the client's prices.** Two or three options that differ in scope or speed, not just in price. Show the maths. Mark the one the client recommends and why, in one line.
7. **Add proof.** One or two short examples like this job, with only facts the client supplied and permission to name. If the client has none, describe a similar project without names or numbers.
8. **Risks and assumptions.** List what the plan depends on. Keep it honest and short.
9. **Close with one next step.** "Reply to choose an option and we'll send the agreement", with the date the offer stays open until.
10. **Check against the brief.** Every buyer need is answered or explicitly out of scope; nothing is promised the client did not confirm; open questions are listed at the end for the client.

**Templates.** For a reusable template, keep the same sections, write standard scope and not-included blocks per service, a price table with formulas or fill-in fields, and fill-in notes in brackets that tell the writer what goes where.

**Checking a draft.** Compare the draft with the brief line by line. Return: missing buyer needs, vague scope words ("support", "ongoing", "as needed"), promises with no owner, prices that don't add up, and risky wording ("guarantee", "unlimited") with fixes.

## Checks

- The opening uses the buyer's own words.
- Scope is deliverables with quantities; there is a not-included list.
- Every price comes from the client's price list or rates, and the totals add up.
- No guaranteed outcome the client does not control.
- Proof is real, permitted and labelled.
- One next step and an offer-valid date.
- Legal terms are the client's own or flagged for their lawyer.

## Output format

1. Summary (buyer's goal in their words).
2. What we'll deliver (scope list) and Not included.
3. Timeline and what we need from you.
4. Options table: what's in each, timing, price, recommended option.
5. Why us (proof, short).
6. Assumptions and risks.
7. Next step and offer date.
8. For the client only: open questions and anything to confirm before sending.

## Example of good output (short)

*Fennel and Rye Kitchens is a made-up restaurant group, and Northpoint Studio a made-up web agency, used only to show the format. Prices are invented and stand in for the agency's own price list.*

**Summary.** You told us your four sites "all look different online" and that bookings "go everywhere: phone, email and two apps". You need one site with one booking system before the spring menu launch on 3 March. We propose a single site with a page per location and one booking flow, live by 24 February.

**Not included:** menu photography, copywriting beyond editing your current text, booking software fees, more than two rounds of design changes.

| | Option A: One site | Option B: One site plus booking set-up | Option C: B plus three months' updates |
|---|---|---|---|
| What's in | Site, 4 location pages, mobile testing | A, plus booking system set-up and staff guide | B, plus up to 4 hours of changes a month |
| Live by | 17 Feb | 24 Feb | 24 Feb |
| Price | $9,500 | $11,200 | $11,200 + $600 a month |

**We recommend B**, because your main problem is scattered bookings, and A does not fix that.

**Next step:** reply with the option you choose by 31 January, and we'll send our standard agreement for you to review.
