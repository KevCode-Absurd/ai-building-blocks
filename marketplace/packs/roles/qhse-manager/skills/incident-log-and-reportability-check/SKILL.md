# Incident log and reportability check

## When to use

- The firm has no way for staff to report incidents and near misses, or only logs injuries.
- An incident has just happened and someone asks "do we have to report this?"
- The OSHA 300A summary is due, or an auditor or customer wants to see the log.
- The same kind of incident keeps happening.

Not for: deciding whether to report to a regulator, deciding to stop work, legal advice or medical advice. Those decisions stay with the client and their competent person; I prepare everything they need.

## What you need

1. Country, state and sector of each site, and the headcount (US recordkeeping depends on size and industry).
2. The facts of the incident: what, where, when, who was involved (IDs, not names, in anything shared beyond the restricted record), injuries, treatment, time off work, witnesses, photos.
3. The current log, if any, and the risk assessment for the task.
4. Who decides on regulator reports and who submits them.

**Access:** level 1 to read logs and reports; level 2 to draft the form, the investigation, any regulator report and any stop-work notice; level 3 to log reported incidents in the agreed tool, assign corrective actions and close them after the check, within your limits. Submitting to a regulator and stopping work are the client's decisions at every level.

## Steps

### 1. Make reporting easy

- A one-page form (paper and phone): what happened, where, when, who was hurt or could have been, immediate action taken, who reported it.
- Tell everyone how to report and that they won't be punished for reporting. In the US this is a legal duty: a reasonable reporting procedure, telling employees how to report and of their right to report, and no retaliation.
- Log near misses as well as injuries. They are the cheapest lessons.

### 2. Immediate response

If anyone is in immediate danger: get people clear, call the emergency services and tell the person in charge now. Then: first aid, make the area safe, preserve the scene if it may be reportable, and note the time everything happened.

### 3. Check reportability and start the clock

Check each incident against the official criteria for its location and write down the result and the reason.

**UK (RIDDOR):**
| Event | What to do | When |
|---|---|---|
| Work-related death, or a specified injury to a worker | Report (online, or phone for these) | Without delay; received within 10 days |
| Worker unable to do normal duties for more than 7 consecutive days (not counting the day of the accident) | Report | Within 15 days of the incident |
| Over-3-day incapacity | Record only (accident book) | — |
| Member of the public taken from the scene to hospital for treatment | Report | Check the timing on HSE's "When do I report" page |
| Diagnosed reportable occupational disease | Report | When diagnosed |
| Listed dangerous occurrence | Report | Without delay |

The responsible person is the employer, some self-employed people, or whoever controls the premises.

**US (OSHA):**
| Event | What to do | When |
|---|---|---|
| Work-related fatality | Report to OSHA | Within 8 hours |
| In-patient hospitalisation, amputation or loss of an eye | Report to OSHA | Within 24 hours |
| Recordable injury or illness | Enter on Form 300 and 301 (if the firm must keep records) | Within 7 calendar days |
| Privacy concern case | Write "privacy case" on the 300 log; keep a separate confidential list | Same |
| Annual summary (300A) | Certified by a company executive and posted | 1 February to 30 April |
| Electronic submission (covered establishments) | Submit through the Injury Tracking Application | By 2 March (2026 cycle) |

Many employers with more than 10 employees must keep the forms; some industries are partly exempt. State plans may set their own rules. Keep records five years after the year they cover.

**EU:** keep the list of occupational accidents and report as national law requires. Check the national authority's page.

With web search connected, I open the official page for the location before giving the result and note the date checked. Without it, I use this table, mark it "check before use" and give the page to check. If I can't tell whether something is reportable, I say so; I never default to "not reportable".

### 4. Prepare the regulator report (level 2)

Draft every field from the facts, list what is still unknown, and show the deadline. The client or competent person decides whether to report and submits it through the regulator's own sign-in. Log the decision, who made it and when.

### 5. Recommend a stop if needed (level 2)

If the incident shows a serious risk that is still present, draft a stop-work recommendation and notice: the activity, the risk, the controls needed before restart, who must sign off the restart. The client decides. In the US, workers may have a right to refuse work posing an imminent risk of death or serious harm, under conditions OSHA sets; don't discourage them.

### 6. Investigate in proportion

Use HSE's four steps (HSG245):
1. **Gather information:** what happened, the sequence, the conditions, the people's accounts (as accounts, not blame), photos, records.
2. **Analyse:** the immediate cause, then the conditions that made it likely: design, equipment, procedure, training, supervision, workload, time pressure.
3. **Identify controls:** rank them with the hierarchy of controls.
4. **Action plan:** owner, date, how you'll check it worked.

Avoid "operator error" as a root cause. Ask why the error was easy to make.

### 7. Close the loop

- Update the risk assessment and checklists.
- Brief the crew (a toolbox talk on the lesson).
- Close each corrective action only after the check shows it worked.
- Review the log monthly for patterns by task, site and time.

## Checks

- Every incident and near miss has a log entry, a reportability result with its reason, and a date checked.
- Deadlines are shown in hours or days from the incident time.
- Any regulator report or stop-work notice is a draft for the client's decision, with who decided recorded.
- Personal and health details sit only in the restricted record; shared versions use IDs.
- Root causes go beyond "human error".
- Corrective actions have owners, dates and effectiveness checks.
- The document starts with: *Draft for review by a competent person. Check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

1. Incident and near-miss report form.
2. Log columns: ID, date and time, site, type, description, injury, days off, reportable (yes, no, unsure) with reason and source, deadline, decision and who decided, investigation link, actions, status.
3. Reportability note for this incident.
4. Draft regulator report (if needed).
5. Draft stop-work notice (if needed).
6. Investigation summary and action plan.
7. Monthly pattern review template.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business: Harbour Fresh Foods, 48 staff, chilled food packing, Bristol, England.*

> **Draft for review by a competent person. Check this with local rules before use.**
>
> **Incident 2026-031.** Tuesday 10 November, 07:40. Packer (ID P-114) slipped on wet floor by washdown area, fractured left wrist (confirmed at hospital, same day).
>
> **Reportability (checked against HSE RIDDOR pages, 10 Nov 2026):** a fracture other than to fingers, thumbs or toes is a specified injury. **Likely reportable.** Report without delay; must be received within 10 days (by 20 November).
> **Prepared:** draft RIDDOR report with all known fields; unknown: witness statement from shift lead. **Decision needed from:** operations director.
>
> **Immediate cause:** wet floor. **Conditions:** washdown runs into the packing shift change; squeegee broken since 2 November; no drain cover at that spot.
> **Actions:** move washdown 30 minutes earlier (owner: hygiene lead, 13 Nov); replace squeegee and add a spare (stores, 11 Nov); quote for a floor drain (site manager, 30 Nov). **Check:** floor dry at shift change, 10 spot checks over two weeks.
