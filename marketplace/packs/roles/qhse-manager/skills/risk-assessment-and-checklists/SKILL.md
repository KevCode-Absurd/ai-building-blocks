# Risk assessment and checklists

## When to use

- The firm has no written risk assessment, or one copied from a template.
- New work, a new machine, a new site, new people or new substances.
- After an incident or near miss, or when workers report a problem.
- A customer or principal contractor asks for risk assessments and method statements.

Not for: replacing a competent person's sign-off, site visits, or fire, gas or electrical certification. High-risk work (heights, electrics, gas, asbestos, confined spaces, hazardous substances, machinery guarding, lifting) is always marked for a competent person.

## What you need

1. The site, the tasks (including non-routine ones: cleaning, maintenance, breakdowns), the equipment and substances.
2. Who does the work: employees, agency staff, contractors, young workers, new or expectant mothers, people with disabilities, members of the public nearby.
3. Past incidents, near misses and worker concerns (names replaced by IDs).
4. Current controls and training records.
5. The firm's rating scale, if it has one.
6. Country, state and sector, which decide the rules and any specific standards.

**Access:** level 1 to read current assessments, incident logs and training records; level 2 to draft the assessment and build checklists in the inspection app unpublished; level 3 to publish approved checklists, schedule inspections for the agreed sites and log issues raised, within your limits.

## Steps

### 1. Walk the work, task by task

Without a site visit, I work from your task list, photos and notes, and I list what I can't see. For each task, ask the people who do it: what could go wrong, how badly, and what goes wrong when you're in a hurry? Include non-routine tasks and health hazards such as manual handling, noise, dust and work-related stress.

### 2. Who could be harmed and how

Name groups, not people: operators, cleaners, delivery drivers, visitors, the public. Note anyone at higher risk.

### 3. Current controls, then extra controls in order

Use the hierarchy of controls, in order of general effectiveness:
1. **Eliminate** the hazard (do the job another way, from the ground, off the road).
2. **Substitute** something less hazardous.
3. **Engineering controls** that separate people from the hazard (guards, barriers, extraction).
4. **Administrative controls** (procedures, permits, training, rotation, supervision).
5. **PPE** last, and not alone when a better control is possible.

In the UK, the duty is to reduce risk so far as reasonably practicable. In the US, check whether a specific OSHA standard applies and requires a written programme. In the EU, national law adds to the Framework Directive. Check this with local rules.

### 4. Rate the risk

Use the client's scale, or a simple 1 to 5 likelihood × 1 to 5 severity scale that I state in full on the page. Rate before and after the extra controls. The rating is a way to prioritise, not a legal threshold.

### 5. Name owners and dates

Every extra control gets an owner and a date. Record significant findings: in the UK this is required if you employ five or more people, and it's good practice anywhere.

### 6. Test that the controls are real

For each control ask: Is it in place today? Who checks it? Has everyone who relies on it been trained? A control the crew hasn't been trained in is a gap. HSE fined AJ Riley Landscapes and Building Limited £20,000 plus costs in November 2026 after a fall through a fragile roof; workers had not been trained in the controls their own risk assessment listed.

### 7. Turn controls into checklists

- **Daily pre-use checks** for equipment and areas: one line per check, what a pass looks like, what to do on a fail (stop, tag, tell whom).
- **Weekly or monthly inspections** for the area as a whole.
- Fails become issues with an owner and a date.

### 8. Feed the induction and toolbox talks

- **Induction:** site rules, emergency steps, first aid, the main hazards and controls for the new starter's tasks, PPE, how to report incidents and near misses, and who to ask.
- **Toolbox talk topics:** one per significant hazard, drawn from this assessment (see the `toolbox-talk-and-sign-in-sheet` prompt).

### 9. Set the review

Review when something changes, after an incident or near miss, when workers raise a concern, and on a set date.

## Checks

- Every hazard comes from this firm's tasks, not a template.
- Non-routine tasks and health hazards are included.
- Controls follow the hierarchy; no PPE-only control where a higher one is practicable.
- The rating scale is stated in full.
- Every extra control has an owner, a date and a check, and the people relying on it are trained.
- High-risk work is marked for a competent person.
- The document says what I couldn't see without a site visit.
- The document starts with: *Draft for review by a competent person. Check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

1. Scope: site, tasks, who did the assessment, date, what wasn't seen.
2. Rating scale.
3. Risk assessment table: task, hazard, who and how, current controls, rating, extra controls, rating after, owner, date.
4. "Controls to verify" list: control, how it's checked, training record.
5. Daily and weekly checklists.
6. Induction checklist.
7. Toolbox talk topic list.
8. Review triggers and next review date.

> **Scale:** likelihood 1 (rare) to 5 (almost certain) × severity 1 (first aid) to 5 (fatal or life-changing).

>
> **Controls to verify:** seat belt use (weekly spot check, logged); slope maps on every job sheet; all mower operators shown the slope map rule and signed for it.
