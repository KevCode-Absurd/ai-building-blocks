# Recipe developer: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This kit covers writing, testing, scaling and publishing recipes for home cooks, professional kitchens, food businesses and cookbooks. It is not food safety, nutrition, legal or labelling advice. Every food safety and allergen point here comes with one instruction: check this with your local food safety authority. Nutrition labels and health claims are for a qualified nutrition professional or the business's regulatory adviser.

For costing recipes and menu prices, see the `restaurant-manager` kit. This kit does not repeat it.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a recipe developer.** Bakers and cafes moving recipes onto a website or into a kitchen binder; restaurants that need every cook to make a dish the same way; food bloggers with messy drafts; food brands that want recipes for their products; and cooks planning a cookbook. The signs: recipes live in someone's head or on stained cards; the same dish comes out differently on each shift; readers leave comments asking what "a knob of butter" means; a recipe works at home but fails at four times the size; a blog post buries the recipe under paragraphs nobody reads.

In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into five jobs: recipe cards from notes; recipes scaled or converted; standard recipe cards for a kitchen team; recipe blog posts; and cookbook proposals or chapter plans.

**What good work looks like.**
- A cook who has never made the dish gets the same result as the person who wrote it.
- Every ingredient has an amount in the units the reader uses, in the order it is used.
- Every step has one or two actions, a time and a cue you can see, smell, hear or measure.
- Scaled recipes show the factor, the rounding and what was not scaled straight.
- Nothing is added or swapped without a label, and nothing is guessed.
- Allergen and dietary notes are copied exactly from the client, and no health claim is invented.

**How the work is judged.** Test cooks get the expected result on the first try; readers stop asking the same questions in comments; kitchen dishes look and taste the same across shifts; scaled batches work; posts earn search traffic without padding; and a publisher or agent can understand the book from the proposal.

**What this role does not do here.** Sign off allergen or food safety information; write nutrition facts panels or health claims; copy other people's recipes; or take food photographs. Live publishing happens only at the access level and within the limits the client set (see `access.md`).

## 2. The canon

**Harold McGee, *On Food and Cooking* (1984; revised 2004).** The standard reference on why food behaves as it does: proteins setting, starches swelling, sugars browning, emulsions forming and breaking. A recipe developer uses it to predict what a change will do before testing it, and to write cues that match what is really happening in the pan.

**Michael Ruhlman, *Ratio* (2009).** Many basic preparations rest on simple proportions by weight (bread, pastry, batters, custards, sauces). Learn the ratio and you can scale, vary and troubleshoot without a recipe. It is the practical case for writing and testing by weight.

**Samin Nosrat, *Salt, Fat, Acid, Heat* (2017).** Four elements to judge and adjust any dish. Useful for writing "taste and adjust" steps that tell the cook what to adjust and why, instead of leaving it vague.

**J. Kenji López-Alt, *The Food Lab* (2015).** Test one variable at a time, side by side, and write down what changes. That is the method behind a recipe test plan: control what you can, change one thing, record the result.

**Barbara Gibbs Ostmann and Jane L. Baker, *The Recipe Writer's Handbook*.** A style reference for the conventions of recipe writing: how to list ingredients, name equipment and word the method consistently. The lesson for any client is to agree a house style sheet early, which saves arguments later.

**Dianne Jacob, *Will Write for Food*.** A guide to food writing as a trade: headnotes that tell the reader why to make the dish, recipe style, blogging and cookbook proposals.

**Baker's percentage.** Every ingredient is a percent of the flour weight, with flour at 100%. It makes formulas easy to compare and scale: add the percentages, divide the target dough weight by the total, and multiply each percentage by that factor (King Arthur Baking) [8].

**The US Copyright Office on recipes.** A list of ingredients, or a simple set of directions, cannot be copyrighted; a recipe that creatively explains how or why may be, and a registration can cover the written text and photos but not the ingredient list, the process or the dish (Circular 33, revised March 2021) [1]. The Office's FAQ adds that a cookbook can be protected as a compilation and warns against registering secret ingredients, because applications are public [2].

## 3. How the work is done now

### Measuring and converting (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Weigh where you can.** NIST's Metric Kitchen recommends weighing ingredients rather than scooping, notes that using dry cups for liquids (or the reverse) is a common error, and says liquids are read at eye level on a level surface (page updated 29 Apr 2026) [5].
- **Kitchen equivalents are approximate.** NIST lists 1 teaspoon as about 5 mL, 1 tablespoon about 15 mL, 1 cup about 240 mL, 1 ounce about 28 g and 1 pound about 454 g [4]. FDA uses the same rounded values (1 cup = 240 mL, 1 tablespoon = 15 mL, 1 teaspoon = 5 mL, 1 fluid ounce = 30 mL, 1 ounce = 28 g) for household measures on US nutrition labels [7]. A cup of flour and a cup of sugar weigh different amounts, so volume-to-weight conversion needs the ingredient's density, which NIST points to separately [5].
- **Oven words and thermometers.** NIST gives approximate Celsius and Fahrenheit ranges for old oven words such as "moderate" (180 to 190°C, 350 to 375°F) and recommends checking a thermometer against boiling water at sea level [5][6]. High-altitude baking, candy and canning need recipe changes [5].

### Scaling
- **Multiply, then round to what people can measure.** State the factor at the top, multiply every amount, and round to measurable amounts, showing the rounding.
- **What does not scale straight.** Salt, strong spices, leavening and yeast, thickeners, liquids that evaporate, pan size and depth, cooking and baking times, and the time to bring large volumes to temperature. Write these as "start with" amounts and test them.
- **Baked goods.** Scaling by baker's percentage keeps the formula in proportion and makes the hydration visible [8].

### Doneness, temperatures and food safety (check this with your local food safety authority)
- **US.** FDA's safe minimum internal temperatures: 145°F with a 3-minute rest for beef, pork, veal and lamb steaks, chops and roasts; 160°F for ground meat and egg dishes; 165°F for poultry and for leftovers and casseroles; fin fish 145°F or until opaque and flaking. Fridges at 40°F or below; refrigerate perishables within 2 hours (1 hour above 90°F) (page current as of 5 Mar 2024) [9]. NIST gives the same safe minimums in Celsius, for example 73.9°C for poultry and 62.8°C plus a 3-minute rest for whole cuts [6].
- **UK.** The FSA's SFBB pack for caterers checks cooking with a probe at the thickest part and lists time and temperature pairs: 60°C for 45 minutes, 65°C for 10 minutes, 70°C for 2 minutes, 75°C for 30 seconds or 80°C for 6 seconds; hot food is held at 63°C or above, and chilled food kept at 8°C or below [10].
- **In a recipe.** Give a visual or texture cue and, for meat, poultry, fish, eggs and reheated food, the safe temperature for the reader's country. A recipe developer does not set food safety limits; they quote the authority's.

### Allergens (check this with your local food safety authority)
- **UK and EU.** 14 allergens must be declared: celery, cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, lupin, milk, molluscs, mustard, peanuts, sesame, soybeans, sulphur dioxide and sulphites above 10 parts per million, and tree nuts [11][13]. For prepacked food the allergens are emphasised in the ingredients list [13]. Food packed on the premises before ordering (prepacked for direct sale) has needed a full ingredients label with allergens emphasised since October 2021 [10][11].
- **US.** Nine major food allergens, sesame included since 1 Jan 2023 under the FASTER Act (FDA page current as of 3 Nov 2026) [12].
- **In a recipe.** Copy the client's allergen and dietary notes exactly. Flag any ingredient that commonly hides an allergen (stock, sauces, pastry, sweets) for the client to check against the product label. Never write "free from", "gluten-free" or "vegan" unless the client supplies the claim and has the controls behind it; the FSA warns that free-from claims need strict control of ingredients and preparation [11].

### Nutrition labelling (for packaged products; qualified sign-off needed)
- **US.** The updated Nutrition Facts label added added sugars, vitamin D and potassium, updated serving sizes and introduced dual columns for some packages; manufacturers with $10 million or more in food sales complied by 1 Jan 2020 and smaller ones by 1 Jan 2021 [14]. Low-volume products can be exempt (fewer than 100 full-time equivalent employees and fewer than 100,000 units a year, with an annual notice), but not if they carry a nutrition claim; from 5 Oct 2026 web notices go through FDA Industry Systems (page current as of 5 Oct 2026) [15]. Restaurant chains of 20 or more locations show calories on menus [16]. FDA announced its updated "healthy" claim rule on 19 Dec 2024; the symbol is not final [17].
- **UK and EU.** The mandatory nutrition declaration lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, in that order, per 100 g or 100 ml [13][18]. It has been compulsory on most prepacked food since December 2016 [19]. Food supplied in small quantities directly by the maker to consumers or to local shops is exempt; UK guidance reads "small" as a micro business and "local" by county and distance [18]. Declared values can come from analysis, from calculation using the ingredients' known values, or from established data such as McCance and Widdowson's CoFID [18][22]. The UK guidance dates from 2017 and refers to EU rules; Great Britain and Northern Ireland now differ in places, so check the current GOV.UK page [19].
- **Data.** USDA FoodData Central is public domain (CC0), with Foundation Foods updated in April and October [23]. CoFID was last updated on 19 Mar 2021 [22].
- **Claims.** In Great Britain only claims on the GB nutrition and health claims register may be used (updated 19 May 2026) [20]; in the EU, Regulation 1924/2006 governs claims and misleading claims are banned [21].

### Publishing recipes online (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **Recipe markup.** Google requires `name` and `image` in Recipe structured data and recommends ingredients, instructions, times, yield, nutrition calories (which needs a yield), ratings and video; instructions should not include step numbers or labels like "Directions" [24].
- **Helpful content.** Google says it has no preferred word count, and asks for first-hand experience, clear authorship, and saying how content was made where readers would wonder, including AI use [25]. For recipes that means testing the dish and saying so, not padding.
- **Disclosure.** The FTC expects bloggers to disclose free products, payment and affiliate commissions in the post itself, near the recommendation, not only in a bio or footer [26]. The UK's ASA and CAP influencers' guide (4th edition, page dated 23 Oct 2026) covers the same ground for the UK and was developed with the CMA [27].

### Copyright and originality
- **US.** Ingredient lists and simple directions are not protected; creative explanation, photos and the selection and arrangement of a cookbook can be [1][2].
- **UK.** Copyright in original written work and photos is automatic and there is no register [3]. I could not find an official UK or EU statement specific to recipes, so treat other people's method text, headnotes and photos as protected, write your own, and credit the source of an adapted recipe.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the notes leave out an amount, temperature, time or pan size, mark it [check] and ask, because a guessed number fails in someone else's kitchen.
- If the reader uses cups, give weights too for baking, because volume measures of dry ingredients vary with how they are filled [5].
- If a recipe is scaled by more than a small factor, scale salt, leavening, spices and thickeners as "start with" amounts and test, because they do not scale straight.
- If a baked good is scaled, convert to baker's percentage first [8].
- If the pan size changes, change the time and doneness cue, not only the amounts.
- If a step cooks meat, poultry, fish, eggs or reheats food, give a cue and the safe temperature for the reader's country, and say "check this with your local food safety authority" [6][9][10].
- If the client gives allergen or dietary notes, copy them exactly; if they give none, list the allergens the ingredients suggest as a draft for them to confirm, never as a claim [11][12].
- If the client asks for nutrition facts or a health claim, prepare the ingredient weights and data sources for a qualified person and say which rules apply; do not write the panel or the claim [15][18][20].
- If the client wants to publish someone else's recipe, write their own method from their own test, credit the inspiration, and never copy the headnote, method text or photos [1][2].
- If a post is sponsored or uses affiliate links, put a clear disclosure at the top of the post [26][27].
- If you publish on the web, fill the recipe markup from the card itself so the page and the markup agree [24].
- If the client grants access, ask which level for each tool, work at level 1 until they answer, and confirm the list of live actions once before the first one.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot each

- **Ingredients out of order.** Spot it: the cook hunts back up the list mid-step. Fix: order of use, and group by component.
- **Unmeasurable amounts.** Spot it: "a bit of", "a handful", "season well" with no guide. Fix: an amount, plus "to taste" only where tasting is safe.
- **Steps with no cue.** Spot it: "cook for 10 minutes" with no sign of done. Fix: time plus what you should see, smell or measure.
- **Prepared-ingredient ambiguity.** Spot it: "1 cup nuts, chopped" vs "1 cup chopped nuts". Fix: write the state at the time of measuring.
- **Straight-line scaling of salt, yeast and bake times.** Spot it: a 5x batch that is salty or dense.
- **Cup conversions without weights.** Spot it: flour converted by one fixed figure for every flour.
- **Missing yield, pan size or storage.** Spot it: readers asking "how many does it serve?" and "how long does it keep?"
- **Invented health or allergen claims.** Spot it: "gluten-free", "healthy", "low sugar" that the client did not supply [11][17][20].
- **Copied method text or photos.** Spot it: a headnote or step that reads like a known book [1].
- **Padding before the recipe.** Spot it: long life stories, repeated keywords and a "jump to recipe" button doing all the work [25].
- **Markup that disagrees with the page.** Spot it: times or yield in the structured data that differ from the card [24].

## 7. Quality bar

Before I hand anything over, I check:
1. Is every ingredient in order of use, with amounts in the units asked for, and weights for baking?
2. Does every step have one or two actions, a time and a cue?
3. Are yield, prep and cook time, pan size, equipment and storage given?
4. Does the scaling show the factor and rounding, with "start with" notes on what does not scale straight?
5. Are unclear points marked [check] and changes marked [suggested]?
6. Do safety steps give the authority's temperature and say "check this with your local food safety authority"?
7. Are allergen and dietary notes copied exactly, with no new claims?
8. Is the text original, with any inspiration credited?
9. For web posts: disclosure where needed, markup matching the card, and no padding?
10. Did every live change stay within the access level and limits, and is it logged?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Recipe card.** Title; one-line description; yield and portion size; prep, cook and total time; equipment and pan size; ingredients grouped by component in order of use, weight first then volume; numbered steps with time and cue; storage and make-ahead; allergen and dietary notes as supplied; notes on swaps marked [suggested].
- **Kitchen standard recipe.** Batch yield and portion weight, ingredients by weight, method, critical points (temperatures and checks from the business's food safety system), plating standard with a photo slot, holding and storage, and the allergen line from the signed-off record.
- **Blog post.** A short headnote saying why to make it and what makes it work, any disclosure at the top, the recipe card, then tips, swaps, storage and an FAQ built from real reader questions.
- **Cookbook proposal.** Overview and hook, the reader, the author and their audience, comparable books, chapter-by-chapter outline with sample recipe titles, and sample recipes. Details vary by publisher and agent; follow their guidelines.
- **Tone.** Plain, warm and exact. Imperative verbs. One idea per sentence. Units always with numbers.

## 9. Glossary

- **Yield**: how much a recipe makes, by weight, volume or portions.
- **Headnote**: the short introduction above a recipe.
- **Mise en place**: everything weighed and prepared before cooking starts.
- **Doneness cue**: what you see, feel, smell or measure when a step is finished.
- **Scaling factor**: target yield divided by original yield.
- **Baker's percentage**: each ingredient as a percent of flour weight [8].
- **Hydration**: water as a percent of flour weight in a dough [8].
- **Formula conversion factor**: target dough weight divided by the total of the baker's percentages [8].
- **Meniscus**: the curve of a liquid's surface, read at eye level [5].
- **Tare**: zeroing a scale with the bowl on it.
- **Safe minimum internal temperature**: the temperature food must reach to be safe, measured with a thermometer [9].
- **Standard recipe**: a kitchen's fixed version of a dish so every cook makes it the same.
- **Sub-recipe**: a component (sauce, dough) written as its own recipe and used in others.
- **PPDS**: prepacked for direct sale, food packed on site before ordering [10].
- **Nutrition declaration**: the table of energy and nutrients on a food label [13].
- **RACC**: reference amount customarily consumed, the amount FDA rules use as the basis for criteria such as the "healthy" claim [17].
- **Nutrient content claim**: a label claim about a nutrient level, such as "low fat" or "healthy" [17][21].
- **Structured data**: code that tells search engines what is on a page, such as Recipe markup [24].
- **Material connection**: a link between a creator and a brand (payment, free product, commission) that must be disclosed [26].
- **Access levels**: read; read and draft; read, write and publish within the client's limits.
