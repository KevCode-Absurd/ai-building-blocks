# Recipe scaling and conversion

## When to use

- A kitchen needs a 4-litre soup as 20 litres, or 12 scones as 60.
- A recipe must fit a different pan or tray.
- A website needs metric and US versions of the same recipe.
- A baker wants a dough formula they can scale to any batch weight.

Do not use it to change the recipe's character without saying so, or to set food safety limits for larger batches. Cooling and holding big batches safely follows the business's food safety system: check this with your local food safety authority.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the recipe, the target yield, and any test results.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** the scaled version saved as a separate draft or a scaling sheet in a copy.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** save the tested scaled version as the new master with a date, or publish the converted card on the website.

## What you need

1. The original recipe with yield, pan size and every amount.
2. The target: yield, batch weight, number of portions or new pan size.
3. Equipment for the new batch: pot or mixer size, oven, trays.
4. Units wanted: metric weights, US cups and spoons, or both; Celsius, Fahrenheit or both.
5. The client's own weights for a cup of each dry ingredient, if they have measured them.

## Steps

### 1. State the factor
Factor = target yield divided by original yield. For pans, use the ratio of areas (length times width, or pi times radius squared) at the same depth. Write the factor at the top.

### 2. Convert to weight first
Convert volume amounts to weight before scaling, using the client's own weighing or, if none, flag the conversion as approximate. Kitchen equivalents (about 240 mL a cup, 15 mL a tablespoon, 5 mL a teaspoon, 28 g an ounce) are rounded values.

### 3. For doughs and batters, use baker's percentage
Express each ingredient as a percent of the flour weight. To scale: add up the percentages, divide the target dough weight by that total to get the flour weight, then multiply each percentage by the flour weight. Round up slightly to allow for loss in the bowl.

### 4. Multiply every amount
Then round to amounts people can measure: grams to the nearest 5 or 10 for large amounts, whole eggs (or weigh beaten egg), spoons to the nearest quarter. Show the rounding.

### 5. Flag what does not scale straight
- Salt, strong spices, chilli: give a "start with" amount below the straight figure, then taste and adjust.
- Leavening and yeast: scale by baker's percentage; in very large batches, the time dough or batter sits before baking matters; mix in batches the equipment can handle.
- Thickeners and gelling agents: start with the straight figure and test the set.
- Liquids that reduce: a deeper pot evaporates less per litre, so start with less liquid and adjust.
- Cooking and baking times: bigger pans and deeper trays take longer; give the cue and a check time, not just a new number.
- Heating and cooling large volumes: follow the business's cooling method and temperature checks.

### 6. Convert units for the reader
Give both units if asked, with the weight as the master. Convert oven temperatures and say whether the oven is fan or conventional.

### 7. Plan a test
Mark the scaled recipe "untested at this size" until it is made once; list what to check (taste, texture, time, yield).

## Checks

- The factor is stated and correct.
- Every amount is multiplied, and the rounding is shown.
- Volume-to-weight conversions name their source or the client's own weighing.
- Items that do not scale straight are flagged with "start with" amounts.
- Pan, time and cue changes are covered, not just amounts.
- Big-batch cooling and holding follow the business's own system: check this with your local food safety authority.
- The scaled version is marked untested until a test confirms it.

## Output format

1. Header: original yield, target yield, factor, equipment
2. Table: ingredient, original amount, straight scaled amount, rounded amount to use, note
3. Method changes (times, cues, batching)
4. Test plan for the first run
5. Questions marked [check]

> **Method changes:** simmer time will be longer to bring 20 L to a simmer; use the cue "onions soft and translucent, no colour" and "soup thick enough to coat a spoon". Cool and store using your kitchen's big-batch cooling method and checks [check this with your local food safety authority].
