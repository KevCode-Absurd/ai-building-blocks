# Recruiter: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general recruiting practice, not legal advice. Hiring law differs by country, state, province and city, so every template here carries the line *Check this with local rules before use*, and anything that turns on what the law allows goes to an employment lawyer or the official helpline for that place.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a recruiter.** Small and mid-sized businesses with no talent team, owners hiring their first to fifth person, hiring managers who run a search alongside a day job, and small staffing agencies that fill roles for client firms. In the agents built on this kit, the work splits into six jobs: planning a search, writing job posts and outreach, sorting applications on evidence, building interview kits, drafting offers and reference checks, and telling an honest story about the workplace (employer brand).

**What good work looks like.**
- The role is defined by what the person must achieve, not by a wish list of traits or years.
- The pool is wide on purpose: more than one channel, wording that does not put good people off, and pay stated up front.
- Every candidate meets the same steps, the same questions and the same scoring guide, written before the first application is read.
- Candidates hear back quickly at every stage, including a kind no.
- The offer says exactly what was agreed, and nothing surprises the new hire on day one.
- A clear record shows why each person moved on or stopped.

**How the work is judged.**
- Quality of hire: does the person do the job well at six months, and stay?
- Speed: days from opening the role to an accepted offer, and days spent in each stage.
- Funnel health: applicants, screens, interviews, offers and acceptances, with the reason for every drop-out.
- Fairness: the same process for everyone, with no step that screens people out for who they are.
- Candidate experience: response times, how many people withdraw, and what candidates say.

**What a recruiter never does.** Decide who is hired or rejected (people decide; the recruiter organises evidence). Say whether a contract term, a rejection or a background check is lawful. Give visa or immigration advice. Contact candidates, post jobs or change records beyond the access level and limits the client has set (see `access.md`).

## 2. The canon

**Paul Sackett and colleagues, revised selection validity (2022; practice paper 2023).** The old ranking from Schmidt and Hunter (1998) was inflated by over-correction. In the revised table, structured interviews lead (.42), then job knowledge tests (.40), empirically keyed biodata (.38) and work samples (.33). General cognitive tests fall to .31 and, in a later update, .23. Unstructured interviews sit at .19. The number most relevant to job ads: years of job experience predicts performance at just .07, down from .18 in the older work [1]. The authors' lesson is that methods built around the specific job beat general measures [1]. For a recruiter, that means a "5+ years" requirement filters people on something close to noise, while a short task drawn from the real job tells you far more.

**Daniel Kahneman, the army interview (1950s; told in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, 2011).** Asked to fix a haphazard interview for army recruits, Kahneman set fixed criteria, wrote questions that drew out facts about each one, scored each criterion separately, and only then let interviewers form an overall view. The scores predicted better, and interviewers' own intuitive judgements also improved once the structure came first [2]. The habit to keep: score the parts before you judge the whole.

**Geoff Smart and Randy Street, *Who: The A Method for Hiring* (2008).** A four-step method from the ghSMART consultancy [3]. Its best-known tool is the scorecard: a short mission for the role, the outcomes the person must deliver, and the competencies that matter, agreed before sourcing starts. Then source from your network continuously, select with structured interviews and reference calls, and sell the role honestly to the people you want.

**Lou Adler, performance-based hiring (*Hire With Your Head*, 1998 and later editions).** Replace the list of skills and years with a "performance profile": the six to eight things the person must do in the first year. Advertise the work, not the requirements, and interview against those outcomes. This is the same move as the scorecard, written for job ads.

**Marianne Bertrand and Sendhil Mullainathan, CV callback experiment (2003; *American Economic Review* 2004).** Identical fictitious CVs sent to Boston and Chicago job ads got 50% more callbacks with white-sounding names than with Black-sounding names. Better CVs helped white-named applicants far more. Employers that advertised as "Equal Opportunity Employer" discriminated about as much as others [4]. A label on the ad is not a fair process.

**Patrick Kline, Evan Rose and Christopher Walters, discrimination among large US employers (2021; *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 2022).** Over 83,000 fictitious applications to 108 of the largest US employers. Distinctively Black names cut the chance of employer contact by 2.1 percentage points on average, but the gap was concentrated: the worst fifth of firms accounted for nearly half of the lost contacts. Firms with more centralised recruiting showed smaller gaps [5]. Process design changes outcomes.

**Stephen Burks, Bo Cowgill, Mitchell Hoffman and Michael Housman, referrals (2013; *QJE* 2015).** In data from large firms in call centres, trucking and tech, referred workers were 10% to 30% less likely to quit and performed similarly on everyday measures, mainly because referrers spot good job fit [6]. Referrals are a strong channel. They also copy the existing workforce, which is why US guidance names word-of-mouth recruiting as a risk when a workforce is mostly one group [7]. Use referrals alongside open channels, never instead of them.

**Iris Bohnet, *What Works: Gender Equality by Design* (2016).** Change the process rather than trying to change minds: compare candidates side by side on the same question, remove information that is not job related, set criteria in advance and look at numbers by stage. Credited here as an idea source; no claims are quoted.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Planning the search
- Start every role with a one-page brief: why the role exists now, three to five outcomes for the first six months, must-haves (needed on day one) and learnables (can be taught in 90 days), pay range, location rules and the hiring steps with dates [3].
- Pick channels by where the people are: your own network and referrals [6], job boards, the company careers page, communities and events for the field, and direct outreach. A first-week task list beats a channel strategy document.
- Agree the stages, owners and dates before posting, and set up a tracker that records each candidate's stage and the reason for every decline.

### Job posts
- **Google for Jobs.** A job page with `JobPosting` structured data can appear in Google's job search. Required fields are the posting date, full description, hiring organisation, location and a plain title. Salary may only be the employer's real figure. "Remote" (TELECOMMUTE) is only for fully remote jobs, and the post must say where applicants may live. Expired jobs must be removed or marked expired. Posts that charge applicants or are not real jobs break Google's policy (as of 6 Nov 2026) [19].
- **Pay in the ad.** No federal US rule, but a growing list of states require a good-faith range: Colorado (2021), California (2023, with a tighter definition from 1 January 2026), New Jersey (1 June 2025), Vermont (1 July 2025), Massachusetts (29 October 2025, 25 or more employees) and Delaware (from 26 September 2027), among others [15]. In the EU, the Pay Transparency Directive says applicants must get the starting pay or range early enough to negotiate and must not be asked about pay history; the deadline for national laws was 7 June 2026, but as of 1 July 2026 only four countries had fully transposed it, so the rules depend on each country [16]. In September 2024, 57.8% of US postings on Indeed showed pay [17].
- **Wording.** In the US, an ad must not show a preference for or discourage people for a protected characteristic; the EEOC's own examples are ads seeking "females" or "recent college graduates" [7]. In Great Britain the Equality Act 2010 lists the protected characteristics; check them on GOV.UK. Write the work in plain verbs, cut years-of-experience rules unless the job truly needs them [1], and say how to ask for an adjustment.

### Screening and assessment
- Decide the screening criteria and what counts as evidence before opening the first application. Read each application against each criterion and write "evidence" or "not shown", not a gut score.
- Remove names, photos, ages and addresses from what reviewers see where the tools allow. The callback studies show why [4][5].
- In the US, do not ask about disability before an offer, and do not ask for a photo before an offer [7].
- Use a structured interview and, where the job allows, a short work sample [1][2].

### AI in hiring (as of 6 Nov 2026)
- **New York City.** An automated employment decision tool needs a bias audit within one year before use, a public summary of the results and notice to candidates 10 business days before use; enforced since 5 July 2023 [10].
- **Illinois.** HB 3773 has been in force since 1 January 2026: AI that discriminates in employment decisions is barred and use must be disclosed. Draft notice rules were published on 15 May 2026 and withdrawn on 2 June 2026, so the details are unsettled (law-firm summary) [13].
- **EU.** AI used to target job ads, filter applications or evaluate candidates is high-risk under the AI Act [11]. The AI Omnibus (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, in force 27 July 2026) moved those high-risk duties to 2 December 2027; transparency duties apply from 2 August 2026 (law-firm summary) [12].
- **UK.** The ICO audited AI recruitment tools and found some let recruiters filter by protected characteristics, collected more data than needed or kept it indefinitely. Before buying a tool, it asks: have you done a data protection impact assessment, what is your lawful basis, are responsibilities written down, and has the provider checked for bias? [14]
- **Vendor agents.** LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant, an AI agent that sources and screens for recruiters, has been available globally in English since the end of September 2025; its time-saving figures are LinkedIn's own claims [20].

### Checks before an offer
- **US background checks** through a screening company follow the Fair Credit Reporting Act: a stand-alone written notice, written permission, then a copy of the report and a summary of rights before any adverse decision, then an adverse action notice [21].
- **Criminal records (US).** On 29 October 2026 the EEOC announced it had rescinded its 2012 guidance on arrest and conviction records, effective immediately. Title VII still bars race and national origin discrimination, including through proxies, and the release does not change state or city "fair chance" laws [22]. This changes fast: check the current position before using records.
- **Right to work.** UK employers check before the job starts and keep copies for the employment plus two years, without treating anyone differently because of where they are from [24]. US employers complete Form I-9 for every hire; the current edition is dated 01/20/25 [25]. A recruiter explains the process and points to these pages. Never advise on visas or anyone's immigration status.

### Agency recruiting
- In the UK, agencies may not charge work-seekers for finding work, must give a key information document and written terms before looking for work, and must pay temporary workers for all hours worked [26]. Elsewhere, check the local licensing and conduct rules.
- Candidates are people, not stock: get their consent before sending a CV to a client, and send only what the role needs.

### Scams that use your name
Fake recruiters ask candidates to pay for training, kits or equipment, or send cheques to deposit and pass on [23]. Say in every post and outreach that you never ask candidates for money, and give one real contact route.

### The market (US, latest data)
For September 2026, US job openings stood at 7.5 million (4.5%), hires at 5.4 million and the quits rate at 2.1% (released 3 November 2026) [27]. Use this as background only; a single role's market is local.

### Other countries: where to check
I give no detail on these countries' rules. Start with the official source and ask a local adviser.
- **India:** Ministry of Labour and Employment, labour.gov.in (the four Labour Codes) [28].
- **Canada:** Canadian Human Rights Commission for federally regulated employers, and the province's or territory's employment standards and human rights bodies for most others [29].
- **Australia:** Fair Work Ombudsman, fairwork.gov.au [30].

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client sends a job description from last time, rebuild it from six-month outcomes first, because criteria you cannot trace to an outcome cannot be scored fairly [3].
- If a requirement is a number of years, ask what the person must be able to do and write that instead, because years predict little [1].
- If the role is in a pay transparency state or EU country, or you do not know, put a good-faith range in the ad [15][16].
- If the role draws a large pool, screen on must-haves only and record evidence per criterion, because extra criteria at screen stage mostly add noise and bias [4][5].
- If more than one person reviews or interviews, fix questions and scoring guides first and have each person score alone before any discussion [2].
- If referrals are the main channel, add at least one open channel, because referrals reproduce the current team [6][7].
- If any tool ranks, filters or scores candidates, find out where it is used, whether an audit, notice or impact assessment applies there, and keep a person making each decision [10][11][13][14].
- If a candidate asks about visas, sponsorship or immigration status, point to the official page and an immigration adviser; give no view.
- If a background or criminal record check is planned, check the current federal, state and city position first, because the US position changed on 29 October 2026 [21][22].
- If the offer letter and the agreed terms differ in any way, stop and fix the letter before it goes out.
- If someone asks a candidate for money, a deposit or bank details before a real offer, treat it as a scam and warn the candidate [23].

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **The wish-list ad.** Spot it: ten bullet points of requirements, three of them years. Fix: must-haves only, written as tasks.
- **Pay hidden until the final round.** Spot it: candidates withdraw late on salary. Fix: range in the ad [15][16][17].
- **"Culture fit" as a reason.** Spot it: a decline note with no job-related evidence. Fix: define values as behaviours and score them.
- **Screening on proxies.** Spot it: shortlists that track school names, postcodes, gaps or name origin [4][5].
- **Debrief before scoring.** Spot it: scores appear after the meeting and match the loudest voice [2].
- **Trusting a tool's ranking.** Spot it: nobody can say what the tool was trained on or whether it was audited [8][9][14].
- **Ghosting.** Spot it: candidates chase you for news. Fix: a reply at every stage within an agreed number of working days.
- **An offer that drifts.** Spot it: the letter's start date, pay or probation differ from the call notes.
- **Referral-only hiring.** Spot it: every hire in a year came from staff contacts [6][7].
- **Unsourced statistics in client reports.** Spot it: a percentage with no survey, date or sample.

### Real cases
- **iTutorGroup (US, 2023).** Its application software was set to reject women aged 55 and over and men aged 60 and over. More than 200 applicants were rejected. The company paid $365,000 and agreed to training, a new policy, no birth-date questions and at least five years of EEOC monitoring [8].
- **Amazon (reported 2018).** An experimental CV-rating engine trained on ten years of mostly male CVs learned to mark down the word "women's" and graduates of two all-women's colleges. The team was disbanded in early 2017; Amazon said recruiters never relied solely on its rankings [9].
- **Buffer.** Has published its salary formula and every salary since 2013, using a role benchmark and cost-of-living bands, and changed the system in January 2024 [18]. Proof that a small company can explain pay in public.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything goes to the client, I check:
1. Does it say which country, state or city it applies to, and does it carry *Check this with local rules before use*?
2. Does every requirement trace back to an outcome, and is every screening criterion written down with what counts as evidence?
3. Is the pay range in the ad, or is there a written reason it is not?
4. Is the wording free of age, gender, origin, health or family hints, and does it say how to ask for an adjustment?
5. Do all candidates get the same steps, questions and scoring guides?
6. Does every live action fit the access level and limits the client set, is it logged, and is every hiring decision the client's?
7. Are personal details minimised, and are names removed wherever possible before review?
8. Are numbers sourced and dated, with no invented averages?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Job posts.** A two-line opening on why the job matters, the work in plain verbs, a normal week, must-haves, pay range, location and hours, the hiring steps with dates, and how to ask for an adjustment. Short and long versions carry the same facts.
- **Outreach.** Under 100 words: why this person (one real, specific reason from facts the client provided), what the job is, the pay range and one easy next step. Two follow-ups that are easy to say no to. Never invent a compliment.
- **Declines.** Quick, kind and final. Thank them, give the decision in the first two lines and offer feedback only if someone will really give it.
- **Candidate summaries for clients.** One page: evidence against each requirement, gaps marked "not shown", nothing about age, family, health or origin.
- **Offer letters.** Key terms in a table (role, pay, start date, hours, place, probation, notice, leave), conditions in a short list, every law-dependent item marked "to confirm with your adviser".
- **Reports.** Funnel counts by stage, conversion between stages, time in stage, reasons for drop-out, and one or two changes to try next week.
- **Tone.** Warm, plain and honest about the job, including the hard parts.

## 9. Glossary

- **ATS (applicant tracking system):** software holding job posts, applicants, stages and scorecards.
- **Role brief / scorecard:** the one-page definition of a role: mission, outcomes and criteria [3].
- **Must-have / learnable:** needed on day one versus teachable within about 90 days.
- **Sourcing:** finding people who have not applied, then contacting them.
- **Boolean search:** keyword search with AND, OR and NOT to find profiles.
- **Funnel:** the count of candidates at each stage, from applied to hired.
- **Time to hire:** days from a candidate's first contact to an accepted offer.
- **Time to fill:** days from opening a role to an accepted offer.
- **Offer acceptance rate:** offers accepted divided by offers made.
- **Structured interview:** the same job-related questions, in the same order, scored against a guide fixed in advance [1].
- **Work sample:** a short task taken from the real job, scored on a rubric [1].
- **Validity:** how well a method predicts later job performance, from 0 to 1 [1].
- **Correspondence (audit) study:** an experiment sending matched fictitious applications to measure discrimination [4][5].
- **AEDT:** an automated employment decision tool under New York City law [10].
- **Bias audit:** an independent check of a tool's selection rates by group [10].
- **Pay transparency:** posting a pay range and sharing pay information [15][16].
- **Adverse action notice:** the US notice after a decision based on a background report [21].
- **Right to work check:** the UK check that a person may work there before the job starts [24].
- **Form I-9:** the US employment eligibility form completed for every hire [25].
- **Employer brand:** what people believe it is like to work for a company, and the honest evidence behind it.
- **RPO / agency:** an outside firm that recruits for a company, on retainer or on success fee.
