# Evidence-based screening

## When to use

- A role has drawn more applications than the team can read carefully.
- Shortlists have been built on impressions, school names or "seems sharp".
- Interviewers each ask their own questions and the debrief turns into a debate.
- A client or manager wants a fair, explainable record of why people moved on.

Do not use it to decide who is rejected or hired. It sorts evidence for a person to decide. Do not feed it data about protected characteristics, health or family.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** applications, CVs and scorecards in the ATS.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** add evidence notes, the scorecard template and interview kits in the ATS, without moving anyone.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** move candidates between stages and send the matching update or decline template, only after the hiring manager has decided on each person, within the confirmed limits. Log every move.

## What you need

1. The agreed role brief: outcomes and four to six criteria, each marked must-have or learnable.
2. The applications, with names, photos, dates of birth, addresses and links to social profiles removed where possible. Replace names with IDs.
3. Who will interview, how long each round is, and the decision date.
4. Any screening tool already in use, and where the role is based, so the tool rules can be checked.

## Steps

### 1. Set the screening criteria

Use only the must-haves. For each one, write what counts as evidence: a project, a result, a responsibility held, a skill shown in a sample. Write what does not count: job titles alone, school names, years alone, gaps in work history. Agree this with the hiring manager before reading a single application.

### 2. Read each application against each criterion

For each applicant and each criterion, write the evidence in a few words, or "not shown". Do not infer from gaps, school or employer prestige, writing style in a non-native language, or anything outside the criteria. Callback studies show identical CVs are treated differently by name; the fix is to judge the evidence row by row.

### 3. Group, do not rank

Sort applicants into three groups: "review first" (evidence for every must-have), "review next" (evidence for most, one unclear), and "not enough evidence". For "review next", write the one question that would settle the gap; a short screening call can ask it. A person reviews all three groups before anyone is declined.

### 4. Write interview questions

For each criterion, one main question and two follow-ups:
- **Past behaviour:** "Tell me about a time you..." for skills shown before.
- **Situation:** "Imagine that..." for situations the person has not met yet.
- **Follow-ups:** "What did you do first?" "What happened next?" "What would you change?"

Add a short work sample where the job allows: the same brief, data and time limit for everyone, under about an hour or paid. Structured interviews and work samples predict job performance better than unstructured chats.

### 5. Build the scorecard

One row per criterion, a 1 to 4 scale with a plain example of each level, and a notes cell for what the candidate actually said or did:
- 1: no evidence, or evidence of the opposite.
- 2: some evidence, but key steps missing.
- 3: clear evidence that meets the standard.
- 4: clear evidence above the standard, with results.

### 6. Plan the rounds

Who asks which questions, in what order, for how long. Opening script (who is in the room, how long, what happens next) and closing script (their questions, the reply date). Every candidate gets the same questions in the same order.

### 7. Score alone, then compare

Each interviewer fills the scorecard alone, straight after the interview, before any discussion. Then build the comparison sheet from the scorecards only: one column per finalist, one row per criterion, the score and the evidence behind it, gaps marked. Discuss only the rows where scores differ by two or more points.

### 8. Record the reasons

One short paragraph per finalist: the evidence for and against on each criterion. If a tool was used anywhere in the process, note which tool, at which step, and that a person made the decision.

## Checks before handing over

- Every criterion comes from the brief, and every question maps to one criterion.
- No row mentions age, gender, origin, religion, health, family, appearance or accent.
- "Not shown" is used instead of guesses.
- Nobody is declined by the sort alone; a person reviewed every group before any stage move or decline went out.
- Scores were entered before the debrief.
- If an automated tool screened or ranked anyone, its use is noted and the client has checked the rules where the role is based (for example New York City's bias audit and notice rules, or Illinois notice rules).
- The document carries: *Check this with local rules before use.*

## Output format

1. Screening criteria with what counts as evidence
2. Application sort table (ID, criterion 1 to 6 evidence, group, question to settle a gap)
3. Interview questions with follow-ups
4. Work sample brief and scoring guide
5. Scorecard (criterion, 1 to 4 guide, notes)
6. Round plan with opening and closing scripts
7. Finalist comparison sheet
8. Decision record template

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Northgate Analytics, a 30-person data consultancy in Manchester, UK, hiring a junior analyst.*

> **Template: check this with local rules before use. Applies to: Manchester, UK, hybrid.**
>
> | ID | Cleans messy data | Explains findings to non-experts | Group | Question to settle a gap |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | A-014 | Built a cleaned dataset from three council spreadsheets for a volunteer group | Wrote a one-page summary for the group's trustees | Review first | None |
> | A-022 | Final-year project: merged survey files, handled missing values | Not shown | Review next | "Tell me about a time you explained a result to someone without a data background." |
> | A-031 | Not shown | Not shown | Not enough evidence | None |
>
> **Scorecard row, "Explains findings to non-experts":** 1 uses jargon throughout; 2 gives the result but not what it means; 3 gives the result, what it means and one action; 4 does all that and checks what the listener understood.
