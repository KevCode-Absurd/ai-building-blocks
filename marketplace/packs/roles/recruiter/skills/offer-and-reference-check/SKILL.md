# Offer and reference check

## When to use

- The client has chosen a finalist and wants the offer ready.
- An offer letter exists, often copied from an old hire, and needs checking against what was agreed on the call.
- The client wants reference calls that tell them something useful.
- The client is nervous about the offer call or a likely counter-offer.

Do not use it to decide pay, to judge whether a term is lawful, or to sign anything off. A qualified employment lawyer or HR adviser reviews the final letter.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the offer template, call notes and emails with the agreed terms.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** create the offer letter and reference sheets in the client's documents or ATS, and save the offer email in drafts.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** send reference requests to referees the candidate agreed to, and send the offer. An offer is outside any standing limit: I ask again for a yes on that candidate's final letter before it goes, even at level 3. Log every action.

## What you need

1. The agreed terms, in the client's words: role title, pay and how it is paid, start date, hours and pattern, place of work, probation, holiday or paid time off, notice, benefits, and any conditions (references, background check, right to work check).
2. The client's offer letter template, if one exists.
3. Notes from the final interview and any promises made to the candidate.
4. The referees the candidate has agreed to, and the candidate's consent to contact them.
5. What the client can move in a negotiation (pay, start date, title, remote days) and what they cannot.

## Steps

### 1. List the agreed terms

Put the terms in a table: term, what was agreed, where it came from (call notes, email), and status (confirmed or [CLIENT TO CONFIRM]). Do not fill a gap with a guess.

### 2. Draft the offer

- Open warmly with the role and the start date.
- Put the key terms in a short table.
- List the conditions plainly: what must happen before the offer is final and by when.
- Say how to accept and by what date.
- Close with who to contact.

Mark every item that depends on local law with "to confirm with your adviser": probation length and effect, notice periods, at-will or contract wording, restrictive covenants, benefits eligibility, overtime status, and any background or right to work check. In the US, a background check through a screening company has its own notice and consent steps. In the UK, the right to work check happens before the job starts.

### 3. Check an existing letter

Compare the letter, line by line, with the agreed terms table. List:
- **Mismatches:** the letter says something different from what was agreed.
- **Gaps:** an agreed term is missing.
- **Extras:** the letter adds a term nobody discussed, such as a non-compete or a clawback.
- **Unclear wording:** a phrase a candidate could read two ways.

Rank them: mismatches first, then extras, then gaps, then unclear wording.

### 4. Write reference questions

Six to eight questions about the work, asked the same way for every finalist:
- How do you know the candidate, and for how long?
- What were they responsible for?
- What result are you proudest of them for?
- How did they handle a hard deadline or a mistake?
- How did they work with others?
- What support helped them do their best work?
- Would you work with them again? Why?

For each, note what to listen for, such as specific examples rather than general praise. Never ask about health, family, age, absence for protected reasons or anything else unrelated to the job. Ask the client to record answers on a sheet, not from memory.

### 5. Prepare the offer call

- A warm opening and the headline terms.
- The reasons the client chose them, based on the evidence from the process.
- Likely questions with honest answers: start date, remote days, growth, training, what happens in probation.
- Counter-offer plan: what can move, by how much (the client sets this), and what cannot. A short line for "Let me check and come back to you by [time]".
- What happens next: written offer, checks, first day.

### 6. Close the loop

Draft the decline notes for the other finalists, to send only after the offer is accepted. Remind the client to keep notes and scorecards for the period their local record rules require, then delete them.

## Checks before handing over

- Every term in the letter matches the agreed terms table, or is flagged.
- No gap is filled by a guess.
- Every law-dependent item says "to confirm with your adviser".
- Reference questions are job-related and the same for every finalist.
- The offer call notes make no promise the client has not approved.
- The letter is in plain words a candidate can read in two minutes.
- The document carries: *Check this with local rules before use.* and notes that a qualified employment lawyer or HR adviser should review the final letter.

## Output format

1. Agreed terms table (term, agreed, source, status)
2. Offer letter draft
3. Letter check: mismatches, extras, gaps, unclear wording
4. Reference questions with what to listen for, and a record sheet
5. Offer call notes and counter-offer plan
6. Decline notes for other finalists (send after acceptance)
7. Open questions for the client and for their adviser

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Copperleaf Kitchens, a 22-person kitchen fitting firm in Leeds, UK, hiring a project coordinator.*

> | Term | Agreed | Source | Letter says | Status |
