# Literature search and review

## When to use

- A client has a question that many studies touch ("Does remote work change productivity in small firms?") and wants to know what the evidence says overall.
- A client has a pile of papers and wants them sorted, compared and summed up.
- Two sources disagree and the client needs to know which to trust for their case.

This is a practical review for decisions, not a formal systematic review for publication. If the client needs a publishable systematic review, I follow the Cochrane Handbook and PRISMA 2020 and say that it will take far longer and needs a second screener.

## What you need

1. The question, and who will use the answer for what decision.
2. Any sources the client already has.
3. Whether web search is on. If it is off, I review only what is supplied and list what to look for.
4. Limits: years, countries, languages, types of study, and a deadline.

Access: level 1 (read) for searching and reading. Level 2 to save the evidence table and draft review in the client's documents or to add new items to a "to check" collection in their reference manager. Level 3, within the client's limits, to update a shared library or knowledge base.

## Steps

### 1. Frame the question

Break it into parts: who (population), what (exposure, product or practice), compared with what, and what outcome. Write inclusion and exclusion rules before reading anything: years, settings, designs, languages. Write what would change the client's decision.

### 2. Build the search (web search on)

- For each part, list synonyms and spelling variants. Join synonyms with OR inside brackets, and join the parts with AND.
- **PubMed** (health, life sciences, workplace health): add MeSH terms, use uppercase AND, OR, NOT, and filters for article type and dates. It gives citations and abstracts; full text is linked where available.
- **Google Scholar** (all fields): run the main string, then quoted key phrases; it caps results at 1,000 per query and is not complete, so do not treat it as the only database.
- **Semantic Scholar** (all fields): free, large, useful for related papers.
- **Grey literature** where it matters: government statistics, regulators, think tanks, trade bodies. Label each by who made it and why.
- **Snowball:** read the reference lists of the best three to five papers (backward) and their "Cited by" lists (forward).

Write everything in a **search log**: database, date, exact string, filters, results count, and how many were kept.

### 3. Supplied-only mode (web search off)

List every source received with its type and date. Do not add sources from memory. If something important seems missing (for example, no study after 2020), say so and suggest search terms the client can run.

### 4. Screen

Screen titles and abstracts against the rules from step 1, then read the full text of those kept. Record why each excluded source was excluded. Check each kept journal article for a retraction or correction notice (Crossref, journal page). Check that unfamiliar journals are real (hijacked journal list, Think. Check. Submit.). Label preprints.

### 5. Build the evidence table

Same fields for every source: citation, question, design, setting and sample, main finding with numbers, limits, funder, strength (strong, fair, weak) and one line on why.

### 6. Synthesise by theme

- Group sources by theme or outcome, not by the order they arrived.
- For each theme: what most sources say, which disagree, and a likely reason (sample, design, years, definitions, who funded it).
- Weigh by strength, not by count. Five weak studies agreeing do not beat one strong one that disagrees.
- Where studies are too different to combine, say so; do not average numbers from different designs.

### 7. Answer and gaps

Give the overall answer in two or three sentences with a confidence level (high, moderate, low, very low) and matching words ("probably", "may"). List gaps: what no source covers, and what to look for next.

## Checks

- Inclusion rules were written before screening.
- The search log is complete enough for someone else to rerun it.
- No source added that was not found in the log or supplied by the client.
- Every kept article was opened (or marked "abstract only").
- Retraction status checked for each journal article relied on.
- Disagreements shown with reasons; nothing averaged across incompatible studies.
- Confidence words match the evidence.
- No medical, legal or investment conclusion; those go to a qualified professional.

## Output format

1. Answer first, with confidence.
2. Themes, each with supporting and disagreeing sources.
3. Evidence table.
4. Gaps and next searches.
5. Method note and search log (or list of supplied sources).
6. Annotated source list if asked: two to four lines per source on what it says, how strong it is and how to use it.

## Example of good output

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a 12-person design agency asks whether a shorter working week changes output in small creative firms. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Answer (low confidence).** Across 9 sources kept from 63 found, small firms that cut hours often reported steady output, but most evidence is from volunteer pilots without comparison groups, and none studied agencies under 20 staff. A shorter week may keep output steady in firms like yours; a three-month internal trial with agreed measures would tell you more than further reading.
>
> **Theme 1: output.** 6 sources report stable or higher output (all before-and-after pilots, self-reported). 1 cohort study found a small fall in billable hours in consulting firms. Likely reason for the gap: self-reported versus timesheet measures.
>
> **Gaps:** no study of client response times; no data on firms under 20 staff.
>
> **Search log (extract):** Google Scholar, 4 Nov 2026, ("four-day week" OR "reduced working hours") AND (productivity OR output) AND (SME OR "small firm"), 2019 to 2026: 48 results, 7 kept.
