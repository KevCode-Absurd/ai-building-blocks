# Paper summary and evidence strength

## When to use

- Someone wants to quote or act on a study, report or white paper and needs to know what it really shows.
- A team needs reading notes on several papers, one entry each.
- A journal club or briefing needs one paper explained, with points to discuss.

Not for medical, legal or investment conclusions. If the paper is about a treatment, a product's health effect, or an investment, I summarise what was found and say plainly that a qualified professional should advise on what to do.

## What you need

1. The full paper or report (PDF or text). An abstract alone gives an "abstract only" summary, clearly labelled.
2. Who will read the summary and what they want to decide.
3. Supplementary files or the protocol, if the paper mentions them.
4. Whether web search is on, so I can check for corrections, retractions or later versions.

Access: level 1 (read) for everything here. Level 2 to save the brief as a draft in the client's documents or notes. Level 3, within the client's limits, to add it to a shared knowledge base or publish it to an internal page.

## Steps

### 1. Read in the right order

Methods, then limitations, then results tables, then the abstract and discussion. Abstracts and press releases tend to be the most confident parts.

### 2. Record the basics

| Field | What to write |
|---|---|
| Question | In one plain sentence |
| Design | Trial with random assignment, cohort, case-control, cross-sectional survey, qualitative, modelling, review |
| Who and where | Population, setting, country, years |
| How many | Sample size, and how many dropped out |
| Comparison | What the group was compared with, if anything |
| Main outcome | What was measured, how and when |
| Funding and conflicts | Who paid; any stated interests |

### 3. Note the findings exactly

Write the main results with the numbers as the authors give them, with units and the group they apply to. Do not round in a way that changes meaning. For each:
- Give the absolute and relative change if possible: 4% to 3% is a 1-point absolute fall and a 25% relative fall.
- Give the confidence interval. If it includes "no effect", the study did not show an effect, but it did not show there is none either.
- A small p-value says the data fit poorly with "no difference"; it does not say the effect is large or important.

### 4. Judge the strength

Look at:
- **Design:** random assignment and a comparison group allow causal claims; observational designs show links.
- **Size and precision:** small samples and wide intervals mean low precision.
- **Bias risks:** how people were chosen, who dropped out, whether outcomes were self-reported, whether measurers knew the groups.
- **Warning lights:** many outcomes tested with one highlighted, flexible analysis choices, a funder with an interest, a striking result in a crowded field.
- **Reporting:** does the paper report what its reporting guideline expects (CONSORT for trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, COREQ for interviews)?
- **Status:** retraction, correction or expression of concern (check Crossref or the journal page when web search is on); preprint or peer reviewed.

Give an overall rating with a reason: strong, fair or weak for this question. For health topics, use GRADE-style wording: "reduces" (high certainty), "probably reduces" (moderate), "may reduce" (low), "very uncertain" (very low).

### 5. Write what it does not show

Who the results may not apply to (other countries, ages, firm sizes, years). What was not measured. Whether it shows cause or only a link. What would be needed to know more.

### 6. Write for the reader

Answer first, in two or three sentences. Then the basics table. Then "how strong is this" and "what it does not show". Explain any term a reader may not know. Cite page or section for each key number. Quote only short phrases; everything else in my own words.

### 7. For several papers

One entry per paper using the same fields, then a short paragraph on how they fit together: where they agree, where they differ and why.

## Checks

- Every number matches the paper, with page or table.
- Nothing comes from outside the paper unless labelled and cited.
- Cause is claimed only where the design supports it.
- Absolute numbers accompany relative ones.
- Certainty wording matches the strength rating.
- Retraction or correction status noted (or "not checked: web search off").
- No medical, legal or investment advice.

## Output format

1. Answer first (2 to 3 sentences).
2. Basics table.
3. Findings with numbers and page references.
4. How strong is this (rating and reasons).
5. What it does not show.
6. Terms explained.
7. Reference in the client's citation style.

## Example of good output

Illustrative structure, not a real business or report: a consultancy asks for a summary of a report, "Four-Day Week Pilot, 2026", for its leadership team. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Answer.** In this pilot, 38 UK firms that cut to a four-day week reported similar revenue over six months, and staff reported less burnout. Firms chose to take part and there was no comparison group, so this shows what happened in these firms, not that the four-day week caused it.
>
> | Question | Does a four-day week change revenue and staff wellbeing? |
> |---|---|
> | Design | Before-and-after pilot, no comparison group |
> | Who | 38 volunteer firms, mostly 10 to 50 staff, UK, Jan to Jun 2026 |
> | How many | 41 started, 38 finished; 2,104 staff surveys at start, 1,688 at end |
> | Main outcomes | Revenue (firm-reported), burnout score (staff survey) |
> | Funding | Paid for by a campaign group that supports shorter weeks (p. 2) |
>
> **How strong is this:** weak for "does it cause", fair for "what happened in volunteer firms". Volunteers may be firms that expected it to work, and a fifth of staff did not answer the final survey (p. 14).
>
> **What it does not show:** effects beyond six months, in firms with shift work, or outside the UK.
