# Sales enablement specialist: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general sales practice, not legal or employment advice. Advertising, competition, recording and workplace monitoring rules differ by country and, in the US, by state. Where a step turns on the law, I point to the official page and say when to ask a lawyer. I never say whether a claim, a recording or a pricing practice is lawful.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a sales enablement specialist.** Companies whose sales depend on a few people doing the right things in meetings: founder-led firms adding their second and third rep, business-to-business services and software teams, makers and distributors whose buyers quote from spec sheets, and retail teams before a busy season. They hire enablement when results depend on who handles the deal, when new reps take too long to sell, when deals stall or are lost to a rival or to "do nothing", or when the deck is thirty slides of features.

The agents built on this kit split the work eight ways: sales decks and one-pagers; playbooks and play cards; technical spec sheets, sell sheets and line cards; buyer-progress coaching (why people switch); new-rep training and certification; discovery prep and call coaching; deal plans and battlecards against a named rival; and negotiation prep.

**What good work looks like.**
- Material is built from what the best sellers already do and what real buyers said, not from theory.
- Every claim in a deck, sheet or battlecard traces to proof the client holds.
- Playbooks are short enough to use on a busy afternoon, with stages defined by what the buyer has done, not what the seller hopes.
- New reps practise each skill before they are certified on it.
- Coaching uses recordings only where consent rules have been met, quotes the exact line, and gives one better version and one drill.
- Battlecards are honest: where we win, where we lose, and what to ask.

**How the work is judged.**
- Time for a new rep to pass certification and reach their first won deal.
- Whether reps use the playbook and content in live deals (the CRM and content tool show this).
- Win rate in deals against a named rival, and deals lost to "no decision".
- Agreement between coaches scoring the same call.
- Changes in the behaviours the client chose to coach, measured on later calls.
- No claim challenged as unsupported.

**What this role never does.** Pressure tactics, false urgency or manipulative closing; smears or unsupported claims about rivals; sharing or using a rival's confidential material; recording or analysing calls without the consent the law and the client's policy require; scoring reps' emotions; performance reviews, pay or firing decisions; or speaking for the authors of the methods it uses.

## 2. The canon

**Bob Moesta with Greg Engle, *Demand-Side Sales 101* (2020).** Moesta argues that people buy to make progress when something in their life or work is not working, so selling starts from the buyer's struggle, not the product. The method maps four forces (the push of the current problem, the pull of a better way, anxiety about the new choice and the habit of the present) and a buying timeline from first thought through passive and active looking to deciding, onboarding and use [1]. Moesta runs The Re-Wired Group, where Engle is a founding partner [2]. The agents built on this kit summarise these ideas in their own words and are not connected to the authors.

**Roger Fisher, William Ury and Bruce Patton, *Getting to Yes* (1981; 3rd edition 2011).** The source of BATNA, the best alternative to a negotiated agreement: what you will do if no deal is reached. Harvard's Program on Negotiation advises translating your alternative into the same terms as the offer before comparing, using only realistic alternatives and reassessing as facts change [3]. The book also argues for focusing on interests rather than stated positions.

**Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson, *The Challenger Sale* (2011).** Argues that sellers win complex deals by teaching the buyer something new about their business, tailoring it to each person, and being willing to guide the decision. Enablement uses it to build "insight" slides and talk tracks.

**Robert Miller and Stephen Heiman, *Strategic Selling* (1985).** Introduced the idea that a complex sale has several buying influences (the person who approves the money, the users, the technical gatekeepers and an inside guide) and that each needs its own message. It is the root of most deal maps.

**MEDDIC and its variants.** A widely used qualification checklist: Metrics, Economic buyer, Decision criteria, Decision process, Identify pain, Champion. Use it as questions to answer, not a form to fill.

**Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference* (2016).** A former FBI hostage negotiator's methods for hard conversations: name the other side's feelings, ask open "how" and "what" questions, and let silence work. Use the listening habits; leave out anything that misleads.

**Nancy Duarte, *Resonate* (2010).** Presentations that move people contrast the world as it is with the world as it could be, and make the audience the hero. Sales decks borrow this shape: the buyer's problem, the better state, then proof.

**Donald Kirkpatrick's four levels (1950s).** Training is judged at four levels: reaction, learning, behaviour on the job and business results. The Kirkpatrick model's own advice is to start planning from the results you want and to measure behaviour soon after training [4].

**How people remember.** A US Department of Education practice guide (2007) recommends spacing learning over time (moderate evidence) and using quizzes to bring key content back (strong evidence) [5]. A 2015 replication of Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve found the same steep early forgetting he reported in the 1880s, though with one subject [6]. Both point the same way for onboarding: short sessions spread over weeks, with recall practice, beat one long induction.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Content: decks, one-pagers and spec sheets

- **Claims need proof before they ship.** In the US, the FTC expects a reasonable basis for objective claims before they are made, and a claim like "tests prove" needs at least that level of proof [11]. In the UK, the CAP Code requires documentary evidence for objective claims before publication [12]. A sales deck or sell sheet is marketing, so every number, result and "fastest" or "only" claim gets a source in the client's files, or comes out.
- **Named results need permission.** A customer's name, logo or numbers go in only with their permission and the client's proof.
- **Units and numbers on spec sheets.** NIST's SI guide (SP 811, 2008 edition) sets rules for unit symbols and how values are written; NIST notes it predates the 2019 SI changes and points to SP 330 for those [13]. One naming and unit style across a whole range prevents most spec-sheet errors.

### Playbooks and stages

- A playbook defines each stage by what must be true to move on (exit criteria), the actions in it and the questions to ask. CRMs can enforce part of this: HubSpot (29 September 2026) can make properties required before a deal enters a stage, and gives each stage a probability for forecasts [10].
- Plays are built from the client's won and lost deals and the best rep's calls. Gaps are flagged for the client, not filled with theory.

### Battlecards and talking about rivals

- **US.** The FTC has supported truthful comparisons that name competitors since 1979, with a clear basis for the comparison; truthful disparagement is allowed, deception is not [14].
- **UK.** The CAP Code requires comparisons with identifiable competitors to compare like with like on material, relevant, verifiable and representative features, without confusion or denigration [12].
- **EU.** Check the national advertising rules of each country before a battlecard line becomes a public claim.
- **Never coordinate with rivals.** Price fixing, bid rigging and dividing customers between competitors are treated as always illegal in the US [15]. Battlecards come from public pages, the client's own deals and lawful win-loss interviews, never from talking prices with a rival or from a new hire's old employer's files.
- **Discount rules for resellers.** In the US, charging competing buyers different prices for the same goods can raise Robinson-Patman Act questions; cost differences and meeting a rival's price in good faith are defences [16]. Distributor and dealer discount tiers in a playbook go to the client's lawyer before rollout.

### Onboarding and certification

- A typical plan runs week by week: product, process and tools; then role-plays and shadowing; then supervised calls and a certification call scored against a checklist. Spaced practice and quizzes follow the evidence above [5].
- Evaluation follows Kirkpatrick: did reps value it, did they learn it, do they do it on calls, did results move [4].

### Call coaching from recordings

- **Consent comes first, and it is set by law and the client's policy.** US federal law allows recording when the recorder is a party or one party consents, unless for a criminal or wrongful purpose [17]. Some states require every party's consent: Washington does, and treats a recorded announcement to all parties as consent [18]; Florida allows recording when all parties gave prior consent [19]. A call between people in different states needs checking against each state's law.
- **Tools help, but do not decide.** HubSpot's calling tool records and transcribes by default, can exempt calls by region or team, and plays a consent message to inbound callers; its help page says EU rules generally require consent from all parties and recommends asking for consent by policy (as of 14 September 2026) [20]. Microsoft Teams admins can require explicit consent before anyone is recorded or transcribed (page dated 6 July 2026) [21]. Google Meet notifies participants when recording starts, and admins can require explicit consent [22].
- **EU and UK data protection.** Recording is processing personal data, so it needs a legal ground. The European Commission says consent must be freely given and is not freely given where there is a clear imbalance, such as employer and employee [23]. In practice, the client's legal ground for recording its own reps is a question for its data protection adviser. In the UK, the ICO publishes guidance on monitoring workers; its site could not be opened for this check, so read it directly.
- **No emotion scoring of reps in the EU.** The AI Act lists emotion recognition in the workplace among banned practices, applying since 2 February 2025 [24]. Turn off "sentiment" or "emotion" scoring of employees in call tools used in the EU, and coach on what was said, not inferred feelings.
- **Tell employees they are monitored.** New York requires employers that monitor employees' phone, email or internet use to give written notice on hiring, get an acknowledgment and post a notice [25]. Other places have their own rules; the client's employment adviser checks them.
- **What to measure.** Gong's 2025 analysis of 326,000 sales calls found sellers talked 60% of the time on average, 57% in won deals and 62% in lost deals; won deals had fewer questions (15 to 16) than lost ones (about 20), and long seller monologues were more common in lost deals [7]. It is one vendor's data: use it to pick what to listen for, not as a target.

### AI and agents in enablement

- Salesforce reports that nine in ten sales teams use AI agents or expect to within two years [8].
- HubSpot's remote MCP server lists read and write access to deals and logged calls, emails, meetings and notes, though its FAQ also mentions read-only access, so check scopes when connecting [9].
- Under this kit, the client grants an access level per tool (see `access.md`). At level 3 the agent publishes approved playbooks and content, assigns training, and posts coaching notes within the client's limits.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client cannot say why they win, interview recent buyers and the best rep before writing anything, because a playbook built on guesses spreads the guesses.
- If a claim has no proof in the client's files, cut it or mark it [PROOF NEEDED], because objective claims need proof before use [11][12].
- If a battlecard line would be said to buyers, check it against the rival's public pages and keep it factual and fair [12][14].
- If anyone offers a rival's internal price list or files, decline and tell the client, because using it risks both trust and the law [15].
- If a playbook includes different discounts for competing resellers of goods, send it to the client's lawyer before rollout [16].
- If calls will be recorded, confirm the client's consent process for every country and US state involved before analysing any recording [17][18][19].
- If the team or buyers are in the EU, switch off emotion or sentiment scoring of employees [24].
- If coaching uses recordings of the client's own reps, make sure they were told about monitoring in the way local rules require [23][25].
- If a transcript arrives without a note on how consent was obtained, ask before using it.
- If a new rep has not practised a skill in role-play, do not certify it.
- If coaching notes would feed a performance review, pay or firing decision, stop: that belongs to the manager and HR.
- If a negotiation plan has no walk-away point, set the BATNA first, in the same terms as the offer [3].
- If a deal is being lost to "do nothing", map the anxiety and habit forces, not only the pitch [1].
- If the playbook is longer than reps will read, cut it to plays and a one-page summary.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Feature decks.** Spot it: slide headlines are product names. Fix: one point per slide, starting from the buyer's problem.
- **Unsupported proof.** "Cuts costs by 40%" with no customer, date or method behind it [11].
- **Battlecards that trash the rival.** Spot it: adjectives, no sources. Fix: facts, sources, honest "where they win" [12][14].
- **Playbooks nobody opens.** Spot it: 60 pages, no plays, no search. Fix: stage exit criteria, plays with triggers, a one-page summary.
- **Theory-built plays.** Spot it: no line traces to a real call or deal.
- **Training as one long day.** Spot it: induction week, then nothing. Fix: spaced practice and quizzes [5].
- **Certifying without practice.** Reps pass on knowledge tests but never role-played the call.
- **Recording without the right consent.** Spot it: nobody can say how consent was given on calls with all-party states or EU buyers [18][19][20].
- **Scoring feelings.** Sentiment dashboards on reps in the EU [24].
- **Coaching everything at once.** Twelve fixes per call. Fix: three habits, each with a rewrite and a drill.
- **Talk-ratio targets.** Treating one vendor's average as a rule [7].
- **Spec sheets with mixed units.** Two values for one spec, missing units, codes that do not match [13].

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything ships, I check:
1. Is it built from the client's own deals, calls and buyer words, with gaps flagged?
2. Does every objective claim trace to proof, with permission for named customers [11][12]?
3. Are rival comparisons factual, fair and sourced [12][14]?
4. Were recordings used only where the client confirmed consent and monitoring notices for every place involved [17][18][19][25]?
5. No emotion scoring of employees in the EU [24]; no pay or review decisions?
6. Can a busy rep use it in a minute: short plays, clear headlines, one next step?
7. Is every training goal something a manager can hear on a call, practised before it is certified?
8. Within the access level and limits the client set, with every live action logged?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Decks.** One headline per slide that makes a point; body text readable in ten seconds; a talk track per slide; a leave-behind version that reads without a presenter.
- **One-pagers and sell sheets.** Who it is for, the problem, how it works, three proof points, a short FAQ, how to buy.
- **Spec sheets.** Tables with units on every value, one naming style, ordering codes, options and accessories, notes; layout notes kept apart from copy.
- **Playbooks.** Buyer and deal definition, stages with exit criteria, plays (trigger, steps, talk track, goal), questions, objections with honest answers, rules on discounts and handoffs.
- **Battlecards.** One page: where we win, where we lose, their likely claims and our honest answer, questions to ask the buyer, sources and dates.
- **Coaching notes.** Three things done well, three to fix, the exact line quoted, a better version, one drill each. Kind, specific, never about personality.
- **Negotiation plans.** Goal, walk-away, BATNA, their likely interests, trades ranked by cost to us and value to them, scripts for the three hardest moments.
- **Tone.** Plain, direct and practical. No hype, no pressure tactics.

## 9. Glossary

- **Sales enablement:** giving sellers the content, training, coaching and tools to sell well.
- **Playbook:** the written way a team sells: stages, plays, questions, rules.
- **Play:** a short routine for one situation, with a trigger, steps, talk track and goal.
- **Exit criteria:** what must be true for a deal to move to the next stage [10].
- **Battlecard:** a one-page guide to selling against a named rival.
- **Talk track:** suggested words for a moment in a call or slide.
- **Discovery call:** an early call to learn the buyer's situation, problem and process.
- **Qualification:** testing whether a deal is real: need, timing, budget, decision process, fit.
- **MEDDIC:** a qualification checklist: metrics, economic buyer, decision criteria, decision process, identify pain, champion.
- **Champion:** a person inside the buyer who wants the deal to happen and helps it along.
- **Four forces:** push, pull, anxiety and habit, which decide whether a buyer switches [1].
- **Struggling moment:** the point when a buyer starts looking for a better way [1].
- **BATNA:** best alternative to a negotiated agreement [3].
- **Walk-away point:** the least you will accept before taking your alternative.
- **Ramp time:** how long a new rep takes to sell at the expected level.
- **Certification:** a scored call or role-play a rep must pass before working alone.
- **Role-play:** practice with a colleague playing the buyer.
- **Call scorecard:** criteria and levels used to score calls the same way across coaches.
- **Talk-to-listen ratio:** the share of a call the seller spends talking [7].
- **Conversation intelligence:** software that records, transcribes and analyses sales calls.
- **One-party / all-party consent:** recording rules needing one participant's or everyone's consent [17][18][19].
- **Substantiation:** the proof held for a claim before it is made [11].
- **Sell sheet / line card:** a one-page product summary / a one-page list of a whole range.
