# Call coaching from recordings

## When to use

- A rep or manager wants specific feedback on a real call.
- A team wants weekly coaching without a full-time coach.
- Managers score calls differently and need one scorecard.
- The client wants to turn the best calls into examples for new reps.

Do not use it to join, listen to or record live calls, to judge a rep for pay, promotion or dismissal, or to infer anyone's emotions.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** transcripts or recordings the client chooses to share, the playbook, the scorecard if one exists.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save scored notes and drills for the manager, unpublished.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** post coaching notes and drills to the agreed people (the rep and their manager, or the manager only), add approved clips to a call library, and log every action, within the confirmed limits.
- **Stays with the client:** switching on recording, consent messages and monitoring notices, and any people decision.

## What you need

1. The transcript or recording, with the date and the call type (discovery, demo, negotiation).
2. **How consent was obtained** for this call, and where each participant was (country, and US state for US callers).
3. Confirmation that the rep was told their calls may be reviewed, in the way the client's policy and local rules require.
4. The playbook stage, the goal of the call and the next step the rep wanted.
5. The scorecard, or the behaviours the client wants to coach.

## Steps

### 1. Check consent before reading

Do not analyse a recording until the client confirms how consent was obtained. Point them to the official sources for each place involved:
- US federal law allows recording by a party or with one party's consent (18 U.S.C. 2511). Some states need every party's consent; Washington (RCW 9.73.030) and Florida (934.03) are two. Calls across states need checking against each.
- In the EU, recording is processing personal data and needs a legal ground under the GDPR; the European Commission notes that consent is not freely given where there is a clear imbalance, such as employer and employee. The client's data protection adviser decides the ground for recording its own staff.
- In the UK, read the Information Commission's guidance on monitoring workers.
- Employers in New York must give written notice of phone, email and internet monitoring on hiring.

If anything is unclear, stop and ask. This is general information; the client checks it with a lawyer.

### 2. Mark the call

Talk time for each side, the number and type of questions, long monologues, objections raised, how each was handled, and how the call ended (was a next step and date agreed?). Gong's 2025 data on 326,000 calls found lost deals had more seller talk time and longer monologues on average; use that to decide what to listen for, not as a target ratio.

### 3. Score it

Use the client's scorecard, or a default: opening, discovery, listening, handling doubts, value in the buyer's terms, next step. Score each 1 to 4 with one line of evidence (a quoted line and its timestamp).

### 4. Pick three habits

Choose the three changes with the biggest effect on this call's goal. For each: the exact line, why it hurt, a better version in the rep's own style, and one drill (a role-play, a rewrite, or a phrase to practise).

### 5. Name what went well

Three specific things done well, with quotes. Praise only what is true.

### 6. Turn notes into next steps

If useful, separate what the buyer said from what the rep assumed, and draft the follow-up email in the buyer's words with the agreed next step.

### 7. For teams: calibrate

Build a one-page scorecard with eight or fewer criteria, a 1-to-4 scale and an example line for each level. Have two managers score the same call, compare, and adjust the examples until they agree.

### 8. Do not score feelings

Do not use sentiment or emotion scores about the rep. In the EU, the AI Act lists emotion recognition in the workplace among banned practices since 2 February 2025. Coach what was said and done.

## Checks

- Consent and monitoring notice confirmed by the client for every place involved, with official sources named.
- Every score has a quoted line as evidence.
- Three fixes, not twelve; each with a rewrite and a drill.
- Praise is specific and true.
- No emotion or personality judgements; no pay or review use.
- Buyer details stay inside the client's tools; examples for training are anonymised.
- At level 3, notes went only to the agreed people and every action is logged.

## Output format

1. Consent check: what the client confirmed, and sources to read
2. Call map: talk time, questions, monologues, objections, ending
3. Scorecard with evidence lines
4. Three things done well
5. Three habits to change: line, why, better version, drill
6. Follow-up email draft, if asked
7. For teams: scorecard with example lines per level

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a reporting tool for small retailers; discovery call by a rep named Ana. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Consent check:** client confirms both parties were in Texas and the buyer heard the recording notice at the start (on the recording at 00:04). Ana's employment notice covers call review. Proceeding.
>
> **Call map:** Ana talked 71% of the time; longest monologue 3 min 40 s (06:10); 6 questions; one price objection (18:30); call ended without a date.
>
> **Habit 1: pitching before the problem.**
> - **Line (02:15):** "So let me show you our dashboard, it's really powerful..."
> - **Why it hurt:** the buyer had not yet said what was wrong with their current reports.
> - **Better:** "Before I show anything, what's the report you dread putting together each week?"
> - **Drill:** in tomorrow's role-play, ask three problem questions before opening the demo.
>
> **Habit 3: no next step.**
> - **Better:** "Shall we look at your own sales data together next Tuesday at 10? I'll send an invite now."
