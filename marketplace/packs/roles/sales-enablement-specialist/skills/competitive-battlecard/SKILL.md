# Competitive battlecard

## When to use

- The same rival shows up in many deals, and win rates against it are falling.
- Reps answer "why not them?" with opinions or insults.
- A big pitch is coming and the buyer is also talking to a named rival.
- A rival changed its prices, product or message and the old card is out of date.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** CRM deals where the rival was involved (won and lost) with notes, win-loss interviews, call transcripts recorded with consent, the rival's public website, pricing page and reviews.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the battlecard as a draft in the content tool, wiki or competitive intelligence tool.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved card to the sales team's space and retire the old one, within the confirmed limits. Any claim about the rival going into public material (website, ads, a buyer-facing comparison sheet) needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. The rival's name and the deals where you met them, with outcomes and notes.
2. The rival's public pages: product, pricing, case studies, terms, reviews. Each with the date you looked.
3. Your own product facts and proof, with sources.
4. Lost-deal notes or interviews in the buyer's words, if any.

Never use: a rival's internal documents, price lists or decks from any source (including a new hire's old employer), anything obtained by posing as a buyer, or anything learned by talking prices with the rival.

## Steps

### 1. Read the deals first

From your won and lost deals against this rival, list why buyers said they chose each side, in their words. Count how often each reason appears. This is the spine of the card; opinions from the sales floor come second.

### 2. Read the rival's public pages

Note what they say about themselves: who they target, main claims, pricing model, terms. Date each note. If their page makes a claim about you, note it exactly.

### 3. Write "where we win" and "where we lose"

Three to five points each, honest and specific. "Where we lose" matters most: it tells reps which deals to qualify out early and which worries to address before the rival raises them.

### 4. Write their likely claims and a fair answer

For each claim the rival makes (or buyers repeat), write a short, factual answer and, where it helps, a question that lets the buyer judge for themselves. Never call their claim a lie unless you can prove it; prefer "here's how to check".

### 5. Write the questions to ask the buyer

Questions that bring out the buyer's needs where you are strong, asked early and openly. "How will you handle the switch-over month?" works better than "Did you know they have no switch-over support?".

### 6. Check every comparison

For each line that compares you with the rival:
- Is it true today, with a source and date?
- Does it compare like with like (same tier, same use, same period)?
- Is the feature material and verifiable by the buyer?
- Does it avoid insulting the rival or confusing the two brands?

These follow the FTC's comparative advertising policy in the US and the CAP Code's comparison rules in the UK; in the EU, check each country's rules. If a line will appear in public material, mark it for a fresh yes and the client's legal review.

### 7. Add landmines and walk-away signs

Situations where the rival is the better fit. Saying so builds trust and saves time.

### 8. Date the card and set a review

Every card shows when it was checked and when it will be checked again.

## Checks

- Every claim about the rival has a public source and a date; every claim about us has proof.
- "Where we lose" is honest and specific.
- No insults, no guesses presented as facts, no confidential material.
- Comparisons are like for like and checkable by the buyer.
- Public-facing claims are flagged for a fresh yes and legal review.
- The card fits on one page.

## Output format

1. Header: rival, date checked, next review date
2. Why buyers chose each side (from deals), with counts
3. Where we win / where we lose
4. Their claims and our fair answer
5. Questions to ask the buyer
6. Landmines and walk-away signs
7. Sources with dates

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a small booking software company, against a larger rival (Rival A). Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Rival A battlecard (checked 6 Nov 2026; review 6 Dec 2026)**
>
> **Why buyers chose (last 9 months, 23 deals; some gave two reasons):** chose us: setup help (9), phone support (6); chose Rival A: marketplace listing (8), lower entry price (5).
>
> **Where we win:** first-month setup done for the client; phone support on every plan; group bookings.
> **Where we lose:** Rival A's marketplace brings new customers; its entry plan costs less; it has more integrations.
>
> **Their claim:** "All-in-one platform." **Fair answer:** "It covers a lot. Ask them which parts are extra on the entry plan; their pricing page lists add-ons (checked 6 Nov)."
>
> **Ask the buyer:** "How many of your bookings come from people who already know you, and how many need finding?"
>
> **Walk away when:** most of the buyer's new customers would come from a marketplace listing.
