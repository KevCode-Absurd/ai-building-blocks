# Sales playbook builder

## When to use

- A founder or top seller is handing selling to two or more reps.
- Results vary widely by rep and nobody can say why.
- New reps take months to become useful.
- The existing playbook is long, out of date or unread.

Do not use it to set up CRM pipelines from scratch, to coach one rep's calls (use `call-coaching-from-recordings`) or to write pressure scripts.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** CRM deals (won, lost, stalled) with notes, call transcripts or notes from the best seller, current decks and playbook.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the playbook and play cards as drafts in the wiki, drive or content platform; propose stage definitions and required fields as a document.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** publish the approved playbook and play cards to the agreed space, link them from CRM stages, and log the changes, within the confirmed limits. Changing CRM stage settings needs a fresh yes.

## What you need

1. Six to twelve months of won and lost deals with notes, or as many as exist.
2. Call notes or transcripts from the best seller and from an average one (recorded with the consent your policy requires).
3. The current CRM stages and what people think each means.
4. The offer, prices and discount rules as the client sets them.
5. Proof the client holds: named customers who agreed, results with dates.
6. Ten minutes with the best seller, or their answers to: "What do you do that others do not?"

## Steps

### 1. Define the buyer and the deal

Who buys, why now, how long it takes, who signs, who can block, and what "no decision" looks like. Use the buyers' own words from notes and transcripts. Where the data is thin, mark it as a guess to test.

### 2. Find what the best seller does differently

Compare the best seller's calls and deals with an average rep's. Look for: questions asked before any pitch, how they handle price, who else they bring in and when, what they send after a call, when they walk away. List each habit with an example line. These become plays.

### 3. Write the stages with exit criteria

For each stage: the goal, the actions, and what must be true about the buyer to move on (for example "buyer has named who signs and agreed a date for a second call"). Define stages by buyer actions, not seller hopes. Suggest CRM fields that make each exit visible.

### 4. Write the plays

One page each, for up to eight common situations (inbound lead, referral, stalled deal, rival in the deal, price push, renewal at risk, trial user, champion leaves). Each play: trigger, steps, what to say (a talk track in plain words), proof to use, and the goal. Write every talk track, email and message in full, word for word, so a new rep can use it as is; never a note on what it should say.

For outbound and follow-up plays (cold email, a sequence, a stalled-deal nudge):
- **One light ask per touch,** the lightest the team's own results support: a yes or no question, an offer to send one useful thing, or who owns this. Never two asks in one message.
- **No call or meeting request in the first touch** unless the team's data shows call asks convert better. If the data shows a link or a calendar invite cut replies, leave it out.
- **Timing runs from the trigger** (the event that makes the message relevant): the first touch goes soon after it, and follow-ups count days from the first touch. Never wait for a calendar window such as a set week of the month.

### 5. Write the qualification guide

Questions that test need, timing, budget, decision process and fit, and what to do when a deal fails a test: nurture, refer out or close. If the team likes a named checklist such as MEDDIC, map the questions to it, but keep the wording plain.

### 6. Add objections, proof and rules

The most common objections from the notes, each with an honest answer and a question back. Proof points only from the client's files, each with its source. Rules: response times, discount limits and who approves them, handoffs to delivery or account managers. Anything not decided by the client is flagged, not invented.

### 7. Cut it down

A playbook people use fits in a few pages plus play cards. Write a one-page summary for new hires: the buyer, the stages, the five plays used most, the rules.

### 8. Mark the gaps

List what the material did not show and what the client must decide (for example, discount authority, or which industries to avoid).

## Checks

- Every play traces to a real deal, call or the best seller's own words.
- Every talk track and message is written in full; each outbound touch has one light ask, timed from its trigger.
- Stages are defined by what the buyer has done, with exit criteria a manager can check.
- Objection answers are honest; no false urgency or pressure tactics.
- Every proof point has a source and permission where a customer is named.
- Discount and pricing rules come from the client; reseller discount tiers are flagged for the client's lawyer.
- The summary fits on one page.
- At level 3, only the agreed space was changed, and every change is logged.

## Output format

1. One-page summary for new hires
2. Buyer and deal definition
3. Stages table: stage, goal, actions, exit criteria, CRM field
4. Play cards (one page each)
5. Qualification guide
6. Objections with honest answers
7. Proof library with sources
8. Rules
9. Gaps and decisions for the client

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a payroll bureau selling to firms with 20 to 200 staff. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

> **Stage 2: Problem confirmed**
> - **Goal:** the buyer describes the payroll problem in their own words and what it costs them.
> - **Actions:** discovery call; ask about the last payroll error and what it took to fix; ask who else feels it.
> - **Exit criteria:** buyer has named the problem, its cost in time or money, and who signs off on a change.
> - **CRM field:** "Problem in buyer's words" (required to enter Stage 3).
>
> **Play: rival already in the deal**
> - **Trigger:** buyer says they are also talking to a larger bureau.
> - **Steps:** ask what made them look at the other firm; ask what would make them choose; send the two-page switching guide.
> - **Talk track:** "That makes sense: they're a good choice for some firms. What's most important for you in the switch-over month?"
> - **Proof:** two named customers who switched mid-year (permission on file).
> - **Goal:** buyer agrees to a side-by-side switch plan call.
>
> **Gap for the client:** who can approve a first-year discount, and up to what limit?
