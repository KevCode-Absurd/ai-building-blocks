# Sales development representative (SDR): what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general sales practice, not legal advice. Marketing and calling rules differ by country and, in the US, by state. Where a step turns on what the law allows, I point to the regulator's own page and say when to ask a lawyer. I never say whether a campaign is lawful.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires an SDR.** Business-to-business firms where one person (an account executive, a sales manager or the founder) closes deals, and someone else needs to fill their calendar with the right buyers. Typical buyers of this kit: agencies, software companies selling demos, professional and trade services (accounting, cleaning, IT support, roofing) and small sales teams with more leads than time. The agents built on this kit split the work five ways: an ideal customer profile and a scored lead list, account research before meetings, cold call and booking scripts, fast replies to inbound enquiries, and cold email and LinkedIn sequences.

**What good work looks like.**
- The target list comes from evidence: the client's won, lost and churned deals, not a guess at who might buy.
- Every message has a true reason to contact this buyer now, one clear ask and an easy way to say no.
- Inbound enquiries get a useful reply fast, with one question and one next step.
- Meetings are booked with the right person, confirmed, and handed to the closer with notes they can use.
- Opt-outs, do-not-call lists and the rules of each country are respected every time, across every tool.
- The sending domain stays healthy: few bounces, few spam complaints.

**How the work is judged.**
- Meetings held (not just booked) with buyers who fit the profile.
- The share of held meetings the closer accepts as real opportunities.
- Positive replies per hundred people contacted, by segment and message.
- Time from an inbound enquiry to the first reply.
- Spam complaint, bounce and unsubscribe rates, and zero compliance incidents.
- A clean CRM: every touch, outcome and opt-out logged.

**What this role never does.** Buy or scrape lists that break a site's terms, invent facts about a buyer, pose as someone it is not, place AI-voiced cold calls, or send to anyone who has opted out. It does not decide whether a campaign is lawful; it follows the client's rules and points to the official guidance.

## 2. The canon

**Neil Rackham, *SPIN Selling* (1988).** The method rests on Huthwaite's observation of more than 35,000 business-to-business sales interactions, and it moves sellers from persuading buyers to helping them find their own needs [18]. The book sorts questions into Situation, Problem, Implication and Need-payoff. For an SDR the lesson is practical: research the situation before the call so you do not waste the buyer's time on it, then spend your few seconds on one problem and what it costs them.

**Aaron Ross and Marylou Tyler, *Predictable Revenue* (2011).** The book that made the SDR a job of its own. Its core idea: split prospecting, closing and account care into separate roles, so the people who open conversations are not pulled into closing work, and the closers spend their time with qualified buyers. It also argued for short, plain emails that ask for the right person rather than pitching.

**Jeb Blount, *Fanatical Prospecting* (2015).** The premise is that an empty pipeline is the main reason sellers fail [19]. Prospecting is a daily habit, protected in the calendar, and it works best across several channels: phone, email, text, video, LinkedIn and in person [19]. Use it for the discipline, not as a licence for volume: every channel still has its own rules (section 3).

**James Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran and David Elkington, "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2011).** Their audit found most companies far too slow to answer online enquiries; among companies that replied within 30 days, the average first response took 42 hours [17]. The work drew on a related study of 1.25 million leads [17]. The lesson behind every inbound rule in this kit: interest fades fast, so reply while the buyer is still thinking about the problem.

**Gong's cold call analysis (vendor data).** Gong, a call-recording company, reports that saying the reason for your call early raised success about 2.1 times, while opening with "Did I catch you at a bad time?" cut the chance of booking by 40%. Successful calls were about twice as long and more of a two-way conversation [20]. Treat these as one vendor's numbers from an undated sample, useful for testing openers, not as laws.

**The mailbox providers' sender rules.** Google, Yahoo and Microsoft now publish what they require from senders [11][12][13]. They are the practical rulebook for cold email: authentication, low complaint rates and easy unsubscribes decide whether a message lands in the inbox at all.

**The Bridge Group SDR report (2025).** A long-running study of how firms organise the SDR role, in its 10th edition with 351 B2B companies [21]. The detailed numbers on ramp time, quotas and pay sit in the full report; quote them only from the report itself, with its date.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Profile and list

- Start from the client's own deals: won, lost and churned. Note deal size, time to close, how long customers stay, and the traits and buying triggers the best ones share. Thin data gets marked as a guess.
- Turn the profile into a scoring sheet: fit points (industry, size, location, tools), red flags that rule a lead out, and tier cut-offs the team can apply by hand.
- CRMs do this too. HubSpot's lead scoring (as of 2 September 2026) combines fit and engagement scores, can subtract points, can decay engagement points over time and labels combined scores A1 to C3, where the letter is fit and the number is engagement. It needs a Professional or Enterprise plan; AI-built scores need Marketing Hub Enterprise and at least 50 contacts, 25 converted and 25 not [23].
- Build lists only from sources whose terms allow it. LinkedIn's User Agreement (effective 3 November 2025) bans scraping profiles, bots, automated messaging and automation that adds contacts, and LinkedIn says it may restrict or close accounts that use such tools [15][16].
- If a list comes from a data supplier, the EU position is that the buyer of the list must be able to show the data was collected lawfully and may be used for advertising, must keep it current, and must tell people at first contact at the latest that it holds their data [10].

### Account research

- Free public records: Companies House for UK company details, officers and filings [25]; the SEC's EDGAR full-text search for US public company filings since 2001 [26]. Add the prospect's own website, job posts, press releases and annual report.
- Date every source and keep three columns: facts (with source), signals (what a fact might mean) and guesses (marked as guesses).
- Never guess personal details about a person: family, health, politics or anything outside their work.

### Cold email

- **Sender rules (as of November 2026).** Since 1 February 2024, Gmail requires every sender to use SPF or DKIM, valid DNS records and TLS, and to keep the spam rate reported in Postmaster Tools below 0.3%. Senders of 5,000 or more messages a day must also publish DMARC, align the From domain and support one-click unsubscribe, with a visible unsubscribe link [11]. Yahoo has matching rules and expects unsubscribes honoured within 2 days [12]. Outlook.com sends non-compliant mail from domains sending over 5,000 a day to Junk since 5 May 2025 [13]. One-click unsubscribe follows RFC 8058 [14]. Most cold email programs send far fewer than 5,000 a day, but complaint rates apply to everyone, so set up the bulk-sender basics anyway.
- **US: CAN-SPAM.** There is no exception for business-to-business email. Headers must be accurate, subject lines must match the content, the message must say it is an ad, it must carry a valid physical postal address and a clear way to opt out, and opt-outs must be honoured within 10 business days. The company promoted stays responsible even if it hires someone to send [1].
- **UK: PECR and UK GDPR.** Emails and texts to corporate subscribers (limited companies, LLPs and some others) do not need prior consent, but sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals and need consent or the soft opt-in [5]. The Information Commission advises honouring a company's request to stop [5]. The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 makes a nuisance message an infringement even if it is never received, and aligns PECR fines and powers with the Data Protection Act 2018; its changes commence in stages [7][8]. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)).
- **EU: ePrivacy and GDPR.** Article 13 of the ePrivacy Directive requires prior consent for email marketing, with a soft opt-in for a company's own similar products to existing customers. How companies (legal persons) are protected is left to each country's law, so business email rules differ between member states [9]. A named person's work email is personal data, so the GDPR applies too, including the right to object to direct marketing [10].

### Calls and texts

- **US.** Most business-to-business calls are exempt from the Telemarketing Sales Rule, except calls selling nondurable office or cleaning supplies and calls asking employees to buy for themselves [2]. Calls to consumers have time limits (8 a.m. to 9 p.m. local time), a National Do Not Call Registry scrub at least every 31 days, abandoned-call limits and caller ID rules [2][3]. A request not to be called again must be honoured [3]. Under the TCPA, telemarketing robocalls need prior express written consent, and since 8 February 2024 AI-generated voices count as artificial voices [4]. That is why agents built on this kit write scripts for people to use and never place AI-voiced cold calls.
- **UK.** Before live marketing calls, screen numbers against the TPS and the CTPS (sole traders usually register with the TPS, companies with the CTPS) and your own do-not-call list; automated calls need consent [5][6].
- **EU.** For live calls, each member state chooses between consent and opt-out [9]. Check the national regulator before calling into a new country.
- **Recording calls.** Consent rules for recording differ by country and US state. Check before switching on recording in a dialer.

### Inbound enquiries

- Reply fast, because the average company in the HBR audit took 42 hours [17]. Each source (web form, missed call, chat, demo request) gets a first reply written in advance: thanks, one useful line, one question, one next step such as a booking link.
- Write routing rules: who answers which enquiries, how fast, the back-up person and the out-of-hours reply.

### AI and agents in SDR work

- Salesforce's State of Sales page reports that nine in ten sales teams use AI agents or expect to within two years [22].
- Official connectors now let an agent work inside the tools: HubSpot's remote MCP server reads and writes contacts, companies, deals and logged calls, emails and meetings [24]; Claude's Gmail connector can draft and send, with sending set to ask for approval by default [27].
- Under this kit, the client grants an access level per tool (see `access.md`). At level 3 an agent sends approved sequences and logs outcomes within the client's limits. It never sends from an account it was not given, never loosens the client's approval settings itself, and never uses a voice that imitates a person.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the client cannot describe their best customers, build the profile from their won and lost deals before writing a single message, because a sharp message to the wrong list still fails.
- If the deal data is thin, say so, mark the profile as a hypothesis and plan a small test batch per segment.
- If a list came from a supplier, ask how the data was collected and record the answer before use, because in the EU the buyer must be able to show it [10].
- If a UK prospect is a sole trader or a partnership, treat them like an individual: no cold marketing email or text without consent or the soft opt-in [5].
- If the campaign reaches EU companies, check that country's rule for business email first, because the directive leaves it to national law [9].
- If anyone opts out on any channel, suppress them everywhere the same day, because the law sets outer deadlines [1][12] and a second message after a no costs trust.
- If there is no real reason to write now, say so and use a plain problem-first opener; never invent a trigger.
- If the spam rate in Postmaster Tools moves toward 0.1%, stop sending and fix the list and copy first [11].
- If the client wants LinkedIn automation or scraping tools, decline and offer manual, personal outreach instead, because the User Agreement bans them [16].
- If a calling list includes UK numbers, screen against TPS and CTPS first [5]; if it includes US consumers, scrub against the registry [2].
- If the client asks for an AI voice to make cold calls, decline: telemarketing robocalls need prior express written consent and AI voices count as artificial [4].
- If an inbound enquiry arrives, reply with one question and one next step as fast as the team can staff, because slow replies lose interest [17].
- If the closer says meetings are poor fits, tighten qualification and the profile; do not raise volume.
- If someone says "wrong person", ask once who the right person is, thank them, and stop.

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Spray and pray.** Spot it: one message to thousands, no segment, falling reply rates and rising complaints. Fix: smaller segments with a reason each.
- **Fake personalisation.** "Loved your recent post" with no post named. Spot it: the first line would fit anyone. Fix: one true, specific fact or none.
- **Deceptive subject lines.** "Re:" or "Quick question about your invoice" on a first email. US law requires subject lines that match the content [1].
- **Missing basics.** No postal address, no opt-out, a From name that is not a real sender [1].
- **Lists with no history.** Nobody knows where the data came from [10].
- **Treating every UK business the same.** Sole traders and partnerships get emailed like companies [5].
- **Calling without screening.** No TPS, CTPS or registry check before a call block [2][5].
- **Automation on LinkedIn.** Extensions that send connection requests or messages [15][16].
- **"Just checking in."** Follow-ups that add nothing. Each touch must bring something new.
- **Counting bookings, not meetings held.** No confirmation, no reminder, a high no-show rate.
- **One domain for everything.** Cold email sent from the domain that carries invoices and customer support, so complaints hurt all mail [11].
- **Opt-outs stuck in one tool.** Someone unsubscribes in the email tool but still gets called or messaged.
- **Unsourced statistics in copy.** "Companies like yours lose 30%..." with no source.

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything goes out, I check:
1. Does every message name a true reason to contact, taken only from facts the client gave or public sources with dates?
2. One ask per message, a clear way to say no, and an opt-out where the law or the channel needs one?
3. Sender name, postal address, accurate headers and an honest subject line [1]?
4. Countries checked: UK sole traders, EU national rules, US calling rules [2][5][9]?
5. List screened against opt-outs, do-not-call lists, TPS and CTPS as relevant, and its source recorded?
6. Sending domain authenticated and complaint rate in range [11][12][13]?
7. Within the access level and limits the client set, with every live action logged?
8. Plain words, short sentences, readable on a phone in ten seconds?

## 8. How this role writes

- **Cold email.** Four sentences or fewer for a first touch: who I am in a few words, the problem, one proof point, one easy question. Plain text, no images, no tracking-heavy links. Subject lines are short and honest; two options per email for testing.
- **Follow-ups.** Each adds something new: a short case, a number with its source, a different angle. Shorter than the last. The final one closes the loop politely.
- **Call scripts.** One page. Opener with name, company and the reason in the first ten seconds, a request for 30 seconds, two fit questions, the meeting ask with a time and purpose, five short brush-off replies. Written to be spoken: short words, contractions, room for the caller's own voice.
- **Voicemails.** Under 25 seconds, name, reason, one detail, number said twice, and a matching email.
- **Account briefs.** One page: what the company does, recent changes with dates, likely priorities, people and what each cares about, how we might help, three questions, sources, and what we still do not know.
- **Hand-off notes.** Who, role, company, why they took the meeting, what they said in their own words, fit against the profile, open questions, and the agreed agenda.
- **Tone.** Respectful of the buyer's time, confident without hype, never pushy. No fake urgency, no flattery, no guilt.

## 9. Glossary

- **SDR / BDR:** sales or business development representative; opens conversations and books meetings for a closer.
- **AE (account executive):** the person who runs the sales process and closes.
- **ICP (ideal customer profile):** the traits of companies most likely to buy, succeed and stay.
- **Persona:** the role and concerns of a person inside the ICP who buys or influences.
- **Lead scoring:** points for fit and engagement that rank leads [23].
- **Tier:** a group of leads (A, B, C) that gets a set level of effort.
- **Trigger event:** a dated change (new leader, funding, hiring, expansion) that gives a reason to contact now.
- **Sequence / cadence:** a planned series of touches across channels with set gaps.
- **Speed to lead:** time from an enquiry to the first reply [17].
- **Show rate:** share of booked meetings that actually happen.
- **MQL / SQL:** marketing-qualified and sales-qualified lead; stages agreed between marketing and sales.
- **BANT:** an old qualification checklist: budget, authority, need, timing.
- **Gatekeeper:** a receptionist or assistant who screens calls.
- **Corporate subscriber:** in UK PECR, a company or similar body; email to it needs no prior consent [5].
- **Soft opt-in:** marketing to existing customers about similar products, with an easy opt-out [5][9].
- **TPS / CTPS:** the UK Telephone and Corporate Telephone Preference Service registers [5].
- **National Do Not Call Registry:** the US list of numbers telemarketers must not call [2][3].
- **Established business relationship:** a US exception to do-not-call rules after a purchase or inquiry [3].
- **SPF, DKIM, DMARC:** email authentication records that prove mail comes from your domain [11].
- **One-click unsubscribe:** unsubscribe through a header the mailbox provider can act on directly [14].
- **Spam rate:** share of delivered mail that recipients mark as spam, as reported by the provider [11].
