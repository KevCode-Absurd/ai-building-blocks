# Account research brief

## When to use

- A first meeting with a new account is booked and the closer has five minutes to prepare.
- An SDR needs a real reason to call a named account.
- A batch of target accounts needs short briefs in the same layout so the team can compare them.
- A deal has several people involved and nobody has mapped who cares about what.

Do not use it to find personal contact details or to guess facts about people.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** CRM history for the account (past deals, emails, call notes), and any shared folder of research.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save the brief as a note or document attached to the account, and draft tasks for open questions.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** update account fields the client named (industry, size, trigger date, stakeholder roles) and share the brief with the meeting owner, within the confirmed limits. Log every change.

Public records need no access: Companies House for UK companies, SEC EDGAR for US public companies, and the company's own website.

## What you need

1. The meeting: who, when, what we sell, what we want from the call.
2. Material on the account: annual report or filings, press releases, news articles, job posts, website pages, CRM notes. Each with its date and where it came from.
3. Names and job titles of the people attending, as the client knows them. Public work information only.
4. Our offer in one paragraph, and one or two customer results we can prove.

## Steps

### 1. Log the sources

List every source with its date and type. Anything older than the latest filing or the latest news gets marked as possibly out of date. If two sources disagree, keep both and say so.

### 2. Pull out the facts

What the company does and for whom, its size (staff, sites, revenue if public), structure (parent, subsidiaries), recent results, and changes in the last year: new leaders, new sites, products, acquisitions, layoffs, funding, regulatory events. Each fact carries its source number.

For UK companies, Companies House shows the registered details, officers and filed accounts. For US public companies, EDGAR full-text search finds annual reports and event filings by keyword, so you can search a company's filings for the problem you solve.

### 3. Read the signals

For each fact, write what it might mean for us, as a signal, not a conclusion. "Hiring four warehouse supervisors (job posts, 2 Nov) could mean a new shift or a new site." Rate each signal: strong (several sources point the same way), weak (one source), or none.

### 4. Map the people

For each person: role, likely priorities from their job and the company's situation, how they might judge us, and how they relate to the others. Mark every assumption as a guess. Note the gaps: who else is likely involved but not yet known (finance sign-off, IT, operations).

Never add family, health, politics, age or anything outside their work, even if it is public.

### 5. Link to our offer

Pick one or two priorities the company most likely has, and link each to something we do, with proof. If nothing links well, say so: a meeting with no fit is worth knowing about before it happens.

### 6. Write three questions

Questions that show the homework and open the buyer up, built on a fact: "Your annual report mentions consolidating three warehouses next year. How are you planning the change for the night shift?" Avoid questions the research already answered.

### 7. List what we still do not know

The gaps the closer should fill in the meeting, in order of importance.

### 8. For a batch

Use the same headings for every account, cut to half a page each, and end with a comparison table: account, best signal, strength, fit, suggested next step.

## Checks

- Every fact has a source and a date; signals and guesses are labelled.
- Nothing about a person outside their work.
- The link to our offer is honest; weak fit is called weak.
- The three questions are specific to this account.
- The brief fits on one page and can be read in five minutes.
- At level 2 or 3, only the named fields changed, and the change log is updated.

## Output format

1. Header: account, meeting date, attendees, our goal
2. Company in five lines
3. Recent changes (dated, sourced)
4. Signals and what they might mean
5. People map (role, likely priorities, guesses marked)
6. How we might help, with proof
7. Three questions
8. What we still do not know
9. Sources (numbered, dated)

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business for illustration: Harbourside Freight, a regional logistics firm, meeting a seller of shift-scheduling software.*

> **Goal:** agree a pilot conversation with the COO.
>
> **Recent changes**
> - Opened a second cross-dock site in Bristol, March 2026 [2].
> - Posted four night-shift supervisor roles, 2 to 4 November 2026 [4].
> - Annual report: driver and warehouse overtime "above plan" [1].
>
> **Signals**
> - Strong: growth across two sites with more night shifts [2][4]. Scheduling may now be spread across spreadsheets per site (*guess*).
> - Weak: overtime cost pressure, one line in one report [1].
>
> **People**
> - COO (attending): likely cares about overtime cost and cover at the new site. *Guess from [1][2].*
> - Finance sign-off: not yet known. Ask who approves software over the budget threshold.
>
> **Questions**
> 1. How do you build rotas across both sites today, and who owns that?
> 2. The report mentions overtime above plan. Which shifts drive most of it?
> 3. What would a good first month at the Bristol site look like for you?
>
> **Still unknown:** current tool, budget cycle, who else signs.
