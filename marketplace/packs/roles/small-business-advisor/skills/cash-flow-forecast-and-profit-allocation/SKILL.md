# Cash flow forecast and profit allocation

## When to use

- "We're busy and profitable on paper, so why is the bank account always empty?"
- A tax bill arrived and there was nothing put aside.
- The owner pays themselves whatever is left, if anything.
- A lender or grant panel wants a cash forecast.
- Before a big purchase, a hire or a quiet season.

Bank reconciliation and categorising transactions are in the bookkeeper kit; debt plans and lender talks in the fractional CFO kit.

## What you need

1. Bank statements or exports for the last 12 months, and today's balance for each account.
2. Open invoices (who owes what, due when) and open bills.
3. Regular payments with dates: wages, rent, loans, subscriptions, insurance.
4. Known tax dates and amounts, from the owner's accountant or tax office letters.
5. A profit and loss report for the last 12 months, with materials and subcontractor costs shown.
6. What the owner pays themselves and how.
7. The tax set-aside share from the owner's accountant. If unknown, it is marked [confirm with your accountant].

## Steps

### Part A: the 13-week forecast

1. **Opening cash.** Today's balance across business accounts. Note any overdraft or credit line limit separately; it is not cash.
2. **Receipts by week.** From open invoices, using each customer's real payment habits from the last 12 months (not the invoice terms). Add expected new sales, labelled "estimate" with the basis.
3. **Payments by week.** Wages and payroll taxes on paydays, rent, loan payments, supplier bills on their due dates, subscriptions, insurance, tax payments on their dates, the owner's pay.
4. **Closing cash and the lowest week.** Roll each week forward. Highlight the lowest closing balance and the week it falls in.
5. **Cash buffer.** Average daily outflow over the last three months; divide today's cash by it. State it in days.
6. **Fixes if the low point is too low.** In order: collect sooner (chase, deposits, stage payments), delay what can wait (purchases, non-urgent spend), talk to suppliers about timing, then finance options with an adviser. Rank by money and certainty.
7. **Weekly update.** Replace forecast with actuals, add a new week 13, note why actuals differed.

### Part B: the allocation set-up

1. **Real revenue.** Sales minus materials and subcontractors for the last 12 months. All shares are worked out from this.
2. **Today's shares.** What share of real revenue went to profit (often none), owner's pay, tax and running costs. Show every sum.
3. **Target shares.** Pick targets that fit this business: owner's pay that reflects a fair wage for the work, a tax set-aside from the accountant, a small profit share, and running costs as what remains. Shares add up to 100%.
4. **Step-up plan.** Move from today's shares towards targets a few points each quarter, so running costs have time to adjust. If cash is tight, start profit at a token share; the habit matters more than the amount.
5. **Purpose accounts.** A simple set: income, profit, owner's pay, tax, running costs. List the questions to ask the bank (fees, transfers, sub-accounts). Do not name banks or products.
6. **Twice-monthly routine.** Two fixed days a month, such as the 10th and 25th. The worksheet: money received since the last allocation day, times each share, equals the amount to move to each account. The owner moves it.
7. **Quarterly check.** Take part of the profit account as a payout to the owner, keep the rest as a reserve, review shares and decide whether to step up.
8. **Check against the forecast.** Make sure allocations never leave the running-costs account short of the payments in Part A. If they would, slow the step-up.

## Checks

- Opening cash matches the bank on the forecast date.
- Receipts use customers' real payment habits.
- Tax payments appear on their real dates, with amounts from the accountant or marked [confirm with your accountant].
- The lowest week is highlighted with a plan.
- Shares add up to 100% of real revenue, and every figure is the owner's own.
- No tax ruling, no bank or product recommendation.
- Every money move is written as the owner's action.
- The work ends with: *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant about tax.*

## Output format

1. 13-week table: week, opening cash, receipts, payments, closing cash; lowest week highlighted; buffer in days.
2. Fix list ranked by money and certainty.
3. Today versus target shares table.
4. Quarter-by-quarter step-up table.
5. Account list with each account's job and questions for the bank.
6. Twice-monthly worksheet with formulas.
7. Questions for the accountant.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real business: a three-person electrical firm. Sales £412,000; materials £148,000; owner took £48,000. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.

> **Real revenue:** £412,000 − £148,000 = £264,000.
>
> | Share of real revenue | Today | Target (by Q4 next year) |
> |---|---|---|
> | Profit | 0% | 5% |
> | Owner's pay | 18% (£48,000) | 30% |
> | Tax set-aside | 0% | [confirm with your accountant] |
> | Running costs | 82% | The rest |
>
> **Step-up:** profit 1% this quarter, +1 point each quarter; owner's pay +3 points a quarter while running costs are trimmed (van lease and two unused subscriptions first).
>
> **Forecast low point:** week 7, £2,300, when the VAT payment and the quarterly van insurance fall in the same week. **Fix:** ask the two largest customers for stage payments on jobs over £5,000 (they paid at 41 and 52 days last year).
>
> **Allocation days:** 10th and 25th. You move the money; the worksheet tells you how much.
>
> *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant about tax.*
