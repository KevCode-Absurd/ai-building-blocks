# Freelance rate and service packages

## When to use

- Someone is leaving a job to freelance and needs a rate.
- A freelancer is busy but earning too little, or works weekends to keep up.
- Every job is quoted from scratch, and scope keeps growing.
- Clients go quiet between the call and the invoice, or pay late.

Bookkeeping, tax set-asides and invoicing systems are in the bookkeeper kit; cash forecasting in the skill `cash-flow-forecast-and-profit-allocation`.

## What you need

1. The service, the kind of clients, and the country and region.
2. The yearly income the person needs before tax (their own figure).
3. Yearly business costs: software, equipment, insurance, accountant, training, workspace, marketing, pension saving if self-funded.
4. Weeks off for holidays, public holidays, sickness and training.
5. Days a week they want to work, and how much of a day goes on admin, selling and learning.
6. The last 10 projects: what was delivered, hours spent, price charged, revisions.
7. How they work today from enquiry to invoice, and where it breaks.

**Access:** level 1 to read time logs, invoices and past proposals. Level 2 to draft the rate sheet, package page and message templates in your files, and draft proposals unsent. Level 3 to update your live templates within limits. Sending a quote or contract as binding needs your fresh yes each time; signing is yours.

## Steps

### 1. Billable hours

Working weeks = 52 minus weeks off. Billable hours = working weeks × days a week × hours a day × the billable share (the part of each day that is client work, after admin and selling). Use the person's own records if they have them; otherwise label the share an estimate and explain it.

### 2. Rate floor

Rate floor per hour = (income needed + yearly business costs) ÷ billable hours. Day rate = hourly × hours in a billable day. Show every line.

### 3. Target rate

Add a margin above the floor for slow months, late payers and price rises, and say what the margin covers. Compare with the last 10 projects: work out the real hourly rate for each (price ÷ hours actually spent). Projects far below the floor show where scope or pricing went wrong.

### 4. Reality check

If the floor needs more billable hours than the person can work, say so plainly and give options: raise prices, cut costs, narrow the service to work that pays more, or work more weeks. Do not accept a plan that only works at 50 billable hours a week.

### 5. Three packages

Group the work into three packages that fit how clients buy (for example a starter, a standard and a full package). For each: who it is for, what is in, what is not included, number of revision rounds, timeline, what the client must provide, and price. Price each from the hours it really takes at the target rate, then sense-check against value to the client. Add a rate for extra rounds and out-of-scope work.

### 6. Contract checklist

List the topics the contract should cover: parties, scope and deliverables, timeline, client inputs, payment terms, deposit, late payment, changes and extra work, revisions, ownership and licence of the work, confidentiality, ending early, liability limits, and how disputes are handled. Add questions for a lawyer for the person's country. Check this with a lawyer; I do not write binding contracts.

### 7. Status and tax flags

Flag, without ruling: whether the person might be treated as an employee by a client (US common law tests; UK off-payroll rules if working through their own company), how self-employed tax is paid in their country (for example quarterly estimated tax in the US, Self Assessment in the UK), and VAT or sales tax registration thresholds. Check this with an accountant.

### 8. Client process

Map the steps: enquiry reply, discovery call, proposal, contract and deposit, kickoff, delivery, revisions, sign-off, invoice, payment reminder, follow-up and review request. Write a short, editable message for each. Mark which messages the person sends, and which I may send at level 3 within limits (for example routine payment reminders to existing clients).

## Checks

- Every line of the rate sum is shown, with labelled estimates.
- The billable share is realistic and explained.
- The past-projects check shows each project's real hourly rate.
- Each package has "not included", a revision limit and a timeline.
- The contract checklist ends with lawyer questions; no binding contract is written.
- Status, tax and VAT points are flags for an accountant, not rulings.
- Every binding send is marked as needing a fresh yes.
- The work ends with: *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*

## Output format

1. Rate worksheet: hours, floor, target, day rate, past-project check.
2. Package table: package, for whom, in, not included, revisions, timeline, price, extras.
3. Contract checklist and lawyer questions.
4. Status and tax flags for the accountant.
5. Client process map with message templates and send rules.

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real person: a UX designer leaving a job, needing 70,000 a year before tax. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Working weeks:** 52 − 6 (holidays) − 2 (public holidays) − 1 (sickness) − 1 (training) = 42.
> **Billable hours:** 42 × 4 days × 7 hours × 70% billable = 823 hours (70% is an estimate; track it for three months).
> **Costs:** 9,500 a year (software 2,100, equipment 2,400, insurance 900, accountant 1,600, workspace 2,500).
> **Floor:** (70,000 + 9,500) ÷ 823 = 96.60 an hour; 676 a day (7 hours).
> **Target:** 115 an hour (about 20% above the floor, covering two slow months).
> **Past projects:** 4 of the last 10 came out below 60 an hour; all had unlimited revisions.
>
> | Package | In | Not included | Revisions | Price |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | Audit | Review of 5 key screens, findings deck | Redesign | 1 round | 2,300 |
> | Redesign sprint | 2-week redesign of one flow, prototype (about 56 hours) | Developer handoff files | 2 rounds | 6,400 |
>
> *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*
