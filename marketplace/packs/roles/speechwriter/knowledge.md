# Speechwriter: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform features and access rules change; each one carries its source and the date it was true. This is general practice, not legal or medical advice.

Related kits, so this file does not repeat them: the ghostwriter kit (`ghostwriter`) for voice capture, story interviews and AI disclosure; the presentation designer kit (`presentation-designer`) for slides; the PR specialist kit (`pr-specialist`) for media interviews and statements; the internal communications kit (`internal-communications-specialist`) for town halls and leadership updates to staff.

## 1. What this role is for

A speechwriter turns what someone wants to say into words that work out loud, in the time they have, in front of the room they will face.

**Who hires it.**

- **People with a personal occasion:** a best man, a maid of honour, a parent of the bride or groom, a couple, a grandchild giving a eulogy, a colleague giving a leaving speech, someone raising a glass at a big birthday. Most have never written a speech and are nervous.
- **People speaking for work:** founders pitching investors or buyers, managers giving remarks at a launch or award, experts giving a conference talk, nonprofit leaders speaking at a fundraiser, owners welcoming staff or guests.

**What good work looks like.**

- It sounds like the speaker on a good day, not like a writer. Friends in the room recognise the voice.
- Every story, name, date and number came from the speaker or a checked source. Nothing is invented to get a laugh or a tear.
- It has one clear point or feeling, and the room could say it back afterwards.
- It fits the slot. A five-minute slot gets a speech that runs under five minutes when read aloud at the speaker's own pace, with room for laughter.
- The speaker can deliver it: short sentences, words they can say, names spelt out how they sound, and pauses marked.
- Everyone in the room can follow it: people who are deaf or hard of hearing, people who cannot see the slides, people listening in a second language, children and grandparents.

**How it is judged.** First by the speaker: do they feel ready, and does it sound like them? Then by the room: did people laugh where they should, go quiet where they should, and remember the point? For pitches and talks, by what happened next: the meeting, the follow-up, the invitation, the yes or the clear reason for a no.

**What this role will not do.** Invent stories, quotes, credentials or results. Write jokes that put someone down. Choose religious readings or rites (the family and the officiant do that). Promise funding, deals or wins. Deliver or coach the speech live. Write a speech that discloses a listed company's inside information outside its proper channel (section 3).

## 2. The canon

- Aristotle, *Rhetoric*. Read it for the foundations of persuasion.
- Cicero, *De Oratore*. Read it for the education and range of the orator.
- Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria*. Read it for the training of a speaker.
- Peggy Noonan, *What I Saw at the Revolution*. Read it for a speechwriter's own account of the job.
- Sam Leith, *You Talkin' to Me?*. Read it for a modern, readable guide to rhetoric.
- Max Atkinson, *Lend Me Your Ears*. Read it for speaking to live audiences.
- Chris Anderson, *TED Talks: The Official TED Guide to Public Speaking*. Read it for planning and giving a talk.
- Nancy Duarte, *Resonate*. Read it for story structure in presentations.

For writing craft (Orwell, Zinsser, Pinker, Minto) see the ghostwriter kit; for slides (Reynolds, Duarte's slide work, Mayer) see the presentation designer kit.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Intake

Ask before writing. A speech built on a thin brief fills up with general words.

- **The occasion:** what, where, when, and the order of speeches. Who speaks before and after?
- **The speaker:** their relationship to the person or the room, how confident they are, how they talk, words they never say.
- **The room:** how many people, who they are (ages, languages, colleagues, family), what they already know, and what they expect.
- **The slot:** minutes allowed, and whether that is a hard limit (a service, a broadcast, a pitch slot) or a guide.
- **The point:** the one thing the room should feel or remember. For business talks, the one thing they should do next.
- **The material:** stories, names, dates, facts, notes, slides, past speeches, feedback from earlier pitches. Each story with who was there and whether anyone named has agreed to be mentioned.
- **The avoid list:** topics, people, jokes and words to leave out. For weddings this often includes exes, money, health and drinking; for eulogies, often how the person died.
- **The setting:** microphone, lectern, screen, captions, interpreter, livestream or recording, and whether the text will be published afterwards.
- **Access:** which tools I may see or edit, at which level (section 3, "Connected tools and access").

### Structure that works out loud

Listeners cannot reread. So a speech says where it is going, goes there, and says where it has been.

**Personal speeches** (wedding, eulogy, retirement, toast):

1. Who I am to this person, in one or two lines.
2. Two or three true stories that show who they are. For a happy occasion, usually one that makes people laugh and one that makes them feel something.
3. What those stories mean: the quality they show.
4. The turn to the room or the person: thanks, a wish, a farewell.
5. The close: the toast, the last words, or "thank you".

A eulogy keeps the same bones but slows down. Kind humour is often welcome, but the family decides. How the person died stays out unless the family asks for it.

**Business talks:**

1. A hook: a moment, a question or a fact the room did not expect.
2. One real case or story, told with specifics.
3. Up to three points that come out of that story.
4. One ask or call to action.

**Pitches** follow the order an investor or buyer needs. Sequoia's guide to a business plan runs: company purpose, problem, solution, why now, market potential, competition, business model, team, financials, vision [18]. A spoken pitch compresses this: problem, solution, proof, ask, with the market and team folded in. The speaker then prepares short answers to the questions most likely to come.

**Remarks** (launches, awards, openings): thanks to the right people by name, one story, and a clear close. Remarks fail when the thank-you list eats the time.

**A real structure to borrow.** On his first day as Microsoft's chief executive (4 February 2014), Satya Nadella wrote to all staff using four plain questions as headings: who am I, why am I here, why are we here, and what do we do next [19]. It was an email, not a speech, but the same four questions make a sound frame for any new leader's first remarks: personal, honest about motive, tied to the shared purpose, and ending with a clear ask.

### Writing for the ear

Plain-language rules written for the page apply even more to the ear.

- **Short sentences.** GOV.UK splits sentences over 25 words [1]. Spoken sentences should usually be shorter still, because the listener holds the whole sentence in memory.
- **Active voice and present tense** where accurate; verbs, not nouns made from verbs ("we decided", not "a decision was made") [3].
- **Plain, familiar words**, and specialist terms explained the first time [1].
- **Contractions.** GOV.UK allows positive contractions such as "you'll" [1]. They make speech sound natural.
- **Signposts.** "Three things happened that week." "The second thing." "So here is what I am asking."
- **Repetition with a purpose.** A key line said at the start and again at the end; a phrase that returns at each turn. On the page this looks repetitive; in the room it helps people follow.
- **Numbers.** Round them, say what they mean, and use one or two. A room hears "about one in four" more easily than "24.6%".
- **Names.** Write how they sound: "Aisling (ASH-ling)".
- **Multilingual rooms.** Shorter sentences, fewer idioms, and no jokes that depend on wordplay. The EU's clear writing guide exists in all 24 official languages if the speaker or interpreter wants one [4].

### Timing

- **Plan from a rate, then measure.** The National Center for Voice and Speech gives about 150 words a minute as the average rate for US English speakers [5]. That is a general figure, not a speech rate. Speeches with pauses, laughter and nerves can run slower, and some agents in this role plan at about 130 words a minute to leave that room. Treat any planning rate as a starting guess and replace it with the speaker's own timed read.
- **Put the count on top.** Word count, planned minutes and the rate used, on the first line of every draft.
- **Cut, never speed up.** If it runs long, cut a story or a point. Asking a nervous speaker to talk faster makes it worse.
- **Leave room.** Laughter, applause, a slide change or a glass being raised all take time.

### Rehearsal

The script is not finished until the speaker has said it out loud.

- **First read:** the speaker reads it aloud alone and marks any word they trip on or would never say. Change those words.
- **Timed read:** read at a natural pace with a timer. Fix the length.
- **Stand-up read:** standing, with the notes or cards they will use on the day, to one friendly listener.
- **Room or tech run:** in the room if possible, with the microphone, screen and captions. Microsoft's guidance for large Teams events is to always schedule a dry run [12].

Tools that help (as of 6 Nov 2026):

- **PowerPoint Speaker Coach** checks pacing, pitch, filler words, informal speech, euphemisms, culturally sensitive terms, and reading slide text aloud. It runs on Windows, Mac, web and mobile, but only understands English and needs an English interface. Its report disappears when closed, so take a screenshot [6]. It is not available to government customers in GCC [6].
- **PowerPoint Presenter View** shows the current and next slide, the speaker notes, a timer and the time; the notes text can be made larger [7].
- **Google Slides presenter view** shows speaker notes; audience Q&A is available while presenting [8].
- **Teams events:** Speaker Coach can be used during events if the admin allows it [12].

### Delivery versions

Write three versions from one approved script:

1. **The full script:** clean text for approval and for anyone who will read it later.
2. **The delivery version:** large type, short lines, one thought per line, with [pause], [look up], [slide 4] and [raise glass] marks, and stories labelled so the speaker can find their place.
3. **Cue cards or a one-page outline:** the opening line, the story names, the turn and the closing line. For speakers who prefer not to read word for word.

### Captions and access

Plan access with the organiser before the day, not at the lectern.

**What speakers do** (W3C checklist, updated 31 Aug 2022) [13]:

- Send slides and handouts in advance in formats people can adapt (not only PDF).
- Give captioners and interpreters the script, names, acronyms and terms early.
- Describe what is on the slides and what happens in the room ("about half of you raised your hand").
- Speak clearly and not too fast; pause between topics; stay visible and in good light.
- Repeat audience questions into the microphone before answering.
- Caption any video and provide text for audio.

**Live captions.** WCAG 2.2 success criterion 1.2.4 (level AA) asks for captions on live audio in synchronised media; it is aimed at broadcasts rather than two-way calls (page updated 9 Mar 2026) [14]. CART is real-time captioning by a professional captioner [13]. Automatic captions are now built into common tools:

- **PowerPoint** shows live captions or translated subtitles in more than 60 subtitle languages. It needs a fast connection throughout, works best with a headset microphone, and sends speech to Microsoft's speech service [9].
- **Google Slides** captions work in Chrome, Edge and Safari, have no punctuation, are not stored, and caption only the presenter's voice; Google says accuracy varies with accent and intonation and suggests asking the audience before turning them on [10]. Google's presenting help says captions are English only [8].
- **Microsoft Teams** lets any participant turn on live captions. Translated captions in meetings need a Teams Premium or Microsoft Copilot licence, CART captions are supported, and Teams does not save captions [11]. In events optimised for large audiences, only AI-generated captions are available, and organisers can choose up to 10 caption translation languages without Teams Premium [12].

Automatic captions get names, accents and jargon wrong. For a high-stakes event, a eulogy with many names, or a deaf guest who relies on captions, book a human captioner and send them the script.

**Rules by region** (general information, not legal advice):

- **US:** the ADA's effective communication rules cover state and local governments (Title II) and businesses and nonprofits that serve the public (Title III). Auxiliary aids include CART, captioning and sign language interpreters. Under Title II the person's choice of aid gets primary consideration (ADA.gov, updated 28 Feb 2020) [15].
- **UK:** government guidance says to use BSL interpreters regulated by the NRCPD, add captions too, offer formats at the same time as the standard version, and make large print 16 point or more; Easy Read can take weeks to commission (updated 17 Jun 2026) [16].
- **EU:** the European Accessibility Act has applied since 28 June 2025 to listed products and services such as e-commerce, banking and e-books; it does not name speeches or meetings [17]. Duties for events come from national law and the venue; ask the organiser.

### Quotes, poems and lyrics

Speeches often borrow a line. Credit it, check it was really said by the person named, and keep it short.

- **US:** fair use is decided case by case on four factors; there is no safe word count or percentage [20].
- **UK:** fair dealing allows quotation with sufficient acknowledgement; usually only part of a work may be used [21].

A whole poem read at a funeral or wedding is a common request. Reading it in the room is different from printing it in an order of service or posting a recording online; for printed or published use, check the rights holder's terms.

### Business speeches and market rules

If the speaker works for a listed company, a speech can move the share price.

- **US:** Regulation FD bars listed issuers from disclosing material nonpublic information selectively to market professionals or holders who may trade on it; an unintentional disclosure must be made public promptly [22].
- **UK and EU:** UK MAR, the UK version of EU MAR, bans unlawful disclosure of inside information and requires issuers to disclose it promptly [23].

So any new figure, forecast, deal or result in a listed company's speech goes past investor relations or the legal team before the speaker says it. See the PR specialist kit for disclosure timing.

### Nerves

Most first-time speakers are nervous, and a well-timed, well-rehearsed script helps. If fear of speaking stops someone doing things, or social anxiety has a big impact on their life, the NHS suggests seeing a GP, and in England people can refer themselves to NHS talking therapies [24]. A speechwriter does not diagnose or treat; outside the UK, the speaker's own doctor is the place to start.

### Publishing the speech afterwards

If the text will be posted, say which version it is: the prepared script or what was actually said. Add captions or a transcript to any recording [13]. Publishing under the speaker's name needs their sign-off on the final text.

### Connected tools and access (as of 6 Nov 2026)

Access is granted per tool in three levels: read (level 1), read and draft (level 2), and read, write and publish within the client's limits (level 3). Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1. At level 2 I write in a copy or in suggesting mode and add speaker notes to a draft deck. At level 3 I update the master script and the live deck's speaker notes and caption settings, within the files and dates the client set. Publishing the speech text under the speaker's name, sending it to press, and any statement made on behalf of an organisation need a fresh yes each time. Tool details are in access.md.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Each figure has a source, a date and a caveat.

- **Speaking rate:** about 150 words a minute for US English speakers (NCVS) [5]. *Caveat:* a general average, not a speech rate; time the speaker's own read.
- **Sentence length:** split sentences over 25 words (GOV.UK) [1]. *Caveat:* a page rule; spoken sentences should usually be shorter.
- **Literacy:** 1 in 6 adults in England have very poor literacy skills (GOV.UK, citing the Literacy Trust) [1]. *Caveat:* definitions differ by nation.
- **Subtitle languages:** more than 60 in PowerPoint live subtitles [9]. *Caveat:* some spoken languages are in preview with lower accuracy.
- **Caption translation in large Teams events:** up to 10 languages without Teams Premium (Microsoft Learn, updated 4 Nov 2026) [12].
- **Large print:** 16 point or more (GOV.UK) [16].
- **Captions:** live captions are a WCAG level AA criterion for synchronised media [14].

No source I checked gives a reliable standard length for a wedding speech, toast or eulogy. Use the slot the couple, family or organiser gives, and the speaker's timed read.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the brief has no true stories, ask for them before drafting, because a speech without specifics turns into general praise nobody remembers.
- If a story names someone other than the speaker or the subject, check that person is happy to be mentioned, because the room will look at them.
- If a joke could embarrass anyone in the room, including children and older relatives, cut it, because one bad joke is what the room remembers.
- If a draft runs over time at the speaker's own pace, cut a story or a point rather than asking them to speak faster.
- If a spoken sentence needs a second breath, split it [1].
- If the room includes people listening in a second language, use shorter sentences and drop wordplay [4].
- If a deaf or hard of hearing guest is coming, or the event is recorded or streamed, plan captions with the organiser, and prefer a human captioner when names and terms matter [13][14].
- If slides carry information, the speaker says it aloud too, so people who cannot see the screen are not left out [13].
- If the speaker works for a listed company and the talk includes new figures, forecasts or deals, send it to investor relations or legal first [22][23].
- If a quote is borrowed, check who really said it and credit them; if a whole poem or song is going into print or online, check the rights [20][21].
- If a step would publish the speech or send it outside the client, check the access level and get the speaker's fresh yes.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **The CV speech.** A list of achievements with no story. Spot it: no scene, no moment, no other person speaking.
- **The inside joke.** A story only five people understand. Spot it: it needs explaining to anyone outside the group.
- **Written for the eye.** Long sentences, brackets and footnote-style asides. Spot it: the speaker runs out of breath or loses their place when reading aloud.
- **Over time.** Spot it: no word count or timed read on the draft.
- **The slide reader.** The script repeats what is on the slides. Spot it: Speaker Coach flags reading slide text [6], or the notes match the slides word for word.
- **Invented colour.** A made-up quote or a story "improved" past what happened. Spot it: no source for a line in the brief.
- **The long thank-you list.** Spot it: more than a few names in a row with nothing said about any of them.
- **The weak ending.** The speech stops instead of closing. Spot it: the last line is not the toast, the ask or the point.
- **Captions as an afterthought.** Spot it: nobody has sent the captioner the names, or the slides go out only as a PDF on the day [13].
- **Market news in a speech.** Spot it: a listed company's speaker plans to "share some numbers for the first time" at a conference [22][23].

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can I say the point in one sentence, and does the speech land it at the end?
2. Is every story, name, date and number from the speaker or a checked source, with any doubt marked [check]?
3. Does it sound like the speaker when read aloud by them?
4. Does it fit the slot at their timed pace, with room for laughter or applause?
5. Is the humour kind, and safe for everyone likely to be in the room?
6. Is every sentence sayable in one breath, with names spelt how they sound?
7. Is the delivery version marked with pauses, slide cues and the close?
8. Are captions, interpreters and materials arranged, and has the captioner got the names and terms [13]?
9. Are quotes credited and checked [20][21]?
10. For a listed company, has investor relations or legal cleared any new figures [22][23]?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** Full script; delivery version; cue cards or a one-page outline; timing sheet; rehearsal plan; likely questions with short answers (pitches and talks); captioner and interpreter brief; a "check list" of every fact still marked [check].

**Script header.** Occasion, speaker, date, slot in minutes, word count, planning rate, version number.

**Conventions.** One sentence or thought per line in the delivery version. Marks in square brackets: [pause], [slow], [look at them], [slide 4], [raise glass], [check]. Names with a sound-out on first use. Stories given short labels ("the Fiesta story") so the speaker can find them. British or American spelling to match the speaker. Contractions where the speaker would use them.

**Tone.** The speaker's, not mine. Warm for weddings, gentle for eulogies, direct for pitches, generous for remarks. No stock phrases ("without further ado", "it goes without saying") unless the speaker really talks that way.

## 9. Glossary

- **Slot:** the time a speaker is given.
- **Planning rate:** the words a minute used to estimate length before the speaker's timed read [5].
- **Timed read:** the speaker reading the script aloud at their natural pace, with a timer.
- **Delivery version:** the script laid out for speaking, with short lines and cue marks.
- **Cue card:** a small card with the key lines and story labels.
- **Signpost:** a phrase that tells listeners where the speech is going.
- **Callback:** a line or image from early in a speech that returns at the end.
- **Hook:** the opening moment that makes the room pay attention.
- **The ask:** the one thing a business talk or pitch asks the room to do.
- **Remarks:** a short, formal speech at a launch, award or opening.
- **Presenter view:** the speaker's screen with notes, the next slide and a timer [7].
- **Speaker Coach:** PowerPoint's rehearsal feedback tool [6].
- **Captions:** a text version of speech and important sounds [13].
- **CART:** real-time captioning by a professional captioner, also called live captioning [13].
- **Automatic captions:** captions made by speech recognition, with variable accuracy [10].
- **BSL interpreter:** a British Sign Language interpreter; in the UK, regulated by the NRCPD [16].
- **Auxiliary aids:** under the ADA, the aids and services that make communication effective, such as CART and interpreters [15].
- **Fair use / fair dealing:** the US and UK copyright exceptions that may allow short quotation [20][21].
- **Regulation FD:** the US rule against selective disclosure by listed companies [22].
- **Inside information:** under UK and EU MAR, information that must be disclosed properly and not leaked [23].
