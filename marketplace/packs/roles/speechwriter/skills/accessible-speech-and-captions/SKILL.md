# Accessible speech and captions

A speech that only works for people who can see the slides and hear every word leaves people out. Most of the fixes are in the script and the preparation, not on the day. This skill builds them in and gives captioners and interpreters what they need to do their job well.

## When to use

- A talk with slides, charts, video or demos.
- An event that is livestreamed, recorded or hybrid.
- A guest or audience member who is deaf, hard of hearing, blind or partially sighted, or listening in a second language.
- A booked captioner or sign language interpreter.
- A eulogy or wedding speech full of names, where automatic captions would struggle.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the script, deck and event details. Level 2 to add spoken descriptions and notes in a copy, and to draft the captioner and interpreter brief. Level 3 to update the live deck, set caption options in the files the speaker named, and send the brief to the people the speaker named. Publishing a recording or transcript needs a fresh yes.

- The final or near-final script and slides.
- The event format: in person, online, hybrid; the platform; whether it is recorded.
- What the organiser has booked: human captioner (CART), interpreter, hearing loop, screen for captions.
- Any access needs the speaker or organiser knows about.

## Steps

1. **Ask the organiser early.** What access is booked, and what do attendees need? The W3C's event checklist puts this at registration, with arrangements made early.
2. **Say what the slides show.** For every slide with meaning (a chart, a photo, a quote), add a spoken line to the script: "This chart shows sales doubling in the second year." Describe audience reactions too: "About half of you raised a hand." Nobody should need to see the screen to follow.
3. **Plain words for the room.** Short sentences, terms explained the first time, few idioms and no jokes that depend on wordplay. This helps second-language listeners, interpreters and captions at once.
4. **Mark the pace.** Add [pause] between sections. Interpreters and captioners need the speaker to go at a pace they can follow; W3C notes sign languages are not word-for-word translations.
5. **Choose captions.**
   - **Human captioner (CART):** best when names, terms and accuracy matter, for large or recorded events, and when a guest relies on captions. Book through the organiser.
   - **PowerPoint live captions:** captions or translated subtitles in more than 60 subtitle languages; needs a fast connection all the way through; a headset microphone helps; speech is sent to Microsoft's speech service.
   - **Google Slides captions:** Chrome, Edge or Safari; no punctuation; not stored; only the presenter is captioned; Google's help says English only; ask the audience before turning them on.
   - **Microsoft Teams:** any participant can turn on live captions; translated captions in meetings need a Teams Premium or Microsoft Copilot licence; Teams does not save captions; large-audience events offer AI captions only.
   Automatic captions get names and accents wrong. Test them in the room with the speaker's voice.
6. **Write the captioner and interpreter brief.** The script, the running order, every name with how it sounds, acronyms with what they stand for, technical terms, song or reading titles, and any words in another language. Send it as early as the organiser allows.
7. **Send materials in advance.** Slides and handouts in formats people can adapt (a Word or Google document, or a web page), not only a PDF. Caption any video and give text for any audio.
8. **On the day.** Repeat audience questions into the microphone before answering. Stay in good light and face the room or camera. Keep the interpreter lit and visible.
9. **After.** If the talk is recorded or published, add captions or a transcript, and say whether the text is the prepared script or what was said.

**Rules by region (general information, not legal advice):** in the US, the ADA's effective communication rules cover state and local governments and businesses and nonprofits that serve the public, with CART, captioning and interpreters among the aids. In the UK, government guidance says to use NRCPD-regulated BSL interpreters, add captions too, and offer large print at 16 point or more. In the EU, the European Accessibility Act covers listed products and services, not speeches as such; event duties come from national law. Ask the organiser what they must provide.

## Checks

- Every meaningful slide is described in the spoken script.
- Audience reactions and questions are spoken aloud.
- The captioner and interpreter have names, terms and the script in advance.
- The caption choice fits the stakes; automatic captions have been tested in the room.
- Materials are sent early in an adaptable format.
- Video has captions; audio has text.
- Any recording or transcript is labelled and captioned before publishing.

## Output format

1. Access plan: what is booked, who provides it, and what the speaker does.
2. Script with spoken descriptions marked [describe].
3. Captioner and interpreter brief.
4. Caption settings or instructions for the tool in use.
5. Day-of and after-event checklist.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a credit union's annual members' meeting. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Access plan:** Annual members' meeting, hybrid, 80 in the room and about 200 online, recorded. Organiser has booked a CART captioner shown on a second screen and on the stream, and a BSL interpreter. PowerPoint live subtitles as a backup if the captioner's link fails.

**Script (extract):**

> [slide 3] [describe] This chart shows our loans to members for each of the last five years. The line rises every year. This year it is twice what it was five years ago.

**Captioner and interpreter brief (extract):**

| Word | Say it | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Siobhán Ní Bhriain | shiv-AWN nee VREE-in | Chair, speaks second |
| CU | "credit union" | Spoken in full; slide shows "CU" |
| [Business] | as written | Our name; flag any spelling a captioner could get wrong |

**Send by:** Friday, with the final script and slides as a Word file and a PDF.
