# Speech from stories

A personal speech works when the room recognises the person being spoken about. That comes from true, specific stories, not from praise. This skill turns the speaker's memories into a speech that fits the slot, sounds like them and ends well.

## When to use

- A wedding speech for the best man, maid of honour, a parent or the couple.
- A eulogy or memorial speech, or a written tribute for an order of service, a paper or online.
- A retirement or leaving speech, for a colleague or for yourself.
- A birthday or anniversary toast.
- A speech the speaker has already written that runs long or has too many inside jokes.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the notes, voice-note transcripts and past drafts the speaker shares. Level 2 to write in a copy or in suggesting mode. Level 3 to update the master script and share it with the people the speaker named. Posting the speech or a recording online under anyone's name needs a fresh yes.

- The occasion, date, running order, and the minutes allowed.
- Who the speaker is to the person, and how the speaker talks (a voice note helps).
- Every story, name, date and detail, in the speaker's words.
- The avoid list: people, topics and jokes to leave out.
- Who is in the room: children, older relatives, colleagues, people listening in a second language, anyone deaf or hard of hearing.
- For a eulogy: what the family wants included, whether humour is welcome, and whether the officiant has a time or content limit.

## Steps

1. **Read everything and list the facts.** Every name, date and story goes on a fact list with where it came from. Anything unclear is marked [check].
2. **Keep the avoid list beside you.** Read it again before each draft.
3. **Pick two or three stories.** Choose the ones that show who the person is, not what they achieved. For a happy occasion, aim for one that makes people laugh and one that makes them feel something. Drop stories only a few people would understand.
4. **Name the quality.** For each story, write in one line what it shows ("he gets lost, but he never leaves anyone behind"). That line becomes the turn after the story.
5. **Shape it.** Who I am to them (one or two lines). The stories, each followed by what it shows. What they mean to me or to us. The turn to the person or the room. The close: toast, farewell, thanks or good wishes.
6. **Write the way the speaker talks.** Short sentences. Contractions if they use them. Plain words. Names spelt how they sound on first use. One idea per sentence. No stock phrases unless the speaker really says them.
7. **Check the humour.** Kind teasing only, always followed by warmth. Safe for children and grandparents. No jokes about exes, money, health, drinking, weight or anyone in the room who has not agreed. If a laugh depends on embarrassing someone, cut it.
8. **Time it.** Count the words. Divide by a planning rate to estimate minutes, then replace that with the speaker's own timed read as soon as they have one. The National Center for Voice and Speech gives about 150 words a minute as the average for US English speakers; speeches with pauses and laughter can run slower. Put the word count, planned minutes and rate on the first line.
9. **Cut to fit.** Cut a story or a list, never ask the speaker to talk faster. Keep the close whole.
10. **Write the delivery version.** Large type, one thought per line, [pause] after each laugh line and before the close, [look at them] at the turn, [raise glass] for a toast, and short labels for each story.
11. **Final check.** Every fact is on the fact list. Anything uncertain is still marked [check]. Then send it for the speaker's read-aloud.

**For eulogies:** keep it gentle and slower. Leave out how the person died unless the family asks for it. Include the person's own words or sayings only if the family supplied them. Check names of family members carefully, including who is mentioned and who is not. Religious readings and rites are the family's and the officiant's choice.

**For a polish job:** keep the speaker's best stories and their voice. Remove inside jokes, long lists of names and anything over time. Return a short note of what was cut and why.

## Checks

- The speech has one clear feeling, and the close lands it.
- Every story, name and date is from the speaker; uncertain items are marked [check].
- Nothing from the avoid list appears.
- The humour is kind and safe for everyone likely to be in the room.
- Every sentence can be said in one breath.
- The word count, minutes and rate are on the first line, and it fits the slot.
- The delivery version has pause marks and a clear close.
- Private details (health, family conflict, cause of death) are left out unless asked for.

## Output format

1. Header: occasion, speaker, slot, word count, planning rate, estimated minutes, version.
2. The full script.
3. The delivery version.
4. Cue cards: opening line, story labels, the turn, closing line.
5. Fact list with [check] items, and a note of anything left out and why.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not real people: a best man's speech at his brother's wedding. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Header:** Best man speech, Tom and Aisling's wedding. Slot 5 minutes. 560 words. Planned at 130 words a minute: about 4 minutes 20 seconds, leaving time for laughter. Version 2. Replace with your timed read.

**Delivery version (extract):**

> For those who don't know me, I'm Dan. Tom's little brother.
> [pause]
> Tom taught me to drive. In his old Fiesta. In the car park behind the leisure centre.
> He said, "It's simple. Just don't hit anything."
> [pause for laugh]
> I hit a trolley.
> He didn't shout. He got out, put the trolley back, got in, and said, "Again."
> [slow]
> That's Tom. He doesn't give up on people.
> [look at Aisling]
> Aisling, you've found someone who will always say "again".

**Fact list (extract):** Fiesta, from brief. Leisure centre car park: [check] with Dan, the brief says only "a car park".
