# Talk or pitch script

A business talk is remembered for one story and one point. A pitch is judged on whether the listener understands the problem, believes the proof and knows what is being asked. This skill builds both from the speaker's real material, and fixes a pitch after the noes without rewriting what already works.

## When to use

- A conference, meetup or industry talk of 10 to 20 minutes.
- An investor, buyer or partner pitch: a 60-second version and a 3-minute version.
- Remarks at a launch, award, opening or anniversary.
- A pitch that keeps getting a no, with the reasons the listeners gave.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the notes, slides, data and feedback the speaker shares. Level 2 to write the script in a copy and add speaker notes to a copy of the deck. Level 3 to update the master script and the live deck's notes within the files the speaker named. Sending the script to press or organisers as a published text, and any listed-company speech with new figures, needs a fresh yes.

- The audience, the setting, the time slot and the one thing they should remember or do.
- One real case or story the speaker can stand behind, with its facts.
- Notes, slides, data and past talks.
- For pitches: the problem, the product, the proof (customers, results, pilots), the team, the money asked for and what it pays for.
- For fixes: the script used and every reason given for a no.
- Whether the speaker works for a listed company.

## Steps

1. **Write the point in one sentence.** If the speaker cannot agree on one sentence, stop and settle it first. Everything else serves it.
2. **Choose the case.** One true, specific story: a customer, a failure, a day that changed things. It needs a person, a place, a moment and a result. No composite or invented cases. If the case uses a client's name or results, check the speaker has permission.
3. **Draft the talk shape.** Hook (the moment, question or surprising fact), the story, up to three points that come out of it, the ask. Each point gets one example and one line the room can repeat.
4. **Draft the pitch shape.** Problem, solution, proof, ask, in that order. For a longer pitch, fold in why now, the market, competition, the business model and the team; Sequoia's business plan guide gives a full order (company purpose, problem, solution, why now, market potential, competition, business model, team, financials, vision) that a 3-minute pitch can compress. Keep financials to what the speaker can defend.
5. **Draft remarks.** Thanks by name to the few who matter most, each with one specific line; one story; the close. Put a long thank-you list in the printed programme instead.
6. **Write for the ear.** Short sentences, active voice, plain words, contractions. Signposts ("the second thing"). One or two round numbers, each with what it means. Repeat the key line at the start and the end. Mark names with how they sound.
7. **Separate slides from speech.** The speaker says what the slide shows, so nobody is left out, but the slide does not carry the script. Detail goes in notes or a handout.
8. **Prepare for questions.** List the five questions the room is most likely to ask, including the hardest one. Write a short, true answer to each, and "I don't know yet, here is how we'll find out" where that is the honest answer.
9. **Add timing.** Word count and planned minutes on top; a time mark at each section ("by 4:00 you should be starting point two"); [pause] before the ask.
10. **Check market rules.** If the speaker works for a listed company and the talk includes new figures, forecasts or deals, send it to investor relations or legal before rehearsal. In the US, Regulation FD bars selective disclosure of material nonpublic information; in the UK and EU, market abuse rules govern inside information.
11. **For a pitch fix:** list each no with its reason. Group the reasons. Find the one part of the pitch that caused most of them (usually the problem is unclear, the proof is thin, or the ask is vague). Change that part, keep the rest, and add an answer for each repeated doubt. Never add claims the speaker cannot back.

## Checks

- One-sentence point, said at the start and at the end.
- One true case, with permission for any named client.
- Every fact, number and credential is the speaker's and can be backed; nothing invented.
- No promise of results, funding or returns.
- Sentences short enough to say in one breath.
- Five likely questions with honest answers.
- Time marks add up to the slot.
- Listed company: investor relations or legal has cleared new figures.

## Output format

1. Header: event, audience, slot, word count, planned minutes, version.
2. The one-sentence point.
3. The script with section labels and time marks.
4. Speaker notes per slide, if there are slides.
5. Likely questions and answers.
6. For a fix: the no-reasons table, the part changed, and the new version.

## Example of good output (short)

*Riverside Food Hub is a made-up food bank, used only to show the format.*

**Point:** When the flood came, the food bank that knew its neighbours by name got food out first.

**Opening (0:00 to 1:00):**

> On the second night of the flood, our van was under a metre of water.
> [pause]
> And by morning, we had fed 200 families.
> Not because we had a plan. Because we had a list of names.

**Point one (by 4:00):** Know who you serve before the crisis. One example: Mrs K's phone list of 40 neighbours who could not drive.

**The ask (14:00 to 15:00):** "Before you leave today, write down three names of people in your area who would need you first. That's your plan."

**Likely question:** "How did you keep the list private?" Answer: "Names and phone numbers only, kept by two volunteers, deleted when the emergency ended." [check] with the speaker.
