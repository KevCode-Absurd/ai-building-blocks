# Timing and rehearsal plan

A script is only finished when the speaker has said it out loud in the time they have. This skill measures the script, cuts it to fit, gives the speaker something easy to read from, and plans rehearsal so the day holds no surprises.

## When to use

- A finished draft that nobody has read aloud yet.
- A speech that runs over its slot.
- A nervous first-time speaker.
- A talk with slides, captions, a livestream or a tight running order.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the script and deck. Level 2 to lay out the delivery version in a copy and add timing marks and notes to a copy of the deck. Level 3 to update the live deck's speaker notes and subtitle settings within the files the speaker named. The rehearsal tools that listen to the voice run on the speaker's own device; I give the settings and read the results they share.

- The approved script and any slides.
- The slot, and whether it is a hard limit.
- The speaker's timed read, if they have done one.
- The setting: lectern or handheld microphone, screen, presenter display, captions, livestream.

## Steps

1. **Count and estimate.** Count the words. Estimate minutes with a planning rate. The National Center for Voice and Speech gives about 150 words a minute as the average for US English speakers; speeches with pauses, laughter and nerves can run slower, so a lower planning rate such as 130 leaves room. State the rate used. This is only an estimate.
2. **Get the real number.** Ask the speaker to read aloud at a natural pace with a phone timer and send the time. From now on use their rate, not the estimate.
3. **Add the extras.** Add time for expected laughter, applause, a slide change, a video or a toast. Leave a margin before a hard limit.
4. **Cut to fit.** If it runs long, cut whole units: a story, a point, a list, a second example. Do not trim words from every sentence; that makes it harder to say. Never cut the close. Never ask the speaker to talk faster.
5. **Lay out the delivery version.** Large type (at least 16 point if printed; GOV.UK treats 16 point and above as large print), one thought per line, wide margins, page numbers, no sentence split across pages. Add [pause], [slow], [look up], [slide 4], [raise glass] and short story labels. Mark names with how they sound.
6. **Make cue cards.** One card per section: the first line word for word, the story label, the key line, and the last line of the section. The final card has the close word for word.
7. **Plan the rehearsals.** Plan these four runs:
   - Run 1, alone, aloud: mark every word the speaker trips on or would never say, and change those words.
   - Run 2, timed: confirm the length.
   - Run 3, standing, to one friendly listener, with the cards or delivery version they will use.
   - Run 4, in the room or on the platform if possible, with the microphone, screen, captions and clicker. For large online events, Microsoft advises always scheduling a dry run.
8. **Use tools where they help.**
   - PowerPoint Speaker Coach gives feedback on pacing, pitch, filler words, informal speech, euphemisms, culturally sensitive terms and reading slide text. It understands English only and its report disappears when closed, so the speaker should screenshot it.
   - PowerPoint Presenter View shows the notes, the next slide and a timer; make the notes text larger.
   - Google Slides presenter view shows speaker notes.
   Tools give hints, not verdicts. A human listener catches more.
9. **Plan the day.** Water, where to stand, who introduces the speaker, the first line memorised, the printed delivery version as a backup even if using notes on screen, and what to do if the slides fail (keep talking; the script does not depend on them).

## Checks

- The time comes from the speaker's own read, not only an estimate.
- Extras (laughter, applause, video) are added, with a margin before a hard limit.
- Cuts removed whole units, and the close is intact.
- The delivery version is large, one thought per line, with marks and story labels.
- Cue cards exist for speakers who will not read word for word.
- Four rehearsals are scheduled, including one in the room or on the platform.
- There is a backup if slides, captions or the screen fail.

## Output format

1. Timing sheet: words, planning rate, timed read, extras, total, slot, margin.
2. Cut list: what was cut, why, and the time saved.
3. Delivery version.
4. Cue cards.
5. Rehearsal plan with dates, who listens, and what each run checks.
6. Day-of checklist.

## Example of good output (short)

*Priya and the Greenhill Tennis Club are made up, used only to show the format.*

**Timing sheet:** Retirement speech for the club coach. Slot 6 minutes, hard limit (dinner service starts). Script 890 words. Planning rate 130: about 6:50. Priya's timed read: 7:20. Laughter expected after three lines: add about 20 seconds. Total 7:40. Over by 1:40, plus a 30-second margin: cut about 2:10.

**Cut list:**

| Cut | Why | Saves |
|---|---|---|
| The 1998 tournament story | Third story; the room only knows the first two | 1:10 |
| List of eight committee names | Thanks by name go in the printed menu instead | 0:40 |
| Second example of his patience | Point already made | 0:25 |

**New total:** 5:25 including laughter. Margin 35 seconds. Close unchanged.

**Rehearsal plan:** Mon, alone aloud. Tue, timed. Thu, standing, to her partner, with cards. Sat 5pm, in the clubhouse with the microphone before guests arrive.
