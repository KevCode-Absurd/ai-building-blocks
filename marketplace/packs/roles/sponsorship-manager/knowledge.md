# Sponsorship manager: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Disclosure rules, tax treatment and platform features change, so each rule says when it was true. This is general practice, not legal or tax advice: contracts, VAT and US unrelated business income questions go to the client's lawyer or a qualified accountant.

Nearby kits, not repeated here: event manager (`https://agents.useaiblocks.com/packs/roles/event-manager/knowledge.md`: trade show and event sponsorship from the buyer's side, run sheets and attendee data), fundraising specialist (charity appeals, Gift Aid, donor thanks), community manager (outside communities and ambassador disclosure) and email marketing specialist (consent and deliverability in depth).

## 1. What this role is for

Festivals, sports clubs, charities, schools, conferences, meetups, podcasts, newsletters and YouTube channels hire this role. They own something an audience cares about (an event, a team, a show, a list) and need businesses to pay to be associated with it. Most have no sales team: a founder, a committee member or a producer sells sponsorship on top of another job. The agents in this role cover five jobs:

- **Inventory and tiers:** list everything the property can offer, value it from real audience numbers, and package it into a few tiers with limits.
- **The deck:** the story, the audience facts, the tiers and how to say yes.
- **Finding sponsors:** score the client's contact list for fit, budget signs and warmth, then write short, tailored pitches and follow-ups.
- **Proposals and contracts:** a proposal for one sponsor built on that sponsor's goals, then a deliverables schedule for the contract the client signs.
- **Fulfilment and renewal:** a tracker of every promise with due dates, owners and proof, disclosures done right, and an end-of-season report that makes renewal easy.

What good work looks like:

- **Every audience number has a source and a date.** Ticket scans, host downloads counted to a standard, platform analytics; never estimates dressed as facts.
- **Sponsors buy outcomes, not logos.** Proposals start from what the sponsor wants (reach a group, be seen supporting a cause, meet buyers, get content) and show how the package serves it.
- **Promises are kept and proven.** Each deliverable has a date, an owner and proof (photo, screenshot, link, recording, scan count).
- **Ads look like ads.** Paid mentions, ad reads and sponsored posts are labelled clearly, up front.
- **No overclaiming.** No promise of sales for the sponsor, no implied partnership that has ended, no tax claims.

How it is judged: sponsorship income signed against target, renewal rate, share of promises delivered on time with proof, sponsor satisfaction at the wrap-up, and a clean record with regulators and platforms.

## 2. The canon

**Kim Skildum-Reid.** Her *Sponsorship Seeker's Toolkit* (fourth edition, with Anne-Marie Grey) is a step-by-step guide for rightsholders, and *The Corporate Sponsorship Toolkit* (second edition) is written for sponsors; its core idea is that sponsorship works best when it ties a brand to something the audience already cares about [22]. Her site offers a free generic inventory template and the white paper "Last Generation Sponsorship Redux", rewritten for 2025 [23]. The habits this kit takes from that school: sell to the sponsor's objectives, list every asset before pricing, and plan with the sponsor how they will use the deal, not just where the logo goes.

**The sponsor's side.** Read Skildum-Reid's free sponsor-side guidelines template [23] to see what a sponsor's brief asks of a proposal: objectives, target audience, brand fit and what partners must provide. Good seekers answer those questions before they are asked.

**The profession.** The European Sponsorship Association runs awards with case studies, a Diploma, an introduction course and a Sales Certificate [24]. Its annual Sponsorship Market Overview, provided by Nielsen Sports, is free to members only [25].

**Further reading (credited, no claims drawn from them here):** T. Bettina Cornwell, *Sponsorship in Marketing*, an academic overview of how sponsorship works.

**Rules as canon.** For this role the disclosure codes are as basic as any book: the UK CAP Code section 2 [1], the ASA/CAP influencer guidance [2][3], the FTC's Endorsement Guides [8][9] and the EU's unfair commercial practices rules [12][13]. Section 3.3 sets them out.

## 3. How the work is done now

### 3.1 Inventory and tiers

- **List everything first.** For a club: logos on kit, equipment, minibus and pitch-side banners; the website and email footers; mentions in event publicity and season reports; naming a cup or event; hosting a sponsor's own event at the venue [18]. For an event: naming rights, stage mentions, signage, stands, sampling, tickets and hospitality, the app and emails. For a podcast: pre-roll, mid-roll and post-roll reads, episode or series naming, show notes links. For a newsletter: the top slot, a mid-issue slot, a dedicated send, classified lines. Skildum-Reid's generic inventory is a useful checklist [23].
- **Ask for more than cash.** Clubs can also ask for kit, product discounts or facilities [18]. Value-in-kind deals still need a written list of what each side gives.
- **Value from the buyer's numbers.** Price each item from the audience it reaches and what the sponsor would otherwise pay to reach those people, using the client's own data. Never invent a reach figure or a market rate.
- **Build two to four tiers** with clear names, what each includes, and a limit on how many are sold (one title partner, a few gold, more supporters). Exclusivity by category (one bank, one brewery) is a valuable right; price it and write it into the contract.
- **Check category limits early.** Gambling ads must not have strong appeal to under-18s under the CAP Code; in 2023 the ASA ruled against a bet365-promoted tweet featuring Arsenal captain Granit Xhaka because top-club footballers are high-risk for under-18 appeal [5]. A junior team, a family event or a youth audience changes which sponsors and assets fit. Alcohol, HFSS food and other categories have their own codes: check them before offering inventory.

### 3.2 Audience numbers you can stand behind

- **Podcasts.** IAB Tech Lab's Podcast Measurement Technical Guidelines are at version 2.3 (September 2026) [19]. Downloads are counted by IP address plus user agent within a stated window, a download must include enough content to play one minute, bots and data-centre traffic are filtered, and a dynamically inserted ad counts only if all its bytes were downloaded [20]. Version 2.3 adds video podcasts and uses "podcast consumer" instead of "listener" [20]. Ask whether the host follows the guidelines, and never add up episode uniques as reach: it overstates the audience [20].
- **Newsletters.** Opens are unreliable (see the email marketing kit), so sell on subscribers, clicks and past sponsor results. Ad networks price by results: beehiiv's Ad Network pays by CPM per 1,000 unique opens or CPC per verified unique click, tracks for 72 hours after sending, and needs a paid plan [21]. For direct deals, agree the pricing basis (per slot, per click or per 1,000 subscribers) in writing.
- **Events and clubs.** Ticket scans, registrations, gate counts, member numbers, match attendance and social analytics, each with its date and source. Say which are estimates.
- **Every number in a deck carries a footnote:** source, period and date.

### 3.3 Disclosure: ads must look like ads

**UK (as of November 2026).** Marketing must be obviously identifiable, and marketers must make their commercial intent clear; advertorials need a label such as "advertisement feature" (CAP Code rules 2.1, 2.3 and 2.4) [1]. ASA/CAP advice dated 6 August 2026 says content is an ad when there is payment or another incentive, a commercial connection, own products or an affiliate link; use "Ad", "Advert", "Advertising" or "Ad feature" up front; words such as "Sponsored", "Supported by", "Gifted", "In association with" or "Thanks to X" are not recommended on their own; on YouTube the label belongs in the title or thumbnail; Instagram's Paid partnership label may not be enough alone [2]. The ASA's influencers' guide is now in a fourth, 2026 edition (page dated 23 October 2026), made with the CMA and covering the CMA's enforcement powers [3]. Under the DMCC Act, from 6 April 2025, paid editorial promotion not made clear is a banned practice, as is falsely claiming approval or endorsement by a public or private body, and the CMA can enforce directly [7].

Note the word "sponsored". The ASA's advice is written for social media, but its rule of thumb (say "Ad" rather than rely on "sponsored by") is the safe default for paid reads and posts in other media too [2]. A sponsor's name on a banner is a credit; a host or creator talking up the sponsor's product is an ad. When in doubt, label it "Ad".

**US.** The FTC says an endorsement made out loud needs at least an audible disclosure, "Sponsored by XYZ" is clearer than "Sponsored", and disclosures belong next to the endorsement, not only in a description or the comments [8]. Influencers disclose any financial, employment, personal or family relationship and free products, in the video itself and repeated in live streams [9]. The guidance does not name podcasts, so apply the audio rule: say it in the read [8]. Advertisers answer for their agencies and need a programme of training, disclosure instructions, monitoring and follow-up [8].

*Named case: Warner Bros. (FTC, 2016).* Warner Bros. paid influencers from hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars to post gameplay videos of *Middle Earth: Shadow of Mordor*, told them to be positive and not mention bugs, and had disclosures placed below the "Show More" fold. The videos drew more than 5.5 million views [10]. The final order, approved 21 November 2016, bars presenting sponsored videos as independent opinion and requires clear disclosure, influencer education and monitoring [10][11]. The lesson for a podcast or channel selling sponsorship: the sponsor's script and the host's disclosure are both on the record.

**EU.** Sponsored editorial content must be made clear by images, words or sound, and paid or gifted promotions must be identified (Your Europe, checked 28 April 2026) [13]. The European Commission's Influencer Legal Hub sets out the laws that apply and cites a 2025 study finding only 5.4% of posts labelled as advertising [12].

**Platforms.** On YouTube, tick "my video includes branded content" under Paid promotion to add a disclosure label; creators and partners remain responsible for the law [14]. Platform labels help but do not replace the creator's own clear disclosure [2][8].

### 3.4 Claims about the partnership

- **Only claim a partnership that exists now.** In October 2024 the ASA upheld a complaint against The BLAC Awards, whose ads used the Royal Air Force logo and "in partnership with" wording from a 2020 sponsorship with no evidence it was still current [4]. Remove sponsor logos and "partner" wording when a deal ends, and get written permission for each logo use.
- **Sponsor activation ads are judged on what people take from them.** In July 2025 the ASA did not uphold complaints about Aramco's ads announcing its Aston Martin Formula One partnership, finding they would be read as performance claims, not environmental ones [6]. When a client helps a sponsor make content, check every claim it makes.
- **No promised results.** Do not promise the sponsor sales, leads or brand lift; promise deliverables and report what happened.

### 3.5 Charities and nonprofits: the tax lines

These are flags for the client's accountant, not rulings.

- **US.** A qualified sponsorship payment, where the sponsor gets only use or acknowledgment of its name, logo or product lines, is not unrelated business income (IRS Publication 598, Rev. March 2021). Advertising is different: qualitative or comparative language, price or savings information, endorsements and inducements to buy. Payments contingent on attendance or ratings, payments for trade show activity and acknowledgments in a regular periodical (such as a monthly journal) do not qualify. Benefits such as tickets are assessed separately [15]. So the wording of sponsor credits and a sponsored newsletter can change the tax treatment: ask a qualified accountant before selling.
- **UK.** HMRC says sponsorship where the sponsor gets significant benefits is a standard-rated business supply, and taxable sponsorship income could take a small charity over the VAT registration threshold; flags, stickers or a name in a list of supporters are insignificant; a donation kept separate from a sponsorship deal, with benefits not conditional on it, can be outside VAT; programme and brochure advertising is business income (VAT Notice 701/1, updated 19 October 2026) [16].
- **Commercial participators.** If a business tells customers that part of its sales goes to a charity, the charity needs a written agreement before it starts and the business must make a solicitation statement; trustees check the partner's fit and reputation (CC20, updated 3 February 2026) [17].
- **Gift Aid** is for individuals' gifts, not company payments; see the fundraising specialist kit.

### 3.6 Prospecting and outreach

- **Score, then call the best first.** Fit (does the sponsor want this audience?), budget signs (past sponsorships, ads, local presence, growth), and warmth (who already knows them). Start with existing contacts and past sponsors.
- **Email rules.** US: CAN-SPAM has no business-to-business exception; messages need accurate headers and subject lines, a postal address and an opt-out honoured within 10 business days, and the promoting company stays liable (FTC guide, edited January 2024) [26]. UK: marketing emails to individual customers need permission and an easy unsubscribe [27]; emails to companies and LLPs need no prior consent, but sole traders and some partnerships count as individuals [29]. EU: marketing emails need prior consent, with an existing-customer exception, and people can object at any time [28]. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)).
- **Short pitches win replies.** One reason this sponsor fits, one audience fact, one idea for how they could use the deal, one ask (a 15-minute call). A forwardable note helps the contact sell it inside their company.

### 3.7 Proposals, contracts and money

- **A proposal is about the sponsor:** their goals in their words, the audience match, the package, an activation idea, the price, dates, and next steps.
- **The contract** (drafted by the client or its lawyer) should carry a deliverables schedule: each benefit, quantity, date, owner, proof, approval of creative, category exclusivity, logo use and when it ends, disclosure wording, payment terms, and what happens if a deliverable is missed (a make-good). The agent drafts the schedule; sending a contract or quote as binding needs the client's fresh yes, and signing is the client's.
- **Invoices and refunds** are prepared at level 2 and sent or issued by the client. Bank or payout details are never added or changed by the agent.

### 3.8 Fulfilment, proof and renewal

The fulfilment tracker lists every promise to every sponsor: deliverable, due date, owner, status, proof link. Proof is collected as it happens (photos of signage, screenshots of posts with the label visible, episode timestamps of ad reads, scan counts, email reports). Do not "take the money and run": keeping obligations is what keeps the sponsor [18]. The season wrap-up report shows each promise, the proof, the audience numbers with sources, anything missed and the make-good, and one or two ideas for next year. Send it before the renewal conversation, not with the invoice.

### 3.9 Working in the client's tools

Agents work in the client's CRM, spreadsheets, deck tools, email, newsletter and podcast platforms, YouTube Studio and shared drives at the level the client grants: 1 (read), 2 (read and draft) or 3 (read, write and publish within limits). At level 3 they update the pipeline, send approved pitches to approved prospect lists, publish approved sponsor posts with the right labels, and keep the tracker. Contracts or quotes sent as binding, formal replies to a sponsor's complaint or claim, deleting data, sending to a new audience and anything that names a new recipient for money need a fresh yes every time. See access.md.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Use these for judgement, not as targets.

- **Disclosure gap (EU):** a 2025 study cited by the European Commission found only 5.4% of posts labelled as advertising [12]. *Caveat:* figure as quoted by the Commission's hub; method not checked here.
- **Warner Bros. (FTC, 2016):** influencer payments from hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars per creator; more than 5.5 million views [10]. *Caveat:* one 2016 campaign; useful as a warning, not a price guide.
- **Podcast counting rules (IAB Tech Lab v2.3, September 2026):** one minute of playable content per counted download; full ad bytes for a counted inserted ad [20]. *Caveat:* a standard, not an audience size; hosts that do not follow it may report higher numbers.
- **Newsletter ad networks (beehiiv, Nov 2026):** paid by CPM per 1,000 unique opens or CPC per verified click; 72-hour tracking; payouts on the 20th [21]. *Caveat:* one network's model; opens are an unreliable measure.
- **European market size:** ESA's Nielsen Sports overview is members-only [25], so this kit quotes no market figure.

There is no trustworthy public "price per attendee", "CPM for a podcast read" or sponsor renewal rate that fits a small club, event or show. Price from the client's own audience and past deals, and test.

## 5. Decision rules

- If an audience number has no source and date, leave it out of the deck, because sponsors and regulators treat invented reach as misleading.
- If a deliverable is a paid mention, read or post, label it "Ad" up front (UK) and disclose it in the same channel as the endorsement (US), because "sponsored by" or a platform tag alone may not be enough [2][8].
- If a sponsor wants a script with claims, check each claim and have the sponsor confirm it can back it up before it airs [8].
- If a partnership has ended, remove the logo and "partner" wording everywhere before the next campaign [4].
- If a sponsor is in gambling, alcohol or another restricted category and the audience includes under-18s, check the relevant code before offering the inventory [5].
- If the client is a US nonprofit, flag credits that use comparative language, prices, endorsements or calls to buy, and any fee tied to attendance, for the accountant [15].
- If the client is a UK charity selling benefits to a sponsor, flag VAT for the accountant [16].
- If a business will tell customers part of its sales goes to the charity, flag the need for a written agreement first [17].
- If the prospect list was bought or scraped, do not email it until the client confirms how it was collected and which rules apply [26][27][28].
- If a sponsor misses a payment or a deliverable is missed, prepare the reminder or make-good proposal for the client; do not change terms yourself.
- If a contract or quote will be sent as binding, get the client's fresh yes for that version.
- If anyone asks to change bank details or pay a new recipient, stop and tell the client; treat a surprise request as possible fraud.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Inflated reach.** Spot it: summed episode downloads called "listeners", or "impressions" with no source [20].
- **Tiers with no limits.** Spot it: unlimited "gold" packages, so no tier feels scarce.
- **Logo-only packages.** Spot it: a deck that lists placements but never mentions the sponsor's goals [22].
- **Weak labels.** Spot it: "#sp", "thanks to our friends at", or a label only in the description [2][9].
- **Stale partner claims.** Spot it: last year's sponsors still on the website or in ads [4].
- **Wrong sponsor for the audience.** Spot it: a betting brand on junior kit or youth content [5].
- **Promises without proof.** Spot it: a wrap-up report with no screenshots, timestamps or counts.
- **Tax wording by accident.** Spot it: a US nonprofit thanking a sponsor with "the best deals in town, 20% off" [15].
- **Unconsented outreach.** Spot it: a purchased list loaded straight into a sequence [26][27].
- **Renewal ask with no report.** Spot it: the renewal email arrives before any results.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Does every audience number have a source, period and date?
2. Does each tier have clear contents, a price, a limit and a reason to buy?
3. Does the proposal start from this sponsor's goals and offer one activation idea?
4. Are all paid mentions labelled the way the current UK, US or EU guidance expects [1][2][8][13]?
5. Are partnership claims and logos current and approved [4]?
6. Have restricted categories been checked against the audience [5]?
7. Are tax and VAT questions flagged for the accountant, with no ruling given [15][16]?
8. Does every prospect on the send list have a lawful basis for contact [26][27][28]?
9. Does the tracker list every promise with a date, owner and proof?
10. Is the access level for each tool written down, and is every binding contract, money step and new audience marked for a fresh yes?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.**

- *Inventory sheet:* asset, quantity, audience reached (source), value basis, tier.
- *Deck:* 8 to 12 slides: the property, the audience (sourced), why sponsors join, tiers, activation ideas, past sponsor proof (named only with permission), how to say yes.
- *Prospect sheet:* company, fit, budget signs, warmth, score, reason, next step.
- *Pitch:* under 150 words, one ask, plus two follow-ups and a forwardable note.
- *Proposal:* two pages for one sponsor.
- *Tracker and wrap-up report:* every promise with proof, then a one-page summary and next year's ideas.

**Tone.** Confident and specific, never pushy. Lead with the sponsor's benefit. Use the sponsor's name and goals. Numbers in figures with footnotes. No hype words and no promised results. Notes to the client are kept separate from anything sponsors see.

## 9. Glossary

- **Rightsholder (property):** the club, event, show or list that sells sponsorship.
- **Inventory:** every asset the property can offer a sponsor [23].
- **Tier:** a package of assets at a set price, with a limit on how many are sold.
- **Title or naming rights:** the sponsor's name in the property's name.
- **Category exclusivity:** a promise not to sell to the sponsor's competitors in the same category.
- **Activation:** what the sponsor does to use the deal, beyond the fee.
- **Value-in-kind (VIK):** goods or services given instead of cash [18].
- **Deliverable:** one promised benefit, with a date and proof.
- **Make-good:** a replacement benefit when a deliverable is missed.
- **Fulfilment report (wrap-up):** proof of every promise, sent before renewal.
- **Host-read ad:** a sponsor message read by the podcast host.
- **Dynamic ad insertion:** ads stitched into an episode at download time [20].
- **Podcast consumer:** IAB v2.3's term for a unique listener or viewer [20].
- **CPM:** price per 1,000 impressions, or per 1,000 unique opens on some newsletter networks [21].
- **CPC:** price per click [21].
- **Advertorial:** paid content in an editorial style that must be labelled [1][13].
- **Commercial participator:** a business that says part of its sales go to a charity [17].
- **Qualified sponsorship payment:** a US sponsor payment for acknowledgment only, not advertising [15].
- **Solicitation statement:** the UK statement a commercial participator makes about the charity's share [17].
