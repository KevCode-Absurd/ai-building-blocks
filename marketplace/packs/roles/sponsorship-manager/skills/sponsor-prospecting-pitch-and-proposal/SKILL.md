# Sponsor prospecting, pitch and proposal

Sponsorship is sold to people who already half-know you. This skill starts with the client's own contacts, picks the few most likely to say yes, writes to each as if the client had done their homework, and turns interest into a proposal built on what that sponsor wants.

## When to use

- Tiers and a deck exist, and the client has a list of businesses they know or have met.
- Past sponsors need a renewal pitch, or lapsed sponsors a win-back.
- A prospect has said "send me something" and needs a proposal.
- The client keeps sending the same generic email to everyone.

Not for: finding or buying contact details, emailing people without a lawful basis, signing or sending contracts as binding without the client's yes, or tax advice.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read the contact list, CRM, past deals and the deck. Level 2 to draft scores, emails and the proposal in the CRM, email tool or shared drive. Level 3 to send approved pitches and follow-ups to the approved prospect list and update deal stages, within the client's limits. Quotes or contracts sent as binding, and any new audience, need a fresh yes every time.

- The tiers, prices and deck.
- The contact list the client already has: company, contact, how they know them, past sponsorship or ads, notes.
- What the client knows of each company's goals (new store, hiring, launch, community profile).
- The client's email tool and how each contact's details were collected.
- Who signs the emails and who can approve a proposal.

## Steps

1. **Score each prospect** from the client's notes on three factors, each 1 to 3: fit (does this company want this audience, and does the audience suit the brand?), budget signs (past sponsorships, advertising, local growth, size), and warmth (who knows them, and how well). Write one line of reason per score. Mark unknowns as unknown, not low.
2. **Rank and cut.** Sort by total, then by warmth. Pick a first wave the client can follow up personally. Past sponsors and warm contacts come first.
3. **Check the basis for contact.** For each contact, note how the details were collected. US: CAN-SPAM covers business email too (accurate headers, honest subject, postal address, opt-out honoured within 10 business days). UK: marketing emails to individuals need permission; for company addresses, check the Information Commission's guidance. EU: marketing email needs prior consent with limited exceptions. Contacts without a clear basis go to the client to decide or to approach by phone or in person.
4. **Write the pitch** (under 150 words): a subject line naming their goal or something specific; one line on why this audience fits them; one audience fact with its source; one activation idea for them; one ask (a 15-minute call). Signed by a real person.
5. **Write two follow-ups,** each with a new reason (a new fact, a deadline the client really has, a relevant result), then stop. Add a forwardable note the contact can send to their boss.
6. **Build the proposal** for an interested sponsor, two pages: their goals in their words; the audience match with sources; the package (tier or custom); one or two activation ideas; what they get, item by item; price and payment terms; dates; next steps.
7. **Draft the deliverables schedule** for the contract: each benefit, quantity, date, owner, proof, creative approval, logo use and when it ends, disclosure wording for paid mentions, category exclusivity, payment dates, and a make-good for anything missed. The client's lawyer drafts or checks the contract itself.
8. **Prepare the send list.** At level 3, show the prospect list, messages and dates, get one yes, then send and log. Update CRM stages as replies arrive; hand hot replies to the client the same day.

## Checks

- Every score has a reason from the client's notes; nothing is guessed about a company's budget.
- No contact detail was found, scraped or bought by the agent.
- Every recipient has a basis for contact recorded, and every email has an opt-out where the rules need one.
- Pitches promise deliverables, not results; audience facts are sourced.
- The proposal starts with the sponsor's goals.
- The deliverables schedule names proof and a make-good for each item.
- Quotes and contracts are marked "draft until approved".

## Output format

1. **Prospect sheet:** company, contact, fit, budget signs, warmth, total, reason, basis for contact, next step.
2. **First-wave call list** with talking points.
3. **Pitch, two follow-ups and a forwardable note** per prospect.
4. **Proposal** for one sponsor.
5. **Deliverables schedule** for the contract.
6. **Send list and questions for the client.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a podcast for trades business owners. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

| Company | Fit | Budget signs | Warmth | Total | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Prospect A (van hire) | 3 | 2 | 3 | 8 | Rents vans to sole traders; host met the founder at a trade show |
| Prospect B (accounting) | 3 | 3 | 1 | 7 | Sells to small trades firms; runs podcast ads elsewhere; no contact yet |

**Pitch (to Prospect A):** "Subject: Your vans, in front of trades owners every week. Hi Sam, good to meet you at the trade show. [Podcast] gets about 6,000 downloads an episode (host stats to the IAB standard, September 2026), and 7 in 10 listeners who answered our survey run their own trades business. An idea: a monthly 'van of the month' segment where a listener tells us how they use theirs. Could we take 15 minutes next week to see if it fits? Priya, producer, [Podcast]."

**Deliverables schedule extract:** "Mid-roll host read, 60 seconds, episodes 41 to 52, labelled as an ad at the start of the read; proof: episode timestamp and host download report; make-good: one extra read per missed episode."
