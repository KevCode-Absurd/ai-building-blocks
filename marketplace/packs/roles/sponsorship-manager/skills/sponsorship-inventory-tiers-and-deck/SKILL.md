# Sponsorship inventory, tiers and deck

Most first-time sponsorship decks sell logos. Sponsors buy access to an audience that cares, and a way to use it. This skill starts from a full list of what the property owns, prices it from real numbers, packages it so the top tier feels scarce, and tells the story in a deck a busy marketing manager can read in five minutes.

## When to use

- A festival, club, conference, charity event, podcast, newsletter or channel wants sponsors and has no packages.
- Packages exist but every deal is different and nobody knows what is sold.
- The deck is a list of logo spots with no audience facts or sponsor benefits.
- A new season, venue or format changes what can be offered.

Not for: tax or VAT rulings on sponsorship income, contract drafting, or inventing audience figures or past sponsor names. For tax, ask a qualified accountant.

## What you need

**Access:** level 1 to read audience data, past deals and the brand guide. Level 2 to build the inventory sheet and the deck as drafts in the client's spreadsheet and deck tool (Google Slides, PowerPoint, Canva). Level 3 to publish an approved sponsorship page or media kit within the client's limits.

- The property: what it is, dates, places, format, and what is new this year.
- Audience numbers with sources and dates: attendance or ticket scans, members, downloads counted to the IAB Tech Lab standard where possible, subscribers and clicks, social followers and engagement.
- Who the audience is: location, age bands, interests, buying power, if the client knows.
- Every asset the client could offer, including ones never sold.
- Past sponsors and results, and permission to name each one.
- Any category no-go list, and whether the audience includes under-18s.
- Whether the client is a US nonprofit or a UK charity.

## Steps

1. **List the inventory.** Walk through every touchpoint: naming rights; stage, pitch-side and venue signage; kit, equipment and vehicles; tickets and hospitality; stands and sampling; the website, app, emails and email footers; social posts; podcast pre-, mid- and post-roll reads; newsletter top and mid slots and dedicated sends; event programmes; the sponsor's own event at the venue; data and content rights. Skildum-Reid's free generic inventory is a good checklist. Mark which assets the client is willing to sell.
2. **Attach a sourced audience number to each asset.** "Pitch-side banner: 2,400 average home attendance, club gate counts 2025/26 season". Where there is no number, say so; do not estimate silently.
3. **Value each asset.** Use the client's own past prices, what the sponsor would otherwise pay to reach the same people, and the client's costs to deliver. Write the basis next to each value. Never quote a "market rate" without a source.
4. **Check categories.** If the audience includes under-18s, flag gambling (CAP Code: no strong appeal to under-18s; the ASA ruled against a bet365-promoted post featuring a top footballer in 2023), alcohol and other restricted categories for the client's decision before they appear in any tier.
5. **Build two to four tiers.** Name them plainly. For each: what's included, price, how many are for sale, category exclusivity (if any), and term. Put the scarce assets (naming, exclusivity, the host's voice) in the top tier. Keep a small "supporter" tier for local businesses.
6. **Flag tax lines.** US nonprofit: credits that use comparative language, prices, endorsements or calls to buy, payments tied to attendance, and acknowledgments in a regular periodical may not count as qualified sponsorship payments (IRS Publication 598). UK charity: sponsorship with significant benefits is a VAT-able business supply (VAT Notice 701/1). Note these for the accountant; give no ruling.
7. **Write the deck story,** 8 to 12 slides: the property in one line; the audience with footnoted numbers; why sponsors join (what the audience cares about and how a sponsor can be part of it); the tiers; two or three activation ideas; proof from past sponsors (named only with written permission); how to say yes (contact, dates, next step).
8. **Write a one-page media kit** version for email.
9. **Prepare the go-live list.** At level 3, list the pages to publish and get one yes before anything goes live.

## Checks

- Every audience number has a source, period and date; estimates are labelled.
- Every value shows its basis; no invented market rates.
- Each tier has a limit, and the top tier holds the scarce assets.
- Past sponsors appear only with written permission; no lapsed partner is implied as current.
- Restricted categories are checked against the audience.
- Tax questions are flagged for a qualified accountant, with no ruling given.
- No promised results for the sponsor (sales, leads, brand lift).

## Output format

1. **Inventory sheet:** asset, quantity, audience (source and date), value basis, value, tier, notes.
2. **Tier table:** tier, contents, price, number for sale, exclusivity, term.
3. **Deck:** slide-by-slide headline, body text and footnotes.
4. **One-page media kit.**
5. **Flags:** categories, tax questions for the accountant, missing data.

## Example of good output (short)

*Harbourside Food Festival is a made-up event, used only to show the format. All numbers are invented.*

| Tier | Includes | Price | For sale |
|---|---|---|---|
| Presenting partner | Name in festival title, main stage naming, category exclusivity, 20 VIP passes, 3 newsletter features | £18,000 | 1 |
| Stage partner | Second stage naming, 8 VIP passes, 1 newsletter feature, banners | £6,000 | 2 |
| Local supporter | Logo on site map and website, 2 passes, social thank-you | £750 | 20 |

**Audience slide:** "11,800 visitors over two days.¹ 62% live within 15 miles.² 4,300 newsletter subscribers.³"
¹ Gate scans, July 2026. ² Ticket postcodes, July 2026 (paid tickets only). ³ Mailing list count, 1 October 2026.

**Note for the client:** the festival is run by a charity. Ask your accountant whether the presenting partner's benefits make the fee VAT-able, before quoting prices with or without VAT.
