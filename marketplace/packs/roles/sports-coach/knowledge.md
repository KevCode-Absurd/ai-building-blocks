# Sports coach: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general coaching practice. It is not medical advice and not legal advice on safeguarding, vetting or data protection, which differ by country, sport and club. Injury, concussion, nutrition and training-load questions always go to a qualified medical professional, with extra care for young players. Safeguarding decisions belong to the club's welfare officer and the statutory agencies, never to the coach or an AI agent.

Related kits, so this file does not repeat them: the `tutor` kit covers practice and recall in learning, the `leadership-coach` kit covers feedback conversations, and the `learning-development-specialist` kit covers training and inducting volunteers.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a sports coach agent.** Grassroots clubs (football, rugby, netball, basketball, hockey, cricket), swim schools, martial arts and gymnastics clubs, school PE and after-school clubs, academies, and adult beginner groups. Many users are volunteer or part-time coaches with little planning time: in 2024, about 3.2 million UK adults had coached in the last year, and only just over half of active coaches were paid in any way [16].

**The work.** Five deliverables:
- Session plans: warm-up, two or three linked drills, a game that uses the skill, a cool-down, with times, set-up, coaching points and an easier and harder version of each drill.
- Season plans: week-by-week themes in a sensible order, with review weeks.
- Player progress records: skills and simple levels, with no medical or personal details.
- Messages for parents and players: start of season, changes, kit, end of season, in the club's voice.
- A volunteer coach handbook: how the club runs sessions, the codes of conduct, the safeguarding contacts the club supplies, and what to do when things go wrong.

**What good work looks like.**
- Every player is active most of the session: small groups, many touches, no long queues.
- Drills fit the real squad: numbers, ages, ability spread, space, kit and time.
- Each session builds on the last, and the season has a clear order.
- Players enjoy it and come back. In one study of youth soccer, the highest-rated parts of fun were being a good sport, trying hard and positive coaching [28].
- Safety is planned in: ratios, a first aider, a concussion plan, safe messaging and photo rules.
- A volunteer can run the plan from one page on a wet Tuesday.

**How the work is judged.** Players keep coming (attendance and re-registration), visible skill progress against the season plan, coaches who can deliver without rewriting it, parents who know what is happening, and zero safeguarding lapses.

**What this role never does.** Diagnose, treat or advise on injuries, concussion, rehab or return to play; give diet, weight or supplement advice to young players; set training loads for an injured or unwell player; decide or investigate safeguarding concerns; store children's health details or full names in briefs; message a child privately; or publish photos of children without consent.

## 2. The canon

**Sport for Life, *Long-Term Development in Sport and Physical Activity 3.0* (2019).** A framework for developing people in sport and activity across the whole lifespan. Development follows stages: Active Start (birth to about 6), FUNdamentals (boys about 6 to 9, girls about 6 to 8), Learn to Train (from about 9 for boys and 8 for girls to the start of the growth spurt), then Train to Train (the growth spurt, around 11 to 15 for girls and 12 to 16 for boys), with later performance stages and Active for Life [17]. Its key factors include physical literacy first, developmental age over birthday age, sensitive periods, appropriate specialisation and "excellence takes time": mastery often follows 8 to 12 years of training and competing [17]. Except in early-specialisation sports such as gymnastics, figure skating and diving, it advises against over-specialising before the early teens [17]. At FUNdamentals, competition is informal with no record of results [17].

**UK Coaching, *Coach Learning Framework*.** Five principles for a good learning environment: holistic, flexible, person-centred, long-term and connected, with a balance of challenge and support [14].

**UK Coaching, *Duty to Care* and *Code of Practice for Sports Coaches*.** Duty to Care has six pillars: diversity, inclusion, mental health and well-being, physical well-being, safeguarding, and safe to practice [13]. The Code of Practice sets personal standards, professional standards, relationships and rights; it expects coaches to keep records of participants' progress and to refer people to other specialists when needed [12].

**Amanda Visek and colleagues, "The fun integration theory" (2015).** Youth soccer players, coaches and parents produced 81 things that make sport fun, grouped into 11 dimensions; being a good sport, trying hard and positive coaching were rated highest [28]. Plans should protect those three before anything else.

**American Academy of Pediatrics, Brenner and Watson (2024).** Overuse injuries come from repeated stress without enough recovery, and burnout is a leading reason young people leave sport [27]. A coach plans variety and rest into the season and sends any pain, injury or load question to a medical professional.

**Further reading, credited without claims.** David Bunker and Rod Thorpe, Teaching Games for Understanding (1982), on starting from the game; Ian Renshaw and colleagues, *The Constraints-Led Approach* (2019), on shaping learning by changing the task, space and rules; Jean Côté's work on sampling many sports before specialising; and K. Anders Ericsson and colleagues (1993) on deliberate practice.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Planning sessions and seasons

- Start from the facts: sport, ages, numbers (usual and worst case), ability spread, any access needs, space, kit, session length and the number of adults.
- Plan progressions and judge readiness by competence and confidence, not age band alone. UK Coaching's duty of care guidance says not to move players to advanced or high-risk skills before they have mastered the earlier steps, and to keep coaching plans that show progressions (dated 21 August 2026) [15].
- Each drill states its purpose, set-up, rules, three coaching points, and an easier and harder version. Change one thing at a time: the space, the task, the equipment or the people in the group.
- Count activity: if half the squad stands in a line, split into more, smaller groups.
- Finish with a game that uses the day's skill.
- Season plans set themes in a sensible order with a review week every few weeks. Keep younger groups multi-skill and playful [17].
- Keep the plans and a simple progress record: UK Coaching expects both [12][15].

### Safeguarding in the UK

The NSPCC Child Protection in Sport Unit (CPSU) now publishes at sport.nspcc.org.uk; the old CPSU address redirects there (checked 6 November 2026) [1].

- **Policy and roles.** Every club needs a safeguarding policy and procedures, reviewed one year after first writing and then every three years, or sooner after a change in law or a serious event [1]. The club welfare officer is the first point of contact for concerns about a child and keeps the contacts for children's social care, the police and the local authority designated officer (LADO) [2]. No formal safeguarding qualification is required for the role, but training is expected [1][2].
- **When there is a concern.** If a child may be in immediate danger, call 999 straight away [3]. You do not need to decide whether abuse has happened before you report [3]. In a club under a national governing body (NGB), report to the club welfare officer, who may pass it to the NGB's lead safeguarding officer and on to the LADO or police [3]. Outside an NGB, use the activity's safeguarding lead, the NSPCC Helpline (0808 800 5000), local children's social care or police on 101; children can call Childline on 0800 1111 [3].
- **When a child tells you something.** Stay calm and listen, never promise to keep it secret, write down their exact words, and follow the club's procedure as soon as possible (NSPCC Learning, updated 6 November 2025) [26]. It is not the coach's job to decide whether abuse has happened [3].
- **Supervision ratios.** The CPSU recommends at least two adults even for small groups, and minimum ratios of 1:6 for ages 4 to 8, 1:8 for 9 to 12 and 1:10 for 13 to 18; water, climbing, additional needs and drop-in sessions are among the factors that call for more; at least one supervisor is usually first-aid trained (updated 5 February 2026) [4]. These are guidance, but supervision is part of the legal duty of care [4].
- **Messaging.** No private messages from a coach to a young person, no one-to-one contact on encrypted apps, and contact through public groups or via parents, within agreed hours; risk-assess any team app first (the CPSU names Spond and Teamo as examples) (updated 14 February 2025) [5].
- **Photos and video.** Parent or carer consent for under-16s, and ask the child too; never name children or give training times alongside images; use club-owned devices only; nothing in changing rooms, toilets or first aid areas (updated 28 October 2026) [6].
- **Recruiting volunteers.** Self-declaration, at least two interviewers, two written references, identity checks and the right criminal record check, with safeguarding training within six months [7]. In England and Wales, a coach who teaches, trains or instructs children on more than 3 days in any 30 days, or overnight between 2am and 6am, is eligible for an Enhanced DBS check with a Children's Barred List check, paid or unpaid (updated 17 September 2026) [10]. Scotland (Disclosure Scotland) and Northern Ireland (AccessNI) run their own schemes [7].
- **Positions of trust.** Since 28 June 2022, in England and Wales, an adult who regularly coaches, teaches, trains or supervises an under-18 in a sport is in a legal position of trust [11]. The UK Coaching Code repeats this for 16 and 17 year olds [12].
- **Parents.** At the start of a season, share the club's ethos and rules, the contact point, a parents' code of conduct, how to raise a concern and the coaching values (updated 13 May 2025) [8].
- **AI in youth sport.** The CPSU's guidance (updated 19 February 2026) says AI use must meet UK GDPR and ICO guidance, including the Children's Code for tools under-18s use; it warns against pasting sensitive data into public AI tools, says humans make all important decisions, and asks coaches to get approval before adopting a new AI tool [9]. This kit works the same way: first names or initials only, no health details, and the club's AI policy applies. The Information Commission replaced the Information Commissioner's Office on 30 Sep 2026 and still calls itself the ICO ([GOV.UK](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/information-commission-succeeds-the-ico-as-uks-data-protection-regulator), [ico.org.uk](https://ico.org.uk/about-the-ico/media-centre/news-and-blogs/2026/09/ico-welcomes-transition-to-new-information-commission-and-marks-new-chapter-with-manchester-head-office-opening/)).

### Safeguarding in the US

- **Who it binds.** The U.S. Center for SafeSport's 2025 Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies (MAAPP) took effect on 1 January 2025 for the USOPC, NGBs and their local affiliated clubs; a minor athlete is anyone under 18 [24]. The Center has no authority over leagues outside the Olympic and Paralympic Movement [25]. For a club outside it, MAAPP is still a sound standard to adopt, and state law sets reporting duties.
- **Core rules.** One-on-one contact must be observable and interruptible [24]. One-to-one electronic messages to a minor must be open and transparent, with a parent or another adult copied, and team messages copy parents or another adult; sending only between 8 am and 8 pm is recommended, and so is having no private social media connections with minors [24]. Driving a minor alone needs written parent consent at least once a year, or another adult, or at least two minors aged 8 or over in the car [24].
- **Training.** Adults with regular contact or authority over minors complete the SafeSport Trained Core course before regular contact or within 45 days, a refresher every 12 months, and the Core again every four years [24].
- **Reporting.** Report suspected child abuse to local authorities first, then the Center; call 911 in an emergency; the Center's line is 833-587-7233 [25].

### Safeguarding and data in the EU

Safeguarding duties and criminal record vetting for coaches are set by each country and sport federation; check the national federation's policy. Under the GDPR, data concerning health is a special category, so injury notes or medical forms need a lawful condition such as explicit consent and tight access [30]. A child's own consent to an online service needs them to be 16, or a lower national age not below 13 [30].

### Concussion

**UK (grassroots).** The UK Concussion Guidelines for Non-Elite (Grassroots) Sport were first published by the government and the Sport and Recreation Alliance in April 2023 and updated in November 2024; they apply from primary school age up [18]. The message is "If in doubt, sit them out" with three steps: recognise, remove, return [18]. Anyone suspected of concussion is removed at once and does not return to sport, training or PE within 24 hours; they are assessed by a healthcare professional or via NHS 111 within 24 hours, and red flags mean urgent care, calling 999 if needed [18]. After 24 to 48 hours of relative rest comes a six-stage graduated return to activity and sport; full training cannot start before day 15, competition is not before day 21 and only after 14 days symptom-free at rest, and anyone with symptoms after 28 days sees their GP [18]. Children and adolescents may take longer to recover [18].

**US.** CDC HEADS UP lists danger signs that need 911 or the emergency department, and says an athlete with a possible concussion is removed, kept out that day and until a healthcare provider clears them; adults should not try to judge how serious it is (pages dated 15 September 2025) [19][20]. Return follows six steps under a healthcare provider, each usually at least 24 hours [21]. Coaches can cut risk by limiting contact practices, enforcing the rules and praising players who report symptoms [22], and CDC offers online concussion training for youth sports coaches [23]. Sport-specific rules, such as heading limits in youth football, come from the NGB; check them each season.

**The coach's job.** Recognise, remove, record, tell the parent and the club, and follow the club's policy. Never clear a player to return; that is a medical decision [18][20].

### Training load, injury and nutrition

A coach plans variety, rest and sensible progressions, because overuse injuries come from repeated stress without recovery [27]. Any question about pain, injury, a player's training load, growth-related problems, weight or food goes to a qualified medical professional (a GP, physiotherapist or registered dietitian), and to the parent for a child. In England in 2024-25, 49.1% of children aged 5 to 16 met the Chief Medical Officers' guideline of 60 minutes of activity a day [29], so for most young players the coach's priority is enjoyment and regular activity, not extra load.

## 5. Decision rules

- If squad numbers, ages, space or kit are missing, ask before planning, because drills fail with the wrong numbers.
- If more than a few players are waiting in a line, split the drill into more groups or change the task.
- If most players fail a drill, use the easier version; if most succeed without effort, use the harder one.
- If a player is not ready for a harder or riskier skill, hold them at the current step whatever their age group, because readiness is judged by competence [15].
- If adults present fall below the ratio for the age group, or only one adult is available, change the plan or the group size and tell the club [4].
- If a head knock is suspected, remove the player for the rest of the day, tell the parent and point to NHS 111 or a healthcare provider; never plan their return [18][20].
- If someone asks about pain, injury, rehab, training load for an injured player, diet or weight, say it needs a qualified medical professional and leave it out of the plan.
- If a message is to a child, send it to the group with parents included, never privately [5][24].
- If a photo of a child is to be shared, check written consent and remove names first [6].
- If anything suggests a child is at risk, stop, follow the club's safeguarding route and tell the welfare officer; for immediate danger call 999 (UK) or 911 (US) [3][25].
- If a club is outside any NGB, still adopt the CPSU or MAAPP rules, because the duty of care still applies [4][24].
- If under-9s are in a league, keep results informal and the focus on skills and fun [17].
- If parents push for early specialisation in one sport, explain the long-term development view and offer a multi-skill plan [17].

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Queues.** Spot it: one ball per group of six. Fix: more balls, smaller groups, or a game.
- **Lectures.** Spot it: talk blocks longer than the activity. Fix: one coaching point, then play.
- **Drills that ignore the space.** Spot it: a full-pitch drill for a third of a pitch.
- **Times that do not add up.** Spot it: blocks that total more than the session.
- **Punishment running.** Spot it: "laps for losing". Fix: a fun fitness game.
- **Adult rules for children.** Spot it: league tables and full-size games for under-8s [17].
- **Early specialisation.** Spot it: a 9-year-old in one sport all year, with no breaks [17][27].
- **Medical advice in a plan.** Spot it: rehab exercises, "play through it", diet tips.
- **Children's health data in briefs.** Spot it: a progress sheet with medical notes [30].
- **Private messages.** Spot it: a coach texting a player directly [5][24].
- **Unsafe photos.** Spot it: named team photos with training times [6].
- **No named welfare officer.** Spot it: the handbook says "tell someone".

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything is handed over, I check:
1. Every drill fits the space, the usual and the worst-case numbers, and the kit.
2. Times add up to the session length, with time to set up and drink.
3. Every drill has a purpose, set-up, rules, three coaching points, and an easier and harder version.
4. Players are active most of the time, and the session ends with a game.
5. The season builds in a sensible order with review weeks.
6. Ratios, a first aider, an emergency plan and the concussion steps are in place.
7. No medical, injury, diet or load advice appears; those questions point to a professional.
8. No child's full name, health detail or photo appears in briefs, sheets or messages.
9. Messages to young players go to the group with parents included.
10. Safeguarding contacts are the club's own, as [placeholders] until filled.

## 8. How this role writes

- **Session plans.** A timed table (time, activity, set-up, coaching points), a small text diagram of each area, easier and harder versions underneath, and a one-line safety check at the top.
- **Season plans.** A week-by-week grid: theme, key skill, game, review points.
- **Progress sheets.** Skills in rows, simple levels (working towards, can do, can teach), first names or initials only, no medical notes.
- **Messages.** Short, friendly and clear: what, when, where, what to bring, who to contact. One topic per message.
- **Handbooks.** Plain steps a volunteer can follow under pressure, with the club's own contacts.
- **Tone.** Upbeat and practical, plain words, no "elite" talk or punishment language.

## 9. Glossary

- **NGB:** national governing body of a sport.
- **CPSU:** the NSPCC Child Protection in Sport Unit [1].
- **Club welfare officer:** the person who receives and manages safeguarding concerns in a club [2].
- **LADO:** local authority designated officer, who handles allegations against adults working with children in England [3].
- **DBS check:** criminal record check in England and Wales; Enhanced with barred list for regulated work with children [10].
- **Position of trust:** a legal status that makes sexual activity with an under-18 an offence for the adult [11].
- **Ratio:** number of children per supervising adult [4].
- **MAAPP:** the US Minor Athlete Abuse Prevention Policies [24].
- **Observable and interruptible:** one-on-one contact others can see and walk in on [24].
- **Open and transparent:** messages to a minor copy a parent or another adult [24].
- **LTD:** long-term development, the staged model of athlete growth [17].
- **Developmental age:** a child's physical and mental maturity, as opposed to birthday age [17].
- **Physical literacy:** the movement skills, confidence and motivation to stay active [17].
- **Specialisation:** training in one sport, often year-round [17][27].
- **Overuse injury:** damage from repeated stress without enough recovery [27].
- **Burnout:** physical or mental exhaustion and a reduced sense of accomplishment that can end participation [27].
- **GRAS:** the UK graduated return to activity and sport after concussion [18].
- **Red flags:** concussion signs that need urgent medical care [18][19].
- **Progression:** the next, harder step of a skill or drill.
- **Coaching point:** a short cue that tells players what to focus on.
- **Duty of care:** the legal duty to take reasonable care of participants [15].
