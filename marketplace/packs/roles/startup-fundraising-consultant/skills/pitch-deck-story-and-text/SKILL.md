# Pitch deck story and slide text

## When to use

- A founder is preparing a pre-seed, seed or Series A raise and needs the deck written.
- An existing deck is long, unclear, or investors keep asking the same questions.
- A short teaser version is needed for first contact (see the teaser prompt).
- Before a round, to check every claim against the numbers.

The deck is a communication to possible investors, so the rules on offering securities can apply to it. Before it is sent or shown in public, the founder's lawyer confirms which rules apply (in the US the SEC's exemption pages, in the UK the FCA financial promotion rules, in the EU the prospectus and crowdfunding rules). Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.

## What you need

1. The round: amount, instrument (SAFE, note or priced round), and what the money gets the company to (milestones and months of runway).
2. Monthly metrics for at least 12 months: revenue, customers, growth, retention, burn, cash.
3. The financial model or forecast, with its assumptions.
4. Customer evidence: quotes with permission, case results, pipeline.
5. Market sources the founder trusts, with dates.
6. Team backgrounds relevant to the problem.
7. Competitors and the alternatives customers use today.
8. Any existing deck and investor feedback.

**Access:** level 1 to read the deck, model and metrics; level 2 to write in a copy of the deck; level 3 to update the master deck within the founder's limits. Sending the deck to anyone follows the outreach rules: only to the approved list, after the lawyer has confirmed the route.

## Steps

### 1. Write down the ask

One line: "Raising 1.5 million on a post-money SAFE to reach X by month 18." Everything in the deck serves this line.

### 2. Find the one-line story

Who has the problem, why now, why this team. Test it against the traction: if traction is thin, the story leans on the insight and the team, not on inflated early numbers.

### 3. Write ten headlines first

One per slide, each a full claim, not a label. A common order (Sequoia's outline is a good checklist): purpose, problem, solution, why now, traction, market, business model, competition, team, the ask and use of funds. Read the ten headlines alone: they should make the argument without the body text. Reorder until they do.

### 4. Add proof under each headline

Two or three lines with a number and its source. One idea per slide. A visual note: what the chart or image should show (for example "monthly revenue bar chart, 12 months, actuals only").

### 5. Check every number

For each figure: the source file, the date, and whether it is actual, estimate or projection. Mark anything without a source "source?" and keep it out until it has one. Label projections clearly and keep actuals next to them. Market size: show how it was built (customers you can reach times price), with sources, not just a top-down total.

### 6. Read it as an investor would

List the 20 to 25 questions an investor is likely to ask in a first meeting (churn, sales cycle, why now, competition, use of funds, what the round gets you to, team gaps). Mark which slide answers each. Fix the slides that leave important ones open, or add an appendix slide.

### 7. Write speaker notes and the facts list

Speaker notes in plain speech, one short paragraph per slide. Then a list of facts still needed from the founder.

### 8. Check language

No promise or hint of returns. No "no competition", "conservative projections" or "guaranteed". Estimates labelled. Customer names and quotes only with permission.

## Checks

- The ten headlines tell the story on their own.
- Every number has a source and a date, or is marked "source?".
- Projections are labelled and sit beside actuals.
- The ask states amount, instrument and what it gets to.
- The investor question list maps to slides.
- No returns promised; competition shown honestly.
- Founder reminded that the lawyer confirms the route before the deck is shared. *Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*

## Output format

1. The one-line ask and one-line story.
2. Ten headlines.
3. Slide-by-slide: headline, body (two or three lines), source per number, visual note.
4. Speaker notes.
5. Investor question map: question, slide, answered (yes, partly, no).
6. Facts still needed and numbers marked "source?".

## Example of good output (short)

Illustrative structure, not a real company: a startup making scheduling software for home care agencies. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.

> **Ask:** 1.2 million on a post-money SAFE to reach 1 million annual recurring revenue by month 20.
>
> | # | Headline | Proof (source) | Visual |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | 2 | Agencies lose a full day a week rebuilding rotas by hand | 31 of 40 customers said so in onboarding calls (call notes, Jan to Sep) | Quote from one coordinator, with permission |
> | 5 | 52 agencies pay us 34,000 a month, up from 9,000 a year ago | Billing export, Oct | Monthly revenue bars, actuals only |
> | 6 | 4,100 agencies in our first two regions fit our customer profile | Regulator register count, Sep; our price times count = estimate | Bottom-up build, labelled estimate |
>
> **Open investor questions:** churn after year one (no data yet: say so on slide 5); why agencies don't use their existing payroll tool's rota (add to slide 8).
>
> *Text and visual notes only. Your lawyer confirms the rules before the deck is shared. Check this with local rules and ask a qualified accountant or lawyer.*
