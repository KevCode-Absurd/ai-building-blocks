# Product manual and quick start

The manual in the box is often the only support a buyer gets before deciding the product is broken. This skill writes manuals and quick start cards that match what people really do, in the order they do it, with pictures and words that say the same thing.

## When to use

- A new product needs a manual, a quick start card or assembly steps.
- Returns or one-star reviews mention setup, assembly or confusing instructions.
- The product is going on sale in new countries and the manual must be ready for translation.
- A care and maintenance card or troubleshooting chart is needed for the box or a product page.

Not for: certifying safety or legal compliance, drawing the illustrations, or packaging marketing copy.

## What you need

- **Specs, parts and tools list,** engineer notes, and photos, a video or a prototype.
- **Safety information** from the engineer or compliance lead: hazards, warnings already agreed, required symbols.
- **Support questions, returns reasons and reviews** that mention setup or use.
- **Markets of sale** and their languages.
- **Format:** print size, page count, PDF, or a page on the product site.
- **Access level:** writing from shared files needs level 1. Placing text into a shared draft layout needs level 2. Publishing the PDF to the website or product page needs level 3 within the client's limits.

## Steps

1. **Walk through the product.** From the prototype, photos or video, list every action a buyer takes from opening the box to first use, then everyday use, cleaning and storage. Note where people hesitate or could go wrong.
2. **Mine the problems.** Group returns reasons, tickets and reviews by step. These become warnings, tips and troubleshooting rows.
3. **Plan the parts:** safety summary; what is in the box (with part letters matching drawings); tools needed; assembly or setup; first use; everyday use; care and maintenance with how often; troubleshooting; specifications; support and warranty contact. A quick start card covers only the steps to first use.
4. **Write the steps.** One action per step, starting with a verb, in the order people actually do it. Say where before what ("On the base, press the release button"). After key steps, say what the buyer should see, hear or feel ("You will hear a click").
5. **Write a picture brief** for each step that needs one: what the drawing must show, the viewing angle, the part letters, arrows and callouts. Words and drawings must say the same thing; nothing is shown only in the picture.
6. **Place warnings where they apply.** Put each warning at the step where the hazard arises, not only at the front. Use the signal word, hazard, consequence and avoidance structure the compliance lead specifies. Mark every warning "for compliance review"; do not invent warning wording for regulated hazards.
7. **Build the troubleshooting chart** from real questions and returns: problem, likely cause, fix, and when to stop and contact support.
8. **Write care notes** with how often to do each task and which parts to replace.
9. **Check language and market rules with the client.** In the EU, instructions and safety information must be in a language consumers in the country of sale easily understand, and importers and distributors check those language rules; the EU Machinery Regulation, which applies from 20 January 2027, sets out when instructions may be supplied digitally. In Great Britain, consumer products must be safe in normal or reasonably foreseeable use. In the US, rules depend on the product category. List what the compliance expert must confirm.
10. **Write for translation.** Short sentences, one term per part (always "base", never also "stand"), no idioms, numbers and units in a consistent format.
11. **Check consistency:** terms used the same way every time, part letters match drawings, steps match the product, reading level plain. List every place where notes and photos disagree.
12. **Deliver.** At level 2, place text in a draft layout for the designer. At level 3, publish the approved PDF to the named product pages, log it and report it.

## Checks

- Every step is one action, in real order, with a result where it matters.
- Every part named in the steps appears in the in-the-box list and the drawings.
- Each warning sits at its hazard and is marked for compliance review.
- Troubleshooting rows come from real problems, each with a cause and a fix.
- Terms are consistent; nothing appears only in a picture.
- The markets of sale and their language needs are listed for the client.
- Open questions where specs, photos and notes disagree are listed.

## Output format

1. **Manual text** in reading order, with step numbers and part letters.
2. **Picture briefs,** one per illustrated step.
3. **Warnings list** for compliance review: location, hazard, proposed wording, status.
4. **Troubleshooting chart.**
5. **Quick start card** (if asked).
6. **Open questions** and **market notes.**

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a brand selling a folding laundry rack in the UK and Ireland. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

**Unfold the rack**

1. Stand the rack upright with the feet (A) on the floor.
2. Pull the two side wings (B) outwards until they are level.
   You will hear a click when each wing locks.
3. Push down on the centre bar (C) to check it is locked. It should not move.

> **CAUTION** (for compliance review): Keep fingers clear of the hinges (D) while unfolding. Hinges can pinch.

**Picture brief, step 2:** front view, rack upright, both wings half open; arrows show the outward pull; label wings B and hinges D; inset close-up of the lock with a "click" symbol.

**Troubleshooting:**

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| A wing will not lock | Wing not fully level | Lift the wing slightly, then pull out until it clicks. |
| Rack wobbles | Centre bar not locked | Push down on the centre bar (C) until it stops moving. |

**Open question:** the spec says "max load 15 kg" but the box photo says "20 kg". Which is right?
