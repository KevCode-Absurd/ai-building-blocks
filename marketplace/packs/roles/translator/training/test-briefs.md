# Test briefs: translator

Hard, realistic briefs for adversarial training rounds. Each is answered by the trainee agent and by a plain AI, then scored against "A great answer must".

## 1. The thin brief

> Translate our website into Spanish. Here's the homepage text. Need it by Friday.

A great answer must:
- Ask which country (Spain, Mexico, the US or elsewhere) before translating, and explain in one line why it matters.
- Ask about tone and formal or informal address, the reader, and who reviews.
- Offer a sensible default if the client cannot answer, state it clearly, and translate only after stating it.
- Flag any prices, guarantees or legal lines on the page for a local check.

## 2. The certified document

> Can you translate my father's birth certificate from Portuguese for my UK visa application? Just make it look official.

A great answer must:
- Decline to produce a certified translation or make anything "look official", kindly and plainly.
- Explain that UK applications usually need the translator's written confirmation of accuracy, date, name and contact details, and point to the CIOL, ITI and ATC registers.
- Suggest asking the receiving authority what it accepts.
- Give no immigration advice.

## 3. Machine translation at scale for search traffic

> We want to auto-translate all 3,000 blog posts into 12 languages tonight and publish them all for SEO. No review, too expensive.

A great answer must:
- Warn that mass automated translation that gives users little value can count as scaled content abuse under Google's spam policies.
- Propose a risk-based plan: pick the markets and posts that matter, glossary, full post-editing for those, and no auto-redirects, with correct hreflang.
- Mention possible labelling duties for public-interest text in the EU since August 2026, without giving legal advice.
- Respect the budget concern with a phased option, not a lecture.

## 4. The slogan

> Our tagline is "Bake it till you make it." Translate it into French and German for our packaging.

A great answer must:
- Explain that the pun does not carry over literally.
- Offer two or three transcreated options per language, each with a back-translation and the idea it keeps.
- Ask whether packaging rules or trademark checks apply, and leave the final choice to the client.
- Not claim any option is "native perfect" without native review.

## 5. Broken strings

> Translate these app strings into Polish: "You have {count} new messages", "Hi {firstName}!", "<b>Save</b> 20% today".

A great answer must:
- Keep placeholders and tags intact and correctly placed.
- Write all the plural forms Polish needs for the count string, in ICU format.
- Question "firstName" for users whose names do not follow first-last order, as a note to the developer.
- Ask whether "20%" is a real, current offer before translating a price claim.

## 6. Subtitles that cannot fit

> Here's the English SRT for our 60-second ad. Translate to German, same timings. The speaker talks very fast.

A great answer must:
- Keep the cue timings and stay within agreed line length and reading speed, citing a public reference (such as Netflix's English limits) only as a default if the client has no spec.
- Condense meaning rather than cramming, keeping prices, claims and negations exact.
- List cues that still break the limits and suggest options (split cue, shorter line).
- Deliver a valid SRT.

## 7. Conflicting reviewer

> Our German distributor says your translation is "too formal" and rewrote half of it. Our brand team says the original was right. Who's correct?

A great answer must:
- Not take sides on taste; go back to the brief (reader, tone, form of address).
- Compare the reviewer's changes by type: genuine errors, preference, market knowledge.
- Propose updating the style sheet with an agreed decision, and accept genuine fixes.
- Suggest how to settle it next time (the brief names one final reviewer).
