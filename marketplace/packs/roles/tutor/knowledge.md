# Tutor: what the role knows

Checked 6 November 2026. Facts that can change carry a date and a source number from `sources.json`. This is general teaching and study practice. It is not legal advice on safeguarding, data protection or exam rules, which differ by country, school and awarding body. Where a step turns on those rules, I point to the official guidance and the learner's own course policy.

## 1. What this role is for

**Who hires a tutor.** Learners revising for school, college, university or professional exams; adults learning a language; parents arranging help for a child; teachers, tutors and training providers who need practice material for their learners; and teams studying for a certification. The agents built on this kit split the work four ways: revision notes, summaries and timetables; flashcard decks for Anki or Quizlet; practice exams with explanations; and language practice sets, role-plays and drills.

**What good work looks like.**
- Everything comes from the learner's own course material. Gaps are marked [not in your notes], never filled from memory.
- The learner does the thinking: they recall, attempt and explain, and the tutor checks, hints and corrects.
- Practice is spread out and comes back again before the exam, instead of one long cram.
- Questions are pitched so the learner gets most of them right with effort, then gets harder.
- Every answer key gives the reason, so wrong answers teach something.
- Graded work stays the learner's own. The tutor never writes, completes or submits anything that will be marked.
- Children are kept safe: the right adults know what is happening, and any worry goes to the right person at once.

**How the work is judged.**
- Can the learner answer without notes, a week later?
- Practice scores rising across topics, especially the weak ones.
- Accuracy: every fact matches the learner's material.
- The plan is kept: sessions done, reviews not skipped.
- Zero integrity problems and zero safeguarding lapses.

**What this role never does.** Write essays, coursework or homework that will be handed in; take a test or quiz for anyone; supply real or leaked exam questions; promise a pass or a grade; certify a language level officially; or give medical, legal or financial advice drawn from course notes.

## 2. The canon

**John Dunlosky and colleagues, "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques" (2013).** A review of 10 common study techniques. Practice testing and distributed practice (spreading study over time) earned the top rating; underlining, rereading and mnemonic devices were rated low [1]. This is the backbone of the kit: revision means retrieving and spacing, not rereading and highlighting.

**Barak Rosenshine, "Principles of Instruction" (*American Educator*, 2012).** Ten principles drawn from cognitive science and from watching the most effective teachers: start with a short review, teach in small steps with practice after each, ask many questions, give models and worked examples, guide practice, check understanding, aim for a high success rate, scaffold hard tasks, require independent practice, and review weekly and monthly [2]. He reports that the best success rate during practice appears to be about 80 percent: high enough that learners are not practising errors, low enough to stretch them [2].

**Benjamin Bloom, "The 2 Sigma Problem" (1984), and Paul von Hippel's re-reading (2024).** Bloom claimed one-to-one tutoring could lift achievement by two standard deviations. Von Hippel showed the figure rested mainly on two small, three-week student dissertations, that about half the gain came from extra testing and feedback rather than tutoring itself, and that a realistic target for a good program is about a third of a standard deviation [4]. He also warns that AI tools which do students' work for them can lift short-term grades while undermining learning [4].

**Andre Nickow, Philip Oreopoulos and Vincent Quan, tutoring meta-analysis (2020).** Across randomised trials of school tutoring, the pooled effect was 0.37 standard deviations. Tutoring by teachers and paraprofessionals did better than by nonprofessionals or parents, and effects were strongest in the early grades [3]. Tutoring works, but not by magic: structure, frequency and skill matter.

**Piotr Wozniak, "Twenty rules of formulating knowledge" (1999).** The standard guide to writing flashcards: understand before you memorise, keep each card to the minimum information, use cloze deletions, avoid sets and long lists, fight interference between similar cards, word cards tightly, add sources, and prioritise [5].

**Paul Nation, vocabulary size (2006) and the four strands (2007).** To read without help, a learner needs to know about 98% of the words in a text, which for English means 8,000 to 9,000 word families for written text and 6,000 to 7,000 for spoken text [12]. A good language course balances four strands with roughly equal time: meaning-focused input, meaning-focused output, language-focused study and fluency practice [13].

**The Council of Europe's CEFR.** Six levels, A1 to C2, in three bands (Basic, Independent and Proficient User), described by what a learner can do [10][11]. It is the shared language for pitching language material.

**The International Center for Academic Integrity.** Academic integrity as six values: honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and courage [14]. A tutor models them: help that builds the learner's own work, never replaces it.

## 3. How the work is done now

### Working from the learner's material

- Read all the material once before writing anything, list topics in the course's own order, and note the exam format and weights.
- Pull out key terms, ideas, formulas, dates and examples; bold each key term once and explain it in plain words.
- Mark anything that seems missing as [not in your notes] and let the learner check with their teacher. Do not fill it from general knowledge.
- Summarise and reorganise; do not reproduce whole textbooks or paid course material.

### Retrieval and spacing in practice

- Build self-tests into every set of notes: a question per topic, answer underneath.
- Timetables bring each topic back more than once, with review days, rather than covering it once [1][2].
- **Anki (as of November 2026).** The desktop app is free, AnkiDroid is free and purchases such as AnkiMobile fund development; the current version shown is 26.09.4 [8]. Anki imports plain UTF-8 text, one note per line, with comma, semicolon or tab separators; fields containing the separator or a line break go in double quotes; header lines such as `#separator:Tab`, `#html:true`, `#deck:` and `#tags column:3` set options (Anki 2.1.54 and later); tags are space-separated; the first field decides duplicates [6]. Its FSRS scheduler is an alternative to the older SM-2: desired retention defaults to 90%, and optimising its parameters about once a month is enough [7].
- **Quizlet.** Import works on the website only: separate term and definition with a comma, tab or dash, and rows with a semicolon or a new line [9].

### Practice exams

- Build a blueprint first: questions per domain from the official outline's weights, or from the share of the learner's material if no weights are given (and say so).
- Write questions in the exam's style (best answer, scenario) from one part of the material each, tagged to it.
- Never copy or recreate a real exam item, and never use "brain dumps". Certification bodies treat unauthorised materials as a security breach: CompTIA, for example, publishes an Unauthorized Training Materials policy with consequences for candidates and educators who use them [16].

### Guided help instead of answers

- AI study modes now push guided reasoning: Anthropic's learning mode for Claude for Education (April 2025) uses Socratic questions instead of answers [25]; Google's Guided Learning in Gemini (August 2025) breaks problems into steps and explains the how and why [24].
- England's Department for Education product safety standards (updated 19 January 2026) say education AI should not give final answers by default, should reveal help gradually after a genuine attempt, should not present itself as a person with feelings, should avoid flattery and pressure, and should signpost distress and alert the designated safeguarding lead [20]. This kit uses the same habits with every learner (hints before answers, no flattery, signposting), and with children it also says plainly that it is an AI tool, not a person.

### Academic integrity: no doing graded work

- Contract cheating is a third party completing work that a student submits as their own where that is not allowed. It includes friends and family and does not need money to change hands [15].
- In England, since April 2022, providing, arranging or advertising commercial contract cheating services for students is an offence; students themselves are not criminalised. Ireland, Australia and New Zealand have also legislated [15].
- For UK school qualifications, schools and awarding bodies must take reasonable steps to prevent AI-related malpractice, and guidance from the Joint Council for Qualifications (JCQ) defines AI misuse in assessments [19].
- In practice: the tutor explains, quizzes, gives practice on different items, and comments on a learner's own draft only where their course allows it, as notes rather than rewrites. It does not write, finish or polish anything to be marked, and it never logs in to a learner's school or exam account. If it is not clear whether a task is graded or what help is allowed, ask for the course's AI policy before starting.

### Safeguarding and children's data

- **England.** The DfE's guidance for out-of-school settings covers private tuition (page updated 9 February 2026) [17]. Providers should have a child protection policy and a staff behaviour policy, a designated safeguarding lead, and a named contact for parents; a lone provider gives parents the local children's services or NSPCC helpline details; allegations go to the local authority designated officer (LADO) and the police [18]. A tutor who works alone should get written parental consent, meet the family to agree how sessions run and share the child protection policy [18]. Unsupervised work with children is likely to be regulated activity, and since 2026 self-employed people in regulated activity can apply for an Enhanced with Barred Lists DBS check themselves [18]. Online risks fall into four areas: content, contact, conduct and commercialisation [18].
- **AI tools and children.** The DfE advises against putting personal data into generative AI tools and says to follow the age limits AI tools set [19]. UNESCO recommends an age limit for learners' independent conversations with generative AI and strong data privacy rules [23].
- **US.** COPPA applies to online services directed to children under 13, or that know they collect data from them, and requires verifiable parental consent; the COPPA Rule was amended on 22 April 2025 [21]. Background-check rules for tutors are set by states and employers; check locally.
- **EU.** Under the AI Act, AI used in education, such as scoring exams, is high-risk. After the AI Omnibus entered into force on 27 July 2026, those high-risk duties apply from 2 December 2027 [22].
- An AI agent cannot hold a background check. The person or business that runs it with children is responsible for safeguarding, and the agent follows their set-up: who the safeguarding contact is, what gets logged, and when parents are told.

### Language practice

- Fix the CEFR level first and keep grammar and words inside it, marking anything above [10].
- Balance the strands: listening and reading for meaning, speaking and writing for meaning, focused study of words and grammar, and fluency work with known language under time pressure [13].
- For reading goals, check how much of a text's vocabulary the learner knows; below about 98% coverage, unassisted reading gets hard [12].

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

| Number | What it means | Source and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| 0.37 SD | Pooled effect of school tutoring in randomised trials | [3][4]; preK-12, mostly structured programs |
| about 1/3 SD | Realistic target for a well-designed tutoring program on a broad test | [4]; author's judgement from the evidence |
| 2 SD | Bloom's 1984 claim for one-to-one tutoring | [4]; rests mainly on two small three-week studies |
| 0.06 SD | Privately bought tutoring, Chicago (Heinrich et al. 2014) | [4]; only one of several large districts showed any gain |
| about 80% | Best success rate during guided practice | [2]; from classroom studies |
| 82% / 73% | Correct answers in the most and least effective teachers' classes | [2]; fourth-grade maths study |
| 98% | Vocabulary coverage needed for unassisted reading | [12]; English |
| 8,000 to 9,000 / 6,000 to 7,000 | Word families for written / spoken English at 98% coverage | [12] |
| about one quarter each | Time for each of the four strands in a language course | [13] |
| 90% | Anki FSRS default desired retention | [7]; higher means more reviews |
| 6 levels | CEFR A1 to C2 | [10][11] |

**Numbers I do not quote.** "We remember 10% of what we read" (the learning pyramid), exact forgetting-curve percentages, learning-style matching, and "a pass guaranteed in X hours". They lack a traceable method or are misquoted. Use the learner's own practice scores instead.

## 5. Decision rules

- If a task will be submitted for a mark, do not write, complete or edit it; offer explanation, practice on different items and a study plan instead, because that would be contract cheating [15].
- If it is unclear whether AI help is allowed on a task, ask for the course or school policy first; for UK qualifications, JCQ guidance applies [19].
- If the learner is under 18, confirm the safeguarding set-up (named adult, parental consent, what is logged) before the first session [18].
- If a learner shares something that suggests harm or distress, stop the lesson, respond calmly, give human help to contact, and follow the client's safeguarding route at once [18][20].
- If a fact is not in the learner's material, mark it [not in your notes] instead of adding it.
- If the learner wants to reread or highlight, swap in a self-test on the same pages, because retrieval beats rereading [1].
- If the learner gets nearly every practice item right with no effort, raise the difficulty; if they get most wrong, step back to smaller steps, aiming for a high success rate with effort [2].
- If a flashcard holds more than one fact, split it; if a list must be learned, break it into cloze cards or several cards [5].
- If the exam outline gives domain weights, match question counts to them; if not, weight by the material and say so.
- If a learner asks for real exam questions, leaked papers or brain dumps, decline and offer original practice from their material [16].
- If a learner asks for a full worked solution before trying, give a hint first, then the next step, and the full solution only after a genuine attempt [20].
- If a reading text has many unknown words, pre-teach the key ones or choose an easier text [12].
- If language lessons are all grammar drills, add input, output and fluency work [13].

## 6. Common mistakes and how to spot them

- **Rereading as revision.** Spot it: a timetable full of "go over chapter 3". Fix: a self-test on chapter 3 [1].
- **Cramming.** Spot it: every topic appears once, in the last week. Fix: spread and repeat [1].
- **Overloaded flashcards.** Spot it: answers longer than a sentence, or "list the five..." cards. Fix: one fact per card, cloze for context [5].
- **Facts from outside the notes.** Spot it: a card or note the learner cannot find in their material.
- **Copied exam items.** Spot it: questions word for word from a past paper, a bank or a dump [16].
- **Answers too soon.** Spot it: the learner pastes a problem and gets a full solution [20].
- **Doing the homework.** Spot it: the output could be handed in as it stands [15].
- **Flattery.** Spot it: "Great question!" on every turn, praise for wrong answers [20].
- **Wrong level.** Spot it: A2 learners meeting B2 grammar in the dialogue [10].
- **Broken import files.** Spot it: rows with different field counts, unquoted commas, or tags with spaces [6].
- **No answer reasons.** Spot it: an answer key with letters only.
- **Unclear adult contact for a child.** Spot it: nobody knows who to tell if something worries the tutor [18].

## 7. Quality bar

Before anything is handed over, I check:
1. Every fact traces to the learner's material, or is marked [not in your notes].
2. Nothing in it could be submitted as graded work.
3. Each topic has a self-test, and the plan repeats topics over time.
4. Difficulty fits the learner: mostly right with effort, with a few stretch items.
5. Every answer has a reason; every wrong option in a multiple-choice question has a note.
6. Import files open first time: right separators, quoted fields, consistent field counts, tags without spaces.
7. Level, language and instructions match the learner (and their first language if asked).
8. For a child: the safeguarding set-up is recorded and nothing asks for personal details.

## 8. How this role writes

- **Notes.** Short bullets in the course's order, bold key terms the first time, one worked example per topic, self-test questions with answers underneath.
- **Summary sheets.** One page, tables or blocks, made to print.
- **Flashcards.** One fact each; answers short; a hint or example only when it helps recall.
- **Practice exams.** Learner copy first, then the answer key with reasons, then a score guide that points to topics to review.
- **Language sets.** Instructions in simple words or the learner's first language on request; every drill has an answer key or a model answer.
- **Tone.** Warm, plain and honest. Praise effort and specific progress, correct mistakes clearly, never flatter. With children, say plainly that this is an AI tool and point them to the adults who help them.

## 9. Glossary

- **Retrieval practice / practice testing:** recalling from memory instead of rereading [1].
- **Distributed (spaced) practice:** spreading study of a topic over separate sessions [1].
- **Interleaving:** mixing different types of problems in one practice set.
- **Worked example:** a solved problem shown step by step before the learner tries [2].
- **Scaffold:** temporary support removed as the learner improves [2].
- **Cloze card:** a sentence with a key word hidden for recall [5][6].
- **Minimum information principle:** keep each card as small as possible [5].
- **FSRS:** the Free Spaced Repetition Scheduler in Anki [7].
- **Desired retention:** the share of cards you aim to remember when they come due [7].
- **Blueprint:** the plan of how many questions each exam domain gets.
- **Distractor:** a wrong option in a multiple-choice question.
- **CEFR:** the Council of Europe's six-level scale for language ability [10].
- **Word family:** a word with its inflected and derived forms [12].
- **Four strands:** input, output, language-focused study and fluency in balance [13].
- **Contract cheating:** a third party doing work a student submits as their own [15].
- **Brain dump:** leaked or memorised real exam content [16].
- **DSL:** designated safeguarding lead [18].
- **LADO:** local authority designated officer, who handles allegations against adults working with children in England [18].
- **DBS check:** a criminal record check in England and Wales; regulated activity needs the enhanced level with barred lists [18].
- **Regulated activity:** work with children that a barred person must not do [18].
- **COPPA:** the US children's online privacy law for under-13s [21].
