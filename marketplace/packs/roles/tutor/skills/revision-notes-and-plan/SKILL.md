# Revision notes and plan

## When to use

- A learner has pages of notes, slides or a chapter and an exam coming.
- The notes are too long to reread, or the learner keeps mixing up similar topics.
- A one-page summary or formula sheet is needed to print.
- The learner needs a realistic plan from today to the exam.

Do not use it to write an essay, coursework or any answer that will be handed in. Offer the integrity check prompt if that is what is being asked.

## Access needed

- **Level 1 (read):** the course folder, notes and syllabus; past practice scores.
- **Level 2 (read and draft):** save notes, summary and glossary as new documents; draft calendar sessions.
- **Level 3 (read, write and publish):** share the finished notes to the named folder or people, and add the timetable sessions to the learner's calendar, within the confirmed limits. Log every action.

## What you need

1. The material: notes, slides, chapters or reading notes, pasted or shared.
2. The exam: date, format (essay, short answer, multiple choice), length, and the syllabus or topic weights if the course gives them.
3. What feels hard, and any past practice scores.
4. For a timetable: start date, hours available each day of the week, days off.
5. Age and level, so the language fits.

## Steps

### 1. Read everything once

Before writing, read all the material and list the topics in the order the course uses. Note where the material spends most space; if the learner gives no weights, that is your best guide, and you say so.

### 2. Check the task

Confirm the notes are for the learner's own revision. If any part is coursework or an assignment to submit, leave it out and explain why.

### 3. Pull out what matters per topic

Key ideas, terms, dates, formulas, steps and examples, all from the material. Nothing from outside. If something seems missing (a term used but never defined, a formula with no units), mark it [not in your notes] so the learner can check with their teacher.

### 4. Write the notes

- Short bullets under topic headings in course order.
- Bold each key term the first time and explain it in plain words.
- One worked example per topic, taken or built from the material, step by step.
- Two or three self-test questions per topic with the answer underneath, so the learner can cover and recall.
- A "watch out" line for the points the learner says they mix up, with the difference spelled out.

### 5. Make the summary sheet (if asked)

Cut to what fits on one printed page: the key ideas, terms, formulas or dates, in a table or blocks. If it does not fit, say what was cut.

### 6. Build the glossary (if asked)

Every key term with a plain definition and an example, A to Z or by topic.

### 7. Build the timetable

- Count the study days and hours to the exam.
- Share hours by topic weight, then add extra time for weak topics.
- Each topic comes back at least twice after first study, with gaps that widen, because spaced practice beats one long session.
- Every session starts with a short self-test on earlier topics and ends with a few questions on today's.
- Leave review days near the end and a lighter day before the exam.
- Put practice papers or past questions (from the learner's own course) in the last weeks.
- Keep it realistic: if the hours do not fit the material, say what to cut or shorten.

### 8. Check against the material

Every fact matches the notes. Headings follow the course order. Nothing is longer than it needs to be.

## Checks

- No fact without a place in the learner's material; gaps marked [not in your notes].
- Every topic has self-test questions with answers.
- The timetable repeats each topic and fits the hours given.
- Nothing could be handed in as graded work.
- Plain words that fit the learner's age and level.
- At level 3, only the agreed folder and calendar were changed, and the log is updated.

## Output format

1. Topic list in course order, with weights and where they came from
2. Revision notes per topic: bullets, bold terms, worked example, self-test questions, watch-out line
3. Summary sheet (one page)
4. Glossary
5. Timetable table: date, time, topic, task (learn, self-test, review, practice paper)
6. Gaps marked [not in your notes] and questions for the teacher

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up learner for illustration: Sam, a GCSE History student revising the Cold War, who mixes up the two Berlin crises.*

> **Topic 4: Berlin, 1948 to 1949 and 1958 to 1961**
> - **Berlin Blockade** (June 1948 to May 1949): the Soviet Union cut road, rail and canal links to West Berlin.
> - **Berlin Airlift:** the Western allies flew supplies in until the blockade was lifted.
> - **Berlin Wall** (August 1961): built to stop East Germans leaving through West Berlin.
>
> **Watch out:** the Blockade was about *getting supplies in* (1948). The Wall was about *stopping people getting out* (1961).
>
> **Self-test**
> 1. Why did the Soviet Union lift the blockade? *Answer: see your notes p.12, the airlift kept the city supplied.*
> 2. Which crisis ended with a physical barrier? *Answer: the second, in 1961.*
>
> [not in your notes]: the exact number of flights in the airlift. Check with your teacher if you need it.
>
> | Date | 17:00 to 17:30 | 17:30 to 18:00 |
> |---|---|---|
> | Mon 10 Nov | Self-test: Topics 1 to 3 | Learn: Topic 4 |
> | Thu 13 Nov | Self-test: Topic 4 | Learn: Topic 5 |
> | Mon 17 Nov | Review: Topics 2 and 4 | Practice question from your course pack |
