# Video producer: what the role knows

Checked 2026-11-06. Sources are numbered [n] and listed in sources.md with the date each was checked. Platform specs, music rules and AI rules change often: each one carries its source and the date it was true. This is general production practice, not legal advice. Licences, contracts and rights questions go to the client's own lawyer or the rights owner.

## 1. What this role is for

A business hires a video producer when it has something to show and no clear way to get from an idea to a finished file. A software company needs a 90-second explainer for its home page. A cafe wants a short vertical video of a new menu. A consultant wants a logo sting and title cards for a YouTube series. A founder has a phone, a friend and a Saturday, and needs to know what to film.

This role sits before and around the build. It turns a message into a script, the script into shots, the shots into a plan someone can film or animate, and the finished cut into files that each platform accepts. It does not have to hold the camera. Its job is that nobody on the shoot or in the edit has to guess.

**Who hires it.** Software and service businesses with products that are hard to picture; shops and restaurants making short social videos; creators and coaches running a channel; agencies that need a storyboard before an animator starts; teams briefing a freelance editor or an AI video tool.

**What good work looks like.**

- One idea per video, stated before any shot is planned.
- A script whose words fit the time when read aloud at the real speaker's pace.
- A shot table in which every line of voiceover has a picture, and every picture has a reason.
- On-screen text short enough to read in the time it is up, placed where the app's buttons do not cover it.
- Files that match each platform's current spec, with captions, at the right loudness.
- A rights log: every song, clip, font, logo and face accounted for, with its licence.
- Nothing invented: no features the product lacks, no numbers or reviews the client cannot prove.

**How it is judged.** By whether the viewer gets the one idea and acts. By whether the shoot or animation runs without a call back to ask "what did you mean here?". By whether the video goes live without a copyright claim, a rejected upload or a caption complaint. And by whether the finished cut matches the agreed plan in length and content.

**What an AI producer delivers.** Scripts, storyboards, shot tables, shoot plans, motion specs and delivery specs anyone can build from. With level 2 or 3 access to a connected tool it can also build drafts, cut captions, export files and, within the client's limits, upload and schedule.

## 2. The canon

**Books practitioners return to.** Credited here as a reading list; this kit makes no specific claim from them.

- Steven D. Katz, *Film Directing: Shot by Shot* (1991). Read it for how scenes become shots and storyboards.
- Walter Murch, *In the Blink of an Eye* (2nd edition, 2001). Read it for how an editor decides where to cut.
- Blain Brown, *Cinematography: Theory and Practice*. Read it for shot sizes, lenses, camera moves and light.
- Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston, *The Illusion of Life: Disney Animation* (1981). Read it for the classic principles of animated movement.
- Richard Williams, *The Animator's Survival Kit*. Read it for timing and spacing in motion work.
- Lee LeFever, *The Art of Explanation* (2012). Read it for how short explainer videos build context before detail.

**Published standards that act as the craft's rulebook.** These are the documents I check against, because they carry tested numbers.

- **Netflix's Timed Text Style Guide** [7][8]. Netflix publishes the rules its subtitle vendors must meet: line length, reading speed, minimum and maximum time on screen, line breaks. I use them as the floor for any on-screen words, not only subtitles, because they are the most detailed public reading-speed rules I could check.
- **EBU R 95, safe areas for 16:9 television** [12]. Where to keep action and graphics so no screen crops them.
- **EBU R 128, loudness** [13]. The European broadcast loudness standard.
- **W3C WCAG 2.2** [9][10][11]. Captions, flashing limits and moving content on the web.
- **Each platform's own help pages** [1]-[6]. The final word on sizes, lengths and file settings.

## 3. How the work is done now

### From brief to one idea

Before writing, I pin down: who watches, where (home page, YouTube, a Reel, a sales call, a trade-show loop), with or without sound, how long, the one idea the viewer must leave with, the one action after, what the product really does, what footage, brand assets and people exist, the budget, and who builds the video (a filmer, an editor, an animator, an AI tool or the client). If the client wants five ideas in 60 seconds, I offer a series instead.

### The script, in two columns

Explainers and most business videos are written as a two-column script: what we **hear** (voiceover, dialogue, music and sound) on the left, what we **see** (picture, on-screen text, motion) on the right, in timed rows. The picture column shows what the words do not say. A typical explainer arc runs: the problem, the moment it changes, how it works in a few steps, proof the client can back up, and the next step.

**Timing.** Speaking pace differs by voice and language, so I do not trust a words-per-minute rule. I read the script aloud at the speaker's pace with a stopwatch, or ask the client to, and cut until it fits with room to breathe. Every claim in the script must trace to the client's notes, site or documentation.

### Storyboards and shot tables

A storyboard does not need drawings. A shot table is often more useful to a filmer or animator: one row per shot with shot number, timecode in and out, length, what we see, shot size and angle, camera or motion, on-screen text, audio, and the transition. Shots use only people, places, products and footage the brief has; anything missing is marked "[needs: ...]". The lengths must add up to the brief's total. A style frame note sets colour, light, type and pace. An asset list closes it: shots to film, graphics to make, music and sound effects to license.

For a shoot, the storyboard becomes a **shoot plan**: shots grouped by location and set-up rather than story order, a day schedule, a kit list, who appears, and the consent and location permissions needed. Anyone who can be recognised on camera agrees in writing before filming; I write the consent list, and the client or their lawyer supplies the form.

### On-screen text

On-screen words must stay up long enough to read. The best public numbers I found are subtitle rules. Netflix's English (USA) guide caps reading speed at 20 characters per second for adult programmes and 17 for children's, allows 42 characters per line and two lines at most [7], and keeps each subtitle on screen for at least five-sixths of a second and at most 7 seconds [8]. So a 30-character line needs at least 1.5 seconds for adults (30 ÷ 20). Kinetic text that moves while it is read needs longer, so I test it at full speed on the target screen.

### Safe areas and framing

- **16:9 for TV or large screens:** EBU R 95 keeps action within a 3.5% margin and graphics within a 5% margin on every edge [12]. I use the graphics-safe margin for lower thirds and titles in any landscape video.
- **9:16 vertical:** Meta's Reels ad spec asks for at least 14% at the top, 35% at the bottom and 6% at each side to be kept free of text, logos and key content, so the app's labels and buttons do not cover them (Meta Ads Guide, checked 6 Nov 2026) [4]. I use the same zones for organic Reels as a cautious default.
- **Reframing:** when one shoot feeds several shapes, I frame the subject so a 9:16 crop and a 16:9 crop both work, and mark the crop for each shot. YouTube advises against adding black bars or padding to the file itself; the player adapts to the video's shape [2].

### Motion design

A motion spec is a keyframe table: time (seconds and frames), layer, the property that changes (position, scale, opacity, rotation), start and end values, and the easing. Titles and lower thirds get pixel sizes, safe margins and time on screen, and a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text or 3:1 for large text (WCAG 2.2 AA) [30]; over moving footage, a solid panel keeps that true in every frame. The frame rate matches the source footage: YouTube asks for video encoded at the frame rate it was recorded, and lists 24, 25, 30, 48, 50 and 60 fps as common [1]. Two limits apply to every motion piece:

- **Flashing.** WCAG 2.3.1 says nothing should flash more than three times in any one second unless it stays below the general and red flash thresholds [10]. Strobing logo reveals and rapid colour cuts fail this; they can trigger seizures.
- **Autoplay on the web.** WCAG 2.2.2 says moving content that starts by itself, lasts more than five seconds and plays alongside other content needs a way to pause, stop or hide it [11]. A looping header video on a site needs a pause button.

### Sound and loudness

Broadcast in Europe targets an average programme loudness of -23 LUFS under EBU R 128 (version 5.0, 21 Nov 2023) [13]. Online platforms set their own playback levels; I found no official social-platform loudness figure I could check, so I mix to the client's delivery spec if they have one and otherwise keep dialogue clear and consistent across the video. A video that must work with the sound off carries its message in pictures and on-screen text.

### Captions

WCAG 2.2 level A asks for captions on all prerecorded audio in synchronised media; captions name speakers when it is unclear and include sounds that matter to understanding [9]. Open captions are burned into the picture; closed captions can be turned on and off [9]. YouTube accepts SubRip (.srt) and SubViewer (.sbv) as plain UTF-8 with no styling, and WebVTT when position matters [6]. LinkedIn video ads accept SRT files with plain text only [5]. I write captions from the final script, then correct them against the final audio.

### Music and footage licensing basics

These are the basics a producer must know to ask the right questions. They are not rulings; the client confirms with the rights owner or a lawyer.

- **A song on a recording is usually two works.** The U.S. Copyright Office explains that a recording of a song can contain the musical work (the composition and lyrics) and the sound recording, and that the two are commonly owned and licensed separately [14]. Putting a commercial track under a video usually means permission for both: the composition from its publisher, and the recording from its owner, often a label.
- **UK and EU.** UK copyright is automatic, has no register, and covers musical works, sound recordings and film [20]. A transfer of UK copyright needs a written, signed assignment, and owners can license through a collecting society [21]. In the EU, copyright is also automatic, lasts 70 years after the author's death, and licences can be exclusive or not, and limited by time, territory and use [22].
- **Platform music libraries have limits.** YouTube's Audio Library tracks are described as copyright-safe on YouTube and are not claimed through Content ID; Creative Commons tracks there need credit in the description; YouTube takes no responsibility for "royalty-free" music from elsewhere [16]. Meta's Music Guidelines (effective 26 Mar 2024) say commercial or non-personal use of music needs the right licences, and that the more music a video holds, the more likely it is to be limited [17]. A track available inside an app is not automatically cleared for a business's ad.
- **Claims and strikes differ.** On YouTube, a Content ID claim is an automatic match that lets the owner block, monetise or track the video; it usually affects that video only. A valid takedown request can bring a copyright strike against the channel [15]. Removing or replacing the claimed audio clears a claim [15].
- **"Royalty-free" is a licence, not a gift.** It usually means no per-use royalty, not no rules. Read who may use it, on which platforms, in ads or not, for how long, where, in how many projects, and whether credit is required.
- **Creative Commons.** All six CC licences require credit; NC licences allow non-commercial use only, and ND licences allow no adaptations [18]. For a business video, NC material is out and ND material cannot be cut or edited.
- **Stock footage.** Free libraries still set terms. Pexels, for example, allows commercial use and editing with no credit needed, but bars showing identifiable people in a bad light, implying that people or brands in a clip endorse a product, and using its media as a trademark [19].
- **Who owns the finished video.** Under US law, a commissioned work counts as a work made for hire only in listed categories, which include part of an audiovisual work, and only when both sides sign a written agreement [23]; otherwise ownership moves only by a signed writing [24]. In the UK a transfer also needs a signed, written assignment [21]. The producer flags this at the start; the client's lawyer writes the clause.

### AI-made pictures, voices and music

- **YouTube** requires creators to disclose realistic content made or altered with AI: a real person seeming to say or do something they did not, altered footage of real events or places, realistic scenes that never happened, and AI music that is the main focus. Disclosure is not needed for clearly unrealistic content, minor fixes, or AI help with scripts and ideas. It is set under "AI use" in YouTube Studio [25].
- **EU.** Article 50 of the AI Act requires deployers to disclose deepfakes (AI image, audio or video that resembles real people, places or events), with a lighter duty for evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work; it applies from 2 August 2026 [26].
- **US copyright.** The Copyright Office's January 2025 report says prompts alone do not make an AI output protectable; human expressive input or real modification can [27]. An AI-generated mascot or sequence may not be something the client can own; I say so before they build on it.

### Connected tools (as of 6 Nov 2026)

Canva's MCP server lets an AI assistant create and edit designs, use brand kits and templates, resize designs and export MP4 among other formats [28]. Descript's MCP connector, signed in with the client's Descript account, can import media, run edits such as captions and filler-word removal, publish to a share link, and export transcripts (including SRT) and timelines for other editors; its page says it cannot yet export an MP4 on request [29]. Access is granted per tool, in three levels: read (level 1), read and draft (level 2), and read, write and publish within the client's limits (level 3). Until the client says otherwise, I work at level 1.

## 4. Benchmarks and numbers

Each figure has a source, a date and a caveat. Platform figures were checked on 6 Nov 2026.

- **Reading speed for on-screen words:** up to 20 characters per second for adults and 17 for children; 42 characters per line; two lines (Netflix, change log to 19 Dec 2025) [7]. *Caveat:* written for subtitles; moving or decorative text needs more time.
- **Time on screen per caption or text card:** at least five-sixths of a second, at most 7 seconds (Netflix) [8].
- **Safe areas:** 3.5% action-safe and 5% graphics-safe on each edge of 16:9 (EBU R 95 v1.1, 2017) [12]; vertical ad safe zones of 14% top, 35% bottom, 6% sides (Meta, Reels ads) [4]. *Caveat:* apps change their overlays without notice.
- **Loudness:** -23 LUFS programme loudness for European broadcast (EBU R 128 v5.0, Nov 2023) [13]. *Caveat:* not an online-platform figure.
- **Flashing:** no more than three flashes in any one second unless below the thresholds (WCAG 2.2, page updated 17 Sep 2025) [10].
- **Autoplay:** moving content over five seconds that starts by itself needs pause, stop or hide on the web (WCAG 2.2) [11].
- **Lengths:** Shorts up to 3 minutes [3]; Reels ads up to 15 minutes [4]; LinkedIn video ads 3 seconds to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 seconds recommended by LinkedIn [5].
- **YouTube bitrates:** 8 Mbps for 1080p at 24 to 30 fps; 35 to 45 Mbps for 4K at the same rates [1]. *Caveat:* YouTube says a bitrate limit is not required.

No source I checked gives a trustworthy ideal length or completion rate for explainers or social videos. Use the client's own analytics (audience retention, average view duration) and test.

## 5. Decision rules

- If the brief has no single idea, ask for it before scripting, because a video that says five things lands none.
- If the script does not fit the time when read aloud, cut words rather than speed up the voice, because rushed voiceover loses viewers and captions overflow.
- If a line of on-screen text cannot be read at the subtitle reading speed in the time it is up, shorten it or hold it longer [7][8].
- If the video may be watched without sound, carry the message in pictures and text, and burn in captions or supply a caption file [9].
- If a frame will be cropped to another shape, plan the crop for every shot before filming, because reframing in the edit cannot recover what was not shot.
- If a platform publishes a spec, follow it over any general rule here, because its upload system decides what is accepted.
- If anything flashes or strobes, check it against the three-flashes rule before delivery [10].
- If a song, clip, font or logo has no written licence that covers this use, platform and term, replace it or get the licence in writing first [14][17][18].
- If a person can be recognised, get written consent before filming, because a reshoot costs more than a form.
- If the client wants a commercial song "because everyone uses it", explain the two permissions it needs and offer cleared alternatives [14][16].
- If realistic AI-made people, places or events appear, plan the disclosure in the edit and the upload [25][26].
- If a step would publish, spend or license something, check the access level first: draft at level 2, act live only with level 3 and within the client's limits, and get a fresh yes for anything that commits the client to terms.

## 6. Common mistakes, and how to spot them

- **Too many ideas.** Spot it: the script needs a list to summarise it; the call to action changes halfway.
- **Words and pictures saying the same thing.** Spot it: the picture column reads "show the product" next to a line about the product. Each picture should add what the words leave out.
- **Scripts written to be read, not heard.** Spot it: long sentences, brackets, numbers with decimals; the read-through runs long.
- **Text that cannot be read in time.** Spot it: a 60-character line up for one second; key words in the bottom third of a vertical frame where the app's buttons sit [4][7].
- **Shot lengths that do not add up.** Spot it: the table totals 74 seconds for a 60-second brief.
- **Storyboards that invent.** Spot it: a happy customer, an office or a feature the client never mentioned.
- **Music used because the editor liked it.** Spot it: no licence on file; a claim arrives after upload [15].
- **Burned-in black bars.** Spot it: a vertical video inside a 16:9 frame uploaded to YouTube [2].
- **One master for every platform.** Spot it: the same 16:9 file posted as a Reel, with titles in the crop zone.
- **Flashing logo stings.** Spot it: a reveal that strobes white several times in a second [10].
- **No caption file, or captions from the draft script.** Spot it: captions that do not match what is said.

## 7. Quality bar: what an expert checks before shipping

1. Can a stranger say the one idea and the next step after one viewing?
2. Does every voiceover line have a picture, and does every claim trace to the client's facts?
3. Do shot lengths add up to the agreed time?
4. Is every on-screen line readable at the subtitle reading speed, and inside the safe area for each shape [4][7][12]?
5. Do the file settings match each platform's current spec, with the date checked [1]-[5]?
6. Are captions accurate to the final audio, with speakers and important sounds [9]?
7. Is there no flashing above the threshold, and a pause control for autoplaying web loops [10][11]?
8. Is every song, clip, font, logo and recognisable face in the rights log with its licence or consent?
9. Is any realistic AI content disclosed where the platform or law requires [25][26]?
10. Could the filmer, editor or animator build it from the documents alone?

## 8. How this role writes

**Deliverables.** A one-page brief; a two-column script with timings; a shot table; a shoot plan (locations, schedule, kit, consent list); a motion spec (keyframe table, easing, sizes); a delivery spec per platform; a caption file; a rights log; and a change log between rounds.

**Conventions.** Timecode as HH:MM:SS:FF, with the frame rate stated at the top. Shots numbered in order, with letters for inserts (12, 12A). Shot sizes abbreviated and defined once (WS, MS, CU). Exact on-screen text in quotes with its character count. Missing items marked "[needs: ...]". File names that carry the project, shape, length and version (for example, "ProjectName_9x16_30s_v03"). Plain words, short sentences, numbers as numerals with units (s, fps, px, Mbps, LUFS). Every spec says where its rule came from ("Meta Reels ad spec, checked 6 Nov 2026").

## 9. Glossary

- **A-roll and B-roll:** the main footage (usually someone speaking) and the supporting shots cut over it.
- **Aspect ratio:** the shape of the frame, width to height (16:9, 9:16, 1:1, 4:5) [2][4].
- **Action-safe and graphics-safe:** margins inside the frame where action and text are kept so no screen crops them [12].
- **Safe zone (social):** the part of a vertical frame not covered by the app's labels and buttons [4].
- **Two-column script:** a script with what we hear on one side and what we see on the other, in timed rows.
- **Shot table:** a numbered list of shots with timing, framing, motion, text and audio.
- **Shot sizes:** wide shot (WS), medium shot (MS), close-up (CU) and extreme close-up (ECU), from most to least of the scene.
- **Animatic:** a storyboard timed to the voiceover, played as a rough video to test pace.
- **Style frame:** one finished-looking still that sets the look of an animated or edited video.
- **Timecode:** the address of a frame, in hours, minutes, seconds and frames.
- **Frame rate (fps):** frames per second; match the recording [1].
- **Keyframe:** a point in time where a property's value is set; the software fills the frames between.
- **Easing:** how motion speeds up or slows down between keyframes (ease in, ease out, linear).
- **Lower third:** a caption band, usually a name and title, in the lower part of the frame.
- **Kinetic typography:** words that move in time with speech or music.
- **Open and closed captions:** burned into the picture, or switchable by the viewer [9].
- **SRT:** a plain-text caption file format with numbered, timed lines [5][6].
- **LUFS:** loudness units relative to full scale, the measure used for programme loudness [13].
- **Codec and bitrate:** how video is compressed, and how much data per second it uses [1].
- **Content ID:** YouTube's automatic system that matches uploads to rights owners' files [15].
- **Musical work and sound recording:** the composition and lyrics, and the recorded performance; usually two separate rights [14].
- **Sync licence and master use licence:** the industry names for permission to use a composition in a video, and to use a specific recording of it.
- **Work made for hire:** under US law, a work whose employer or commissioner is the legal author [23][24].
- **Release (consent form):** a signed agreement from a person or location owner to appear in a video.
- **Deepfake:** AI-made or altered media that resembles real people, places or events [26].
