# Two-column explainer script

An explainer has one job: make one thing make sense to one kind of viewer, fast. The script is where that is won or lost. This skill writes it in two columns so the animator, editor or filmer sees exactly what to build, and the client sees exactly what will be said.

## When to use

- A 30 to 120 second explainer for a home page, onboarding or a sales call.
- A product demo walkthrough recorded on screen, for one key task.
- A short series: several videos that each explain one feature, step or idea, with a shared opening and closing.

Not for: video ads built to test hooks on paid social, or long YouTube channel scripts.

## What you need

- Product facts in the client's words: website, product notes, sales deck, help docs. Features must be real and current.
- The viewer: who they are and what they already know.
- Where it plays: page, platform, sound on or off, shape (16:9, 9:16, 1:1).
- Target length and the one action after watching.
- Who builds it (animator, editor, filmer, AI video tool) and any brand rules.
- **Access I have** in each tool. Steps 1 to 8 need level 1. Step 9 (building the draft in a connected tool, such as a Canva or Descript project) needs level 2. Nothing in this skill publishes; publishing belongs to the delivery skill and needs level 3.

## Steps

1. **Find the one idea.** Write one sentence the viewer must leave with, in the viewer's words, not the product team's. If the client gives five, rank them and park the rest for a series.
2. **Plan the arc.** Use five beats unless the brief says otherwise: the problem the viewer recognises; the moment it changes; how it works, in no more than three steps; proof the client can back up (a named customer with permission, a real number with a source, or a live demo); the next step. For a demo, replace the arc with one task from start to finish, in the order a user clicks.
3. **Write the voiceover (left column).** Short spoken sentences. One idea per sentence. No brackets, no abbreviations the voice would trip on, numbers written as they are said. Every claim traces to the client's material; mark any that does not as "[proof needed]".
4. **Time it by ear.** Read the voiceover aloud at the real speaker's pace with a stopwatch, or ask the client to. Do not rely on a words-per-minute rule. If it runs long, cut whole ideas, not breaths. Leave pauses for key pictures to land.
5. **Write the picture (right column).** For each row: what we see, what moves, any on-screen text in quotes with its character count, and sound notes. Show what the words do not say: if the voice says "it sorts your invoices", the picture shows the messy pile becoming three neat stacks, not the words "it sorts your invoices".
6. **Check on-screen text timing.** Use Netflix's English subtitle limits as a floor: no more than 20 characters per second for adults (17 for children), 42 characters per line, two lines, and at least five-sixths of a second on screen (checked 6 Nov 2026). Divide characters by 20 to get the minimum seconds. Moving text needs longer.
7. **Plan for sound off.** If the video may autoplay muted, the picture column must carry the story alone: key words on screen, burned-in or file captions, and a clear last frame.
8. **Add opening tests and notes.** Write two alternative opening lines to test. List open questions, claims needing proof, assets needed ("[needs: product screen recording of the export step]") and any rights items (music, stock, logos, people).
9. **Level 2: build the draft.** With level 2 access in the client's tool, create a draft project named "DRAFT" with the script as text or scene notes. Do not share or export. Log what you created.

## Checks

- The one idea is stated at the top and every row serves it.
- The read-aloud time fits the target, with the time recorded.
- Every feature exists today; every number and customer name has a source and permission.
- Every on-screen line passes the reading-speed floor.
- The picture column adds information instead of repeating the voice.
- The last row states the one action, on screen and in the voice.
- No invented testimonials, results or before-and-after claims.

## Output format

1. **Header:** video name, length, shape, where it plays, sound on or off, the one idea, the one action, access I have.
2. **Script table:** row number, timecode in and out, voiceover (exact), picture and motion, on-screen text (exact, with character count), sound.
3. **Timing note:** read-aloud time and who read it.
4. **Two alternative openings.**
5. **Open items:** claims needing proof, assets needed, rights questions.
6. **Action log** for any level 2 work.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: an invoicing app with a 45-second home page explainer, 16:9, sound on with captions. One idea: "[Business] chases late invoices so you don't have to." Action: start a free trial. Access I have: Canva level 1. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context.*

| # | Time | We hear | We see | On-screen text (chars) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 00:00-00:06 | You did the work. Three weeks later, you're still waiting to be paid. | A freelancer's desk; a calendar flips; an invoice marked "Sent" sits untouched | "21 days overdue" (15) |
| 2 | 00:06-00:13 | Chasing feels awkward, so it slips. | The freelancer starts typing a reminder email, deletes it | none |
| 3 | 00:13-00:24 | [Business] sends polite reminders on the schedule you choose. | Screen recording [needs: reminder schedule screen]; three reminder dates light up | "You pick the dates" (18) |
| 4 | 00:24-00:35 | When they pay, it marks the invoice paid and tells you. | Phone notification "Invoice paid"; invoice status turns green | "Paid. Marked. Done." (19) |
| 5 | 00:35-00:45 | Try it free. Let [Business] do the chasing. | Logo end card, trial button, web address | "Start your free trial" (21) |

*Timing: read aloud by the founder, 41 seconds. Line 3 is "[proof needed]" until the client confirms custom reminder dates exist in the current version.*

**Alternative openings:** "Late invoices aren't a money problem. They're a chasing problem." / "How many invoices are you waiting on right now?"
