# Fair rota and rules check

A good rota covers the busy times, stays inside the wage budget, follows the rules and feels fair to the people on it. This skill builds it in that order and shows the checks, so nothing that breaks a rule is published quietly.

Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant. This skill checks against the rules the client confirms and points to official guidance; it gives no employment-law advice.

## When to use

- Every week or fortnight, before the rota goes out.
- When the same people keep getting the late or weekend shifts.
- When overtime or wage cost keeps beating the budget.
- When the business opens a site, changes hours or hires several part-timers.

## What you need

1. Staffing needs by hour and role (from the `staffing-levels-from-demand` skill or the client).
2. Staff list with ID or first name, role, skills, contract hours, availability, approved time off and an under-18 flag.
3. Pay rates by role or person, overtime and premium rules, and the wage budget for the week.
4. **The client's written rules**, confirmed for the location: breaks, rest between shifts, weekly rest, maximum and average hours, overtime, night work, under-18 limits, and any scheduling law (advance notice, change pay, rest between shifts).
5. The last four to eight weeks of rotas, to measure fairness.
6. **Access:** level 1 to read; level 2 to draft in the rota tool; level 3 to publish only after the client's fresh yes for that rota.

## Steps

### 1. Write the frame
Week, site, opening hours, budget, the rules in use and their source. If a rule is not written, list it as [NEEDED] and use only what the client states. Point to the official page for the location (for example GOV.UK working time pages, the US Department of Labor and the state or city, the European Commission's working time pages).

### 2. Place fixed and hardest shifts first
Opening, closing, skilled roles, late and weekend shifts, and any shift that needs a specific skill or an adult on site. Use people's availability and contract hours. Then fill the remaining hours.

### 3. Spread the unpopular shifts
Count late, weekend and holiday shifts per person over the last four to eight weeks and this week. Give the next one to whoever has had fewest, unless they asked otherwise. Keep start times stable for each person where demand allows.

### 4. Check every shift
For each person and day:
- Break given where the rules need one, of the right length.
- Rest between the end of one shift and the start of the next (for example a close then an open).
- Weekly rest and days off.
- Daily and weekly hours, and any average-hours limit over the reference period.
- Night work limits for night workers.
- Under-18 rules: maximum hours, latest and earliest times, longer breaks and rest.
- Overtime as the client's rules and law define it (in the US, federal overtime counts hours over 40 in a fixed workweek, with no averaging).
- Where a scheduling law applies: posted far enough ahead, good-faith estimate respected, and the cost of any change after posting.
List each break of a rule with the person, the shift, the rule and a fix.

### 5. Cost it
Hours by person and day, normal and overtime or premium hours, wage cost by day and week at the client's rates, and the gap to budget.

### 6. Fix gaps and conflicts
Gaps not filled, rule breaks, and budget overruns, each with options: swap, split a shift, use a cross-trained person, adjust a quiet hour, open an approved shift to staff, or ask for budget. Show the cost of each option. Never hide a gap by bending a rule.

### 7. Draft (level 2) and publish (fresh yes)
At level 2, build the draft in the rota tool where staff cannot see it. Show the client the rota, the rule check, the fairness count and the cost. Publishing needs the client's fresh yes for this rota. At level 3, after that yes, publish and send the agreed "rota is up" message, and log it.

### 8. Changes after publishing
Any change to a person's hours or shifts after publishing needs the client's fresh yes. Where a scheduling law applies, show the change pay it would trigger before the client decides.

## Checks

- Every shift passes the rules the client confirmed, or the break of a rule is listed with a fix.
- Under-18 rules were checked first for flagged staff.
- Fairness counts are shown for late and weekend shifts.
- Cost totals tie to hours and rates; overtime is counted the way the rules require.
- The output carries: *Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant.*
- Nothing was published and no hours were changed without a fresh yes.

## Output format

1. Summary: week, site, hours, cost against budget, gaps, rule breaks.
2. Rota grid: person by day, start, end, break, total hours.
3. Rule check table: person, shift, rule, result, fix.
4. Fairness table: late, weekend and holiday shifts per person, last weeks and this week.
5. Cost table by day.
6. Gaps and options with cost.
7. Action log (level 3 only).

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a gym in Leeds with 14 staff. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

> **Week of 9 March:** 412 hours, £5,486 against a £5,600 budget. 1 gap, 2 rule issues.
>
> | Person | Shift | Rule | Result | Fix |
> |---|---|---|---|---|
> | S-04 | Tue close 22:00, Wed open 06:00 | 11 hours' rest between working days | 8 hours: fails | Move Wed open to S-09 (no extra cost) |
> | S-11 (under 18) | Sat 07:00 to 13:30 | Break of 30 minutes over 4.5 hours | No break planned | Add 30 minutes at 10:30 |
>
> **Fairness (last 6 weeks + this week):** weekend shifts range 3 to 6 per person; S-02 and S-07 at 6 get none next week.
> **Gap:** Sunday 16:00 to 20:00, one instructor. Options: offer as an open shift to qualified staff (about £60), or close the studio slot (no cost, lost class).
> **Next step:** your yes to publish, then the "rota is up" message to the team.
>
> *Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant.*
