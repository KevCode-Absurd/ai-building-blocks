# Headcount plan and cost

A hire costs far more than the salary line, and it costs money before it produces. This skill shows the full cost and timing of each hire, tests the plan against a bad case, and lays out the options so the owner can decide.

Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant. Tax, benefit and pension rates must be confirmed with payroll or the accountant; this skill uses the rates given and labels every one.

## When to use

- Setting next year's budget or hiring plan.
- Before approving a new role.
- When growth means the team is stretched and the owner is unsure what to hire first.
- When a slower quarter raises the question of pausing hiring.

## What you need

1. Current team: roles, hours, pay (by role or ID, no names needed), start dates.
2. Roles to add, with a reason for each: the work it covers, and what happens if it waits.
3. Revenue plan by month and current cash; the cash floor the owner never wants to go under.
4. On-cost rates confirmed by payroll or the accountant: employer taxes, pension, benefits, insurance.
5. One-off costs: recruiting, equipment, software seats, training.
6. Ramp-up time per role: weeks until the hire works at full output.
7. Work demand by team if a capacity view is needed (hours of work per month).
8. **Access:** level 1 to the budget sheet and HR export; level 2 to draft the plan in a copy; level 3 to update the agreed tab of the live plan.

## Steps

### 1. Cost one role fully
For each role, a monthly cost sheet:
- Pay: salary or hourly rate × expected hours, with overtime if the role will carry it.
- Employer taxes at the confirmed rates. For reference only: US employer Social Security is 6.2% of wages up to $184,500 and Medicare 1.45% for 2026, plus state taxes; UK employer National Insurance is 15% above £5,000 a year for 2026-27, with an Employment Allowance some employers can claim. Use the client's confirmed figures, not these.
- Pension and benefits at the client's rates. A national average (benefits were 30.0% of US private-industry compensation cost in June 2026, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics) is a sense check only.
- Equipment, software seats and any space cost.
- One-off: recruiting, onboarding and training time, spread over the start month or months.
Show the total monthly cost and the first-year cost.

### 2. Place hires on a timeline
Start month for each role, working back from when the work needs covering: recruiting time plus notice periods plus ramp-up. Show cost from the start month and output from after ramp-up.

### 3. Test against cash and a slow case
Add the plan to the client's monthly revenue and cash. Show the normal case and a slow-sales case (the client chooses how slow). Mark the month cash would fall below the floor, and which hire to pause first to avoid it. For a full driver-based model, hand over to the `driver-based-model` skill in the `fpa-analyst` kit.

### 4. Compare options for each need
For each role: full-time employee, part-time, contractor or agency, or a tool or outsourced service. Cost each over 12 months, and note control, availability, knowledge kept in the business and risk. If a contractor would work like an employee (fixed hours, the client's tools, no end date), flag worker status for the adviser rather than costing it as the cheap option.

### 5. Rank the roles
By the work each covers, the risk and cost of waiting, and its cost. One line of reasoning per role. The owner decides the order.

### 6. Capacity view (if asked)
Hours of work needed per month by team against hours available after holiday and absence (client's figures). Show gaps by month and when they appear, and which hire or option closes each.

### 7. Write it up
One page for the owner: the plan, total cost by month, cash low point in each case, the order of hires and the triggers to pause or speed up. Every rate labelled with its source and date.

## Checks

- Every on-cost rate is confirmed by the client or labelled as a placeholder to confirm.
- Cost starts at the start month, and output starts after ramp-up.
- The slow case is shown and the cash floor is respected.
- Contractor options that look like employment are flagged, not chosen for cost.
- No person is named for hiring, cutting or pay decisions.
- The output carries: *Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant.*

## Output format

1. Summary: hires, first-year cost, cash low point (normal and slow), pause trigger.
2. Cost sheet per role (monthly lines).
3. Hiring timeline with ramp-up.
4. Cash test table by month, normal and slow.
5. Options comparison per need.
6. Ranked roles with reasons.
7. Capacity view (if asked).
8. Assumptions and rates with sources.

## Example of good output (short)

*Made-up business: Brightwell Labels, a 12-person print studio in Manchester. Figures are invented for illustration.*

> **Plan:** two hires: a print operator (March) and an account manager (June). Cost from start dates to December: £52,800. Cash low point £38,000 in August (normal) and £9,000 in September (slow, sales 15% below plan), under the £20,000 floor.
>
> | Print operator, monthly | £ |
> |---|---|
> | Pay (£26,500 a year) | 2,208 |
> | Employer NI at 15% above £5,000 a year (rate confirmed by payroll) | 269 |
> | Pension at 3% of qualifying earnings (placeholder: confirm) | 51 |
> | Safety kit and training (months 1 to 2 only) | 450 |
> | **Total, month 1** | **2,978** |
>
> **Ramp-up:** 6 weeks to full output. **Option:** agency operator for March to May at £3,400 a month, then hire: costs about £1,720 more over those three months, but the agency can be stopped at a week's notice if sales slow.
> **Pause trigger:** if April sales are more than 10% below plan, move the account manager to September.
>
> *Check this with local rules, or ask a qualified employment adviser or accountant.*
