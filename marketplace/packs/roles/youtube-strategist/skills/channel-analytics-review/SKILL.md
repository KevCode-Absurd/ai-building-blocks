# Channel analytics review

Views alone do not say what to do next. This skill reads the channel the way YouTube's own reports are built: where viewers came from, whether they stayed, and whether they came back, by series. It ends with a few changes the client can make, not a dashboard. It works from exports and screenshots, or directly in YouTube Studio with level 1 access; it says plainly when a number was not shared.

## When to use

- A monthly or quarterly review.
- Growth has stalled, or a series has dropped.
- Before a new channel plan or a back-catalogue refresh.
- A client asks "what is working?" and has only seen view counts.

## What you need

- **Exports or screenshots for the last 90 days (and the 90 before, if possible):** per video, views, impressions, impressions click-through rate, average view duration, watch time, subscribers gained; traffic sources by video; new, casual and regular viewers; audience retention for the top and bottom videos.
- **The channel plan:** promise and series, so videos can be grouped.
- **Business results:** clicks, enquiries or sales the client can tie to videos.
- **Any notices:** policy warnings, strikes, copyright claims, Partner Program messages.

**Access:** level 1 is enough to read. Applying the refresh to live videos needs level 3 within the client's limits; level 2 prepares the changes in a sheet or on private copies.

## Steps

1. **Group videos by series** and by format (long, Shorts, live). Note uploads per series in the period.
2. **Read reach.** Impressions and click-through rate per series and per traffic source. YouTube says half of channels and videos fall between 2% and 10% CTR, that widely shown videos naturally get lower rates, and that end screens and external sites are excluded (YouTube Help, checked 6 Nov 2026). Compare a video with the channel's own past, not with a universal target.
3. **Read where views came from.** YouTube search, Suggested videos, Browse features, Shorts feed, Playlists, Channel pages, External. Search growth says the topic map works; suggested and browse growth says viewers are satisfied enough to be shown more.
4. **Read whether viewers stayed.** Average view duration and the audience retention graph for top and bottom videos; the intro figure (still watching at 30 seconds) against typical retention, which uses the channel's 10 latest videos of similar length.
5. **Read whether they came back.** New, casual and regular viewers; subscribers gained per series.
6. **Look for the clickbait pattern.** High CTR with low average view duration and falling impressions: fix the promise or the opening.
7. **Tie to the business.** Clicks, enquiries or sales per series. A small series that brings buyers beats a large one that brings none.
8. **Check policy health.** Strikes or warnings; videos at risk under the inauthentic or reused content rules; sponsorships without the paid promotion setting; realistic AI without the AI use setting; made-for-kids settings that look wrong.
9. **Pick three to five changes.** Each with the evidence, the change, and how it will be read next time. Prefer changes to the next videos over edits to old ones.
10. **Build the back-catalogue refresh.** Up to 20 older videos that get views but little search traffic, or that rank for a search their title does not match. For each: the new title in the searched words, a description whose first lines answer the query, chapters (00:00 first, at least three, each at least 10 seconds), a pinned comment, and the end-screen target. Change one video at a time and note the date, so effects can be read.

## Checks

- Every finding names its number, period and source screen or export.
- Missing data is listed, not guessed.
- Changes are few, specific and tied to evidence.
- No promise that a change will bring views.
- Refresh titles still match what the video delivers.
- Policy risks are flagged with the page to read.

## Output format

1. **Headline:** three sentences on what grew, what stalled and why.
2. **Series table:** uploads, views, CTR, average view duration, main traffic sources, returning viewers, business results.
3. **Findings:** each with evidence.
4. **Changes:** three to five, with how to read them next time.
5. **Back-catalogue refresh table:** video, old title, new title, description opening, chapters, pinned comment, end-screen target.
6. **Policy health** and **data still needed**.

## Example of good output (short)

*Illustrative structure, not a real business: a baking school that sells online courses through a YouTube channel. Copy the shape, never the content: every fact comes from the client's own context. Figures only show the shape.*

**Headline:** The Sourdough Basics series grew search traffic and brought most course sign-ups. The Kitchen Vlog series drew clicks but viewers left early. Shorts brought new viewers who rarely watched a long video.

| Series | Uploads | Main sources | Avg view duration | Returning viewers | Course clicks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sourdough Basics | 6 | YouTube search, Suggested | Held near typical | Rising | Most of the period's clicks |
| Kitchen Vlog | 5 | Browse | Below typical; intro weak | Flat | Few |
| Shorts | 12 | Shorts feed | n/a | Low | Very few |

**Changes:**
1. Make Sourdough Basics the main series: two of every three long uploads. Read search traffic to the series next quarter.
2. Rewrite Kitchen Vlog openings to show the finished bake first; read the intro figure.
3. Link each Short to its matching Sourdough Basics video; read Shorts-to-long views.

**Refresh (one row):** "Sunday bake with me" becomes "Sourdough starter not rising? 5 fixes"; chapters added; pinned comment lists the five fixes.

*Why this works:* findings are read by series and source, tied to sign-ups, and each change says how it will be judged.
