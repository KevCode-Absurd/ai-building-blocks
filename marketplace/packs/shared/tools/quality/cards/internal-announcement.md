Families: internal-announcement; also: staff announcement, all-hands email, company announcement, team update, change announcement, reorg announcement, policy change notice, leadership memo, internal comms

**WHO:** An internal communications lead who has announced reorgs, policy changes and new tools to whole companies. Optimises for every employee knowing within one read what changes for them and what they must do.

**MUST-HAVES**
1. What changes, in the first two sentences, in plain words. No warm-up, no "exciting news".
2. When: the date it starts, and any key dates before then (training, deadlines, the last day of the old way).
3. Who it affects: which teams, locations or roles, and who it does not affect. If impact differs by group, a short table.
4. Why, told straight: the real reason in one to three sentences. If there is bad news, say it plainly and say what support is offered.
5. What people must do, as a short numbered list with dates, and what happens if they do nothing.
6. Where to ask: a named owner or channel, a FAQ or a session with a date, and how to raise a concern privately.

**FAILURE MODES**
1. Spin or corporate euphemism ("rightsizing", "exciting journey") that hides what is happening.
2. The change buried after paragraphs of context.
3. No date, no action, or no owner for questions.
4. Promises the company has not made (no job losses, no pay change) or details not yet decided stated as fact.
5. One message for everyone when groups are affected very differently.
6. Tone that clashes with the news, such as upbeat language about job cuts.

**SHAPE:** Subject line that names the change; then what changes, when, who it affects, why, what you need to do (numbered), where to ask. Usually 150 to 400 words. A manager talking-points note and a short FAQ only if asked, labeled and separate.

**RULES AND RISKS:** Changes to pay, hours, roles, benefits or jobs may need HR and legal review, and in some places must go through worker consultation before announcing. Do not share personal or confidential details about named people. Market-sensitive news follows the company's disclosure rules. Mark anything not yet decided.

**DONE MEANS:** Any employee could read it once and say what changes, when, whether it affects them, what they must do, and who to ask, and no one would feel misled a month later.
