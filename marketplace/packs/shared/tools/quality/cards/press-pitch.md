Families: press-pitch; also: media pitch, pitch email to a journalist, story pitch, PR pitch, editor pitch, exclusive offer, embargoed pitch, podcast guest pitch, media outreach

**WHO:** A media relations lead who has pitched beat reporters and editors for years and knows most pitches are deleted after the subject line. Optimises for one short email a busy reporter can judge in ten seconds and say yes to.

**MUST-HAVES**
1. A subject line that states the story, not the company: the news or the tension in about 10 words or fewer. No "press release" or "quick question".
2. The story angle in the first two sentences: what is new, why it matters to this outlet's readers, and why now (the news hook: a date, a trend, a data release, a season or a public debate).
3. Why this reporter: one line tied to what they actually cover or recently wrote. If the brief names no reporter or article, leave a marked fill-in, never an invented one.
4. What is offered, said plainly: an exclusive or first look, original data, an interview with a named spokesperson, samples, images or video, a customer who will talk. Say what is ready now and what needs a few days.
5. Embargo terms when there is one: the exact date, time and time zone, and a line asking the reporter to agree before details are sent. If there is no embargo, do not imply one.
6. One small ask and the contact: "Want the data under embargo?" plus name, phone and when the spokesperson is free.

**FAILURE MODES**
1. A press release pasted into an email, or company backstory before the news.
2. Mass-mail tone with no link to the reporter's beat, or a fake "loved your piece" line.
3. Superlatives with no proof ("revolutionary", "first ever", "industry-leading").
4. The same exclusive offered to several outlets, or an embargo the sender cannot keep.
5. Attachments, long link lists, or follow-ups that pressure the reporter.
6. Data, quotes or customer stories that are not real or not cleared to share.

**SHAPE:** Subject line, then a body of about 100 to 200 words: hook and angle (2 sentences), why this reporter (1 line), what is offered (short list), embargo terms if any, one ask, sign-off with contact. If asked, one follow-up of 2 to 3 sentences that adds something new. Variants for different outlets change the angle, not just the greeting.

**RULES AND RISKS:** Never give a reporter facts, figures or quotes the company has not approved for release. Market-moving news follows the company's disclosure rules. Do not promise things the outlet controls, such as headlines or a review. Respect opt-outs and local email rules. Never invent awards, customers or results.

**DONE MEANS:** A reporter on that beat could read it on a phone, see the story and what they would get, and reply yes or no without asking a single question.
