Families: product-announcement; also: launch announcement, press release, feature announcement, release notes, launch email, launch blog post, news release, changelog post

**WHO:** A former reporter turned communications lead. Optimises for news a busy reader grasps in two lines, with every claim provable.

**MUST-HAVES**
1. A headline stating the news as plain fact (who did what, what changes for the reader), not a slogan.
2. A first paragraph with who, what, when, where and why it matters, plus availability, price and where to get it.
3. The benefit in the customer's words, then specifics: what it does, who it is for, limits, and sourced proof.
4. Quotes from real, named people, approved by them, adding opinion rather than repeating facts.
5. One clear call to action; channel versions (release, email, social, in-product note) keep every fact identical; boilerplate and press contact on the release.

**FAILURE MODES**
1. Hype words ("revolutionary", "seamless", "leading") with no proof.
2. Buried news: history or vision before what is new.
3. Invented quotes, numbers, customers or "first" and "only" claims.
4. A feature list with no reason to care or act.
5. One text pasted to every channel; missing date, price or availability.

**SHAPE**
Core piece: headline, subhead, dated lead paragraph, two to four short body paragraphs (benefit, how it works, proof, availability and price), one or two quotes, call to action, boilerplate and contact. Press release 300 to 500 words. Customer email 100 to 200 words. Social: two or three short variants when social is asked for. Release note: title, one-line outcome, what changed, help link.

**RULES AND RISKS**
Substantiate claims before publishing; comparisons must be fair and checkable. Call it "new" only for a limited period (check local rules). Embargoes bind only those who agreed; state the time zone. Market-sensitive news at a listed company needs disclosure sign-off and release to everyone at once. Disclose paid or gifted coverage. Show prices with mandatory fees.

**DONE MEANS:** A reader who stops after the first paragraph knows what is new, who it is for and how to get it, and every line passes a fact check.
