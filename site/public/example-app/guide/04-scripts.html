Write scripts

Guide, chapter 4 of 20

A script is the argument of an ad, line by line, with the evidence under each line. It can become a video, a static, a carousel or a brief for a creator.

A script written from principles, with 3 hooks, its primary text and headlines, cost 13 to 15 cents on 3 test scripts on 2026-10-05 (see "What a blind judge found" below). The older path cost about 25 cents.

How long a script runs

A script is as long as its buyer needs, unless you pick a length.

You chooseWhat happensWhat the line under the choice says
Let the story decide (where it starts)The buyer's stage decides. A buyer who does not know the problem yet is told a longer story. A buyer who is ready to buy is told a short oneAbout how long that is for the buyer you picked, at the speaker's pace: "about 45 s to about 1 min 40 s for this buyer" for a solution aware buyer when the speaker's age is not known
15 seconds, 30 seconds, 1 minute, 2 to 3 minutes, 5 to 10 minutes, or 10 minutesThe script is held to that length, whatever the buyer's stage. 10 minutes is for a long-form video, said in fullHow long it runs, about how many words that is for its speaker, and a count of clips at 10 seconds each: "about 30 s · about 100 words · 3 clips of up to 10 seconds" when the speaker's age is not known. The video itself usually has more clips than this line says, because a clip is now 1 scene of about 6 seconds (see Production)

A length you chose may run 15% under or over. The length check then reads the script against it, and what it finds is advice. Under the script, the word count says what you chose ("you chose 30 seconds, about 99 words"). A script written from principles with no length chosen has no target, and its length is not flagged. A script with Check claims on is written the older way, always to a length: from Generate scripts that is 1 minute when you chose none.

At a short length, a script for a buyer who needs the long story tells 1 moment of it and not the whole history.

Where the choice is:

  • Generate scripts, under Length.
  • The script studio, under Length in the controls on the left. It sets the length of the next script you generate from there.
  • The Script builder, as "Length of each script".
  • The chat: say it in your message ("3 scripts of 30 seconds", "a 10 minute script"). Say no length and each script runs as long as its buyer needs.

A native ad keeps its own lengths.

How fast a script is said

From 2026-10-08 a length is time. How many words fill it follows how fast the speaker talks. The app holds 1 table of spoken words a second, by the speaker's age:

The speaker's ageSpoken words a second
Under 303.6
30 to 443.4
45 to 593.2
60 to 743.0
75 and over2.8
Not known3.3

Speech that runs past a minute is counted at 0.9 of that. The speaker of a script is the presenter the brand's creative kit casts for the script's persona, or else the persona's own age.

So the same length holds more words for a younger speaker. 30 seconds is about 108 words for a speaker under 30, 99 when the age is not known, and 90 for a speaker of 60 to 74.

  • The writer is given the words of a chosen length for that speaker. The writer of a script written from principles is also sent 1 line on the pace: the speaker's age band, about how many words a minute they say, and to write lines that are said in 1 run with no pause. Nothing checks a script against that line.
  • The read time on a script is its words at the same pace. The script screen says whose pace it is: "102 words, said at about 200 words a minute by a speaker aged 30 to 44".
  • The video made from the script is timed from the same table (see Production).

The table rests on published studies of how fast people speak. The 0.9 for long speech is a starting choice, not measured. Before this, every script was counted at 2.6 words a second and the video at another number, so a script written as 30 seconds was a video of 23.

How a script is checked now (phases 45 to 47)

The diagram above is the older path. It is still used for a script written from a reference ad, a native ad, a script with Check claims on, and a script written with SCRIPT_FLOW=off. Every other script is written from principles, and goes through this:

flowchart TD
  W[The writer sets down a 5-line story brief, then writes the script] --> C[Code checks: no model]
  C --> R[A second reader and a fact checker read it]
  R --> Q{A fault?}
  Q -- yes --> P[Only the faulty lines are patched] --> R2[Only the patched lines are read again]
  R2 --> Q
  Q -- no, or 2 loops done --> H[Further hooks, each a full version]
  H --> T[Primary text and headline, then an opening and a headline for each hook]
  T --> K[1 last reader holds those words to the script]

The story brief (phase 45). Before the first line, the same call writes 5 short lines: who speaks, the before, the turning point, the after, and each figure that will be said and whose it is. It is in the script's notes. The caption writer and the patcher are sent it, so they tell the same story. SCRIPT_BRIEF=off writes without it.

Do not invent facts. The writer is free in story, voice and persuasion. It is not free in facts. Anything said about the product, how it works, a price, a result, a number, another product, or what the buyer tried or pays must be in what the writer was sent. The same rule is in the prompts for hooks and for the primary text.

The fact checker. 1 call lists every factual statement in the script. Beside each it puts the number of the fact the brand holds that the statement rests on. It reads against everything the brand holds (every fact of the product, every claim, the offer in use, the buyer's record, the brief), not only the short sheet the writer was sent, so a true fact is not flagged. Code, not the model, settles the verdict:

VerdictWhat it meansWhat happens
HeldIt cites a fact that existsLeft alone
StretchedIt says more than the fact it citesThe extra part is taken out
InventedIt cites no factTaken out, or cut back to what is held
SceneA picture the story paints: the buyer's morning, the tub at the back of the cupboardListed, never sent back. A "scene" with a number in it is not a scene: it is treated as a claim

An example from a test script: "There are 28 packs in the pouch" was held by the product's own line "Pouch of 28 daily sachets". "And the greens happen. Every day, without a pep talk." rested on nothing and was taken out. "Your card gets charged again this week" was a scene and was left alone.

The second reader. A model that did not write or fix the script reads it against 12 named checks: makes sense, tells 1 story, 1 speaker, can be said aloud, simple words, sounds like the brand, facts are true, doubts answered with proof, the offer said like a person, ends on the after, nothing new after the payoff, no stock lines. It only reports.

Code checks. No model. A sentence too long for 1 breath, 3 thoughts chained in 1 sentence, a run of fragments, a figure said twice, a figure held nowhere, a second speaker, 2 prices in a line, more of the deal than this buyer is told, a new figure after the after. The script is not done while one of these is still there.

Fixes are patches. A fault is not fixed by writing the script again. The patcher is sent the story in a few lines, the faulty lines with 1 neighbour each side, what is wrong, and for a fact fault the few held facts that bear on it. Only the lines it changes come back and are put in place. A fact fault is fixed by saying less. Then only the patched lines are read again.

The loop stops at 2. With 3 loops, each of 3 test scripts used all 3 and cost 16 to 20 cents, and a blind judge found them no cleaner. What is still open when it stops is written on the script.

The offer is said by stage. An unaware or problem aware buyer is told no price, no deal and no guarantee. A solution aware or product aware buyer is told 1 thing, once: what it costs to try, or what makes trying safe. A most aware buyer can be told the whole deal. The full terms are added under the primary text by code. Moving How hard it sells on the taste bar (chapter 19) changes how much of the offer is sent.

Doubts and proofs. The writer is sent at most 3 of the persona's doubts, the ones closest to the idea, each with the only proof that may answer it (chapter 14). A doubt with no proof is not sent, so the script cannot raise a doubt it cannot answer.

The last reader. The primary text, each headline and each further hook are written after the script is fixed. 1 reader that did not write them holds every sentence to the script: a fact the script does not state, a contradiction, another speaker, more of the deal, an opening that does not hand off to the next sentence, a headline that is a slogan. It fixes the sentence or cuts it. Measured on 3 scripts: 1.6 to 1.7 cents, 6 to 7 seconds.

Primary texts that do not retell each other (2026-10-08). When you ask for more than 1 primary text for a script, each version is handed its own place to start before it is written: a moment and a way in, and for a long story a place and who else is there. Who speaks, what happened and every fact stay the script's. The writer is also told the phrases the brand's recent texts keep using, so it says them another way. The headline writer is told the buyer's stage and what a headline does there: for an unaware or problem aware buyer it names no product, no brand, no pack and no price. Chapter 5 has the whole of it, under "Words that differ, and figures that stay whole".

A script you edited by hand is never rewritten by these steps. The edit pass reports and suggests.

The script studio

The script studio: controls on the left, lines and evidence in the middle, the audit on the right

Left: the controls. The kind of script, feel, length, person and tense, how the problem is entered, the story shape, the register, the hook type, and the themes to draw on. Change a control and regenerate to get a different take on the same concept.

Middle: the lines. Each line has:

  • its beat (hook, problem, promise, mechanism, proof, objection, offer, call to action),
  • the spoken words,
  • the on-screen text,
  • the evidence under it: theme chips for customer words, green claim chips for facts,
  • a visual note for whoever films or generates it.

Edit any line. "Save as new version" keeps the old one; Versions shows the history.

Right: the checks.

  • Voice checks: whether it sounds like a person talking, and like this brand.
  • Auditor: about 20 checks. The rule checks cover promise before mechanism, the product not named too early, every mechanism and proof line citing a substantiated claim, no forbidden claims, guardrails kept, contractions, reading level, length. The judged checks cover whether the problem is a real coping behaviour rather than a symptom list, whether the claim is strong enough, whether the call to action is complete.

A failed check is shown on its line. It does not stop you approving the script: fix the line, or approve it as it is. "Re-run audit" checks again after an edit.

The copy pass

After a script is written, and before anything is done about how it sounds, a second model reads it the way a direct-response copywriter reads a draft and sharpens the lines that don't sell. It is a different model from the writer, so it is not marking its own work. The same pass runs on the words of every image ad: the on-image headline, the points, the primary text and the headline field.

What it looks for (the full list is the copy-principles skill, distilled from Ogilvy on Advertising and the writers it draws on):

It asksIt changes
Is there 1 promise, in the buyer's terms?A line with no promise, or 5
Could the product next to it on the shelf say this?A claim any rival could make
Is this a fact or an opinion?"Premium", "powerful", "the best" become the fact that earns them, or go
Does the headline or hook work alone?A clever, vague or blind first line
Does the first paragraph grab?An opening that states the obvious or introduces the brand
Is the proof as big as the claim?A boast, a superlative, a named competitor
Does it ask for the order?A call to action that only names the action

What it may not do: change a claim, a number, a price, an offer term, the guarantee, a customer's quoted words, the order of the lines, or the person and tense. It may put a fact in place of an adjective only if the fact is in the brand's own material; code refuses any edit that brings in a number from anywhere else, or drops one. If its edits together break an audit rule, none are applied.

A good draft gets 0 to 5 changes. Each change, and each suggestion that was refused and why, is listed in the script's notes, and the Versions view shows the draft as first written next to what ships.

Two rule checks back it up on every script and every ad text: a word any competitor could use ("premium", "world-class", "revolutionary"), and urgency with nothing behind it ("limited time only" with no date or number). The script audit also asks 2 more questions: the shelf test, and "so what?" (does any line say why the buyer cares, beyond the mechanism).

Writing for the voice

What a script says and how it sounds are different skills. Every script, and every word a customer reads on an image or in the feed, is held to the same rules:

RuleWhat the app does
1 idea per sentence, the verb earlyThe writer is told; a sentence over 28 words (40 for copy that is read) is flagged as too long for 1 breath
Plain words, at the level of the audienceThe reading level is measured. A general audience is held to grade 7; an audience that uses the category's terms (athletes who say "fast-twitch") to grade 9, and those terms may stay
Vary sentence length: long, long, short4 very short sentences in a row, or 5 of the same length, are flagged
Contractions always"Do not" and "it is" are flagged
End on the word that carries the weightA hook or a last line that ends on "it", "that", "of", "too" is flagged
Hold 1 person and 1 tenseThe person the script was asked for is compared with the one it is written in
No "it's not X, it's Y", no em dashesFlagged

Who the brand talks to is worked out once, from the customers' own words, and stored as the brand's voice level: general public, informed, or professional. It sets the reading level for every script and every piece of copy.

After a script is written the voice pass reads it as if aloud and fixes how lines sound, never what they say; the brand voice check then makes it sound like this brand; the claims audit runs last, on the final words. Each rule above that still fails shows as a warning in the script's checks.

The same brief can be many scripts, but a new grammar is not a new ad. Person, tense and register change how an ad feels: a UGC script in the second person, a calm documentary in the third, a diary in the first. A reviewer who read 5 such scripts found 2 or 3 real ads among them: the same tub, the same proof, the same closing line, retold. So each new script for a concept is now told how every earlier one opens, proves and closes, which approved claims none of them has used, and which phrases are already spent, and it is checked afterwards: a script that shares its phrases, opening, proof and close with a sibling is flagged as that sibling told another way.

The specifics

Rules a model does not hold on its own, so code counts them on every script:

CheckWhat it catches
A number with no source"The ninety-four dollar charge": a figure that is in no approved claim, offer or guarantee. Numbers said in words are read too
A limit dropped"80% take it daily" where the claim says "of surveyed customers"; "21 vitamins" where it says "and minerals"; a guarantee without "first order"
A first-order price aloneThe introductory price quoted with no word of what it becomes
The guarantee twiceEach beat once
A hook in another personA "you" script with an "I" hook
A made-up resultA speaker who reports what the product did for them when no approved claim says it (read by a model)

Each is a warning with the line it is on. Story colour (a husband, a wedding photo) is the writer's to use; a price or a result is not.

What it should be made as

The script comes first. Only when the argument exists does the app ask what the ad should look like. Every script is read once more after it is written, and the box "What it should be made as" in the script studio shows the answer:

  • The load-bearing beat. The 1 beat that does most of the persuading: the mechanism, the proof, the offer or the problem. If the writer meant one beat to carry the script and another does as written, the box says so: either that is the ad, or the first beat needs more of the script.
  • What the script needs from a surface. 6 properties, each marked must, helps or not needed: a believed human face, showing what a camera cannot, a controlled order, sound, held attention, reading as a person.
  • Each kind of ad scored against those needs. 1 person to camera, animation, mixed video, long-form native static, designed static, carousel. A surface is recommended when it has everything the script must have, possible when what it lacks can be worked around, and a poor fit when a beat the script rests on has nowhere to go.
  • Length. Whether the script fits its length, the length it is best made at, and where a longer version would get its length.

See the full check opens the table and the reason for every mark. Check again reads the script as it stands now, after an edit (about 1 cent).

Nothing here stops you: a script can be made as anything. A poor fit is a warning that the ad will come back from the editor without getting better, because the problem is in where a beat was put, not in how it was made.

Hooks

Each script comes with several hook variants: the script's own opening and alternatives of other types, each with its on-screen overlay and first frame. Each is graded. Test hooks against each other with the same body.

The script opens on its best hook. If the opening it was written with grades below the bar and another hook clears it, the better one is moved to line 1, the audit says so, and the first opening is kept as a variant. Read line 2 after a swap: it was written to follow the first opening.

Each hook is a full version (phase 46). A script keeps up to 5 hooks. Each one is a whole version of the ad: its own opening, then the same body. The script screen shows them as Version 1, Version 2 and so on. Version 1 is the main one: assets are made from it unless you pick another where you make them. A hook is written to hand off to the line that follows it.

Each version has its own headline and caption opening (phase 47). For every hook after the first, 1 small call writes the first line of the primary text and the headline of that version. The rest of the primary text is shared, so 5 hooks do not cost 5 captions. Measured on 7 calls: 0.7 to 1 cent, 4 to 7 seconds.

Known weak spot. The hand-off from a further hook into the body is the fault a blind judge still finds most often in hooks. Read line 2 of every version before you film it.

Similar scripts

The list shows how close each script is to its nearest sibling. Two scripts that share most of their wording are 1 test, not 2. The "Similar pairs" filter finds them. A deliberate hook swap can be kept and marked as such.

Your own script

Create, then Ads, then Upload your own script takes a Word file, a PDF or pasted text. It is kept word for word, split into lines, checked, and can then be made in any format. Nothing is rewritten.

From a reference ad

In References, open an ad and press Make an ad from this. You choose how closely to follow it, each one step deeper than the last:

You chooseWhat the writer is sentWhat comes back
Loose inspirationOnly the idea of the ad: what it is and the 1 reason it worksYour own script on that idea
Keep what makes it workWhy it worked, its best parts and what was weak. Not its order of stepsYour own script, built on what made it work
Recreate it for my brandThe ad step by step: what happens, and what each step doesEach step redone with what does that job for your brand. A step that does not fit is changed or dropped, and the script says which

No way copies another brand's ad: its words, claims, people and setting are never carried over.

For your own ad made in a new format with its words unchanged, tick "This is my own ad".

Written for where the buyer is

A concept says where its buyer is (unaware, problem aware, solution aware, product aware, most aware) and how the ad argues (a plain claim, a bigger claim, a mechanism, a sharper mechanism, identity). The writer is sent a short brief for each: where to open, what the ad's work is, when the product arrives and how hard to ask. Change the stage on the concept and the next script is written differently. The briefs are in Inspect, Skills and prompts, buyer-stage.

Scripts planned from the gaps

Plan from the gaps at the top of the Scripts screen opens Coverage (chapter 18). It plans 6, 12 or 24 scripts that differ on purpose: each is 1 buyer, 1 stage of awareness, 1 way of arguing and 1 format, picked from what the account is missing. It also writes variants of an ad that is working.

When a script is ready

Approve it, then Make assets to send it to Production (chapter 8).

To send a script to someone outside the app, use Copy (the whole script as text) or the PDF, Word and Text downloads at the top of the script. A download is the saved version: save your edits first.
