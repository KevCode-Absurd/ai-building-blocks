Calculate what a visitor is worth before judging whether clicks are expensive

Connect conversion, order value and contribution to an acquisition-cost ceiling. Keep first-order economics separate from mature-cohort repeat value, and replace universal revenue-per-visitor targets with the brand’s actual inputs.

When this strategy makes sense

Use when reviewing paid acquisition with reliable spend, traffic, order and variable-cost data. If repeat-purchase evidence is immature, begin with first-order economics and mark future value as uncertain.

In this guide

  2. Calculate revenue per visitor correctly

Put the inputs on one denominator

A click price is neither cheap nor expensive in isolation. Its usefulness depends on how often the resulting visitor buys and how much contribution the purchase leaves. Start with a clearly defined traffic population and period. Keep spend, arriving visits, orders and revenue aligned. Exclude unrelated channels when evaluating a specific paid campaign.

Clicks and landing-page visits are not interchangeable. Some clicks never become recorded sessions, and measurement systems can define visits differently. If the conversion rate uses landing-page visits, compare it with cost per arriving visit. If it uses clicks, use cost per click. Reconcile the difference before treating a calculated ceiling as precise.

Calculate revenue per visitor correctly

Use conversion rate as a decimal. Revenue per visitor equals conversion rate multiplied by average order value. Revenue per 100 visitors equals that result multiplied by 100. With 3 orders per 100 visitors and an average order value of 100, the calculation is 0.03×100=3 per visitor, or 300 per 100 visitors.

The number 300 is not a universal scalability requirement. A category with lower traffic costs and stronger contribution may work below it; an expensive acquisition channel with weak margins can fail above it. Revenue is only the first layer of the calculation.

Replace the revenue ceiling with contribution

Define contribution before advertising as net order revenue less the variable costs included in the analysis: product cost, fulfillment, payment fees, relevant shipping subsidy, discounts already reflected in net revenue and an appropriate allowance for returns. Avoid subtracting a discount twice. State which costs are missing so the output is not mistaken for net business profit.

In the hypothetical example, each 100 order leaves 40 before ads. Three orders therefore leave 120 across 100 visitors, or 1.20 per visitor. Paying 3 for each arriving visitor costs 300 and leaves a 180 loss before fixed costs. Paying 1 costs 100 and leaves 20 across the same 100 visitors. Both cases have the same revenue per visitor; their commercial results differ.

The first-order acquisition-cost ceiling per customer is the pre-ad contribution per first order. The corresponding visitor ceiling is conversion rate multiplied by that contribution. Treat this as a simplified ceiling, not a recommended bid: uncertainty, cost changes and desired contribution can require additional headroom.

Add repeat value only when it is observed

Select acquisition cohorts old enough to have completed the intended observation window. A customer acquired last week cannot contribute a fully observed twelve-month history. Include all eligible acquired customers in the denominator, including those who never repeat, rather than averaging only repeat purchasers.

Calculate contribution from later orders using their actual net revenue and variable costs. Deduct relevant retention costs where the analysis intends to include them. Email and SMS are not literally free acquisition replacements. Also check whether the cohort's product, offer and customer mix resemble the traffic being acquired now.

A hypothetical customer cohort might average 40 first-order contribution and 80 additional contribution over twelve months. At 60 acquisition cost, the first order leaves a 20 deficit and the observed total leaves 60 before any excluded costs. That does not mean the business receives the 80 immediately or that a new cohort will behave identically. Record the timing of recovery and a conservative scenario.

Diagnose the lever before changing spend

If contribution per visitor is weak, examine conversion, order composition and variable costs separately. Increasing average order value with a deep discount can lower contribution. Selling a large quantity of one replenishable item can raise the first order while delaying or reducing repeat demand. Compare the complete use and purchase cycle before celebrating the larger basket.

Use a worksheet with traffic definition, conversion rate, net order value, contribution per order, contribution per visitor, acquisition cost, cohort maturity and recovery timing. The decision is whether the observed economics support the proposed acquisition level. When inputs are incomplete, improve measurement and bound the exposure; do not manufacture certainty by substituting an industry benchmark for the brand's own evidence.

Your implementation checklist

Put the method to work

  1. 1

    Align visits, orders and revenue to the same population and window.

  2. 2

    Calculate revenue and pre-ad contribution per visitor.

  3. 3

    Compare the contribution ceiling with actual traffic costs on the same denominator.

  4. 4

    Estimate repeat contribution from mature comparable cohorts.

  5. 5

    Set an explicit decision rule that accounts for uncertainty and time to recover acquisition cost.

Worked application

Hypothetical: 100 paid landing-page visits produce 3 orders at 100 each. Revenue per visitor is 3. If contribution before ads is 40 per order, contribution per visitor is 1.20. Paying 3 per arriving visitor consumes all revenue and loses 180 before fixed costs; it is not break-even profit.

Measure and decide

Contribution after acquisition cost per visitor and per acquired customer, with clearly defined cost coverage and observation window. For repeat value, use mature cohort contribution and the observed time needed to recover acquisition cost.

Where this can fail

Revenue divided by ad spend is not profit. Do not multiply a decimal conversion rate by100 twice, confuse clicks with sessions, or justify present losses using an unobserved lifetime-value forecast.
