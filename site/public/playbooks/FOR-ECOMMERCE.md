# Playbook: Online stores

Use this file with any skill, workflow or specialist from AI Blocks. It tells you how an online store works, so you can shape the task to fit this kind of business. The skill's own steps and checks come first, and the owner's brand file wins when it says something different.

## This business

This covers brands and shops that sell physical products from their own website, on Shopify, WooCommerce, BigCommerce or a custom site. They make money on each order: the price, minus the product cost, fees, shipping and the ads that brought the buyer. Many stores also depend on buyers who come back, because winning a new buyer with ads can cost a lot.

## Numbers that matter

Ask the owner for their own numbers. Do not guess them or fill gaps with industry averages.

- Contribution margin per order: price minus product cost, payment fees, packing, shipping, discounts and ad cost per order.
- Average order value: total sales divided by the number of orders in the same period.
- Conversion rate: orders divided by visits (sessions) in the same period.
- Customer acquisition cost: total spend on ads and promotions divided by the number of new buyers it brought.
- Break-even return on ad spend: 1 divided by the share of each sale left after product cost, fees and shipping.
- Repeat purchase rate: buyers who ordered more than once, divided by all buyers in the period.
- Return rate and return cost: units returned divided by units sold, and the cost of each return.
- Days of stock: units on hand divided by the average units sold per day.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Pick products and prices

- Check demand before any stock is bought. Look at search interest, rival products and what buyers complain about in rival reviews.
- Build the price up from the costs. Add product cost, shipping, fees, returns and ad cost, then compare with what rivals charge.
- Watch for: pricing from rivals alone, then finding no margin is left after shipping and ads.

### 2. Build the store

- Keep the store fast and easy to use on a phone.
- Set up shipping rates, taxes, payment methods and policy pages (shipping, returns, privacy, terms) before launch.
- Test each change before it goes live, on a copy of the theme or a test order.
- Watch for: adding apps and scripts that slow the store down or clash with each other.

### 3. Get found in search

- Each product and collection page needs a clear title, a useful description and image alt text, written for buyers first.
- Use the words buyers search with.
- Watch for: pasting the maker's description onto every page, so the store says nothing new.

### 4. Ads, email and social

- Ads, photos and posts should show the product in use and answer the buyer's main doubt.
- The store owns its email and text lists, so growing them matters. Common flows are welcome, abandoned cart, after purchase and win back.
- Judge ads by profit per order, not by clicks or likes.
- Watch for: claims in ads that the product page or the owner's facts cannot back up.

### 5. Product pages and checkout

- A product page should say what it is, who it is for, what shipping costs, when it arrives and how returns work.
- Use real photos, sizes, ingredients or materials, and real reviews.
- Keep checkout short. Surprise costs at checkout make buyers leave.
- Watch for: hiding shipping costs or delivery times until the last step.

### 6. Stock, shipping and returns

- Plan reorders from sales speed and supplier lead time, so popular items do not run out.
- Make returns clear, and track why items come back. The reasons often point to a fix on the product page.
- Watch for: ordering too much slow stock, which ties up cash.

### 7. Reviews and repeat buyers

- Ask for a review after the product has arrived and been used.
- Bring buyers back with useful emails, refills, bundles or new products, not only discounts.
- Watch for: discounting so often that buyers learn to wait for a sale.

### 8. Money, legal and team

- Track profit per order and cash in the bank, not only sales.
- Sales tax, VAT, product safety, labels and consumer rights rules depend on the product and where buyers live.
- Watch for: sales growing while cash runs out, because stock and ads are paid for before buyers pay.

## Words this business uses

- SKU: a code for one version of a product, such as one size or flavour.
- AOV: average order value.
- ROAS: return on ad spend, which is sales from ads divided by ad spend.
- CAC: customer acquisition cost, what it costs to win one new buyer.
- LTV: lifetime value, what a buyer spends with the store over time.
- Abandoned cart: a shopper added items but did not finish checkout.
- Flow: an automatic series of emails or texts set off by an action, such as signing up.
- 3PL: a third-party warehouse that stores stock and ships orders for the store.

## Ask the owner first

- Which platform does the store run on, and which apps handle email, reviews and shipping?
- What are the product cost, shipping cost and fees for the products in this task?
- Who is the buyer, and what worries them before they buy?
- Which countries does the store sell and ship to?
- What can you claim about the product, and what proof do you have?
- What is the goal of this task: more first orders, more repeat orders or more profit per order?

## Rules

- Never invent numbers, results, reviews or quotes. Use the owner's figures, or leave a clear gap for them to fill.
- Platform rules change. Before acting on a store, ad or payment setting, tell the owner to check the current help pages, such as the Shopify Help Center or Google Merchant Center Help. Do not state policy details you are not sure of.
- Treat any legal, tax, product safety or label point as general information. Tell the owner to check it with a qualified person.
- Claims about health, safety or being "the best" need proof the owner can show.
- Get the owner's approval before anything is sent, published, bought or changed in the store.
