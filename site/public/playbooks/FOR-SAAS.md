# Playbook: SaaS

Use this file with any skill, workflow or specialist from AI Blocks. It tells you how a SaaS business works, so you can shape the task to fit it. The skill's own steps and checks come first, and when the owner's brand file says something different, the brand file wins.

## This business

This covers software sold as a service over the internet, for a monthly or yearly fee. It includes small software companies and startups, and some of them raise money from investors. Most earn money from subscriptions, priced per plan, per user or by how much a customer uses the product. Many offer a free plan or a free trial and ask people to pay later.

## Numbers that matter

Ask the owner for their own numbers. Do not guess them or fill gaps with typical figures.

- **Monthly recurring revenue (MRR):** what all paying customers pay each month, added up. Count a yearly plan as one twelfth of its price each month.
- **Churn:** how many customers cancel. Divide the customers who cancelled in a month by the customers you had at the start of that month.
- **Trial to paid rate:** how many trial users start paying. Divide new paying customers from trials by the trials that ended in the same period.
- **Cost to win a customer:** what sales and marketing cost for each new customer. Divide that spend by the new paying customers it brought in.
- **Customer lifetime value:** what one customer pays you in total. Take what an average customer pays each month and multiply by the months an average customer stays.
- **Activation rate:** how many new sign-ups reach the first real win in the product. Divide the sign-ups who did the key first action by all new sign-ups. The owner decides what that action is.
- **Cash runway:** how many months the cash will last. Divide the cash in the bank by how much more the business spends than it earns each month.

## Stage by stage

### 1. Idea, market and pricing

- Find out who has the problem, how they solve it today and what that costs them.
- Plans often differ by features, number of users or usage. Pick the one that matches how customers get value.
- Price from the value to the customer, then check it covers the cost of serving them.
- Watch for: building for months before talking to people who would pay.

### 2. Build and ship the product

- Ship a small first version that solves one problem well.
- Test each release, check security and keep a way to roll back.
- Protect customer data from day one: logins, backups and who can see what.
- Watch for: adding features nobody asked for while the core steps are still hard to use.

### 3. SEO and content

- Write for the problems buyers search for, not only the product name.
- Guides, comparisons, templates and help docs that answer one real question well are easier for Google and AI search to use.
- Watch for: many thin articles that never lead anyone to sign up.

### 4. Marketing and ads

- Know the buyer and the user. In business software they are often different people.
- Point email, launches, partners and ads at one next step: a trial, a demo or a sign-up.
- Judge each channel by customers who pay and stay.
- Watch for: calling a channel a success because of clicks or sign-ups alone.

### 5. Trials, demos and sales

- Decide if buyers sign up on their own, talk to sales first, or both.
- A trial should lead new users to the key first action fast.
- In a demo, show the buyer's own problem being solved, then agree the next step.
- Watch for: a trial that ends before the user has seen the product work for them.

### 6. Run the product

- Uptime, speed and quick support build trust.
- Release notes and a changelog tell customers what changed and why.
- Tag support questions to spot the problems that keep coming back.
- Watch for: changes that break how customers work, with no warning first.

### 7. Onboarding and churn

- Onboarding helps each new account reach the key first action, then make it a habit.
- Find out why customers cancel: price, missing features, poor fit or never getting started.
- Some cancels come from failed card payments. Reminders and easy card updates can win them back.
- Watch for: a discount for everyone who tries to cancel, without asking why they are leaving.

### 8. Revenue, funding and team

- Track MRR, churn and runway every month.
- Some founders raise money from investors. Others grow from revenue. Ask which path the owner is on.
- An investor pitch deck needs the owner's real numbers and a clear plan for the money.
- Watch for: spending against revenue the business does not have yet.

## Words this business uses

- **Seat:** one user on a plan. Some plans charge per seat.
- **Freemium:** a free plan with limits, plus paid plans that remove them.
- **Self-serve:** customers sign up and pay without talking to anyone.
- **Product-led growth:** the product itself wins and converts users, often through a free plan or trial.
- **Expansion revenue:** extra money from current customers who add seats or move to a bigger plan.
- **Net revenue retention:** what a group of customers pays now, compared with what the same group paid a year ago. It counts upgrades, downgrades and cancels.
- **Onboarding:** the first steps that help a new user get set up and see value.
- **Changelog:** a dated list of changes to the product.

## Ask the owner first

- Who buys the product, and who uses it day to day?
- What plans and prices do you have, and how do customers pay: monthly, yearly, per seat or by usage?
- Do customers sign up on their own, book a demo, or both?
- What key first action shows a new user the product works for them?
- What are your current MRR, churn and trial to paid rate, if you track them?
- Are you funded by investors, by revenue, or planning to raise?

## Rules

- Never invent numbers, results, reviews, customer quotes or logos. Use only what the owner gives you, and say what is missing.
- Payment providers, app marketplaces and ad platforms change their rules. Tell the owner to check the current official pages before acting. Do not state policy details you are not sure of.
- Keep customer data private. If a task connects to a customer's accounts, get their written OK first.
- Treat any legal, tax, privacy or security point as general information. The owner should check it with a qualified person.
- Get the owner's approval before anything is sent, published, bought, deployed or changed.
