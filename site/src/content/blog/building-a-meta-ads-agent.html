The short version

We are building an AI media buyer for Meta advertising, designed for direct-to-consumer brands and the performance agencies that manage their accounts. The agent reads the ad account alongside the store's own sales records and works out which campaigns actually make a profit after ad costs. It then proposes pauses, budget changes and tests that a person must approve before anything goes live. Each media buyer can develop their own version of the agent, with their own rules and limits, because experienced buyers rarely manage accounts in exactly the same way.

This article explains, layer by layer, how the agent is being constructed, and it distinguishes carefully between the components that are finished and those that are still waiting. It begins with what media buyers at a performance agency told us they needed, then describes the seven layers of the agent, from the data it collects to the approvals it requests, and then explains how we evaluate the agent against plain Claude before trusting it with a real account. The final sections describe how a media buyer converts the base agent into a personal version, and which weaknesses we already know about.

All of the work so far has been tested on a single pilot account belonging to a jewellery brand, so every figure from that account appears here only in general terms. The pack currently contains 285 automated tests, and every one of them passes.

Why we started with Meta media buying

Our long-term goal is a library of verified AI agents, each shaped by people who have done a particular job for many years. We needed a specific starting point, and Meta media buying was the natural choice because it is the work our founder does himself. The judgement built into the agent therefore comes from direct experience and not from secondhand descriptions of the job.

Media buying also possesses a characteristic that makes it unusually suitable for an agent, since the feedback arrives every day in measurable numbers. A campaign either earned more than it cost or it did not, and the evidence is recorded in the advertising account and in the store's order history. That daily scoreboard makes it possible to verify whether the agent's recommendations would have helped, which is considerably harder in disciplines where results take months to appear or depend largely on taste.

The underlying problem is also concrete and expensive. A media buyer at an agency typically manages between one and five brand accounts, and a substantial proportion of every working day disappears into extracting numbers, assembling reports and constructing pivot tables before any genuine decision is made. Automating that routine preparation is the most direct way an agent can give an agency additional capacity without requiring additional hires.

What media buyers told us they needed

Before constructing the later layers, we interviewed ten people at a direct-to-consumer performance marketing agency, including both of its co-founders and eight of its media buyers. We followed those individual conversations with a group call lasting more than an hour, during which we demonstrated the complete agent to the team and discussed each of their requests in detail. The agency will evaluate the agent on its own accounts in the coming weeks.

The first theme concerned time. Daily checks, data extraction and reporting consume the hours that buyers would prefer to spend thinking strategically about each account, so the agent needed to absorb that routine preparation before attempting anything more ambitious.

The second theme concerned profitability. Clients frequently evaluate an agency on return on ad spend, whereas business owners care about the money that remains after advertising, and a respectable ROAS can still conceal losses once product costs, shipping and returns are included in the calculation.

The third theme concerned discipline. Unprofitable campaigns tend to continue for too long while successful ones are often scaled too aggressively, so the buyers wanted consistent pause and scaling rules applied across every account, together with the freedom to override those rules whenever they could justify an exception.

The fourth theme concerned control. Nobody was willing to surrender an account on the first day, approvals needed to happen inside the messaging applications the team already uses, and the agent would have to earn additional independence gradually, one category of action at a time.

The fifth theme concerned creative. Because Meta's delivery system now relies heavily on the creative itself to find buyers, the team wanted to understand which angles, hooks and formats perform for which audiences and placements, and they wanted Meta's automatic creative enhancements kept firmly under their control. Taken together, these requests define an agent that performs the daily routine, follows explicit rules, and asks permission before it spends or moves any money.

Layer 1: collecting reliable data

Every conclusion the agent reaches depends on the data it reads, so the first layer is a small warehouse kept separately for each brand. The agent pulls daily campaign, ad set and ad results through Meta's official Ads connector, which the owner signs in to without sharing a password or a developer key. It also collects placement data, audience breakdowns, pixel diagnostics and Meta's own quality rankings. A read-only API token remains available as an alternative for teams that prefer one.

On the store side, the owner supplies an order export together with the store's own monthly sales report, which serves as the authoritative record of revenue. On the pilot account, the agent's revenue calculations matched that report within one percent in all twenty-four months examined. The warehouse itself is a DuckDB database populated with the open-source loading tool dlt, and a privacy setting determines whether the agent may examine individual order records or only aggregated summaries.

Two regular checks keep this data accurate over time. Every night the agent refreshes the last seven days, because Meta keeps crediting purchases for several days after a click. Every week and month it compares the warehouse's totals with Meta's own totals, and it pulls any period again when the gap is more than one percent. On the pilot, the monthly totals matched an independent extraction from Meta to the paisa, and repeating the synchronisation introduced no duplicate records.

The agent also drafts a sales calendar from campaign names and ad copy, since a campaign named after a sale usually marks sale days. The owner then confirms or corrects that draft. Results during sales differ sharply from results on ordinary days, so this calendar affects almost every later judgement the agent makes.

Layer 2: evaluating every campaign on profit

The profitability layer is ordinary arithmetic written in Python. No language model is involved, so the same inputs always give the same results. It works out each brand's break-even ROAS from the brand's own margins, shipping costs, payment fees, refunds and undelivered cash-on-delivery orders. It then reports profit after advertising for every campaign and every product category.

Product costs come in three tiers, and every figure the agent reports names the tier it came from. Exact costs for each product are used wherever the brand can supply them. Category margins cover products without an exact cost, and an estimated store-wide margin is used only when nothing better exists.

The owner can edit any cost on the console. Each edit applies from the date it is made, so last month's profit never changes silently. Restating past figures is a separate, deliberate action that is recorded in the log. Brands whose costs follow a commodity price, such as gold for a jeweller, can supply a price index so that past orders are re-costed and margins are compared at one price level.

On the pilot account, this layer altered the discussion immediately. The ROAS that Meta reported appeared respectable on its own terms, yet it sat considerably below the account's break-even level, which meant the business was spending most of its gross profit on advertising before paying any of its overheads.

Layer 3: verifying the tracking before trusting it

An agent that acts on faulty tracking will make confident and costly mistakes, so a tracking check runs before any analysis is trusted. It examines how accurately the pixel and the Conversions API identify buyers, whether duplicate events are removed correctly, how many purchases the server reports compared with the orders the store actually received, and whether testing or staging environments are sending events into the live pixel. The results are combined into a tracking score out of one hundred, displayed on the console's Signals screen.

On the pilot, purchase matching was strong and duplicate events were handled correctly. Even so, the check found two problems that a person reading the dashboard would probably have missed. The server was reporting about two purchase events for every purchase seen in the browser, and a staging copy of the website was sending events into the live pixel. Both issues went to the owner to fix.

Layer 4: identifying what drives results

Once the data and profit figures are reliable, the agent looks for the patterns an experienced media buyer would look for, at a speed that manual analysis cannot match. It divides results by date, weekday, promotional and ordinary days, placement, age and gender, creative format, and new versus returning customers. It also runs single and multiple regressions at the level of the day, the ad set, the individual ad and the video, reporting accuracy on held-out data so that a pattern which merely fits historical results is easy to recognise.

A scenario grid then asks how the conclusions would change if only sixty to one hundred percent of the sales Meta claims were truly caused by the ads. No lift test has yet measured that share on the pilot. Any recommendation that survives only when Meta's attribution is accepted at face value is explicitly labelled as fragile.

An early-warning model, which is a compact logistic regression trained on the brand's own previous launches, identifies new ads that are likely to fail by their seventh day. The model may be used only after it satisfies demanding thresholds on held-out launches: at least ninety percent precision, an improvement of three points over the base rate, and no more than a fifth of successful ads incorrectly classified as failures. On the pilot it achieved ninety-eight percent precision across eighty-one launches, although only eight of those launches were successful, so we regard the result as encouraging rather than conclusive.

The newest addition is a set of blindspot checks that run every day for any brand. They look for problems that ROAS and revenue tend to hide. These include a falling number of customers hidden by rising prices, entry-level products slowly disappearing from the sales mix, and ad sets that fail to exclude existing customers. They also cover retargeting audiences that are almost empty, orders that carry no campaign tags, and revenue targets with no profit floor beneath them.

For creative analysis, the agent classifies each ad by type using its name and copy, an approach that covered approximately ninety-seven percent of spending on the pilot, and it applies automated image and video tagging to the highest-spending ads. Video measures such as hook rate and hold rate feed into the regressions. On the pilot, a stronger opening hook increased the click-through rate, yet the video measures did not predict ROAS, which illustrates why such questions need measurement before anyone acts on intuition.

Layer 5: rules, expert knowledge and the owner's habits

The agent draws on a library of media-buying principles and case studies. It retrieves the relevant ones for each question and adjusts the weight of each principle according to its measured track record. A separate process pins the version of Meta's API in use and watches Meta's policy and developer documents for changes that could affect the agent.

Above that knowledge sit the rules the buyers requested. By default, a campaign is paused when its fourteen-day ROAS falls below the lower of its break-even level and three quarters of the account's seven-day ROAS, or when it spends three times the target cost per purchase without generating a sale. New launches receive a grace period, nothing is paused during a confirmed promotion, and budgets increase by no more than twenty percent at a time. These defaults are deliberately flexible, so a buyer can override any of them provided they record a justification.

Beneath those flexible defaults sit mandatory rules the agent cannot violate, and automated validators examine every proposal against them before the owner sees it. One example is the sale-mirage restriction, which prevents the agent from scaling an ad outside a promotion when that ad succeeds only during promotions; on the pilot, the validators correctly blocked all twenty-three test cases of this kind. A compliance inspection also reviews live ads for prohibited claims and insecure links, and it identified seven live ads on the pilot that required attention.

Two further components capture what the owner knows from experience. A structured founder interview works through real decisions from the account's history, and the owner's answers become confirmed rules for that particular brand. A bias auditor examines the account's history for recurring habits worth correcting, and on the pilot it found that roughly three in four launches never recovered their spending, that pauses occurred a median of about eight days later than the evidence justified, and that most budget increases exceeded twenty percent in a single step. The auditor drafts a safeguard for each habit, and none of them takes effect until the owner accepts it.

Layer 6: approvals, the autonomy dial and safe changes

Every proposal, approval, rejection, rule check and outcome is recorded in a decision ledger as an append-only event, and each event is cryptographically linked to the previous one through a hash chain. If anyone alters the history, the chain no longer verifies, so the record of what the agent proposed and who approved it remains trustworthy over time.

Approval requests arrive as cards in Slack, Microsoft Teams, WhatsApp or email, and each card describes the proposed change, the reasoning behind it, the money at stake and the method for reversing it. Email additionally supports batch responses, allowing an owner to approve several items and reject others in a single reply. These channels are fully constructed, but until the owner connects one of them, the approval cards remain queued in an outbox.

An autonomy setting decides, for each type of action, whether the agent only watches, asks every time, or acts within set limits and reports afterwards. Every new brand begins at "ask every time." The agent can pause campaigns, adjust budgets, launch drafted campaigns and move a campaign through a ladder of cost caps and bid caps. After each change, it reads the result back from Meta to confirm the change took effect as intended. Default launch settings enable tracking parameters, disable Meta's automatic creative enhancements and exclude weak placements unless the owner decides otherwise. Simulation mode is enabled by default, and no live change has yet been made on the pilot account.

Layer 7: testing the agent before trusting it

We evaluate the agent in four complementary ways, because each method detects mistakes that the others would probably miss.

A walk-forward simulator replays the account month by month. The agent's settings are learned only from earlier months, so it cannot benefit from hindsight. The best result with hindsight is shown separately, as a ceiling and never as an achievement. The owner selects a business goal, such as revenue, gross profit, ROAS or order volume, with weights and minimum levels, and the simulator finds the settings that best serve it. The pilot owner put revenue first, and the replay showed about 8.5 percent more revenue than the existing rules would have produced. That goal does not yet include a profit floor, which one of the blindspot checks reports.

A golden evaluation suite contains fifty fixed cases, comprising twenty-eight rule and calculation cases that must always pass and twenty-two genuine historical decision days labelled according to what subsequently happened. The current pack passes every mandatory case and scores forty-two of fifty overall.

Versioning with replay treats every modification to the agent as a new, frozen version. Before a brand adopts it, the new version is replayed across the brand's ten most recent decision days alongside the version already running, and it is promoted only with a documented reason, while reverting to an earlier version takes a single step and alters no brand data. On the pilot, one candidate version was withheld because it performed worse during the replay.

A question benchmark presents twenty genuine questions from owners and media buyers to both the agent and plain Claude Opus, using identical data, and two Claude models evaluate the answers blind across six criteria. In the first round, plain Claude won thirty of forty verdicts, which showed us that the agent's answers contained too much jargon and too many outdated figures. After corrections, the agent won thirty-four of forty, although we report that improvement cautiously, since the plain answers in the second round were the original texts evaluated again, the judging is comparative, and Claude is assessing Claude. The most important evaluation is still ahead: the owner's own blind grading, in which the agent must outperform plain Claude by thirty percent across at least fifteen questions.

How a media buyer develops their own version

We build the base agent and give it to media buyers, and each buyer then develops a personal version based on their own judgement. That approach comes straight from the interviews. Every buyer manages accounts a little differently, with different thresholds for pausing, different appetites for scaling and different views about which placements to trust. A single fixed agent would satisfy nobody for long.

A buyer can adjust the settings file, changing values such as the launch grace period, the pause threshold, the scaling step or the sale hold. These changes always stay within limits that the hard rules protect. A buyer can also add personal rules, which the validators then enforce like any other, and can complete the founder interview for each brand, so that each brand's facts and the owner's preferences become part of that brand's records.

Rejections also shape the agent. When a buyer turns down a proposal and records a reason, the learning process converts that reason into a single drafted change, replays it across twelve months of history, and applies it only after the buyer approves. A second learning process evaluates every pause and budget increase after seven, fourteen and thirty days, so the influence of each rule gradually follows its actual results, and over time a buyer's version reflects both their judgement and the evidence from their own accounts.

Agency mode allows a single installation to serve many brands, providing a brand selector and a separation barrier that prevents one brand's data from reaching another brand's analysis. The agent operates inside the buyer's own environment using the buyer's own Claude subscription, so no separate API key is required. While the buyers work with it, we observe which settings they modify and why, because those modifications reveal what the base agent still lacks.

What is not finished yet

We would rather describe the limitations directly than allow the list of features to suggest that the work is complete.

The agent has been calibrated on a single brand, so we cannot yet confirm that its default thresholds remain appropriate for other product categories and price points. No holdout or lift experiment has been conducted on the pilot, which means the proportion of sales that Meta genuinely causes remains unknown and most of the analysis still rests on correlation. Orders on the pilot carry no campaign identifiers, so campaigns are evaluated at category or store-wide margins rather than exact ones, and only website orders are included, with Google Ads spending by campaign not yet incorporated.

No live change has been made to the account, and the first one depends on the owner connecting an approval channel. The owner's blind grading, which serves as the gate for the current phase, has not yet taken place, and the downloadable pack, with three installation options and a guided onboarding process, remains the final planned phase of the project.

Three prompts you can use today

You can start judging your own account the way the agent does without installing anything. These prompts work in ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini with your own exports, and they follow the same steps the agent takes.

Prompt: Work out your break-even ROAS

You are a careful e-commerce analyst. Work out my break-even ROAS for Meta ads.

My numbers (fill in what you know, write "unknown" for the rest):
- Average order value:
- Product cost as a share of the selling price, overall or by category:
- Shipping and packaging cost per order:
- Payment gateway fee (%):
- Share of orders refunded or returned:
- Share of cash-on-delivery orders that are never delivered, and the cost of each one:

Steps:
1. Work out gross profit per order after every cost above. Show each line.
2. Break-even ROAS = average order value / gross profit per order. Show the sum.
3. For every "unknown", use a cautious assumption, label it ASSUMED and say how much the answer moves if it is wrong.
4. Tell me which single number I should measure properly first, and why.

Prompt: Find your pause and scale habits in an export

You are auditing a Meta ad account for decision habits.

I will paste a campaign export with these columns by day: date, campaign name, status, daily budget, spend, purchases, purchase value.
My break-even ROAS is: [number]

Find and count:
1. Campaigns that ran for 7 or more days in a row below break-even before they were paused. How many days late was each pause, and what did those days cost?
2. Budget increases larger than 20% in one step. What happened to cost per purchase in the 7 days after each one?
3. Launches that never reached break-even over their whole life, and the total they spent.

Then write three rules I could follow from tomorrow, each one testable with this same export. Do not guess numbers that are not in the data.

[paste export]

Prompt: Write the rules file for your own version

Help me write my own rules file for an AI media buyer, so it runs accounts the way I do.

Interview me one question at a time. Cover:
- how long a new campaign gets before I judge it, and on what number;
- when I pause, and the exceptions I always make (sales, launches, seasons);
- how fast I scale a winner, and what stops me;
- which placements, audiences and automatic Meta features I trust or switch off;
- what the agent must always ask me before doing.

After the interview, write the rules as a numbered list. Mark each one SOFT (I can override it with a reason) or HARD (never broken). Add one line per rule saying how to check it was followed.

Questions people ask

Does the agent change my ads on its own?

The agent changes nothing unless you allow it. Every new brand begins at "ask every time," every proposed change arrives as a card you approve or reject, and dry-run mode is on by default. Later, you can let the agent act on its own within limits for one type of action, such as small budget increases, while it keeps asking about everything else.

Does it need my Meta password or a developer key?

The agent reads your account through Meta's official Ads connector, which you authorise yourself, so it never needs your password. A read-only API token is an optional alternative for teams that prefer one, and sending profit data back to Meta is a separate add-on that the pilot account decided not to use.

Where does my data live?

The agent operates inside your own environment, using your own Claude subscription, and it keeps each brand's data in a separate warehouse. In agency mode, a separation barrier prevents information from one brand from reaching another brand's analysis.

Why judge campaigns on profit when Meta reports ROAS?

ROAS ignores what the products cost, what shipping and returns cost, and how many orders are never delivered, so two campaigns with identical ROAS can leave very different amounts of money in the business. A ROAS that appears healthy can therefore still sit below the break-even level that the business actually requires.

When can I try it?

The agency we interviewed is evaluating the agent on its own accounts first, and the downloadable pack will follow once that evaluation is complete. Until then, the three prompts above follow the same analytical sequence the agent uses, applied to your own exports.
