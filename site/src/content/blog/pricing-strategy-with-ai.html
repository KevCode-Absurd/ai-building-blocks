How to price a product, in short

Here is how to price a product in four moves, starting with your costs. First, add up every cost of one sale, so you know your floor, which is the lowest price you can survive on. Second, find out what the product is worth to your buyer and what they would pay elsewhere, which gives you a rough ceiling. Third, choose a price between the two that leaves a healthy profit, and fourth, test it on a small scale before you change it for everyone.

AI helps most with the arithmetic and the "what if" questions behind every pricing decision. Your AI can lay out your costs, model three prices side by side and show how many sales you could lose before a price rise stops paying for itself. What it can't tell you is what buyers will actually pay, because only real buyers can answer that.

That's the core of a pricing strategy for small business, and this guide walks through each step in turn. It includes two prompts, a cost table to copy, a check for tiered pricing and a method for testing a price increase. All figures marked "example" are invented for this article, so replace them with your own.

Step 1: Know your costs and your margin

Your price has to cover every cost of a sale, not just the product itself. It's easy to count what an item costs to make and forget the fees, delivery, returns and advertising, even though together those can absorb most of your profit.

Two terms often get confused at this stage, and the difference matters. Margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price, while markup is profit as a percentage of the cost. A product that costs $10 and sells for $20 therefore has a 50% margin but a 100% markup. Use margin when you discuss pricing, and always say which measure you mean.

Step 2: Find out what it's worth to your buyer, and what rivals charge

Value is what the product does for the buyer, described in their own terms rather than in yours. A candle might represent an impressive gift, a relaxing evening or a scent that lasts for weeks. To find it, ask what problem the product solves and what the buyer would do without it.

Where to find value

  • Reviews and messages show the words buyers use when they're pleased, and phrases like "worth every penny" or "lasts for weeks" tell you exactly what they believe they're paying for.
  • Short calls with five recent buyers are worth the time, because you can ask why they bought, what else they considered and what would make them stop buying.
  • The alternative they would use instead might be a rival brand, a cheaper version or simply doing nothing, and each of these sets a different ceiling.

How to check competitor prices fairly

Choose three to five products that a typical buyer would genuinely compare with yours. For each, note the public price, the size or quantity, the delivery cost and what's included. Then compare like for like, such as price per 100g, per hour or per month. Record the date you checked each price, because competitor prices change frequently.

Competitor prices are a useful reference point rather than a rule to follow. If you are cheaper, find out whether buyers see your price as a bargain or as a sign of lower quality. If you're more expensive, make sure your product page explains the difference clearly. Our guide to competitor analysis with AI has a complete method and a comparison table.

Step 3: Should you offer good, better and best tiers?

Tiered pricing gives buyers a choice of price instead of a simple yes or no. A "good" option sets a low entry point, while a "best" option captures the buyers who want more. The "better" option in the middle is often the one you hope most people will choose.

Tiers work when each step adds something that buyers clearly and immediately value. They fail when the differences between the steps are confusing, or when the top tier costs you more to deliver than it earns.

  • Each tier is designed for a clearly defined type of buyer, described in one line.
  • Each step up adds something a buyer would notice, such as more quantity, faster delivery or extra help.
  • Every tier remains profitable after all of its delivery and support costs.
  • A typical buyer can understand the difference between the tiers in under ten seconds.
  • There are three tiers or fewer, so the options stay easy to compare.

Step 4: Use AI to model price scenarios

This is the step where your AI saves you the most time. Give it your cost table, your price ideas and what you know about your buyers. Then ask it to calculate the profit at each price, and how many sales each price needs to match today's total profit.

Always ask it to show every calculation, because AI can make arithmetic mistakes and a visible calculation is easy to check. For large tables, use a spreadsheet as well, and our guide on analysing a spreadsheet with AI shows how to combine the two.

Prompt: Model three prices and the sales each one needs

You are a pricing analyst for a small business. Help me compare price options. Show every sum so I can check it.

Product: [what it is and who buys it].
Current price: [price]. Orders per month at that price: [number].
Costs per order: [list each one: product, delivery, fees, ads, returns].
Fees that change with price: [for example, payment fee of X% of price].
Prices I'm thinking about: [price A], [price B], [price C].
What buyers say about value: [paste a few review lines or notes].
Competitor prices, compared like for like: [list with dates checked].

Steps:
1. Work out profit per order and margin at the current price and each new price.
2. For each new price, work out how many orders a month I'd need to match today's total profit.
3. Turn that into the share of orders I could lose (or must gain) and still break even.
4. Say which price looks strongest and why, using only my figures and buyer notes.
5. List the assumptions that matter most, and what would change your view.

Rules:
- Use only my figures. If one is missing, ask me before you continue.
- Don't predict how buyers will react. Say what I'd need to test.
- Give the results as a table: price, profit per order, margin, orders needed, change in orders allowed.

What the sums look like

Using the example candle above, say you move the price from $30 to $33. Payment fees increase slightly, by 3% of the extra $3, so profit per order goes from $9.10 to about $12.01. To keep the same total profit, you need about 76% of today's orders ($9.10 divided by $12.01). In other words, you could lose up to about 24% of orders before the rise stops paying. These are example figures, included only to illustrate the calculation.

That percentage is a guide rather than a forecast, because it tells you what to monitor during a test rather than what will actually happen.

Step 5: Test a price increase before you roll it out

A price change is easier to test than most people expect, because you don't need a large experiment. You do need a clear rule, written down before you start, for what counts as a pass.

  1. Write your pass line before the test begins, and base it on the calculations from Step 4. For example: "Keep the new price if orders drop by less than 15% over four weeks."
  2. Pick a safe place to test, such as one product, one new bundle or new customers only. If possible, keep existing customers on their current price throughout the test period.
  3. Change only one thing at a time, because if you change the price and the photos together, you won't know which one caused the result.
  4. Run the test long enough to cover at least one normal sales cycle, since a week with a holiday or a big advertising push tells you very little.
  5. Check profit as well as orders, because fewer orders at a higher price can still mean more total profit.
  6. Tell existing customers early if the test passes, giving them clear notice, a date and the reason for the change.

Prompt: Check if my offer is ready for a price increase

You are reviewing whether my product is ready for a price increase. Be honest. I'd rather hear the weak points now.

My offer: [what the buyer gets, including delivery, guarantee and support].
Current price: [price]. Proposed price: [price]. Start date: [date].
Why I'm raising it: [costs went up, more value added, or other reason].
What buyers say: [paste review lines, complaints and questions].
Competitor offers, with prices and dates checked: [list].

Steps:
1. List what a buyer gets at the new price that they'd notice and value. Quote buyer words where you can.
2. List the weak points that could make a buyer leave at the new price.
3. Suggest up to three changes to the offer that add value without much cost.
4. Plan a small test: what to change, for whom, for how long and the pass line.
5. Draft a short, honest notice to existing customers with the date and the reason.

Rules: use only what I've given you. Don't invent buyer reactions, rival prices or figures. Mark any guess as a guess.

Worked example: Costco's 2024 membership fee rise

Costco, the warehouse store chain, sells memberships in tiers, and its public announcement from July 2024 shows several ideas from this guide at work. According to Costco's investor release:

  • From 1 September 2024, US and Canada Gold Star, Business and Business add-on members pay $65 a year, an increase of $5.
  • Executive membership went from $120 to $130, made up of the $65 primary membership plus a $65 Executive upgrade.
  • The maximum an Executive member can earn from the annual 2% Reward rose from $1,000 to $1,250.
  • Costco said the increases would affect about 52 million memberships, a little over half of them Executive.

Costco's separate customer service page adds that existing members saw the new fee from their September 2024 renewal onwards.

What a small business can learn

  • The top tier pays for itself for heavy buyers, at least on the reward alone. By our own calculation from the public figures, the $65 upgrade is covered by a 2% reward once a member spends $3,250 a year ($65 divided by 2%). Buyers who spend more therefore have a reason to choose the higher tier. This simple calculation ignores any other benefits that come with Executive membership.
  • The value rose along with the price, because the reward cap went up alongside the Executive fee. That gives the biggest buyers something new in return for the extra cost.
  • Notice came well before the change, since the release went out on 10 July 2024 for a 1 September start. Existing members only moved to the new fee at their next renewal.

We don't know how Costco set these prices or whether it used AI. To apply the same idea to your own business, run prompt 1 with your own figures. For example, say you sell a $20 monthly plan and a $35 plan with extra support. Ask your AI at what level of use the $35 plan pays off for a buyer, and what profit each plan leaves you. Common mistakes, and what to do instead

  • Cost plus a guess ignores value entirely, because adding 50% to cost says nothing about what buyers will pay. Use cost as the floor, then check what the product is worth to them.
  • Copying the cheapest rival is risky when you have higher costs or a better product. Compare like for like, and set a price that suits your own buyers and costs.
  • Forgetting hidden costs such as fees, returns and advertising quietly erodes profit, so use the full cost table in Step 1.
  • Trusting AI calculations without checking them can cost you money, so ask your AI to show every calculation, and check the important ones in a calculator or spreadsheet.
  • Discounting to fix a slow month reduces profit per order quickly, and the free discount profit calculator shows how many extra sales a discount needs before it pays off.
  • Raising prices without warning damages trust, so give regular customers advance notice and an honest explanation.

Questions people ask

What is the simplest way to price a product?

Add up every cost of one sale to find your floor, then check what similar products cost and what buyers say yours is worth. Choose a price between the two that leaves a healthy margin, and test it before you commit.

What is a good profit margin for a small business?

It depends on your industry and your costs, so there is no single right number. Work out what margin covers your overheads, your own pay and the advertising you need to grow, and then set prices that reach it.

How do I raise prices without losing customers?

Test the new price on a small scale first, with a pass line you set in advance. Where you can, add something buyers value, and give regular customers clear notice, a date and an honest reason.

Can ChatGPT help me set my prices?

Yes, it can help with the calculations and the scenarios, provided you give it your real costs and buyer notes. Ask it to show every calculation, and check the important ones yourself. It can't predict how your buyers will react to a new price, which is something only a real test can reveal.

Should I charge less than my competitors?

Not by default, although a lower price can work if your costs are genuinely lower too. Otherwise, you are better off competing on what buyers value most, and explaining on your page why your price is worth paying.
