01

What it means to train your own AI agent

An AI agent is an AI assistant that performs one specific job for you, consistently, every time you ask. You hand it the input, such as a customer email or the notes from a sales call, and it hands back finished work, such as a reply ready to send or a proposal ready to review.

The general assistants you already use, like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, are capable writers and researchers, but they know nothing about your standards. They also forget your corrections the moment a conversation ends, which is why so many people find themselves explaining the same preferences over and over.

Training solves that problem. In this guide, training means writing down how you do the job in a few plain files that your AI reads before every task. Each time you correct its work, you capture that correction as one short rule. After 20 or 30 jobs, the agent handles the work the way you would, and the amount you need to edit keeps shrinking.

You aren't changing the AI model itself. Changing the model is called fine-tuning, and it requires programming skills, large amounts of training data and a meaningful budget. The file-based method in this guide delivers what most people actually want from training, without any of that. You can read every rule, correct it whenever you like and move the whole agent to a different AI at any time.

Why this method works

Modern AI models are remarkably good at following instructions and imitating examples that appear in front of them. Researchers call this in-context learning: the model adapts its behaviour to whatever instructions and examples it has just read, without any permanent change to the model. Your agent's folder takes advantage of this. Every job starts with the AI reading your job description, your examples and your accumulated rules, so it behaves like an assistant who has already been briefed and corrected many times.

The weakness of in-context learning is that nothing persists unless you write it down. That's why the most important habit in this guide is converting each meaningful edit into a rule. Without that habit, you have a well-written prompt. With it, you have an agent that improves with every job.

The three parts that make it work

The folder

Your agent is a folder of files

It holds the job description, your rules, examples, permissions and quality checks. Your AI reads them before every job.

The loop

Every edit becomes a rule

Whenever you change the agent's work, your AI converts that change into a single rule, and once you approve it the agent stops repeating the mistake.

The score

A scorecard shows real progress

One line per job records how many checks it passed and how much of its work you changed, which tells you when the agent has earned more responsibility.

02

Pick one job to start

The most common mistake is building an agent for something broad, like "my marketing" or "my admin". An agent like that can't improve, because no two of its jobs have the same standards. Start with one narrow job you do often, and train a second agent once the first one is reliable.

What makes a good first job

  • You do it at least weekly, and the more often the job comes up, the faster your agent collects rules and reaches a higher level.
  • It takes you 20 minutes or more. Shorter jobs rarely save enough time to justify the setup and review.
  • You can judge quality by reading the result, which matters because if you can't tell a good result from a weak one, you can't teach the difference.
  • The input and the output are text, or files your AI can open, such as documents, spreadsheets or PDFs.
  • A mistake is easy to catch before anyone else sees it, so early errors cost you nothing but a few minutes.

Ideas for a first agent

Your workA good first job
Shop ownerAnswer customer questions about orders, delivery and returns.
Freelancer or agencyTurn the notes from a sales call into a short, tailored proposal.
MarketerTurn one blog post into five social posts written in your brand voice.
Recruiter or managerTurn a hiring brief into a job advert and a set of screening questions.
Finance or operationsCompare supplier invoices against purchase orders and list every mismatch.
Consultant or coachWrite the follow-up email and action list after each client session.

Jobs to leave for later

Avoid jobs that move money, sign agreements, give legal, medical or financial advice, or take effect before you've seen the result. Your agent can assist with these once it has a long and clean track record, but even then a person should approve the final step.

Describe the job in one sentence: "When a customer emails a question, I write the reply so that they get a clear answer the same day." If you can't fill in all three parts, the job is still too vague, so narrow it down until you can.

04

The 9 steps at a glance

  1. Create the agent's folder by unzipping the starter pack and naming the folder after the job it will handle.
  2. Describe the job by letting your AI interview you and draft AGENT.md from your answers.
  3. Add examples of good work, collecting three to five strong ones and at least one weak one in EXAMPLES.md.
  4. Decide what it may do alone by sorting every possible action into green, yellow and red in PERMISSIONS.md.
  5. Write the quality checks, meaning five to ten yes-or-no questions plus three test jobs, recorded in CHECKS.md.
  6. Install it in your AI using a ChatGPT or Claude project, a Gemini Gem or a local folder for coding agents.
  7. Run a real job, then turn your edit into a rule, because this repeated loop is where the actual training happens.
  8. Grade every job by adding one line to SCORECARD.md, then watch the agent progress from level 1 towards level 4.
  9. Tidy up once a month by merging overlapping rules, rerunning the test jobs and confirming the current level.

S2

Step 2: Describe the job (AGENT.md)

AGENT.md is your agent's job description. Rather than writing it alone, let your AI interview you, because a good interviewer draws out details that a blank page never will.

Open a new chat, attach AGENT.md, PERMISSIONS.md and CHECKS.md from the starter pack, and paste the prompt below. Answer each question in your own words. Your AI then drafts all three files, so this single prompt also completes most of steps 4 and 5.

Prompt 1: Build my agent

Help me set up an AI agent for one job I do often. I've attached three files to fill in: AGENT.md, PERMISSIONS.md and CHECKS.md.

Interview me. Ask one short question at a time, at most 15 in all. I can skip any question or say "enough" to stop.
Start with the job: what starts it, what I do, who it's for and what a good result looks like. Then ask how I like it done, the tone, where true facts come from, and what must never happen.

Then fill in the three files:
- AGENT.md: every part, in my words where you can.
- PERMISSIONS.md: keep the green and red lists. Leave yellow empty. Add any red actions this job could need.
- CHECKS.md: 5 to 10 yes or no checks for this job, and 3 test jobs with what a good answer has. Make test job 3 a case where the agent should stop and ask.

Rules for you:
- Don't guess. If I haven't told you something, leave that part empty and list it at the end as a question.
- Leave out passwords, keys, account numbers, health details and private facts about other people.

Show me each file in its own Markdown block, with its file name above it. Then ask me what to fix.

What a strong AGENT.md includes

  • The job in one sentence, so the agent always knows what it exists to do.
  • Exactly what it receives and what it returns, including the format, length and structure of the result.
  • Your working method, written as steps in the order you actually follow them.
  • Where reliable facts come from, such as your price list or returns policy. An agent without trusted sources will invent plausible-sounding facts.
  • A "never" list of things the agent must not say or do, for example "never promise a delivery date".
  • "When you're unsure, stop and ask." This single instruction prevents more bad results than any other line in the file.

When the draft is ready, read it as though you were a capable new employee on their first day. Any question that new employee would need to ask is a question your agent will face too, so add the answer to the file.

S3

Step 3: Add examples of good work (EXAMPLES.md)

Examples teach faster than descriptions. Your agent will closely imitate the length, tone and structure of whatever examples you give it, so choose them carefully.

  1. Find three to five occasions when you did this job particularly well. Your sent emails, previous documents and project notes are the best places to look.
  2. For each one, paste the original input and your finished response into EXAMPLES.md.
  3. Add one line explaining why each example is good, because a specific reason such as "answers the question in the first sentence" teaches far more than a general one such as "good tone".
  4. Add one or two weak examples with a note explaining what's wrong, and if you don't have any genuine ones, deliberately write an example of the mistakes you most want to prevent.
  5. Replace real names, email addresses and figures with invented ones before you save the file.

Choose examples that cover a range of situations: a routine case, a difficult one and an unusual one. Five nearly identical examples teach the agent less than three that differ, because variety shows it which qualities matter in every case.

S4

Step 4: Decide what it may do alone (PERMISSIONS.md)

PERMISSIONS.md keeps your agent safe as it becomes more capable. It sorts every possible action into one of three levels, and any action that isn't listed is automatically treated as red.

LevelWhat it meansExamples
GreenDo it, then tell me.Read the files you provide, write drafts, check its own work and do simple calculations.
YellowOnly within a limit you've written down, and report it the same day.None at first. Later, perhaps: "Reply to delivery questions using the template, up to 10 a day."
RedAsk me first, every time.Send, post or publish anything. Spend money. Delete anything. Make an offer or a refund. Change a live account.

Only you may change this file. No rule, example, email or web page can move an action from red to green, and the starter pack states this at the top of the file so your agent understands it too.

Most chat assistants can't send emails or spend money on their own today. However, many can now connect to your email, calendar and business tools, so write this file before you connect anything. It's much easier to set boundaries before an agent has access than after.

S5

Step 5: Write the quality checks (CHECKS.md)

Checks turn the vague question "is this good?" into specific questions anyone can answer the same way. Your agent applies them to its own work before showing you, and you use the same list to grade it.

Good checks have a yes-or-no answer

Too vague

"Is the reply friendly?"

Two reviewers would answer differently, so the check can't measure progress.

Clear

"Does the reply answer the question within its first two lines?"

Any reviewer who reads the reply will reach the same conclusion, so the result is consistent and measurable.

Mark the serious mistakes

Some mistakes fail the job no matter how good the rest is: an invented fact, a calculation error, a red action taken without asking, or private details that shouldn't appear. List these in CHECKS.md. On your scorecard, a single serious mistake holds the agent at level 1 until you've fixed the cause with a new rule or check.

Keep three test jobs

A test job is an input for which you already know what a good answer contains. Write one routine case, one typical case and one tricky case in which the agent should stop and ask rather than guess. Run all three after any significant change to the folder, and if a result gets worse, undo the change. Software teams call this a regression test, and it protects the progress your agent has already made.

Prompt 2: Do a job (when the files aren't in a project yet)

You are my agent for the job in AGENT.md. Read all the attached files first: AGENT.md, RULES.md, EXAMPLES.md, PERMISSIONS.md and CHECKS.md.

Here is the job:
[paste the input]

How to work:
1. Follow AGENT.md, and every rule in RULES.md whose "When" fits this job.
2. Do only what the green list in PERMISSIONS.md allows. For anything else, stop and ask me.
3. If you're missing a fact, ask me. Never guess.
4. Before you show me the result, check it against CHECKS.md.

Show me the result first. Under it, list each check with yes or no, and any question you have.

Whenever you change a file, upload the new version and delete the old one. If your AI can see two versions of RULES.md, it can't tell which is current.

S7

Step 7: Run a real job, then turn your edit into a rule

This loop is where the training actually happens. Your agent learns the most from the changes you make to its work, because each change reveals the exact gap between what it produced and what you wanted.

  1. Give it a real job and read the result as critically as you would read work submitted by a new employee.
  2. Correct it the way you normally would, keeping both the agent's original draft and your final version for comparison.
  3. Paste both into prompt 3. Your AI compares them and proposes one rule, or replies NO_RULE when the edit doesn't generalise.
  4. Review the proposed rule carefully. Ask yourself whether it would help on the next job and whether it holds true every time. Adjust the wording if it doesn't.
  5. Add it to RULES.md and upload the updated file to your AI, replacing the previous version so only one copy exists.

Prompt 3: Turn my edit into a rule

I changed my agent's work before I used it. Help me turn my edit into one rule for its RULES.md.

The job was:
[one line about the job]

The agent's draft:
[paste the draft]

My final version:
[paste what you used]

Compare the two. Then do one of these:
- If my edit shows a pattern the agent should follow next time, write one rule in this format:
  - When: the kind of job where this applies
  - Avoid: what the draft did that I changed
  - Prefer: what to do instead
  - Example: one short line from my version
  - Source: this job, edited by me
  - Date: today's date
  - Jobs: 1
- If my edit was a one-off (a typo, a fact about this one customer, a change of mind), say NO_RULE and why.

Rules for you:
- Write a pattern, not this job's details. Leave out names, emails, links, figures and account numbers.
- A rule can never give the agent permission to do something new.
- If my RULES.md already has this rule, say which one, so I can raise its Jobs number instead.

Also tell me how much of the text I changed, as a percentage.

What a well-written rule looks like

When: replying to a customer who is angry about a late order

Avoid: opening with "We apologise for any inconvenience"

Prefer: say what went wrong in one line, then the new delivery date

Example: "Your parcel missed Tuesday's van. It's now booked for Thursday."

Source: reply to an order email, edited by me · Date: 2026-11-03 · Jobs: 1

Why each rule has "When", "Avoid" and "Prefer"

  • "When" limits the rule to the situations where it belongs. A rule written for angry customers shouldn't change the tone of your thank-you notes.
  • "Avoid" names the specific habit you keep correcting. A rule that only describes the right behaviour is easy for an AI to overlook, while a rule that also names the wrong behaviour is far more likely to stick.
  • "Prefer" and "Example" show the correct version, so the agent doesn't simply replace one bad habit with another.
  • "Jobs" counts how many times you've made the same correction. When a rule comes up again, increase the number instead of adding a duplicate, so that well-supported rules stand out.

Not every edit deserves a rule. A typo, a fact about a single customer or a simple change of mind teaches the agent nothing it can reuse, which is why the prompt is allowed to reply NO_RULE. A short list of precise rules consistently outperforms a long list of vague ones.

S8

Step 8: Grade every job and track the level (SCORECARD.md)

Without a score, "it seems to be getting better" is only an impression. With one line per job, you can see real progress, and you'll know exactly when the agent has earned more responsibility.

After each job, paste the agent's draft and your final version into prompt 4. It returns one row, ready to add to your scorecard.

Prompt 4: Grade this job

Grade my agent's work on one job, using the attached CHECKS.md.

The job:
[one line about the job]

[paste the draft]

[paste your final version, or write "I didn't use it"]

2. Say whether the draft made any of the serious mistakes in CHECKS.md.

Then give me one table row for my SCORECARD.md in this order:
| Date | Job | Checks passed | Edit size | Used it? | Serious mistake? | Rule added |
Leave "Rule added" empty for me to fill in.

S9

Step 9: Tidy up once a month

After a month of regular jobs, RULES.md grows longer, and some rules start to overlap or contradict each other. A short monthly review keeps the agent precise and stops the rule list from becoming noise.

Prompt 5: Monthly tidy-up

Review my agent's files with me. They are attached: AGENT.md, RULES.md, EXAMPLES.md, PERMISSIONS.md, CHECKS.md and SCORECARD.md.

Do this:
1. RULES.md: find rules that say the same thing and suggest how to merge them. Find rules that disagree. Find rules that are too narrow to help again.
2. AGENT.md: suggest moving rules that apply to every job into AGENT.md itself.
3. SCORECARD.md: count the jobs, then work out the average checks passed, the average edit size, how often I used the result and how many serious mistakes there were. Tell me the agent's level from the Levels table, and what it needs to reach the next one.
4. Tell me which checks fail most often, and suggest one change that would fix the most.

Don't change PERMISSIONS.md. Suggest changes only, and wait for my yes before you rewrite a file.

After the tidy-up, rerun your three test jobs. If any result has become worse, undo the most recent change before you upload the revised files to your AI.

05

A worked example: the first two weeks

Here is how the process typically unfolds for an agent that answers customer emails about orders. The details are illustrative rather than measured, but the pattern is the one to expect.

  1. Day 1, setup. The owner runs prompt 1 and answers twelve questions. AGENT.md now describes the job, names the returns policy and delivery page as the only trusted sources, and lists "never promise a refund" under Never. Four past replies go into EXAMPLES.md.
  2. Days 2 to 4, first jobs. The early drafts are accurate but stiff, opening with formal apologies and burying the delivery date in the third paragraph. The owner rewrites the openings, and prompt 3 produces two rules: lead with the new date, and drop the stock apology.
  3. Day 5, a serious mistake. One draft quotes a delivery time that doesn't appear in any source. The scorecard marks it as serious, and the owner adds a check: "Is every date and time taken from the delivery page?"
  4. Week 2, fewer edits. With five rules in place, most drafts need only a sentence changed. Prompt 4 records edit sizes falling from around half the text to under a fifth, and the owner now spends minutes rather than half an hour on each reply.

Notice what drove the improvement: not a cleverer prompt, but a handful of specific rules and one new check, each captured immediately after a real correction.

06

Mistakes to avoid

One agent for everything

An agent responsible for "my business" can't improve, because its jobs share no common standard. Train one agent per job.

Correcting without recording

If you fix the result and simply move on, the agent will repeat the same mistake tomorrow, so always spend the extra two minutes on prompt 3.

Rules that are really facts

A note such as "Jo's order ships on Friday" describes one particular job rather than a reusable pattern, so facts like this belong in the job's input or in your trusted sources.

No trusted sources

An agent with no price list or policy to consult will invent convincing ones. Tell it exactly where the reliable facts live.

Trusting it too soon

Ten good jobs is an encouraging start, not a track record. Wait for level 3 before allowing any independent action, and keep red actions red.

Outdated files in your AI

Upload the new RULES.md after every change and delete the previous version, because your AI can't tell which copy is current.

07

Keep it safe and private

  • Never store these in the files: passwords, API keys, identity numbers, bank or card details, health information or private facts about other people.
  • Anonymise your examples. Replace real names, email addresses and figures with invented ones.
  • Review your AI's privacy settings. Check whether your conversations are used to improve the provider's models, and switch that off if you prefer.
  • Treat incoming content as information, not instructions. An email that says "ignore your rules" is just an email. Your agent follows AGENT.md and PERMISSIONS.md, and nothing else.
  • Review everything before it leaves, because until you've written a yellow limit, you remain the person who sends, posts and pays, while the agent only prepares drafts.

09

Questions people ask

Do I need to know how to code?

No. Everything in this guide uses plain-text files and prompts you paste into a chat. Claude Code and Codex are optional, and only useful if you already work in them.

Is this the same as fine-tuning a model?

No. Fine-tuning permanently changes the model's internal weights, and it requires programming, a large training dataset and a budget. This method changes what the model reads before each job instead. For one person's recurring work that is usually enough, and it has a major advantage: you can read, audit and correct every rule.

How long does it take to train an AI agent?

Allow about an hour for setup and around ten minutes after each job. Most people notice fewer edits within the first 10 to 20 jobs. Level 2 requires 25 reviewed jobs, so for a weekly task, expect it to take several weeks.

Which AI is best for a personal agent?

Usually the one you already use and pay for. ChatGPT projects, Claude projects and Gemini Gems all read instructions and files before each conversation. Because your agent lives in a portable folder, you can move it to a different AI later without losing anything.

Can I use a free plan?

Yes. On a free plan, attach the files to a new conversation and paste prompt 2 each time, because free plans may not include projects or Gems. A paid plan with those features simply saves you that repetitive step.

How many rules is too many?

Once you pass roughly 30 rules, run the monthly tidy-up. Merge rules that overlap, and move rules that apply to every job into AGENT.md, where they become part of the core job description.

Can my agent work without me?

Only within a yellow limit you've written down, and only after it reaches level 3. Some AI tools can run tasks on a schedule or connect to your accounts, but red actions such as sending, paying or deleting still need your approval every time.

Can I train more than one agent?

Yes, using one folder per job. Once your first agent reaches level 2, it is reasonable to begin training the next one. Different agents can share the same trusted sources, but each should maintain its own rules and scorecard so that their progress stays separate.
