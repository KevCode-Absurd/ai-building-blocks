# Final Interview Guide: Head Coach (New Location)

**Format:** 45 minutes, structured, one or two interviewers
**Use:** Ask every candidate the same eight questions in the same order. Score each answer on its own, straight after the interview and before you discuss the candidate with anyone else.

---

## Before you start

**Timing**

| Segment | Minutes |
|---|---|
| Welcome, role overview, how the interview works | 3 |
| Questions 1–8 (about 4–5 minutes each, including follow-ups) | 36 |
| Candidate's questions | 5 |
| Close and next steps | 1 |

**How to probe:** for behavioural questions, ask for one real example. Then use follow-ups like "What did *you* do?", "What happened next?" and "How did you know it worked?" For scenario questions, let the candidate talk it through. Then add one complication, such as "And then the delivery is late too."

**Keeping it legal and job-related**

- **Never ask about:** age or graduation year, marital or family status, children or childcare, pregnancy, health, disability or medical history, religion, national origin, citizenship or immigration status, race, sexual orientation, gender identity, arrest record, or pay history.
- **If a candidate volunteers any of this,** don't follow up and don't write it down. Steer back to the question.
- **Schedule:** say the role's real requirements (for example, early opens, closes and weekends) and ask everyone the same thing: *"Can you meet this schedule?"* Nothing more.
- **Work authorization and accommodations** are handled by the recruiting and HR process, not in this interview.
- **Write down what the candidate said and did,** not impressions like "great energy" or "not a culture fit."

## The questions

### 1. Team leadership: turning a team around

**Ask:** "Tell me about a time you took over a team or a shift that was underperforming. What did you change first, and what happened?"

**A strong answer includes:**
- A specific starting point with evidence, such as speed of service, turnover, audit scores or guest complaints
- Listening first: talking to the team and working shifts alongside them before making changes
- Clear expectations, set out in a team meeting and in one-on-ones
- A few priorities, not ten
- Holding people accountable fairly, including a strong performer who wasn't meeting standards
- A measurable result, and what they'd do differently

**Red flag:** Blames the previous manager or "bad people" and describes replacing most of the team as the main fix, with no coaching.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No real example, or vague ("I motivated them") | Real example, but the fix was mostly "work harder" or turnover. Thin results | Clear diagnosis, set expectations, coached people, measurable improvement | All of 3, plus built leaders who kept results going without them, and reflects honestly on mistakes |

---

### 2. Hiring hourly team members for an opening

**Ask:** "We're opening this location in about eight weeks and need to hire most of the hourly team. Walk me through how you'd do it."

**A strong answer includes:**
- A headcount plan worked back from projected volume and hours of operation, with a buffer for early turnover
- Several sources of candidates: referrals, local outreach, job boards and hiring events
- A consistent, structured interview, with the same questions and scoring for every candidate
- Fast follow-up so good candidates aren't lost to other offers
- Hiring for attitude and reliability, with skills trained afterwards
- Planned training before opening, including practice service runs
- A diverse mix of strengths across positions and shifts

**Red flag:** Hires on gut feel or "I can tell in five minutes," or describes screening people out on personal characteristics rather than job requirements.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No plan. "Post a job and see who applies" | Has a basic process but no headcount math or timeline. Interviews informally | Headcount plan, several sources, structured interviews, quick offers, training plan | All of 3, plus has opened or staffed a location before and can describe what they'd do differently, including handling early turnover |

---

### 3. Developing hourly team members

**Ask:** "Tell me about someone on an hourly team you developed into a shift lead or manager. What did you do, and how long did it take?"

**A strong answer includes:**
- How they spotted the person's potential, through behaviour as well as speed
- A concrete development plan: stretch tasks, cross-training, leading a shift with backup
- Regular feedback and check-ins
- Letting the person make and learn from mistakes
- The outcome: a promotion, plus what the person went on to do
- Ideally, more than one example, or a habit of planning who could step up next

**Red flag:** Can't name anyone they've developed, or keeps strong people in place because "I can't afford to lose them on the line."

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No example, or development was "they learned on the job" | One example, but light on what they did. Mostly informal | Clear example with a plan, feedback and a promotion result | Several people promoted. Has a repeatable way of planning who steps up next, and talks about it as a core part of the job |

### 4. Food safety

**Ask:** "You arrive to open and the walk-in cooler is reading 46°F. Walk me through the next hour."
*Follow-up if time allows:* "A guest tells the line they have a severe nut allergy. What should the team do?"

**A strong answer includes:**
- Checking the product temperature with a calibrated probe, not just the display
- Finding out how long the product has been above 41°F, using the temperature logs and when it was last checked
- Moving product to working cold storage
- Discarding anything that can't be confirmed safe under the time and temperature rules ("when in doubt, throw it out")
- Calling for repair, logging everything and telling their manager
- Adjusting prep and the menu, being open with the team, and opening safely late rather than unsafely on time
- **Allergy follow-up:** fresh gloves, clean utensils and surfaces, ingredients from fresh containers where possible, checking with a leader, and being honest with the guest if the risk can't be removed

**Red flag:** "Turn the thermostat down and open on time" without checking product temperature or how long it's been warm, or treating allergy requests as a preference.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opens without checking the product, or shows no knowledge of safe holding temperatures | Knows something is wrong but the steps are incomplete (no logs, no discard decision) | Probes the product, checks how long it's been warm, discards correctly, gets it repaired and logs it. Handles the allergy step correctly | All of 3, plus explains how they build food safety into daily habits (logs, line checks, training, the illness reporting policy), and holds a food protection manager certification or knows its requirements well |

### 5. Lunch rush with two people out

**Ask:** "It's 11:15. Two team members have called out, lunch starts at 11:30, and digital orders are already stacking up. What do you do?"
*Complication:* "Twenty minutes in, one station runs out of a key prepped ingredient."

**A strong answer includes:**
- Quick triage: reassign positions to the strongest setup for the people available, with the leader stepping onto the line
- Calling for cover from people off shift or a nearby location
- Managing digital order flow, such as adjusting pickup times or pausing if wait times become unrealistic, rather than making guests wait at the counter
- Simplifying where possible
- Talking to the team calmly
- Handling the stock-out by prepping in quick bursts, telling guests about substitutes and updating the digital menu
- Afterwards, looking at the root cause: call-out patterns and how deep the bench is

**Red flag:** Panics, blames the team, or lets quality and food safety slip ("just push it out").

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| No plan, or plans to "just work faster" | Jumps on the line but doesn't manage the flow of orders or ask for cover | Reassigns positions, calls for cover, manages digital wait times, keeps standards, handles the stock-out | All of 3, plus fixes the root cause afterwards (cross-training, on-call bench, prep levels) and describes a real rush they ran this way |

### 6. Recovering a guest's bad experience

**Ask:** "A regular comes to the counter upset. Her digital order was wrong for the second time this week, and she says she's done with us. How do you handle it?"

**A strong answer includes:**
- Listening without interrupting
- Apologising sincerely without making excuses
- Fixing it right away, by remaking the order and prioritising it
- Doing something extra that fits the situation, within policy
- Using her name and following up
- Then fixing the cause, such as a station or label problem, ticket accuracy, or a coaching moment with the team member involved
- Checking that it doesn't happen a third time

**Red flag:** Argues about whose fault it was, offers a refund without solving the problem, or doesn't think about why it happened twice.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defensive, or makes the guest prove it | Apologises and remakes, but stops there | Listens, apologises, fixes, makes it right, and finds the root cause | All of 3, plus closes the loop with the guest later and turns the root cause into a team habit or a process change, with a real example |

---

### 7. Managing labor

**Ask:** "Last period your labor cost ran four points over target while sales were flat. How would you find out why, and what would you do?"

**A strong answer includes:**
- Comparing scheduled and actual hours, by day and by hour
- Looking for overtime, early clock-ins and late clock-outs, and training hours
- Checking whether the schedule matched the sales forecast by daypart (morning, lunch, afternoon, dinner)
- Using a measure such as sales per labor hour
- Fixing the causes: adjusting the schedule to forecast, sending people home sensibly on slow afternoons, tightening clock-in practices, and planning overtime
- Protecting guest experience and team stability while cutting, rather than just slashing hours

**Red flag:** "Cut hours across the board," or doesn't know how labor percentage is calculated. Also a red flag: any suggestion of off-the-clock work or skipped breaks.

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Can't explain labor % or how to investigate it | Knows the measure but the only fix is cutting hours | Clear investigation (scheduled vs actual, overtime, forecast by daypart) and targeted fixes that protect service | All of 3, plus has managed a P&L with real results (for example, "brought labor from X to Y over Z months") and explains the trade-off with team retention |

---

### 8. Managing food cost

**Ask:** "Your actual food cost is two points above your theoretical. Walk me through how you'd find the gap."

**A strong answer includes:**
- Understanding actual vs theoretical cost
- Checking that the inventory count is accurate first
- Reviewing the waste log
- Checking portioning at each station
- Looking at prep over-production versus prep levels
- Checking receiving accuracy, supplier credits and price changes
- Looking for comps and voids, and possible theft
- Turning findings into action: retraining on portions, adjusting prep levels to forecast, accurate counts, and a weekly review rhythm

**Red flag:** Doesn't know the difference between actual and theoretical food cost, or the answer is "order less."

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Doesn't understand food cost measures | Names one or two causes, with no method | Methodical: checks counts, waste, portions, prep, receiving, then targeted fixes and follow-up | All of 3, plus has closed a real gap and can explain the routines (weekly counts, waste reviews, prep levels) that kept it closed |
