# Decision memo: End-of-lesson recap screen

**Team:** Learning
**Decision needed:** Ship the recap screen to all learners, or not
**Note:** All test figures are hypothetical exercise numbers, not Duolingo data.

## Summary

- **Recommendation: Don't ship as built. Run one follow-up test (described below) before deciding.**
- Day-7 retention rose slightly (54.1% to 54.6%). The reported p = 0.08 doesn't meet our usual bar, and it needs checking: at 400,000 learners per arm, a simple test would put this gap far past significance.
- Lessons per learner per week fell 4.5% (11.2 to 10.7). That's the clearest effect in the test, and it's a cost.
- Most learners (63%) skipped the recap. Among those who viewed it, accuracy on the missed words rose from 71% to 79% in the next session. That figure doesn't yet prove the recap caused the gain, because we have no control comparison for it.
- Shipping now would lock in a known engagement loss for a learning gain we haven't shown. Not shipping costs little, since the feature can come back once we have better evidence.

## The result

| Metric | Control | Test | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day-7 retention | 54.1% (216,400 of 400,000) | 54.6% (218,400 of 400,000) | +0.5 pts (p = 0.08, as reported) |
| Lessons per learner per week | 11.2 | 10.7 | −0.5 (−4.5%) |
| Recap skip rate | — | 63% (about 148,000 viewed) | — |
| Next-session accuracy on missed words (viewers only) | — | 71% → 79% | +8 pts, within group |

The test ran for four weeks with 400,000 learners in each arm.

## How to read each metric

**Day-7 retention.** A 0.5-point gain would be worth having. But the reported p-value doesn't fit the sample size. A plain two-proportion test on the counts above gives a pooled rate of 54.35%, a standard error of 0.11 points and z ≈ 4.5. That means p well below 0.001, with a 95% interval of +0.28 to +0.72 points. A p of 0.08 suggests a different analysis: clustering, a variance correction, a sequential-testing adjustment, or a smaller analyzed group (for example, only learners who joined in week one). Before anyone cites this number, confirm which method was used. Until then, treat retention as "possibly slightly up, not confirmed."

**Lessons per learner per week.** This is the most reliable result. A 4.5% drop across 400,000 learners is very unlikely to be noise, though we should confirm the variance. Two explanations matter:

- *Time cost.* The recap adds a screen to every lesson. If learners keep a fixed daily time budget, they finish fewer lessons. Total minutes might be flat, and the loss is partly a counting effect.
- *Friction.* An extra screen at the moment of finishing may make "one more lesson" less likely. That is a real engagement loss.

We can tell these apart by checking minutes per learner, and by splitting the lesson drop between viewers and skippers. If skippers also lost lessons, even a skippable screen is costing us.

**Skip rate.** 63% skipping means most learners don't value the recap as built. Whatever benefit exists reaches about a third of the test arm, and the average effect on the whole arm is diluted.

**Accuracy among viewers.** This is the most encouraging number and the weakest evidence. Three problems:

1. *No control comparison.* We measured the same learners before and after. We didn't compare control learners' next-session accuracy on *their* most-missed words.
2. *Regression to the mean.* These are, by design, the words a learner got wrong most. Accuracy on the worst words tends to rise next time anyway.
3. *Self-selection.* Learners who chose to view the recap may be more motivated, and would likely improve faster without it.

The real effect could be the full 8 points, a fraction of it, or close to zero.

## The trade-off between learning and engagement

The recap aims to help people learn by putting missed words back in front of them. The cost shows up in engagement: fewer lessons, which feed streaks, XP and the habits that keep people coming back.

We shouldn't treat the lesson count as sacred. If learners do 4.5% fewer lessons but remember more, that may be a good trade. But only if the learning gain is real and lasting. Right now we have a measured cost and an unmeasured benefit.

Review of missed words doesn't have to sit on an extra screen. It could happen inside the next lesson, as practice. Learners can also already review mistakes on demand: Mistakes sessions in the Practice tab are now free for all learners (Duolingo blog, 18 Feb 2026). So the recap partly duplicates a review learners can already choose, and the follow-up should check whether it pulls people away from the Practice tab.

## Risks

**If we ship now:**
- We lock in a roughly 4.5% drop in lessons per week across all learners. That could affect streaks, leaderboards and long-term retention in ways a 4-week test can't show.
- We'd claim a learning win we haven't proven. If it is regression to the mean, we'd pay an engagement cost for nothing.
- A review screen after every lesson may crowd out the Practice tab, where learners choose their own review. The test didn't measure that.

**If we don't ship:**
- If the learning gain is real, we delay a benefit for the third of learners who use the recap.
- We may give up a small retention gain, if the 0.5-point lift holds up.
- The team may read "not shipped" as "learning features lose to engagement." We should be clear this is about evidence, not priorities.

Not shipping is the cheaper mistake. It's fully reversible, and a follow-up test costs a few weeks.

## Recommendation

Don't ship the recap as built. First:

1. Confirm how the retention p-value was calculated.
2. Break down the lesson drop by viewers vs. skippers, and check minutes per learner.
3. Compute next-session accuracy on most-missed words for control learners, so the 71% → 79% figure has a real baseline.

If step 3 shows a clear learning gain over control and step 2 shows the lesson drop is mostly a time effect, reconsider shipping. Otherwise, run the follow-up test.

## Follow-up test

**Arms**, randomized at the learner level:
- **A. Control:** no recap.
- **B. Current recap:** end-of-lesson screen, as tested.
- **C. Built-in review:** no extra screen. The three most-missed words return as exercises early in the learner's next lesson.

**Primary metric:** next-session accuracy on each learner's most-missed words, across *all* learners in each arm, not just recap viewers. That removes self-selection and regression to the mean.

**Guardrails:** lessons per learner per week (no more than a 1% drop vs. control); minutes per learner per day; day-7 and day-14 retention; Practice tab sessions per learner.

**Also measure:** accuracy on the same words a week later, to see whether the gain lasts. Split results by Practice tab users vs. non-users, new vs. experienced learners, and baseline accuracy.

**Sample size.** Inputs: baseline 71%, smallest gain worth acting on 2 points, 80% power, and 5% significance split across two comparisons with control (2.5% each). That needs about 9,600 learners per arm. The guardrails need far more, so keep 400,000 per arm and run four full weeks, as before. That also covers day-14 retention for learners who join in the first two weeks.

| Result after four weeks | Call |
|---|---|
| C beats A by 2+ points on accuracy, and stays inside the lesson guardrail | Ship C |
| B beats A by 2+ points, beats C, and stays inside the lesson guardrail | Ship B |
| Neither beats A by 2 points | Ship neither; keep mistakes review in the Practice tab |
| An arm beats A on accuracy but breaks the lesson guardrail | Don't ship it; redesign and retest |
